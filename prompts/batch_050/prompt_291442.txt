I'm a graduate student preparing a short paper for my department's workshop on distributive ethics, and I'd like detailed comments before I circulate it. The paper defends prioritarianism against the leveling-down objection, but it does so by a route I haven't seen in the standard back-and-forth (which I know mainly at the level of the objection most associated with Derek Parfit's work on equality and priority, plus the usual replies), and I want to know whether the route works or whether I've just renamed the problem. The draft is below; my specific questions come after it.

Draft: "Priority Is About People, Not Shapes"

Prioritarianism holds that a benefit matters more, morally, the worse off its recipient is. The leveling-down objection says this has an absurd implication: if the better-off are lowered to the level of the worse-off, and no one is made better off, the resulting world is in one respect better, since priority-weighted value no longer registers the unmet claims of the worse-off as strongly, or registers less inequality of weight, or something in that neighborhood. The standard prioritarian reply is to grant the point and say the leveled world is better only in one respect and worse all things considered, since no one benefits. My paper argues that both sides of this exchange concede too much. The objection never gets going, because prioritarianism, properly understood, does not rank worlds by their shape at all.

I distinguish two things the view could be. On the state-ranking reading, prioritarianism is a function from distributions of well-being to impersonal values, with weights applied to levels. On the claims reading, prioritarianism is a principle about how to weigh benefits and burdens to persons when we choose among actions: a unit of well-being counts for more when it accrues to someone worse off. I argue only the state-ranking reading is vulnerable to leveling down, because only it evaluates outcomes described purely by their profiles, with no reference to how anyone fared across the options. On the claims reading, leveling down is a pure loss imposed on the better-off with no corresponding gain to anyone, and the principle is simply silent about pure losses, the way a rule about how to divide a pie is silent about whether to burn the pie.

The objector will say silence is damning here: common sense condemns leveling down, so a view that cannot condemn it is incomplete. I accept this and supply the missing piece, which I call the No Free Losses principle: a change that makes some people worse off and no one better off is never an improvement, full stop. I argue this is not an ad hoc patch, because it expresses the same person-regarding thought that motivates priority in the first place: morality is about what happens to people, and a world in which nothing good happens to anyone is not better than one in which something good happens to someone, whatever the shape.

The harder test is what I call the decaying surplus. A is at 100 units of well-being, B at 40, and A's extra 60 rests on some advantage that cannot be transferred or shared, only destroyed (a talent, say, or a sensory capacity B lacks the physiology for). Destroying it levels down. My framework gives the right verdict: no improvement. But then I consider the uncomfortable variation in which B's well-being would actually rise from 40 to 45 if A were brought down, purely because B's sense of inferiority would vanish. I bite the bullet: then leveling down is in one respect better, because it is a genuine benefit to B, and weighting it by B's absolute position is exactly what prioritarianism recommends. I add that if B does not know A exists, the benefit disappears and we are back to a pure loss. My tentative conclusion is that the leveling-down objection targets a telic egalitarianism of outcome-shape and leaves the claims-based prioritarian untouched, at the price of admitting that prioritarianism needs supplementing by something like No Free Losses.

Here is what I'd like comments on. First, accuracy: have I mischaracterized the standard objection or the standard "in one respect better" reply, and is my state-ranking versus claims distinction a fair reconstruction of views people actually hold, or a strawman in one direction? Second, the objection I worry about most: does the No Free Losses principle smuggle in a person-affecting restriction that will generate its own familiar trouble in different-people and different-number cases, and if so, can you sketch the most damaging version of that trouble in a paragraph? Third, does my bullet-biting on the inferiority-feelings case quietly collapse the view back into the shape-based reasoning it was built to escape? Fourth, structure: the decaying surplus section currently does double duty as both a defense and a self-attack; should it be split, shortened, or moved? Finally, please flag the single weakest move in the paper, independent of the other questions, and tell me whether the overall thesis is interesting enough to be worth repairing.
