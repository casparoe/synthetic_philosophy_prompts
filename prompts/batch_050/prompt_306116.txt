I teach a 75-minute practical ethics seminar for 16 advanced undergraduates, and the aggregation problem has suddenly become concrete for us. Last week we discussed a fictional campus budget decision in which administrators could either fund one costly accommodation that prevents a severe, ongoing burden for a single disabled employee or fund many inexpensive improvements that spare hundreds of people minor inconveniences. Half the class immediately treated the larger total benefit as decisive; the other half insisted that the one person with the strongest complaint could not simply be outvoted by a crowd. When I introduced Scanlon’s contractualism afterward, both sides reduced it to the slogan that numbers do not count, which misses the harder question of when the number and strength of individual complaints can properly matter.

Design an original classroom game that makes students encounter this difficulty before I name it. The activity should involve policies that distribute different kinds of burdens across the group, confidential role or complaint cards, at least one decision made without knowing which burden one will personally bear, and a later round in which some information becomes public and participants must try to justify a policy to one another. Please use low-stakes classroom tokens rather than real money or actual discomfort, and build the rules so that an intuitively compelling utilitarian choice eventually collides with an individual complaint that participants feel is not fairly answerable merely by adding up benefits. Include one round involving many trivial burdens against one serious burden and another involving roughly comparable burdens on different numbers of people, so the class sees that rejecting crude summation need not mean treating numbers as always irrelevant.

Give me a runnable plan rather than a menu of possible activities: precise setup, materials, roles, rules, scoring or victory conditions, a minute-by-minute timeline, likely group divisions, and instructions for preventing one confident student from dominating the deliberation. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Scanlon’s justifiability-to-each-person framework, the individualist restriction, and the aggregation problem. Take a definite position on how the activity should present the role of numbers in contractualist reasoning, rather than merely listing competing interpretations, and explain why that presentation is the most defensible for teaching purposes. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to respond without lecturing too early, and which feature of Scanlon’s theory the game inevitably distorts or cannot reproduce.
