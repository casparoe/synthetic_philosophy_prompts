So I run the fact-check desk at a small science-and-ideas magazine, and the thing that eats up most of our time isn't outright falsehoods. It's claims that are technically true but engineered to leave a wrong impression. A politician says their office "found no evidence of misconduct" when nobody ever investigated. A supplement company says its product "was associated with better memory in a clinical study" when the study was eleven people and no control group. A press release says a researcher "has not responded to requests for comment" sent at 11pm the night before publication. Literally defensible, practically a lie. Our legal review keeps shrugging at these because they're true, but our readers get misled all the same.

I want a reusable procedure my checkers can run on any quoted claim like this in under fifteen minutes, grounded in Grice's account of implicature and speaker meaning -- the maxims (quantity, quality, relevance, manner) being the best worked-out story I know of for how a true sentence can communicate a falsehood. We check maybe ten features a month and this comes up three or four times, each time with a public correction on the line if we call it wrong. The procedure should be a sequence of tests in order: how to extract what the sentence literally commits the speaker to versus what an ordinary reader will take away, how to use cancellability as a diagnostic, what to do when the conversational context is contested (a press release isn't a conversation -- Grice wrote about cooperative talk, so tell me how much of the framework survives in adversarial settings like PR and legal statements), and what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. Be explicit about which of Grice's distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped for a checker who hasn't read him, and mark the points where the procedure has to hand off to a human judgment call rather than pretend it's mechanical.

Then run it on three cases. First: the "no evidence of misconduct" one above. Second: a scientist telling us "I wouldn't rule out" a speculative explanation of their results, knowing a journalist will print it as an endorsement. Third, the one I want you to be ruthless about: one of our own headlines, "Study links screen time to teen anxiety," over a piece about a correlational study where the authors themselves were careful. If the procedure convicts our headline, say so -- I need to know it has teeth, not just that it nails our enemies. Also tell me what the procedure can't settle at all: my guess is it can flag misleadingness but not intent, and can't tell a lie from an honest but clumsy phrasing, but I want that stated precisly rather than assumed.
