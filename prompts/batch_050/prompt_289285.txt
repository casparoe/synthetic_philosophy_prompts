Write a satirical white paper, roughly 700–900 words, issued by a fictional efficiency think tank — call it something like the Institute for Evidentiary Rationalism — proposing that courts dispense with individualized evidence and decide cases directly on the statistics. The piece should be written in the earnest, cost-conscious register of a management consultancy (executive summary, projected savings, implementation phases, an objections-and-replies section), and its internal logic should be impeccable: the Institute simply takes seriously what the law already says, namely that civil liability requires only a preponderance of the evidence, and asks why anyone would pay for witnesses when base rates are cheaper and more reliable.

The proposal should build on the canonical problem cases, presented as the Institute's own pilot studies rather than as borrowed thought experiments: the bus company that operates four-fifths of the buses on the road where the plaintiff was forced into a ditch, against whom judgment should therefore enter without further inquiry (with a footnote deploring the sentimental judges who balk at this); the concert promoter who discovers that fewer than half of the thousand attendees held tickets and accordingly sues all thousand, each of whom was more likely than not a gatecrasher; and, in the boldest phase, criminal trials, where the Institute, having fixed proof beyond reasonable doubt at 95 percent, proposes conviction whenever the properly chosen reference class yields a posterior above that line, with the resulting five percent of wrongful convictions rebranded in a chart as the system's acceptable error budget.

The satire should land the real debate without ever lecturing about it. The jokes must come from fidelity: the Institute's arguments should be ones a thoroughgoing probabilist could actually endorse, so that the reader's discomfort tracks the genuine puzzle of what naked statistics lack. To that end, include a passage in which the Institute selects reference classes with cheerful cynicism (a handy table of available partitions, depending on which posterior the client requires); a rebuttal section pointing out, entirely fairly, that the eyewitnesses courts prefer are demonstrably less reliable than actuarial tables — the one argument in the piece that should genuinely sting; a dismissal of the worry that the same statistic would obtain whether or not this defendant did anything as unquantifiable metaphysical anxiety; and a deadpan note that insurance markets already run exactly this system, from which the Institute draws the natural conclusion. No winking, no named real scholars, no strawmanning: the Institute should lose the argument only in the reader's gut, never on paper.
