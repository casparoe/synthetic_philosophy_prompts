I belong to a small philosophy reading group, and this semester we have been working through Hume's essay Of Miracles in his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, including the famous passage about the Indian prince who refused to believe that water could turn solid, and Hume's hypothetical about a universally attested resurrection, where he says he would still doubt. One member of our group, Dana, insists that Hume's reasoning gives a straightforward mechanical test for when to believe testimony about extraordinary events, and she has been cheerfully applying her version of the test to cases we throw at her. Her verdicts so far: when a colleague claimed to have run into a famous actor at the farmers' market, Dana believed her without hesitation; when a news outlet reported that a particular individual had won the national lottery at odds of roughly one in three hundred million, Dana believed it, remarking that somebody had to win; when three independent witnesses at a retreat reported watching a faith healer restore a withered arm, Dana dismissed the story outright, saying deception or shared hysteria was vastly more likely than the event; when a major physics laboratory announced it had measured particles arriving slightly faster than light and asked other labs to check its work, Dana withheld belief and predicted a retraction (she was eventually vindicated); and when a friend with an excellent track record for honesty reported that his chronic back pain had vanished overnight after a healer's treatment, Dana accepted that the pain might genuinely be gone, since spontaneous remission is within the natural order, but refused the supernatural explanation.

At our next meeting I plan to put the following case to her: a neighbor, known to enjoy practical jokes but also known to be scrupulously truthful when asked directly, reports soberly that he shuffled an ordinary deck of cards that morning and it came out in perfect factory order, all four suits in sequence from ace to king. I want to predict Dana's verdict before she gives it, and I would like your help reasoning it out. What is your prediction, and which rule do you take her to be implicitly running? I can see at least two candidates that both fit her track record: a rule that sorts events by type, accepting any report of an event within the experienced natural order no matter how improbable, and rejecting reports contrary to uniform experience no matter how numerous or credible the witnesses; and a rule that weighs the antecedent improbability of the event against the probability that the testimony is false, with no absolute barrier in either direction. These two rules seem to diverge on my shuffle case, and the divergence interests me because of her remark in the lottery case, since a particular shuffle outcome is vastly more improbable than a particular lottery winner. So: give me your prediction and your reasoning, explain how each candidate rule handles each of her five earlier verdicts, and tell me whether the rule you attribute to Dana is actually Hume's maxim or a corruption of it, given how Hume himself treats extraordinary events that merely lack precedent in someone's experience, as with the Indian prince. Finally, if my shuffle case does not in fact discriminate between the candidate rules as well as I think it does, construct the case that would.
