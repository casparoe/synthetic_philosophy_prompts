I'm organizing a two-day workshop on Athenian democracy, and the two keynote participants have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. One defends what I'll call the *rhetoric-and-passion view*: the assembly's decisions at Mytilene in 427, the vote for the Sicilian expedition in 415, and the conviction of Socrates in 399 are three faces of a single phenomenon -- mass decision-making is structurally vulnerable to whoever speaks most effectively in the moment, and the reversal of the Mytilene decree the very next day is not exculpatory but rather further proof that the demos moves with the last speech it heard. The other defends the *information-and-design view*: each episode has a structural explanation that does not impugn collective judgment as such -- the Mytilene reversal shows a working self-correction mechanism triggered by new information and reflection, Sicily was an information and accountability failure that any regime type would have faced, and Socrates' trial was a legal-religious conflict processed through courts, not a plebiscite, so it tells us little about assembly democracy at all.

Write the joint statement these two could both sign at the end of the workshop. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: facts about the three episodes and about the relevant institutions (assembly, courts, generals, speakers) that both accept, plus points of interpretive agreement -- for instance, any episode features both treat as common ground rather than as contested evidence.

2. A disagreements table: each row states one residual disagreement in two columns, where each column is worded so that its own side would accept it as a fair statement of its position. No row should be a strawman of either side.

3. A cruxes section. Before listing the cruxes, both parties have agreed to adopt a new analytic device: classify each episode along three dimensions -- (i) the time between the initial decision and the point of no return, (ii) whether significant new information arrived during that window, and (iii) whether a reversal or revision mechanism was available. For each crux, state the sub-question, say why settling it would move at least one side, and use the three-dimension classification to make each side commit to a prediction: e.g., what the rhetoric-and-passion view predicts about fast, information-poor decisions with no reversal mechanism, versus what the information-and-design view predicts about the same class. Include at least one crux concerning whether the three episodes are one phenomenon or three, and one concerning the proper comparison class (what regime, real or hypothetical, the failures should be measured against).

4. A short closing section identifying any part of their long-running dispute that the collaboration revealed to be verbal rather than substantive, and what each side now concedes would count as evidence against its own view.

Do not include citations, references to specific modern scholars, or quotations from ancient sources; phrase everything in terms of the events and arguments, and I will supply scholarly apparatus myself. If you find that one of the cruxes cannot be turned into a disagreement with distinguishable predictions, say so explicitly rather than forcing it.

End with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating which single crux the document treats as most likely to actually move one of the two sides, and why.
