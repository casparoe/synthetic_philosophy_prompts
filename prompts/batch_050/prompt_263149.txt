Build me a thought experiment in science, from scratch, that's engineered to pit two accounts of how thought experiments work against each other. On the first account (call it the reconstructionist view), every thought experiment is really just an argument in disguise: it yields knowledge only insofar as you can reconstruct it as reasoning from premises you already accept, plus logic. On the second (call it the insight view), at least some thought experiments give you a grip on how nature has to be that goes beyond anything derivable from what you believed beforehand.

Constraints: use only everyday physics — levers, buckets, sealed rooms, falling rocks, floating bodies — nothing relativistic or quantum, nothing a bright teenager couldn't follow. The person running the experiment must genuinely not accept the crucial premise in advance (no smuggling the conclusion in as one of their prior beliefs). And the conclusion has to actually be right, or at least genuinely well-supported, not merely intuitive.

After the scenario: state what verdict each account gives about whether your protagonist comes away with knowledge, and why. Then identify which feature of your case is doing the work in making the two accounts diverge — and give the reconstructionist's best countermove, i.e. how they'd try to rewrite your case as an ordinary argument anyway, and whether you think that rewrite succeeds or changes the subject.
