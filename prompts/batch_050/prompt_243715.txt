I'm drafting a short position paper for a reading group on bounded rationality, and before I circulate it I want to stress-test the package of views I've assembled on logical uncertainty. I've been influenced by the logical induction literature but haven't worked through the technical details, so it's entirely possible I've stitched together commitments that can't coexist. Here is what I currently hold, stated as plainly as I can manage:

(1) All of my beliefs, including my beliefs about purely mathematical claims, should be representable as probabilities and updated on evidence — where I want "evidence" to include things like discovering a proof, checking a computation, or learning that a trusted source has verified a theorem.

(2) The bedrock justification for using probabilities at all is coherence: a rational agent must not be exploitable by a Dutch book. If a proposed belief-state can be Dutch-booked, that settles the matter against it.

(3) My credences must be computable in practice. I am a bounded agent, and a norm of rationality that can only be satisfied by an uncomputable belief-state is not a norm for me — ought implies can.

(4) I assign roughly 0.1 credence to the claim that a specific far-out digit of pi is 7 (uniform over the digits, absent distinguishing information), and I assign high-but-less-than-1 credence to long-open conjectures like Goldbach's on inductive grounds.

(5) When I learn that a trusted source has proved a theorem, I should update sharply toward 1 but never actually arrive at 1 — there is always some residual chance of an error in the proof or in my grasp of it, and certainty about anything seems epistemically immodest.

(6) A good account of logical uncertainty should make the opinions of my future, more-computed self authoritative for my present self: if I knew what I would believe after thinking much longer, I should believe that now.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a primer. Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs only look like they conflict but actually dissolve on inspection? For the genuine ones, say precisely what each side is committed to, rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one identify the cheapest repair — which commitment to weaken, and what that weakening costs the overall picture. One thing I particularly want to know: if my coherence commitment in (2) turns out to be incompatible with my boundedness commitment in (3), does that undermine (2)'s role as the justification for the whole probabilistic framework in (1), or can the Dutch-book standard be sensibly relaxed to an approximate or asymptotic version without losing its force? And separately, is my insistence in (5) on never reaching certainty actually in tension with (6), given that an idealized future reasoner presumably converges on certainty about provable truths? If the set as a whole can be made consistent with one or two surgical revisions, tell me which revisions you'd make; if it needs to be rebuilt from the ground up, say that instead.
