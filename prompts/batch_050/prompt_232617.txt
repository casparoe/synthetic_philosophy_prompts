I'm preparing a seminar unit for a political philosophy course on how institutions classify harms, and I want my students to work through a case rather than read another summary of the literature. The case I'd like you to analyze is the way the international humanitarian system officially "declares" famines. As I understand it, the major classification system used by UN agencies and governments does not define famine by its causes at all; it declares one only when measurable thresholds are crossed -- roughly, a certain share of households facing extreme food gaps, a certain rate of acute malnutrition in children, and a certain number of deaths per day per ten thousand people. Below those numbers, the same situation gets a lesser label like "emergency" or "crisis," and the lesser label unlocks a much weaker response: fewer funding appeals, less press attention, no diplomatic emergency. Historians, meanwhile, argue about particular famines -- the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, the famine during the Great Leap Forward -- in completely different terms: whether they were entitlement failures, market phenomena, policy choices, or atrocities. Sen's famous line of argument is that famines are almost never simple food-availability shortfalls but failures in the distribution of entitlements over food that is often present. But the declaration machinery seems to operate as if none of that were the question.

Please reconstruct what this practice presupposes. Specifically: (1) What does defining famine purely by outcomes -- mortality and malnutrition rates -- rather than by causes presuppose about the relationship between harm and responsibility? Does the practice quietly treat famines as morally neutral events, like earthquakes, and if so, is that a commitment or just a feature? (2) The threshold structure presupposes something philosophically contentious: that there is a sharp line, crossed at some specific death rate, at which a situation changes moral kind. Is this a disguised sorites problem being solved by bureaucratic fiat, or does it presuppose a defensible view -- say, that obligations intensify discontinuously with the scale of suffering, or that scarcity of attention justifies triage categories even where the underlying badness is continuous? (3) Contrast the declaration system with a cause-sensitive practice like the legal category of homicide, where the same death counts entirely differently depending on how it came about. What would a cause-sensitive famine classification have to look like -- would it need something like a doctrine of mens rea for states, and could any institution actually run one?

Finally, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: could someone who fully accepts Sen's entitlement analysis still defend outcome-based, threshold-triggered declarations, perhaps on grounds of epistemic humility or institutional incentive design? And conversely, which parts of the practice would have to change -- not just in labeling but in what responses are triggered -- if it were rebuilt on the premise that large famines are typically political failures rather than natural events? I'd like the analysis to end with a clear statement of which commitments are load-bearing and which are incidental.
