I am a graduate student about to commit three years to a dissertation in normative ethics, and I have settled on the philosophy of promising: the question of why, and in virtue of what, making a promise generates an obligation where none existed before. My prospectus has to defend a research agenda rather than survey the terrain, so I need help deciding which open questions to build the dissertation around.

Here is the menu of live questions as I understand it:

1. The grounding question: what actually does the normative work — the expectations a promise deliberately invites in the promisee, the promisor's voluntary exercise of a normative power over her own future options, or participation in a social practice whose rules confer obligations on anyone who invokes them?
2. The scope questions: whether promises to do something immoral bind at all, whether promises extracted under duress or ignorance are defective or merely weaker, whether promises bind the promisor when the promisee immediately waives them, and whether promises to the dead or the soon-to-die retain force.
3. The unity question: whether promissory obligation is one norm or a cluster of related duties (to not disappoint, to not deceive, to stand by one's word) that standard cases happen to bundle together.
4. The contingency question: how much of the puzzle is about human beings as we are — creatures who need assurance to cooperate — such that a community with perfect transparency or reliable third-party enforcement would have no use for promising at all, and what that would show about the obligation's foundations.
5. The application questions: what a correct theory of promising would imply for contract law, for social-contract arguments in political philosophy, and for commitments made by or to non-human agents such as institutions and AI systems.

Two stipulations to sharpen the task. First, imagine an oracle offers to answer exactly one of these questions definitively and correctly; I get to choose which. Second, assume I have ordinary analytic-philosophy tools and no budget for experiments, so "tractable" means tractable by armchair methods over three years.

Given that, give me a dependency-aware prioritization. Which questions are upstream of which — for instance, does the grounding question have to be settled before any scope question can be, or can scope questions be answered independently on each candidate grounding? Which would be deprioritized because their resolution would leave the rest of the field untouched? Where do the oracle's answer and the dissertation's answer diverge — is the question it would be most valuable to *know* also the question it is most valuable to *work on*, and if not, why? State the criteria you are using (how many downstream debates hang on the question, tractability, neglectedness, proximity to the applications in item 5), weigh them against each other where they conflict, and tell me what consideration, if I came to believe it, would change your ranking. I want a defended agenda I can adapt for the prospectus, not a tour of the literature.
