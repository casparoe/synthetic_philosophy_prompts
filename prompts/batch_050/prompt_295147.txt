At game night last weekend our host did this thing where he put cash in two envelopes, one with twice as much as the other, and let my buddy Dan pick one. Dan opened his and found $80, and the host offered him a chance to swap for the other one. Dan and my other friend Sarah got into a real argument about wether he should.

Dan said obviously swap: the other envelope has either $40 or $160, fifty-fifty, so on average it's worth $100, which beats the $80 in his hand. He said you'd take that bet every single time.

Sarah said the argument has to be bogus, because Dan didn't need to open the envelope at all. Whatever he'd found, $40, $80, $800, the exact same math would tell him to swap. So this "reasoning" would have him swapping back and forth forever without ever actually gaining anything, which means it can't be a real reason.

Dan came back with: fine, but the fifty-fifty assumption is what's really fishy, because the host doesn't have unlimited money. If he'd opened $10,000 he'd know for sure the other envelope has $5,000. So for big amounts you stay put, but for small amounts like $80 doubling is basically just as likely, and his swap logic still works.

Sarah said there's no cutoff that makes any sense. For the other envelope to be fifty-fifty higher-or-lower at every possible amount, the host would have to be exactly as willing to stuff in a million bucks as a single dollar, and no actual person works like that. So in no real version of this game is Dan's math ever exactly right, not even at $80.

They both seemed kind of right to me so I stayed out of it. Can you go through their back-and-forth step by step, say who's right at each point, and what (if anything) they're both missing? Keep it under 300 words.
