I write alternate-history fiction, and I have come to suspect that my instincts about which counterfactuals are respectable are inconsistent. Let me lay out my verdicts on some cases, and then ask what principle, if any, lies beneath them.

1. The wrong turn before Sarajevo in June 1914: had the driver not taken it, the assassin would not have had his second chance. I find this counterfactual illuminating -- it shows how much of the twentieth century hung on contingencies.
2. A butterfly flaps differently over the steppe and the weather shifts enough that the future conqueror of Eurasia dies in infancy. I find this idle, even silly, and I would cut it from any essay or novel.
3. The thirteen days of October 1962 go hot. I find this legitimate and gripping, and note that it is a near-miss, not a remote possibility.
4. In one of my own novels, a letter delayed by a single afternoon unravels a marriage over the following thirty years, and I stand by this as true to life. Yet when a historian argues from a comparably small cause to comparably large consequences, I balk.
5. Alexander lives to seventy instead of dying at Babylon. I feel this is a good question, though I cannot say why it is better than the butterfly.
6. The asteroid misses and the dinosaurs persist. As a piece of evolutionary speculation I accept it; as a premise for human history I reject it, since there is no human history left to narrate.

Here is the tension I cannot resolve. I want my principle to be that counterfactuals earn their keep by exposing contingency -- by showing that small causes had large effects. But the very feature that makes a counterfactual display contingency, namely the chaotic amplification of a small cause, is also what makes its downstream course impossible to trace, and an untraceable counterfactual is just my butterfly again. Meanwhile the counterfactuals whose consequences I *can* trace seem to be precisely those where history is robust and the outcome would have come about anyway -- in which case the counterfactual proves nothing about contingency at all. So my best cases threaten to collapse into my worst, and my tractable ones threaten to be pointless.
