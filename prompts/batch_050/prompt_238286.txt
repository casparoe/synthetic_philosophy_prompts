Say Rousseau gets dropped into the present day and given a month to study how modern states try to figure out "what the people want." Two institutions catch his eye:

1. Citizens' assemblies: ~150 randomly selected citizens, paid for their time, briefed by experts, who deliberate for weeks and produce policy recommendations (the French climate convention and the Irish abortion assembly are the famous ones).

2. "Bridging-based" ranking on social media, like X's Community Notes: a note or post only gets amplified if people who normally disagree politically rate it as accurate. The designers explicitly pitch this as surfacing the view "of the whole" rather than of any faction.

Which of these, if either, would Rousseau recognize as a genuine mechanism for finding the general will, and which would he dismiss as a fancier way of aggregating the mere will of all? I have my own hunch — I think he'd surprisingly prefer #2, since it mechanically filters out factional agreement without requiring the long deliberation he distrusted — but push back hard if you think I've got him wrong.

One wrinkle you also need to address: for #1 he'd presumably object that the assembly only *advises* an elected parliament, so sovereignty is still alienated to representatives. Is there any fix (binding referenda? making the assembly itself sovereign? something else?) that would earn his blessing, or is the small-republic problem fatal either way.

Ground everything in the actual machinery of the Social Contract — general will vs. will of all, his line on partial associations, why near-unanimity matters to him, the legislator if it's relevant — rather than in vibes about what a grumpy communitarian would say.
