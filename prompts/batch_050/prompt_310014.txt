But almost every standard case involves two separate people disagreeing at the same time. What I want to understand is how these two views handle a different case I keep coming back to: disagreement with your own past self. Say I carefully worked through a problem two years ago -- some tricky probability puzzle, or whether a certain ethical theory has an implication people claim it has -- and concluded P. Now I redo the reasoning and conclude not-P, and I honestly can't find the error in either attempt. Assume I've genuinely forgotten nothing relevant, I'm not drunk or tired, and there's no evidence I've gotten dumber. It's just me vs. me.

Please compare how conciliationism and steadfastness treat this self-disagreement case versus the standard two-person case, and use the comparison to say something about what each view is really committed to. Specifically: is the conciliationist's pressure to split the difference really coming from the *otherness* of the peer, or from something else, like distrust of one's own reasoning on this particular occasion? And does the steadfast view have an easier or harder time with the past-self case than with a separate peer? I'm especially interested in whether the two views collapse into each other once the "peer" is literally the same person, or whether there's a real difference that survives. If you think one of the views handles the self-case noticeably better or worse, argue for it rather than just asserting it.

One more wrinkle: my roommate claims that if conciliationism tells you to conciliate with your past self, then believing anything at all becomes weirdly unstable, since you're always potentially in disagreement with some past or future version of yourself. Steadfastness, she says, at least gives you a stable doxastic present. I think this argument moves too fast but I can't quite say where. Please address it somewhere in your answer -- does conciliating about one case really generalize into that kind of instability, or is there a principled stopping point?
