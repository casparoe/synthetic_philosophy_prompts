At a dinner party last weekend I sat through an hour-long argument between two friends about whether you can find out that a work of art is good by being told that it is. Both of them seemed to me to be making genuinely good points, and since neither convinced the other, I would like a point-by-point verdict from someone neutral.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. Maya had just returned from an exhibition she kept calling extraordinary. When Daniel pressed her on what made it good, she cited the gallery's reputation, a review she had read beforehand, and the standing of the artist — and admitted that standing in front of the works themselves she had felt mostly bewildered. Daniel's opening move was that in that case Maya did not yet know the work was good; she knew only that certain people believed it was. He allowed that testimony can transmit plenty about art — a painting's provenance, a novel's plot, the fact that most critics admire a film — but insisted the aesthetic verdict itself is different. His comparison: you can be told everything about a joke, down to its wording, timing, and how loudly the room laughed, and still not be in a position to call it funny, which is why people repeating a joke's reputation laugh in the wrong places at a live reading.

Maya replied that this is special pleading. We rely on testimony for most of what we know in history and science, she said, and we happily use guides and critics for wine and restaurants; demanding firsthand acquaintance only in aesthetics looks like a rule invented to protect a favorite subject. She also argued the position is unlivable: no one has read every classic, yet we are surely right to hold that the canonical works are great, and a stance that strips us of those beliefs until we have personally verified each one leaves everyone starting the canon from scratch. She ended by pointing out that Daniel had himself chosen the film for their group outing on the strength of reviews and then defended it warmly afterward.

Daniel conceded the last point but drew a distinction: he had used the reviews as evidence about where to spend an evening, not as the ground of a judgment — the judgment came only after seeing it, and he would have withdrawn it had the film been bad. His deeper claim was that a verdict held purely on testimony is missing something even when true: he asked us to imagine a person who could recite every analysis ever written of a string quartet but was tone-deaf, and said that whatever that person possesses, it is not what the listener who hears the quartet possesses, and it is unstable in a way the listener's judgment is not — it gets abandoned as soon as fashion or authority shifts.

Maya's countermove, which is where they stalled, was twofold. First, she accused Daniel of conflating three things — knowing that a work is good, being justified in believing it is good, and appreciating its goodness — and said his tone-deaf case shows at most that testimony fails at the third, which no one denied. Second, she argued that his prized firsthand acquaintance is a myth anyway: no one hears a quartet or walks through an exhibition with innocent ears, and his own experience of the film was shaped from the start by the reviews he claimed to have merely used for logistics.

Who is right about which specific points? In particular: does Daniel's evidence-versus-judgment distinction survive Maya's charge of conflation, or does it just relabel it? Does Maya's second argument about mediated perception actually damage Daniel's position or only a straw version of it? And if the three-way distinction she offers is correct, what exactly does each party end up entitled to claim — is there a residue of the dispute that survives it, or does it dissolve entirely? Finally, is there anything both of them are missing that a resolution would need to address?
