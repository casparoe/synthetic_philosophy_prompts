I chair the ethics commission that advises our state's governor, and I need a draft opinion I can bring to the commission next week. Details slightly scrambled for confidentiality but the structure is real.

Nine months ago the state was three days from a government shutdown. Medicaid reimbursements to rural hospitals would have been first to stop, and two hospitals said publically they'd have to close. The holdup was a small caucus whose leader refused to back any budget while a scheduled forensic audit of contracting in his district — where his family has deep business ties — was still on the calendar. The governor's then-chief of staff met him privately and cut a deal: he delivers the votes, the audit gets "deprioritized indefinitely." The budget passed, the hospitals stayed open, and last month the audit was quietly killed by reassigning its staff.

Now a reporter has emails proving the meeting happened and is asking for comment. This will all be public within weeks whatever we do. The governor wants a formal opinion from the commission on what to do now. The live options as we see them:

1. Honor the deal openly: confirm the arrangement, defend it as the right call, leave the audit dead.
2. Honor the deal quietly: confirm the meeting but stonewall on the substance, keep the audit dead.
3. Reneges and disclose: restart the audit, release the emails, take the political hit.
4. Half-measure: restart the audit with a narrowed scope and spin the original deal as routine horsetrading.

Draft the opinion, roughly two pages, recommending one of these. Things I want the reasoning to genuinely engage with rather than gesture at:

- Whether the averted shutdown matters to what we should do *now*, or whether leaning on the good outcome is the move that makes dirty-hands justifications self-ratifying — every rotten bargain was struck for some good reason, by the bargainer's lights.
- Whether there's a morally relevant difference between the chief of staff having made the deal and the governor ratifying it after the fact. The governor says she didn't know at the time; that's plausible but not certain, and the chief of staff no longer works here.
- What each option does to future crisis bargaining. If word gets around that deals with this office don't stick, the next shutdown standoff gets worse. If word gets around that they do stick, we've announced that anti-corruption audits are a currency this administration trades in.
- Whether an institutional ethics opinion is even the right vehicle for "the governor got her hands dirty but it was worth it," or whether that's a judgment for voters and history and the commission should confine itself to what the integrity of the audit process demands.

Be committed: pick one option, defend it, state the strongest case for the runner-up fairly, and then answer it. I don't want a tour of the options — I want a recommendation the commission can vote to adopt or vote down. Skip citations and philosopher name-dropping; if we want framing footnotes I'll add them myself.
