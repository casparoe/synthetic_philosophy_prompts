I got into it with a coworker last week about whether people actually value their privacy. I mentioned surveys where big majorities say they care a lot and would pay to avoid being tracked, and he just shrugged and said "watch what they do, not what they say" -- people hand over their data for a free sandwich app, so clearly they don't value privacy, whatever they tell pollsters. He said it like it was checkmate, and I've heard the same line deployed in arguments about whether people "really" want to quit social media, whether poor people "really" prefer cash transfers to services, etc.

I have a nagging feeling this one-liner is doing way more work than any single principle could legitimately do, but when I pushed back I couldn't articulate which version of it I even object to. So: what are the distinct precise claims hiding inside "watch what they do, not what they say"? I figure there's a big difference between something like "behavior is more reliable data than self-reports because people lie or self-deceive," versus the stronger econ-flavored claim that preferences just ARE choices (so "they say they want X but choose Y" is literally a contradiction, not a conflict), versus the normative claim that what people choose is what's actually good for them so policymakers should defer to it. Probably there are more.

For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible methodological stance, or actually false/overreach? And which readings would my coworker need for his specific move -- concluding people don't value privacy from the fact that they don't pay to protect it? I'm suspicious that move sneakily requires the strongest reading, but I also want to steelman the weaker ones, since "people say they care about lots of things" does seem like real evidence of something. Bonus points if you can say how constraints mess this up -- like, does choosing the cheapest flight that harvests your data reveal you don't value privacy, or that privacy was never actually on the menu at a price you could pay?
