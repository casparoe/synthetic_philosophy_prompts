So my uncle Ray is retiring next month after 37 years as a high school band director, plus teaching tai chi at the community center on Saturdays basically forever, and my mom has voluntold me to give the toast at his retirement dinner. Here's the thing. Ray got into Daoism in his twenties and never got out of it -- wu-wei, effortless action, going with the grain of things. His favorite story is the butcher whose knife stays sharp for nineteen years because he follows the natural seams of the ox instead of hacking through bone. When I was spiraling before my qualifying exams last year he told me I was trying to polish a brick into a mirror, which was annoying and also correct.

This creates a genuine problem for the toast, and it's why I'm asking you instead of just writing it myself. A normal retirement toast is a polished, rehearsed little performance -- exactly the kind of thing Ray has spent decades gently mocking. He will visibly wince if I deliver something that smells like I workshopped it for weeks. But the obvious fix, just winging it, is its own trap: deliberately "being spontaneous" is still a performance, arguably a phonier one, and he'd clock that too. And honestly neither option is really *me* -- I'm a preparer, that's my grain, and forcing myself to improvise would be me hacking through bone.

The audience is mixed: half family who just want to hear nice things about Ray, half his tai chi students and old band kids who will catch every Daoist reference and silently judge them. Write me a roughly four-minute toast that threads this needle -- one that takes wu-wei seriously enough that Ray would approve of the *speech itself*, not just its content. Then, after the toast, give me a short paragraph explaining how you handled the paradox, because I genuinely want to understand whether "trying not to try" is coherent or whether Ray has just been getting away with something for forty years.
