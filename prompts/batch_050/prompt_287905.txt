At a graduate reading group in philosophy, I listened to two members argue about what the Knobe effect actually demonstrates. The case in question: a chairman is told that a new programme will increase profits but also harm the environment; he replies that he cares only about profit and does not care at all about the environment; the programme goes ahead and the environment is harmed. Most people, asked afterwards, say he harmed the environment intentionally. In the mirror-image case, where the side effect is help rather than harm, most people deny that he helped intentionally. Here is roughly how the argument went.

(1) Marta argued that this asymmetry shows the ordinary concept of intentional action is partly normative: whether people count a foreseen side effect as brought about intentionally depends on its moral character, not just on the agent's beliefs and desires. She added that the effect is not confined to the verb 'intentionally' — similar asymmetries appear when people are asked whether the chairman decided, desired, or was in favour of the side effect — so it cannot be a quirk of one word and must reach into how folk psychological concepts themselves work.

(2) Daniel replied that the data show something narrower: judgments of intentionality are tools for assigning praise and blame. Saying 'he did it intentionally' is how one makes blame stick, and saying 'he didn't intend it' would misleadingly let the chairman off the hook, so subjects choose their words to accomplish social work rather than to report a mental state. On his view the experiments measure what people want to do with a sentence, not what their concept of intentional action contains.

(3) Marta countered with the skill cases: a hopelessly unskilled darts player who desperately wants to hit the bullseye and flukes it is judged not to have hit it intentionally, even though there is nothing to blame him for and his desires and partial foresight are all present. Blame cannot explain that denial, she said, so the concept must have substantive conditions beyond the classical belief-plus-desire model — conditions involving norms, whether moral norms or norms of competence. That, she insisted, vindicates her general claim.

(4) Daniel answered that she had just conceded his central point. Once the thesis is diluted from 'the concept of intentional action is moralised' into 'ascriptions of intentionality are shaped by moral norms, competence norms, and conversational pressures', it becomes something his blame-centred account already predicts. He then pressed a methodological worry: surveys record what people say, and what people say is driven by what they are trying to achieve in context, so no amount of vignette data can by itself distinguish a claim about concepts from a claim about the pragmatics of accusation.

(5) Marta closed by saying that, even on his deflated reading, the classical picture of intentionality as a purely descriptive psychological notion is dead, and that this was a vindication of experimental philosophy's method either way. Daniel flatly denied that the method had earned that moral.

I stayed out of it, but I'd like a proper adjudication now. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say which of the two is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
