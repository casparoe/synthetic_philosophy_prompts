I've been arguing with a friend who works on AI safety about iterated amplification and I keep losing, so help me out. Quick background in case the term is fuzzy: the idea is you answer a hard question by breaking it into sub-questions, handing those to copies of a weaker assistant (maybe helped along by a human overseer), combining the answers, and then distilling that whole slow deliberation into a faster model that imitates it, round after round. My friend's line is that none of this is new -- it's just how any big organization works. No CEO can personally verify what ten thousand employees do, so you decompose the work, hire people you trust, let managers aggregate their reports, spot-check at random, and train new staff by having them shadow how the deliberation actually went. On her view, decomposition-based alignment inherits the firm's track record: imperfect, but a solved-enough problem that civilization basically runs on it. She says the burden is on the pessimists to show why this case should be any different.

I want a real audit of the analogy, not cheerleading for either side. First spell out the mapping piece by piece: in an amplification scheme, what corresponds to the CEO, the rank-and-file employees, the middle managers, the hiring process, the onboarding where new staff learn to mimic how the past deliberations went, the random spot-checks, and the company's "culture"? A table is fine. Then tell me where it breaks. Some things I've been chewing on, though don't feel bound by my list: employees show up with their own values, self-interest, and a life outside the firm, which is partly what makes firms hard to align but also weirdly what keeps them honest (people quit, leak, sue, whistleblow), whereas a model's "interests" are whatever training installed and it has no exit option. Firms sit inside markets and legal systems that punish certain kinds of drift from the outside; a distilled model's drift gets punished by... what, exactly? And at the top of an org chart sits a human with relatively stable goals, while in amplification the overseer is part human, part machine, and its own reliability is precisely the thing in question.

The question I actually care about: my friend uses the analogy to argue we should be fairly relaxed, that alignment-by-decomposition is management science plus engineering polish. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument the entire time? If you think it's salvageable, give me the repaired version -- is there a better comparison (an appellate court hierarchy, academic peer review, a relay of translators) and does it license the same confidence, or less? Either way, be explicit about what would change your verdict: which empirical finding about how training goes, or which argument about decomposition, would flip you to the other side, and where is your own reasoning here most likely to fall apart. American English, please.
