I edit a general-reference encyclopedia, and our corrections desk needs a standing procedure for handling challenges to the received account of well-documented atrocities — the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, the Nanjing massacre — when readers or contributors dispute death tolls, intent, or whether the events occurred at all. Design a decision tree that takes an editor from a submitted challenge to one of four to six named verdicts, with legitimate revision, honest error, and denialism among the leaves; you may add leaves the desk turns out to need. Each branch must be a single question answerable by examining the submission and its handling of evidence — without redoing the underlying historical research, without speculating about the author's motives, and phrased so the editor need not know in advance which verdict an answer points toward. The tree must take a stand on the crux of whether demonstrable bad faith, such as fabricated sources, is ever required for a denialism verdict, or whether method alone suffices, and it must use no more than seven branch questions. Conclude by flagging any pair of leaves the tree cannot reliably separate — a sincere crank and a cynical propagandist, perhaps — and saying what the desk should do in those cases. Present the tree first, with no preface or framing, then give the reasoning behind the criteria.
