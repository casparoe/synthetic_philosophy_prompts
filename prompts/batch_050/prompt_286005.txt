I've been rereading Orwell's Politics and the English Language with a mate who does comms for a goverment department, and he half-jokingly admitted his job is basically to do everything the essay warns against, which got me thinking. Write me a mock internal memo from the fictional 'Directorate of Strategic Clarity' at a British government department, addressed to all staff, announcing that plain English has been identified as a strategic and reputational risk and is being phased out across the department's public-facing output.

Here's the catch I want: the memo itself must be written in flawless, crisp, plain English throughout. That IS the joke -- a document banning clarity that is itself perfectly clear -- and the comedy should come from that contradiction slowly dawning on the reader, not from any winking at them. The author should sincerely believe he's defending good communication: he can argue that simple words 'commit' the department to things, that short sentences leave no room for nuance, that clarity is elitist because it lets absolutely anyone check what's actually being said. Bonus points if the writing style he prescribes as the replacement quietly reproduces the exact sins Orwell catalogued (dying metaphors, operators instead of plain verbs, Latin-flavoured diction, words with no meaning left in them) while he insists he is leading a crusade against jargon. He should never notice.

Keep it completely deadpan, in British English, and structure it like a real memo: a reference line, numbered points, maybe one bullet list of 'approved formulations', and a proper sign-off ('For and on behalf of...' style, with an invented name and job title). No footnotes, no asides to the reader, no moment where the mask slips. Hard limit of 550 words.
