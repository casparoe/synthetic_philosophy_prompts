My department's reading group spent the term on know-how, and the two loudest members -- a committed intellectualist who holds that knowing how to do something is ultimately a matter of knowing truths, and a Rylean anti-intellectualist who holds that intelligent performance is prior to and independent of propositional knowledge -- have been talking past each other for months. I've volunteered to draft the joint statement they would produce if forced into an adversarial collaboration, and I'd like you to write that document. It should open with a numbered section of claims both parties can honestly sign, which I expect will have to include some subtle items: that skilled performance often outstrips what the performer can state, that instruction in propositions sometimes improves and sometimes degrades performance, that both sides owe an account of what is lost when a pianist loses her knowledge of music theory but keeps her hands, and that any regress-style argument must be stated carefully enough that it doesn't merely assume what it purports to show.

Then give the disagreements, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position -- no strawman entries -- followed by the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. To keep this concrete, build it around three test cases I'd like both sides to rule on. First, a violinist who, after a selective brain injury, plays as well as ever but can no longer identify a single note by name, explain what she is doing, or recognize her own technique when it's described to her. Second, a chess engine that wins at grandmaster level while producing nothing its designers would count as a statable reason, alongside a human commentator who explains every principle of the position flawlessly and loses to the engine every time. Third, a proficient swimmer who is taught the explicit biomechanics of the stroke and temporarily swims worse while she monitors herself. For each crux, state which of these cases (or what further evidence) both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the correct description of the case must be -- in particular, whether the intellectualist is committed to finding hidden or tacit propositional knowledge in the injured violinist and the engine, and whether the Rylean is committed to denying that the commentator's genuine knowledge of truths amounts to knowing how to play.

Close the statement with a section the two of them write together identifying any part of their dispute they now suspect is verbal rather than substantive -- for instance, whether "knowledge" is being used in the same sense when one side counts tacit states as knowledge and the other reserves the term for what a subject can in principle avow -- and, for each genuinely substantive remainder, one sentence on what would, in principle, settle it.
