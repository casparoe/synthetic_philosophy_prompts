I serve on my university's Research Integrity Committee, and we are deadlocked over a case I keep turning over in my head, including in arguments with my spouse over dinner. Dr. R., a senior cell biologist, had to retract a well-cited paper after a former postdoc admitted fabricating several key images. The postdoc acted alone; nobody alleges Dr. R. fabricated anything. The question is his state of mind. The record shows that a junior lab member emailed him before submission flagging that one figure looked anomalous; he replied within the hour praising the figure and urging that the submission not be delayed; he had password access to the raw data but the logs show he never opened the files. At his interview he insisted, with a sincerity most of the committee found credible, that at the time he genuinely believed the results were sound. One detail makes this harder: three years earlier, he had written to a student warning her to double-check exactly this kind of anomaly in her own data, so he demonstrably can see these red flags when the result is not his own. Our policy, like most, defines research misconduct as conduct committed knowingly, intentionally, or recklessly, and explicitly excludes honest error and negligence.

What splits the committee is whether there is anything between lying and honest error. Dr. R.'s defense submitted a statement from a philosopher arguing that self-deception, strictly so called, is impossible: you cannot believe something and simultaneously believe its negation, and you cannot intentionally get yourself to believe what you already take to be false, because the very intention would give the game away. On that argument, Dr. R. either knew the data were bad and is lying now, which has not been proven, or he sincerely believed in them, in which case the worst available verdict is negligence, which the policy excludes. One of my colleagues answered by circulating the tenth sermon, "Upon Self-Deceit," from Joseph Butler's Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel (1726). Butler builds the sermon on the biblical episode in which the prophet Nathan tells King David about a rich man who seized a poor man's one lamb, and David erupts in condemnation of the man without recognizing that the story describes his own conduct. Butler's point, roughly, is that people habitually judge their own case by a kinder standard than they apply to anyone else, and that this partiality can manufacture a perfectly sincere ignorance: a man does not see his fault precisely because he does not want to, and the not-seeing is itself his doing and his fault. My colleague says this is Dr. R. exactly, and that such motivated not-knowing should count as recklessness, or even knowledge, under our policy. The defense replies that Butler describes a moral failing, not a policy violation, and that punishing someone for a state of mind he genuinely did not have is both unfair and epistemically unworkable.

Please draft the committee's decision memo, around 700 words: a clear bottom line (find research misconduct, decline to find it, or make no finding and refer the policy for amendment), with reasoning both sides can engage. The memo must (i) assess the impossibility argument, including whether there is a coherent middle phenomenon, such as motivationally biased handling of evidence that issues in genuine belief rather than in contradictory beliefs; (ii) say whether, if such a phenomenon exists, it is culpable in a way our policy's standards actually capture, or whether convicting on Butler's picture would stretch the policy beyond what a respondent could fairly anticipate; and (iii) address the evidentiary problem: from the outside, how could a committee ever reliably tell culpable self-deception from mere negligence, and what does the David-and-Nathan structure of Butler's case suggest about where to look? Give the strongest version of the losing side before answering it. End with a final line of exactly this form: Recommendation: [one short sentence stating the bottom line].
