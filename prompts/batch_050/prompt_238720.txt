Moore argues, roughly, that a world of great natural beauty would be better than a world of utter ugliness even if no conscious being ever existed in either to see them — while also holding that the greatest goods by far are the appreciation of beauty and the pleasures of friendship, both of which essentially involve consciousness. I want to know how much weight to give the first claim given that he stakes so much on the second. Please give me your credence that Moore is right about the unseen worlds: that the beautiful one is genuinely better, other things equal, than the ugly one. Decompose the judgment into the sub-questions it depends on — for instance, whether intrinsic value can attach to states of affairs no one ever experiences, whether the doctrine of organic unities (the value of a whole differing from the sum of the values of its parts) survives scrutiny, whether the value of beauty is exhausted by good experiences of it, and whether "better" even has a determinate meaning in a world containing no valuers — with a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and then show how these combine into your final number. Finally, tell me which sub-question your answer is most sensitive to and what argument would move it most. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
