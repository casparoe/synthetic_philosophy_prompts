I'm in a reading group on philosophy of language and we've spent the last month on Brandom's Making It Explicit, and the recurring fight in our group is between the Brandom fans and the people (like me, honestly) who think truth-conditional semantics is obviously the more fundamental story about meaning. We keep talking past each other, so I had the idea of making the two camps produce an actual adversarial-collaboration document instead of another shouting match. You're a philosopher of language who has published on both inferentialism and model-theoretic semantics and is trusted by both sides, so you're the neutral drafter. Think carefully and brainstorm candidate formulations before committing; the hard part is finding wording that neither side will reject as rigged.

Draft the joint statement between a Brandom-style inferentialist (meaning is constituted by the inferential role of expressions in a game of giving and asking for reasons) and a truth-conditional semanticist (meaning is fundamentally a matter of reference and truth conditions, with inferences derived from that). The document needs three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign -- I'd guess these include things like: any adequate semantics must explain how novel sentences are understood, must connect linguistic meaning to what speakers actually do, and must say something about logical vocabulary. Push for more and better agreed claims than my guesses, and flag any item where the two sides only appear to agree because they're reading the same sentence differently. Second, a disagreements table where each row is a single precise point of dispute, with each camp's position stated in terms that camp would itself accept -- cover at least: which notion is explanatory bedrock (inference or truth/reference), how to handle subsentential expressions like singular terms and quantifiers (Brandom's substitution-inferential story versus standard denotation), whether speakers who share no beliefs can still mean the same thing by a word, and what to make of Prior's tonk (does the fact that arbitrary inference rules can trivialize a language show that inference rules need an external check -- and if so, is that check harmony of introduction and elimination rules, which the inferentialist can supply internally, or truth conditions, which the referentialist says were the real check all along?). If at the end you think some of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say exactly which part and show your work. Don't include a bibliography or suggest readings; we just want the document itself.
