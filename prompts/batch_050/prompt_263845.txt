I spent my career as a hydrologist and now, in retirement, sit in on a moral philosophy seminar at the university nearby. Two of its regulars have been circling the same dispute all year. One is a committed Scanlonian contractualist: she holds that moral principles must be justifiable to each person individually, that only the strongest individual complaint against a principle counts, and that no number of trivial complaints can stack up to defeat one grave one. The other is an aggregationist consequentialist: he insists that harms are harms, that enough small burdens imposed on enough people can outweigh a catastrophic burden imposed on one, and that refusing to aggregate leads to absurd verdicts in mass cases. They have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement they could both sign.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about what each view actually says (neither is a straw man) and about the shape of the problem itself. Second, the residual disagreements, each formulated so that the party whose view is described would accept the wording as accurate; keep the wording neutral throughout. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one party. Fourth, for each crux, a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's verdict recorded in advance. Fifth, an explicit note on any part of the dispute they conclude is verbal rather than substantive.

Please incorporate three cases I have devised, since the standard textbook cases seem to me to smuggle in confounds. (a) A water authority can either prevent a flood that would destroy, beyond recovery, the home of one particular family, or — for the same funds — repair drainage so that fifty thousand households avoid recurring ankle-deep flooding that ruins a few weekends and a modest sum each year. (b) A continuum case: pain can be reduced from level ten to zero for one person, or from level one to zero for any number you like; is there a level below which harms become too minor to aggregate at all, and can either side say where or why without arbitrariness? (c) An ex ante case: a policy imposes on each of a million people a one-in-a-million risk of death, versus a policy inflicting a certain week-long severe migraine on the same million; does judging principles ex ante, when each person's prospective complaint is minuscule, dissolve the aggregation problem or merely relocate it?

Finally, I have pressed on the pair an analogy from my old field: summing many independent random measurement errors yields no systematic signal, so perhaps summing many negligible complaints likewise yields nothing that can oppose a grave one. The document should state, in terms both parties accept, whether this analogy tracks a morally relevant distinction or where precisely it breaks down.
