So my department does this thing where the AI-safety-adjacent PhD students have to present to the regular philosophy crowd once a year, and mine is next month. I'm defending a version of AI safety via debate -- roughly: to get truthful answers out of an AI system that's smarter than its human overseers, you train two copies of the system to argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge, and you reward whichever one convinces the judge. The optimistic claim I want to defend is that this scales where direct human feedback doesn't: even when the judge can't verify the answer themselves, a debater who has truth on their side can point out the flaws in any dishonest argument, so honesty is the winning strategy, and we can oversee systems on questions we couldn't check on our own. That's the headline version, I can complicate it a bit in the talk but that's the core.

The audience: mostly epistemologists, plus a couple of philosophers of science and one guy who does social epistemology / argumentation theory and will definitely be there. Almost nobody who follows the ML literature. They will NOT have read any of the debate papers, so their objections will come from their home turf -- testimony, adversarial argumentation, peer disagreement, that kind of thing -- not from inner alignment concerns.
