I run a small company, 14 people, and we ended the year with about 60k extra. I could just split it as equal bonuses but that feels lazy, so I announced I'd spend it on "improving quality of life" for whoever needs it most. Now I've actually talked to everyone and I'm paralyzed, because depending on what "needs it most" means I get four different winners:

- Anna has a chronic pain condition. Proper treatment would eat most of the budget. But she's somehow the most upbeat person in the office — meditation, gratitude journal, the whole thing — and insists she's genuinely happy.
- Ben is miserable. By any self-report survey he's our worst-off employee by miles. But when you dig into it, his misery is mostly that he can't afford the life he thinks he should have — sailing trips, nice restaurants, a watch he's obsessed with. Same salary as everyone else.
- Carla grew up in a rough situation and has really modest expectations. Says she's totally fine, wants nothing, seems sincerely content. But objectively she has the fewest options of anyone — no savings, no qualifications, health stuff she's never even had looked at.
- Dan lost his wife last year. He's devastated and says no amount of money touches it, and he'd honestly resent any attempt to lift his mood — the grief feels like the right response to his situation, not a malfunction to be repaired.

So: if I go by who feels worst, Ben wins, which seems nuts. If I go by who's most constrained in what they can actually do, probably Anna or Carla — but Anna's already happy and Carla would be the first to tell me she's doing great, so am I overriding her own assessment of her life? That feels paternalistic. And Dan is like a standing reminder that low wellbeing isn't always something to fix.

How should I think about this? What am I even trying to equalize or maximize here — and is there one rule that handles all four cases sensibly, or do I just have to pick a currency and swallow that it spits out a crazy verdict in one of them?
