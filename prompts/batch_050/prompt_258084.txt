So I've been roped into giving a talk at my lab's weekly reading group (I work at a mid-size ML company, mostly RL people) and I picked a fight I might regret. My claim: the whole project of defining "low impact" for AI agents via a single scalar penalty on state change is doomed, because any distance measure on world-states smuggles in exactly the value specification it was supposed to avoid -- but we can still get most of what we want from what I call a "counterfactual invariant list": a fixed, human-written set of facts about the world that must remain true under a specific counterfactual (roughly: what would have happened had the agent never been switched on), with the agent free to do anything else. Think of it as a constitution of things-you-may-not-change rather than a metric of how-much-you-changed. My argument is that this sidesteps the offsetting problem (no compensating for a broken vase by freezing all the other vases) and avoids needing a utility function over states, at the cost of accepting that the list will be incomplete and that's fine because completeness was never actually available.

The audience: about fifteen people, mostly applied RL engineers who think in terms of reward hacking and will reach for "but that's just a reward function with extra steps" within the first two minutes, plus two safety-adjacent researchers who know the attainable utility preservation and relative reachability literature well and will want to know why my thing isn't just a worse version of those, plus one visitor from a philosophy department who keeps asking whether "impact" even makes sense without an agent-relative baseline. Nobody in the room has much patience for conceptual hand-waving, and the engineers will definitely bring up that any list I write will either Goodhart or leave a loophole big enough to drive a data center through.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet. The eight-ish objections I'll actually get from this room, in the order they're likely to come, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (engineer vs safety researcher vs philosopher sound different), with what I should just concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and a short reply I can deliver in under two minutes each. Flag where each reply is soft. I especially need help with the baseline problem (had-the-agent-never-acted is doing a lot of work in my proposal and I'm not sure I can defend it against "why not had-the-agent-been-a-rock?") and with the objection that an incomplete invariant list is strictly worse than an incomplete metric because at least a metric fails gracefully. And include the question I'm praying nobody asks, because I suspect there is one and I haven't found it yet.
