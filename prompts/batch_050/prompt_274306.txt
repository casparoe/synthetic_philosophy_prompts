I am a university student taking a second-year course in historiography. As part of a peer-marking exercise, I need an independent assessment of the following practice examination answer. Please provide a grade out of 10 and specific comments justifying that grade, going through the answer point by point. I am looking for a precise assessment of both its strengths and its weaknesses, rather than a general impression. Please write in British English.

Examination question:

Historians’ relation to their own time can take several forms. Selection presentism occurs when present-day concerns influence which topics, questions or evidence historians consider important. Interpretive presentism occurs when present-day concepts or categories are imposed upon past actors whose own categories were significantly different. Evaluative presentism occurs when historians judge past conduct by moral or political standards that were unavailable, marginal or differently understood in the period under investigation.

Consider two historians of nineteenth-century factory reform. Historian A begins studying the subject because of contemporary debates about workplace safety and describes reformers as defenders of universal human rights, although the reformers themselves generally spoke in terms of Christian duty, social order and the responsibilities of property owners. Historian B carefully reconstructs those contemporary categories before separately condemning factory owners according to present-day standards of exploitation and workers’ welfare.

Which forms of presentism are involved in each case? Are any of these forms unavoidable? Is one more intellectually damaging than the others? Can historians escape the standards of their own time, or can they only discipline the way those standards affect their work?

Student’s answer:

Historian A exhibits selection presentism because contemporary debates about workplace safety determine which aspect of the nineteenth century is investigated. This is unavoidable: historians cannot study everything, and their questions must arise from somewhere. There is therefore nothing necessarily wrong with allowing present concerns to influence the selection of a topic.

A also displays evaluative presentism by calling the reformers defenders of universal human rights. Since this description presents the reformers favourably, it is a positive moral judgement based on modern values. However, the description is not interpretive presentism because the reformers were campaigning for humane treatment, and human rights are simply the modern name for that objective. Provided the modern label refers to broadly the same behaviour, it does not matter that the reformers used different concepts.

Historian B avoids presentism in the explanatory part of the project because B reconstructs the historical actors’ own categories before passing judgement. B’s subsequent condemnation is evaluative presentism, but it cannot distort the historical explanation, since the explanation was completed before the moral judgement began. Once explanation and judgement have been separated, each can proceed independently.

Evaluative presentism is the least damaging form because moral judgements are explicit and readers can disregard them if they wish. Selection presentism is more dangerous because it affects the entire shape of the historical account, while interpretive presentism is also dangerous because it may make past actors appear more similar to ourselves than they were.

Historians cannot escape the standards of their own time because their language, questions and interests are all historically situated. It follows that every historical account is equally presentist, and the contrast between presentist and non-presentist history is illusory. Nevertheless, historians can make their work less misleading by examining contemporary sources, identifying differences between past and present concepts, stating their own assumptions and distinguishing causal explanation from moral evaluation. Historians cannot escape their own standards altogether, but they can discipline their use.

Assessment criteria:

- accuracy in defining and distinguishing the three forms of presentism;
- accuracy in applying those distinctions to Historian A and Historian B;
- quality of the argument about which forms are unavoidable and which are damaging;
- logical coherence, especially where the student moves from the claim that present influence is inescapable to conclusions about whether all historical work is equally presentist;
- quality and precision of the final conclusion.
