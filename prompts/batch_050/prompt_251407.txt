I am putting together a short position statement for my graduate seminar on fictional discourse — a one-page declaration of where I stand on names, existence, and truth in fiction, which the students are then invited to attack. Before I circulate it, I want the set stress-tested for internal coherence. Here are the commitments, roughly as I would defend them in print:

(i) Millianism about proper names: the contribution a name makes to the propositions expressed by sentences containing it is simply its bearer, if it has one.
(ii) Anti-creationism about fictional characters: Arthur Conan Doyle did not bring a new entity into being by writing his stories; there is no object — abstract, artifactual, or otherwise — that the name "Sherlock Holmes" denotes. In our mouths the name is genuinely empty.
(iii) "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" is literally true — not merely true-relative-to-a-story, and not merely assertible thanks to a tacit "according to the fiction" prefix. When a child utters it, she speaks the truth, full stop.
(iv) "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is likewise literally true, and there is nothing especially puzzling about its informativeness.
(v) Truth in fiction is generated conservatively: what holds in a story is what the text explicitly states, together with the obvious consequences of those statements against the background of the actual world, except where the story revokes the background.
(vi) No fiction makes contradictions true: an author cannot render an impossibility true in a story merely by stipulating it.
(vii) Holmes and Watson are distinct: "Sherlock Holmes is identical to John Watson" is false, not truth-valueless.
(viii) Comparative claims that cross the boundary between fiction and reality — for instance, that Sherlock Holmes is better known than any detective who ever lived — can be straightforwardly true.

One further datum I would like taken into account: as careful readers have long observed, the Doyle texts locate Watson's war wound in his shoulder in one place and in his leg in another. My working assumption is that principles (v) and (vi) together handle this sort of case cleanly, though I am open to being told otherwise.

Please audit the set as a whole rather than essaying the topic generally. Identify each pair or larger cluster of commitments that stands in genuine tension, grade the severity of each, and state the cheapest revision that would dissolve it along with what that revision costs. Distinguish these from pairs that merely appear incompatible but can be reconciled at no real cost — I would like the audit to discriminate, not to flag everything. Finally, if there is a single revision that would restore coherence to the greatest portion of the set, name it and tell me plainly what I would have to give up.
