I've been thinking about Aumann's agreement theorem and the standard pushback that it "doesn't apply to real disagreements." I'd like a case built to order that makes that pushback precise. Construct a thought experiment in which two agents with a genuinely common prior, both perfect Bayesians, both reporting their honest posteriors to each other, can stably and openly disagree about the probability of a proposition -- and where the *only* condition of the no-disagreement theorem that fails is the common knowledge requirement. In other words: don't cheat by giving them different priors, by making either one sloppy at updating, or by having them withhold information. The failure must live entirely in the epistemic structure of their communication.

Constraints: the setting should be mundane (two analysts, two traders, two doctors -- not infinite hierarchies of imagined agents), the communication setup must be one a real pair of humans could plausibly occupy, and the disagreement must persist even after they have exchanged estimates multiple times, not just at the first announcement.

After presenting the scenario, do the verification work: (1) state the proposition and each agent's posterior; (2) explain level by level why the agents' estimates become mutual knowledge but not common knowledge -- i.e., at which order of "she knows that I know that she knows..." the chain breaks, and why; (3) identify the minimal change to the case that would restore the theorem's conclusion and force convergence; (4) give the strongest objection an Aumann defender could raise against your case and whether it succeeds.

Return your answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "scenario" (the thought experiment itself, one paragraph), "proposition_and_posteriors" (what is believed and at what confidence), "chain_break" (where and why common knowledge fails), "minimal_fix" (the change that restores convergence), "objection" (the best attack on the case), "objection_verdict" (whether the attack succeeds, with brief reasoning).
