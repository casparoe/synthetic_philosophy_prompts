My two flatmates got into a proper argument last night and neither would back down, so I want a verdict. Background: our friend Sam has dangerously high blood pressure, knows it, and refuses to do anything about it because he genuinly just doesn't care.

Maya's position: Sam has a reason to get treatment, full stop, and whether he cares or not doesn't touch that. Tom says that's confused — in a Williams-ish internalist way: a consideration only counts as a reason for someone if it could actually hook into their deliberation starting from the desires, fears, and goals they already have. If nothing like that connects, then "he has a reason" is just a fancy way of saying *we* disapprove.

Maya came back with: fine, but even on your own view Sam has the reason, because deliberating from stuff he already cares about (his kids, being scared of dying) leads straight to the doctor's office. Tom granted that but said she's dodging the interesting case — imagine someone whose motives genuinely don't connect at all, someone totally indifferent to their own future. Maya would still say that person has a reason, and now the "reason" does no work at all, since it couldn't possibly move them.

Maya's reply: if reasons evaporate whenever someone's desires are selfish or apathetic enough, then "I had no reason to help" becomes a get-out-of-jail-free card for anyone who doesn't care — moral reasons can't be held hostage to people's motivational sets like that. Tom said this just assumes external reasons exist because it would be morally convenient, and that the alternative isn't "anything goes": you can still blame people, shame them, or try to change what they care about, you just can't pretend there's a reason inside them doing silent work.

Who's right about which points here? And is there something both of them are missing?
