I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on whether the classic Twin Earth thought experiment in philosophy of language still deserves its canonical status, or whether it survives mainly on textbook inertia. For the benefit of my editors, let me lay out the case as I understand it. In Putnam's scenario, Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth both use the word "water" for the clear, potable liquid that fills their lakes and taps; but the Twin Earth liquid has the chemical structure XYZ rather than H2O. The standard conclusion is that Oscar's word refers to H2O and Twin Oscar's to XYZ even though their internal physical states are identical, so what a speaker means is not fixed by what is inside the head. Putnam sharpens this with a 1750 version, where neither community yet knows any chemistry, precisely to rule out the reply that the two speakers have different descriptions in mind.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but an actual credence, expressed as a number, that the thought experiment succeeds in its strong form: that it establishes semantic externalism about natural-kind terms, meaning that two internally identical speakers can refer to different things with the same term purely in virtue of their environments. I want the reasoning decomposed. Roughly, how much of your credence rides on (a) the robustness of the intuition about the 1750 version, given the objection that pre-scientific speakers on both planets plausibly used "water" for a superficially characterized kind of stuff, so that both Oscars meant the same thing and no externalism follows; (b) whether the argument quietly builds its conclusion into the setup by assuming that being the same liquid is a matter of microphysical constitution rather than observable role; (c) whether "meaning" is one thing the thought experiment can be about at all, or instead an equivocation between reference and sense that the argument itself manufactures; and (d) whether the conclusion, if granted for natural-kind terms, actually generalizes to the wider range of terms externalists extend it to. Give me a rough probability for each sub-question, explain how they combine into your final number, and tell me which sub-question the number is most sensitive to. Finally, because I will have to defend this piece to skeptical editors, tell me how confident you are in your overall assessment, single out the one judgment in your decomposition you are least sure of, and say what argument or consideration would move it most.
