A standard piece of trading-desk wisdom says that accepting an unsolicited trade is like sitting down at a poker table: if you cannot figure out who the sucker is, the sucker is you. This is the informal cousin of the no-trade results in economics -- the observation that, under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, agents who trade purely on private information should never find a willing counterparty, because the very willingness to trade conveys information that cancels the apparent gain. Practitioners use the poker analogy to license a practical rule: refuse trades you cannot explain, and treat a counterparty's eagerness as evidence against you. Audit the analogy. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the cards, the pot, the other players, and the tell. Then identify where the mapping breaks -- for instance, poker is zero-sum while markets contain hedgers, liquidity sellers, and rebalancers whose motives are not informational; the no-trade results rest on idealizations (common priors, common knowledge of rationality) that a poker game approximates but real markets may not; and poker forbids cooperation whereas markets often reward it. Distinguish the disanalogies that undermine the practical rule from those that are real but harmless, say what features of poker the analogy quietly imports into our picture of markets, and state whether the rule survives once those are made explicit. If the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, propose a better one. Finally, state what would change your verdict: which consideration, if established, would show the poker analogy does capture what matters, and where your own reasoning about the disanalogies is most likely to fail.
