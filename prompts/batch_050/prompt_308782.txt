I'm setting the take-home final for my senior seminar on the foundations of subjective probability (philosophy majors; they've read Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" and some de Finetti, and every single one of them has absorbed the slogan that violating the probability axioms means you can be Dutch-booked). I want one essay question that they can't answer by re-deriving the Dutch book theorem and calling it a day.

The question should turn on a passage I'll print at the top of the paper, from the degrees-of-belief section of Ramsey's essay: the paragraph where he introduces measuring someone's belief by proposing a bet and seeing what odds they'll accept, calls that method fundamentally sound, and then in the same breath lists why it's inexact -- diminishing marginal utility of money, the person's eagerness or reluctance to gamble as such, the fact that proposing the bet can change the very state of mind it's supposed to measure, and that the method doesn't even apply to beliefs with no upshot for conduct. So the passage is on the exam; don't worry about reproducing it, and it doesn't count toward any word counts below.

What I want the question to force students into: if Ramsey himself says the measuring instrument distorts what it measures, what exactly do coherence arguments for probabilism establish? They should have to pull apart two projects everyone runs together -- measuring credences versus justifying the probability axioms -- engage with Ramsey's own workaround (fix a value scale first using propositions the agent treats as ethically neutral and believes to degree 1/2, then measure belief against that), and then face the hard bit: does a Dutch book argument need betting quotients to track credences exactly, or only roughly, and what could "roughly" even mean for a theorem?

Deliverables: the question itself (answerable in about 2 hours, ~1200 words), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around the four or five moves a top answer must make, marks per move, plus a one-liner for each on how weaker papers typically botch it. Also flag the single most common confident, fluent, wrong answer I should watch out for.
