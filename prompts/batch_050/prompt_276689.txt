I’m a high school student in a philosophy club, and we recently discussed whether a proof can be genuine mathematical knowledge if no individual can survey the whole proof. One member summarized the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture as follows:

“These two results represent the same basic epistemic shift. Each problem was transformed into a finite checking task that no person could practically inspect, and computers performed the checking. As a result, these proofs differ from traditional proofs not merely in length but in kind: we accept them because we trust software, hardware, and the programmers. Later formalization projects did not fundamentally change this situation, since a computer proof can ultimately only be checked by another computer. For example, Flyspeck confirmed the Kepler conjecture by re-executing Thomas Hales’s original computations. Accepting either theorem is therefore ultimately an act of testimony rather than mathematical knowledge.”

I suspect that the final sentence is too strong, but I do not know enough about formal verification to identify exactly where the argument goes wrong. How fair is this summary? Please distinguish what an expert would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is mistaken. In particular, distinguish computer-assisted calculation from formally verified proof, unsurveyability in practice from uncheckability in principle, and reliance on testimony from the complete absence of mathematical justification. Also consider whether the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture really raise the same epistemic issue. Reach a conclusion about whether the summary’s final claim survives once its errors are corrected.
