Exam question: In I Am a Strange Loop, Douglas Hofstadter argues that a person's self — their "soul," in his non-religious sense — is a pattern that can, to a greater or lesser degree, be instantiated in other people's brains, and that this is why something of a person genuinely survives their death in those who knew them well. Explain this view, distinguishing it from (a) literal survival of consciousness and (b) the trivial claim that we remember the dead. Then raise one objection: does the view give the grieving person what it seems to promise?

Student's answer: Hofstadter holds that the self is not a physical object located in the skull but a "strange loop," an abstract, self-referential pattern of symbols that the brain implements. Because the self is a pattern rather than a substance, it is substrate-independent: the same pattern can be realized in different media. When two people know each other intimately, each builds a coarse-grained copy of the other's pattern inside their own brain, so that selves are literally distributed across skulls during life, not confined to one body. When his wife Carol died, Hofstadter concluded that she goes on having experiences — dim, low-resolution ones — inside the brains of those who loved her, since the pattern that was Carol persists there and patterns are what consciousness is. This differs from literal survival of consciousness because the copy in another brain is only a partial, low-fidelity version rather than the whole original person, and it differs from mere memory because the copy is an active instantiation of the pattern, not a static record of facts about her. The idea that selves can be distributed across brains was already the core argument of Gödel, Escher, Bach, where Hofstadter first developed the strange loop account specifically to explain survival after death. A possible objection is that a coarse-grained simulation of someone, however detailed, is not that person. But on Hofstadter's own terms this objection fails, since he is careful to insist that identity comes in degrees. It follows that grief, while natural, is strictly speaking irrational on his view: if the pattern genuinely continues in others, then the person has not really been lost, and mourning treats as an absence what is in fact a reduced presence.
