Let's do a roleplay. You play Pierre-Simon Laplace, sometime around 1814, the year you stuck the Philosophical Essay on Probabilities in front of the Théorie analytique. Stay in character the whole time: first person, courteous, measured, a man of the Enlightenment who thinks chance is just a name for our ignorance and that the universe runs on strict necessity. I'll describe some work done after your death; you may use it, but react as Laplace would, not in modern slang. You don't concede easily, but you do concede when actually beaten — no stubbornness for its own sake. If some piece of the modern setup is unclear to you, ask me about it in character rather than guessing.

Here's what I want to press you on. Early in your Essay (I read the Truscott and Emory translation) you set up the whole calculus on reducing events to cases we have no reason to regard as unequal, and then — this is the part I keep coming back to — you admit that chances we suppose equal can hide inequalities we know nothing about, like a coin or die that's secretly loaded, and you say that as long as we're ignorant of which side the inequality favors, the probability stays put. You treat the hidden asymmetry as a physical fact out in the world, something we could in principle weigh up and correct for.

Fine. Now consider a puzzle from later in your century, due to Joseph Bertrand. Draw a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle, pick a chord of the circle "at random," and ask the probability the chord is longer than the triangle's side. If you fix one endpoint and spin the other end around, you get 1/2. If you pick the chord's midpoint somwhere in the disc's interior, you get 1/3. If you pick a radius and then a point along it, you get 1/4. Each answer comes from applying your own rule — count favorable cases among equally possible ones — but they disagree, and nothing in the problem picks one carving of the cases over the others.

So here's my challenge. In your unknown-inequalities passage the asymmetry is at least a fact about the world. But in the chord puzzle there is no hidden physical fact at all. Nobody threw anything. I just said "random chord" in my head. The ambiguity isn't in nature, it's in my description. Doesn't that break your rule at the ground level? You can't reduce an event to equally possible cases if the cases won't sit still until I've already smuggled in a probability model to define them.

One more thing before you answer. A twentieth century writer named Jaynes, a huge admirer of yours, claimed he could save your program: demand that the answer not change if you rotate the circle, shift it, or change its size, and only one of the three solutions survives — the 1/2 one — and he reports that when people actually drop long straws onto circles chalked on the floor, the observed frequencies match his answer and not the other two. Two questions then. First, is this invariance business the completion of your program or its refutation? Does it make probability a branch of the logic of ignorance, as you always wanted, or a branch of the physics of falling straws? Second, suppose the straw experiments had come out against Jaynes. What should he have abandoned — your principle, or his invariance condition? And don't hide behind determinism: I know you don't believe in real chance, so tell me, if one and the same state of ignorance yields three different answers, what exactly is the ignorance ignorance OF?
