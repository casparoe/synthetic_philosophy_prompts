I have been thinking about the St. Petersburg paradox and want to find out whether my own reactions to a series of wagers rest on any coherent principle. Assume throughout that I am 40, have a net worth of exactly $200,000 and no debts, that every counterparty is perfectly trustworthy, and that payouts are not taxed. Here are my verdicts:

1. The classic game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on flip n, I receive 2^n dollars. Its expected value is infinite, but I would pay at most about $15 to play once.

2. The same game, except the casino's total bankroll caps the payout at 2^45 dollars, so the expected value is a finite $23. I would pay about $15 again — essentially the same as in Case 1, even though the two games differ enormously in expected value.

3. A single fair coin flip pays $0 or $8. I would pay $3.90. For small stakes I am nearly risk-neutral.

4. A supercharged game: if the first heads occurs on flip n, I receive e^(2^n) dollars. I still would not pay more than about $20. Yet the principle I am inclined to endorse (below) apparently says I should hand over everything I own to play this game, which I find absurd.

5. A lottery with a one-in-a-million chance of paying $1 billion (expected value $1,000). I would cheerfully pay $25 for a ticket, and I notice this seems hard to square with my caution in Cases 1 and 4.

6. Suppose I somehow owed a casino the outcome of a reverse St. Petersburg game — I must pay it 2^n dollars, with my liability capped at my net worth. I would pay roughly $50,000 today to be released from that obligation, which is vastly more than the $15 I would pay for the mirror-image privilege in Case 1.

My tentative principle, following Bernoulli: the most I should pay for any gamble is the price p at which the expected logarithm of my terminal wealth equals the logarithm of my current wealth. Please test this principle against all six verdicts. Where it reproduces a verdict, say so and show the reasoning; where it does not, I want to know precisely which ingredient is at fault — the logarithmic shape, the reliance on terminal wealth, the neglect of probabilities near zero, or something else. If you think the principle should be repaired (say, by bounding utility, weighting probabilities, or evaluating gains and losses relative to a reference point), state the repaired principle exactly, trace it back through every case, and identify which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it condemns as irrational. Also tell me what your preferred principle implies about three situations I did not consider: being offered the game in Case 1 ten thousand times at a fixed price per play; a version where the prizes are years of healthy life rather than dollars; and a version where I have moderate evidence the coin is slightly biased toward heads. If no single principle of reasonable simplicity accommodates all six verdicts, say so plainly and tell me which verdict is cheapest to give up.

Please conclude with a final line of the form: Summary: <one sentence naming the principle you settle on and the verdict(s) it forces me to abandon>.
