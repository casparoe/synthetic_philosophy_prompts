Since my mother's death last spring, following three years of her living with advanced Parkinson's, I have found myself drawn to a minority position in population and wellbeing ethics sometimes called tranquilism: the view that what ultimately matters is not the presence of pleasure, desire-satisfaction, or positive experience, but only the absence of suffering and craving. On this view, a calm, untroubled life with few fulfilled ambitions is not thereby impoverished, because unmet desires do not leave any real deficit once the desiring itself subsides. I have been turning this over for months, and this autumn I am leading a session of my long-standing reading group on Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, which strikes me as an unusually exacting test case for the idea.

Please read the novel through the lens of tranquilism, as sketched above, treating the sketch rather than any published formulation as the position under discussion. What draws me to the pairing is that Kathy, Tommy, and Ruth appear to live with remarkably little resistance to their fate: they are raised to donate their organs and eventually to "complete," and yet the dominant register of the novel is one of quiet accommodation rather than rebellion or despair. A tranquilist might say the Hailsham students have been given, perversely, nearly the best lives available — rich attachment, modest pleasure, and almost no craving for what they cannot have — and that our horror at the novel is sentimentality about projects and futures that tranquilism shows to be optional. But I suspect the novel resists this reading, and I want to know where.

In your reading, address at least: Kathy's calm retrospective narration and whether its evenness represents freedom from craving or merely its suppression; Tommy's childhood rages and his later scream in the field after the deferral rumor collapses, and what that scene implies about whether acceptance was ever genuine; and the trip to Norfolk, with its hope of recovering lost things, as a study in whether deferred longing does harm even when it never becomes acute suffering. Where the novel resists the tranquilist reading, say so plainly rather than forcing the fit, and close by considering whether fiction like this can show something about the badness of thwarted life-narratives that philosophical argument alone cannot capture.
