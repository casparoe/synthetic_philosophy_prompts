Now that I'm retired I've started volunteering at a hospice and reading moral philosphy on the side, and there's an asymmetry I can't stop thinking about. Medicine is almost entirely in the business of relieving and preventing suffering -- curing disease, managing pain, palliative care -- and it essentally never tries to make healthy people happier. There's no department of positive wellbeing at the teaching hospital I used to collaborate with, and a doctor who prescribed mood-lifting drugs to a patient with nothing wrong would probably get struck off. Funny thing is, even the WHO's famous definition of health is about complete wellbeing rather than just the absence of disease, so the system doesn't even seem to follow its own founding document.

So what theory of value does all this presuppose? Is modern medicine implicitly negative utilitarian -- does it only make sense if relieving suffering matters in a way that creating happiness doesn't? Or can a symmetric view, where happiness and suffering count the same, explain the pattern just as well through things like tractability, risk, or do-no-harm? Reconstruct the implicit axiology for me, say whether the practice actually needs it or whether several rival theories would license it just as well, and tell me what concretely would have to change if we rebuilt healthcare on a fully symmetric view of wellbeing.
