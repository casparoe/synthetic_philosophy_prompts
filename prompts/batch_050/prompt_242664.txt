I cover science and ideas for a UK magazine and I'm working on a long read about how "wage slavery" rhetoric has crept back into commentary on gig platforms, zero-hours contracts and warehouse work. A couple of the organisers I interviewed lean on the analogy hard: a delivery rider, they say, is a slave in all but name -- the platform controls the conditions of their labour the way a master controls a slave, ratings and deactivation play the role of the whip, and if that's right then abolition, not regulation, is the morally serious frame. My editor wants a sidebar on whether the analogy survives contact with the theory it borrows from, which is mostly Marx, and I've been roped into drafting it.

So treat "the wage labourer is a slave" as an argument rather than a slogan and audit it for me. Roughly what I need:

- The correspondence table: in the gig-economy case, what plays the role of the slaveholder, the chattel, the market where bodies are bought, the coercion, and the surplus that gets extracted.
- Where it holds: does Marx's actual exploitation machinery -- the distinction between labour-power and labour, surplus value, the worker who is free in a double sense (free to sell his capacity to work, and free of any means of producing otherwise) -- make the analogy look better or worse than a hasty reader of Capital would think? I suspect the fact that exploitation for Marx happens without anyone being forced, through a fair exchange at the going price of labour-power, cuts both ways, and I cant tell which way.
- Where it breaks: what chattel slavery has that wage labour lacks -- ownership of the person rather than purchase of labour-power for a stretch of hours, heritability, the absence of legal personhood -- and, crucially, whether those differences matter for the organisers' abolitionist conclusion or only for the history.
- What the analogy quietly imports: does the borrowed moral horror of slavery do argumentative work that the labour theory of value can't do on its own?
- If there's a better analogy available from inside Marx's own toolkit -- the vampire image for dead labour feeding on the living, or the fourfold alienation stuff from the 1844 manuscripts (from the product, the activity, one's species-being, other workers) -- tell me what it is and what conclusion it would actually license, since I assume "abolish employment" is not on the table.

Be concrete, please -- I have to translate this for readers who've never opened Capital but have almost certainly had dinner brought by someone working under it. And write the answer in British English.
