I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on global catastrophic risks (about 16 advanced undergraduates, one 75-minute session). Design an in-class game that makes participants feel the force of the unilateralist's curse: the situation where an initiative that is good only if undertaken by at most one or a few actors will predictably be undertaken by too many, because each of many well-intentioned actors can launch it alone and the actual decision is effectively made by the most optimistic among them. The game should make two things happen in play rather than in my lecture: first, participants who all share the same goal and have no selfish incentive should still overshoot, so that they experience this as an epistemic and aggregative failure and not as a tragedy of the commons; second, each participant should hold private, noisy evidence about whether launching is worthwhile, so that afterward they can see that what doomed them was selection on the most favorable estimate, not anyone's irrationality. Give me the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that works without real money, and enough rounds or structure that the group can discover for themselves what does and doesn't fix it (deliberation, communication, voluntary restraint, a designated gatekeeper). Then a debrief that maps what happened onto real cases such as geoengineering experiments, gain-of-function research, and active attempts to signal extraterrestrials, and that draws the line between this failure mode and ordinary collective action problems. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- especially players quietly coordinating, which I suspect undermines the setup but might also be the most instructive outcome -- and be honest about which feature of the real phenomenon a one-off classroom game cannot reproduce.
