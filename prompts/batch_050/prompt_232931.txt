I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature on the debate over scientific realism. My editors want the piece to have a clear editorial line, and before I commit to one I want my own set of working commitments stress-tested. Here they are:

First, I'm a selective realist about mature science: the central posits of predictively successful, mature theories -- electrons, genes, tectonic plates -- should be treated as approximately real, not as useful fictions. Second, I take the pessimistic meta-induction seriously as an inductive argument: the history of science is a graveyard of theories that were empirically successful yet centrally wrong, and any realism worth holding has to be built to survive that fact rather than waved past it. Third, I think structural realism is the right way to survive it: what gets retained across theory change is the mathematical or relational structure, as when Fresnel's equations carried over into Maxwell's optics even though the luminiferous ether did not. Fourth, I find the no-miracles argument genuinely compelling: the novel predictive success of mature theories would be an astonishing coincidence if those theories weren't at least latching onto something real. Fifth, I'm a committed anti-realist about the posits of current frontier physics -- strings, extra dimensions, the multiverse -- and intend to say so in the piece. Sixth, I believe we can, right now and without waiting for the next scientific revolution, identify which components of our current best theories are the ones genuinely responsible for their empirical success, and that this is how a journalist should decide which claims to report as discoveries and which as speculation.

Audit this set for me. Which pairs of commitments are in real tension, how severe is each tension, and what would the cheapest fix be in each case -- including what I'd have to concede to make it? Which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection? I'd like the tensions ordered by how much damage they'd do to the piece if a sharp philosopher of science reviewed it, and if one single revision would buy me the most consistency, name it. One more thing: I'm not wedded to any of this, and I don't want a polite going-over. If the set is misframed, if I've imported a confused premise somewhere, or if the task as I've posed it can't be done well, say so plainly rather than working around it.
