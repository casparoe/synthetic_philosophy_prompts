I serve on my university's research ethics board, and at a recent meeting we reviewed a proposal to fund a research team that will develop a statistical tool predicting which criminal defendants will fail to appear at trial, for use by a municipal court in setting bail conditions. The board approved the project, but two of my fellow members — Dana, a statistician, and Marcus, a political theorist — had an extended disagreement afterward that I have been turning over since. I report it here as neutrally as I can.

Dana's position: the well-known impossibility results show that when base rates differ across demographic groups, no non-trivial predictor can be simultaneously calibrated within each group and equalize false-positive and false-negative rates across them. There is therefore no technical fix and no uniquely "fair" model. The committee's only honest course is to pick one criterion, document the choice, and stop pretending there is a right answer. Anything beyond that, she said, is marketing.

Marcus replied that a conflict between criteria does not make the choice among them arbitrary, because the criteria encode different moral notions. Calibration constrains what a score means: a 0.7 should indicate a 70% likelihood in either group. Error-rate parity constrains who bears the costs of the model's mistakes. For a tool whose errors result in people being jailed, the morally salient question is the distribution of wrongful detention, so equalizing false-positive rates takes priority over calibration.

Dana responded that this priority claim ignores what abandoning calibration costs: if a score means different things for different groups, judges either misread it or must apply mental corrections that effectively reintroduce group membership at the point of decision. Moreover, error-rate parity is symmetric — it can be achieved by raising one group's false positives or lowering the other's, and the metric itself is indifferent — so the burdens-of-mistakes rationale does not even select the remedy Marcus wants.

Marcus conceded that point but pressed a different one: the impossibility depends on two premises, imperfect prediction and unequal base rates, and base rates are not laws of nature. A gap in failure-to-appear rates reflects decades of differential policing and enforcement. Treating that gap as a fixed parameter and then triaging among metrics launders an upstream injustice into a technical constraint. The proper response to the theorem is not metric selection but reduced reliance on prediction wherever the inputs are compromised.

Dana countered that this proves too much. Base rates differ across groups for virtually every socially significant outcome — loan default, disease incidence, college dropout. If compromised inputs disqualify prediction wherever group rates diverge, Marcus has ruled out actuarial reasoning in any unequal society, including tools, such as medical risk scores, whose benefits accrue disproportionately to the worse-off group.

Marcus closed by distinguishing domains according to what errors do: a false positive that triggers additional screening is not a false positive that triggers pretrial detention. His claim is not "never predict" but that where the state inflicts the harm, the burden-distribution criterion governs, and elsewhere calibration may suffice — the theorem is an argument for domain-sensitive regulation, precisely the kind of conclusion Dana's "pick one and disclose" framing refuses to contemplate.

I have my own leanings, which I will keep to myself. Before composing your verdict, brainstorm the strongest version of each party's case and the most charitable reconstruction of each move in the exchange; think carefully rather than reaching for a verdict prematurely. Then adjudicate the disagreement point by point: assess, for each of the moves above, whether it succeeds, is overstated, or misses its target. In particular, address whether Dana is right that the impossibility reduces the remaining choice to a documented preference with no right answer; whether Marcus's appeal to the contingent, historically produced character of base rates escapes the theorem or merely changes the subject; whether Dana's "proves too much" charge survives Marcus's domain restriction; and whether either party adequately handles the fact that the impossibility concerns properties of scores rather than of the downstream decisions made on their basis. Conclude with an overall verdict — who is right about what, who is more right on the whole — and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing.
