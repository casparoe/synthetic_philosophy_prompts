I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy working on normative ethics under cosmological infinitude, and I need a piece of writing I can adapt for the discussion section of a paper. Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000–1,300 words between two philosophers, Mara and Jonas, on the following setup.

Assume, for the sake of the dialogue, that cosmologists have established beyond reasonable doubt that the universe is spatially infinite and that matter is distributed ergodically, so that every finite arrangement of matter—including every possible human life and every possible local outcome of any choice—recurs, both exactly and approximately, infinitely many times across space.

Mara defends an uncompromising total-view consequentialism: the moral status of an act is fixed by the difference the act makes to the total amount of value in reality, impartially considered. She accepts that, under the stated cosmology, this difference is always zero or undefined, and she wants to argue that this consequence should revise how we think about morality rather than be dismissed as a reductio of her framework.

Jonas defends a position he calls causal localism: what matters morally is the value of an act's causal consequences compared to what would have happened in its absence, assessed within the region the agent can actually affect. He claims his view preserves the moral force of everyday choices without exotic metaphysics, and he suspects Mara's difficulties are self-inflicted artifacts of insisting on summing value over an infinite totality.

Please observe the following constraints:

- Both characters must be equally competent. Neither is a strawman. Mara should press Jonas on whether his localism covertly abandons the impartiality that made consequentialism attractive in the first place—why should value inside my causal reach count for more than value outside it?—and on cases where an act's causal effects are diffuse, delayed, or unknowable. Jonas should press Mara on whether her view collapses into quietism about all action, and on what, if anything, could practically distinguish a good choice from a bad one on her account.
- At some point they must test both views against two concrete cases: a passerby deciding whether to rescue a drowning child, and a small-magnitude, large-scale action such as donating a modest sum to an effective global charity.
- The exchange must involve real back-and-forth. The characters should engage with each other's actual arguments, concede points where appropriate, refine their positions under pressure, and try to isolate the precise premise on which they disagree, rather than talking past one another.
- They need not reach agreement, but by the end they should jointly formulate, within the dialogue, the single question whose answer would most advance the dispute, and each should state what would change their mind.
- Do not include citations, footnotes, or references to particular publications, and do not let the characters gesture at named literature in place of making arguments; the dialogue should stand entirely on its own reasoning.
- The tone should be that of two colleagues who respect each other and are genuinely trying to get somewhere.
