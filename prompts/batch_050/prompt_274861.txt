I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and for the past several years artificial intelligence has been my main beat. One debate on that beat strikes me as genuinely stuck rather than merely unresolved: whether large language models, and the machine translation systems that came before them, understand anything. The skeptical camp, whose intellectual godfather is Douglas Hofstadter, argues that translating well requires knowing what the words are about. When a sentence says that the city council refused the demonstrators a permit because they feared violence, rendering the pronoun correctly in a language that forces grammatical choices requires knowing who did the fearing, and the skeptic's claim is that this knowledge is not to be found in statistics over text: it belongs to a model of the world the text describes, which the system lacks. The opposing camp replies that modern systems now routinely resolve exactly these ambiguities, that probing and interpretability research finds structured internal representations of syntax, referents, and even spatial and social relations, and that whatever understanding is, it had better be something achievable through learning from prediction error, since that is plausibly all brains do as well. I have interviewed people on both sides; the transcripts from five years ago and from last month are nearly interchangeable, except that the stakes have grown.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is at bottom a disagreement about standards of evidence. The two camps never settled in advance what behavior or what internal structure would count as understanding, so every new capability is absorbed by the skeptics as more sophisticated mimicry and by the believers as further confirmation, and the goalposts migrate with each model release. A philosopher friend presses an alternative diagnosis on me: that the camps are answering different questions under one word, one asking whether the systems possess the functional competence that understanding enables, the other asking whether understanding requires something further — embodiment, a life, phenomenal experience — that no amount of textual prediction could supply.

What I would like is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey of positions. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: a verbal dispute over the word understand, a disguised empirical question about the internal representations of these networks that interpretability research could in principle answer, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or a collision of bedrock intuitions about minds that neither side can argue for from below. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says and does, including how each side metabolizes the same results. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction between kinds of understanding, a specified class of interpretability findings, a re-posed question, an agreed-upon test — or else argue that nothing will move this debate, and why that stasis itself would be a finding worth reporting.
