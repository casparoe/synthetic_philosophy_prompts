I have no philosophical training, but I recently picked up a copy of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's Theodicy (1710) because I had always heard the phrase about ours being the best of all possible worlds and wanted to see the real argument. Near the start of the work, Leibniz argues, in essence, that an infinitely wise and good God, confronted with infinitely many possible worlds, must have chosen the best one — and that the best need not be a world in which every individual part is as good as it could be, any more than the best picture is one in which every region is as bright as possible; the excellence lies in the whole arrangement, where less good elements contribute to the perfection of the total. He also insists that had there been no single best among the possible worlds, God would have created nothing at all.

I keep turning two objections over in my head, and I want to see the strongest case that they can both be answered.

First, the one-tweak objection. In every optimization problem I know from ordinary life — tuning a recipe, adjusting the gears on my bicycle, rearranging a shop floor — there is always some small change that improves one detail while leaving everything else untouched. So it seems there ought to be a possible world exactly like ours except that one particular toothache or one particular landslide is missing, and that world would be strictly better. For Leibniz to be right, it has to be the case that not a single evil in our world can be surgically removed without unraveling something else — every detail must be load-bearing. That strikes me as an astonishingly strong claim about how the contents of a world hang together.

Second, the no-best objection. If for any candidate world there is always a slightly better one (one more happy person, one fewer storm), then there is no best — and by Leibniz's own admission above, God would then have created nothing, which is absurd. So Leibniz seems forced to say that the series of increasingly good worlds has a genuine ceiling rather than rising forever, and I cannot see what would justify that.

Please argue that Leibniz's position is coherent in the face of both objections: that a world can be the best possible whole even while containing particular evils that, considered in isolation, could have been absent, and that there can be a single best world rather than an endless ladder of better and better ones. Where you can, ground the argument in how Leibniz himself conceives of possible worlds and God's choice among them, rather than appealing to mystery or to God's inscrutability. If you think one of the two objections ultimately succeeds, say which one and explain what is left of Leibniz's position once it does.
