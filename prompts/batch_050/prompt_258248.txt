Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether injustice’s recurring victories undermine belief in moral progress. She conceded that particular societies can regress for generations, but said this no more disproves moral progress than an eddy disproves the direction of a river. Her preferred image was that history forms an arc: local movements may curve backward, but the overall trajectory bends toward justice. I objected that the image makes an attitude of hope look like a causal tendency.

Act as a professional philosopher of history with expertise in teleology, providence, and historical explanation. Think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorming stage that identifies several plausible interpretations of the arc image before settling on a structured answer. I am not asking for a bibliography or citations.

Treat my friend’s image as an analogy and audit it rigorously. First, provide the correspondence table: what in history corresponds to the arc, its curvature, its direction, its endpoint, and the forces allegedly bending it? Then examine where the mapping fails. In particular, consider whether an arc ordinarily presupposes a fixed endpoint, a smooth path, an external or internal force maintaining its shape, and a privileged standpoint from which the whole curve can be seen—and whether history possesses any analogous features.

Distinguish carefully among at least five claims the image might be expressing:

1. a retrospective description of selected historical changes;
2. an empirical trend likely to continue;
3. a causal tendency produced by human institutions or moral learning;
4. a teleological process in which history aims at justice;
5. a providential guarantee that justice will ultimately prevail.

For each claim, explain what evidence would support it, whether local or prolonged regressions count against it, and whether it licenses my friend’s conclusion that present injustice may win temporarily but will lose in the long run. Also consider whether justice itself may be multidimensional: could history advance along one dimension, such as legal equality, while regressing along another, so that speaking of a single arc conceals conflicting trajectories?

Finally, assess better analogies if the arc cannot carry the intended conclusion—for example, a ratchet, a pendulum, a river with no fixed mouth, an evolutionary landscape, or a project dependent on continued human effort. Say which analogy best fits a secular causal view and which best fits a providential one, without pretending that the latter is merely a dressed-up version of the former.

Throughout, separate what is relatively settled from what remains genuinely contested among informed historians and philosophers. In particular, do not present moral progress, historical teleology, or providence as an established consensus.
