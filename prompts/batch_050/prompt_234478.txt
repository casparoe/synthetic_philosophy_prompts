I run the design-review board for a mid-sized city government. Any new institution we create -- a permitting office, a rental-inspection regime, a procurement portal, a citizen-complaint tribunal -- comes to us before launch, roughly one proposal a month, and we have to decide whether the design is understood well enough to be approved. Lately I have been reading Hobbes, and I was struck by the opening of the Introduction to Leviathan (1651), where he argues that the artificial is knowable in the way the natural is not:

"Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificiall Animal. For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?"

As I understand Hobbes's wider position, this is the same reason he gives for treating geometry as the model of genuine science: the geometer can demonstrate with certainty because the figures are generated from definitions the geometer set down; we know the watch because we can take it apart and put it back together; and civil philosophy can be exact because the commonwealth is something we make. Natural things, by contrast, we only observe from outside.

I want this turned into a review procedure my board can actually run. Write a protocol -- the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each -- for deciding whether a proposed institution is understood in Hobbes's artificer's sense: can its designers reconstruct its behavior from stated definitions and rules, the way a geometer regenerates a figure or a clockmaker reassembles a watch, rather than merely describing how they hope it will go? The procedure should tell us what to demand as evidence of reconstruction (what the equivalent of definitions, springs, and wheels is for an institution), how to handle components that are themselves natural rather than artificial (the people staffing the office, the landlords being inspected), where reconstruction failure should block approval outright versus trigger a pilot or a monitoring requirement, and which parts of the review are irreducibly judgment calls dressed up as steps. It has to be runnable in a single two-hour meeting by generalists, not philosophers.

Then apply it to three proposals: (1) an automated benefits-eligibility system whose rules are fully specified in code; (2) a new neighborhood dispute-mediation service staffed by trained volunteers; and (3) a consolidated online permitting portal intended to replace five legacy offices whose current practices nobody has fully documented. I expect at least one of these to come out in a way I will not like -- include that outcome and defend it. Finally, say where the procedure systematically misfires: cases where demanding the artificer's knowledge is too strong because grown institutions outperform designed ones precisely by embodying tacit knowledge no one can reconstruct, and whether Hobbes's own criterion has room for that or is simply committed against it.
