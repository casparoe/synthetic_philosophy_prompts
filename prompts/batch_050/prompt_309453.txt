I've been reading around teleosemantics and Dennett's line about free-floating rationales -- reasons that exist in nature without any reasoner behind them, supplied by natural selection rather than by anyone's intentions. I worked up this argument from it and I think it proves way too much, but I can't pin down where:

(1) A trait can be *for* something -- there can be a reason it exists -- without anyone ever having had that reason in mind; selection does the job.
(2) What makes it a reason is just that the trait keeps recurring *because of* the effect in question: the effect explains the trait's continued existence.
(3) But lots of things persist because of their effects without anyone intending them: monopolies, clickbait headlines, urban sprawl, the arms race, earworm choruses.
(4) So those things are *for* something too -- there's a fact of the matter about what, say, inflation or motorway gridlock is for, and we could get it wrong.
(5) So hidden-purpose explanations of social phenomena aren't confused after all; the world is full of purposes nobody has.
(6) But (5) is just teleology run wild -- it licences a sort of secular conspiracism where everything has a function waiting to be unmasked. So something in (1)-(2) has to give, presumably the claim that selection gives us reasons without reasoners.

I like (1) and I'd rather not give it up, and I have a nagging feeling the slide happens around (2) or (3), but I can't articulate why "persists because of its effects" would be enough for wings but not for monopolies. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please answer in British English, and be honest rather than diplomatic: if I've mischaracterised the free-floating rationale idea, or the argument is fine and I should just bite the bullet at (6), or the whole setup rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than politely working around it.
