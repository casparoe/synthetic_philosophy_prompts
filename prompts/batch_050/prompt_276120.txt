**Simpson's paradox is a paradox about explanation, not about data**

Everyone knows the shape of Simpson's paradox. A new treatment for kidney stones beats the old one within small-stone patients and within large-stone patients, yet does worse overall. A university's graduate admissions accept women at a higher rate in nearly every department, yet men at a higher rate university-wide. Two baseball players, one with a better average than the other in each half of the season, swap positions when you pool the halves.

The standard takeaway these days, mostly following the causal-inference crowd, is that the paradox dissolves once you get the causal story straight: decide whether the partitioning variable is a confounder or a collider, and then either the disaggregated or the aggregate number is the one that should guide action. Stone size affects both which treatment you get and your chance of recovery, so trust the stratified numbers. Department choice is downstream of gender, so maybe trust the aggregate.

I want to argue this is too quick, because it treats the paradox as purely a problem for policy and misses why it *feels* like a paradox in the first place. My claim: the feeling of paradox tracks whether the aggregate statistic is doing explanatory work. "The new treatment is better" isn't just a summary of cure rates — we hear it as an explanation of why patients got better. And explanations, unlike raw frequencies, have to survive partitioning. So here's my proposed test, which I'll call the switch test: take any comparative claim of the form "A beats B", partition the data by any variable you might plausibly cite in the same breath as the comparison, and if the comparison reverses in every stratum, then the original claim was never an explanation at all — it was a bookkeeping artifact, and you shouldn't cite it even as *evidence*, let alone as a reason.

Two consequences I want to defend. First, the switch test explains why some Simpson cases feel paradoxical and others don't. The batting-average case barely registers as paradoxical because nobody thought "better hitter" explained anything beyond the hits themselves; the kidney stone case stings because "the better treatment" did. Second — and this is the part I expect pushback on — I think the test has teeth even in randomized trials. If a drug beats placebo in a proper RCT but the effect reverses sign in every pre-specified subgroup, the right conclusion isn't just "effect heterogeneity", it's that there is no single fact of the matter about whether the drug works, and journals should stop letting authors report the pooled effect as the headline. Aggregation is a rhetorical privilege you have to earn by surviving partitioning.
