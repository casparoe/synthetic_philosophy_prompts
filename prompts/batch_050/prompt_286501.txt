So I'm doing my master's thesis on semantic anti-realism (roughly the Dummett-flavored idea that truth can't outrun what could in principle be recognized or known), and my supervisor basically told me not to bother, because Fitch's knowability result already settled this decades ago: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is in fact known, which is definately false, so the knowability principle is dead and any anti-realism that needs it dies too. I've gone through the derivation and it really does look airtight — a few lines of modal and epistemic logic from premises that each seem innocent on their own (knowledge is factive, knowing a conjunction means knowing each conjunct, and so on). So the position I want stress-tested is the strong one my supervisor holds: that Fitch's theorem is a decisive, once-and-for-all refutation of the knowability principle, and thereby of anti-realism.

What would have to be true for that conclusion to be wrong? I want a map of the position's exposure, not a pep talk for anti-realism. For each premise or inference the derivation leans on: what would the logic or the epistemology have to look like for it to fail, and is that a live option people actually work within, or a merely consistent formal possibility nobody can motivate? I'm especially unsure about the move where you restrict the knowability principle to certain "well-behaved" truths — ones that don't stop being true once they come to be known — so that the problematic cases never get generated. My supervisor calls that an ad hoc patch, but is it? Does the restricted principle still let the anti-realist say everything they originally wanted to say, or does it quietly concede the dispute? Also tell me what defenders of the strong reading would themselves have to concede counts against it — for instance, would they have to admit the theorem only touches the fully unrestricted principle, and that a careful anti-realist might never have needed that version in the first place? And be willing to conclude that the strong reading is just very well insulated, and whether that's an intellectual virtue or a sign the philosophical moral has been overextended past what the formal result actually shows.

The thing I'd personally find most persuasive: a case where the derivation goes through fine but the conclusion clearly lacks the anti-realism-killing force people attribute to it — i.e., a demonstration that the theorem and the standard philosophical moral drawn from it come apart.
