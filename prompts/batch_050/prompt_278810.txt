I run the small-grants program at a private science foundation. Roughly twice a month we receive proposals from fields whose scientific status is contested — recent examples include a psychoanalytic outcome study, a machine-learning approach to dream interpretation, and a mindfulness-based therapy for chronic pain. My review board keeps invoking falsifiability as a screening criterion, but applies it inconsistently: sometimes any unfalsifiable-sounding claim disqualifies a proposal, and sometimes an enthusiastic promise to "test the hypotheses" is enough to pass.

Please write a screening procedure my reviewers can actually follow, grounded in what the demarcation problem literature has established about why naive falsificationism fails — the Duhem–Quine problem of auxiliary hypotheses, the fact that young theories are born already facing anomalies, Kuhn's point that normal science rarely abandons theories on a single failed prediction, and Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programs — while retaining the parts of Popper's insight that are practically usable. The procedure must be runnable in under an hour per proposal, specify at each step what evidence counts as sufficient, mark explicitly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, and distinguish three verdicts — unscientific, scientific but bad, and legitimate science that simply is not what this foundation funds — since only the first is an exclusion criterion. Then apply the procedure to two of the proposals above, one of which should be a case where the procedure admits something a skeptical reviewer would rather reject, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
