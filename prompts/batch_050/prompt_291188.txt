I'm a PhD student in social policy (dissertation on benefit conditionality, hence the question) and I keep getting stuck on the two-tier structure of the American welfare state. On one tier you've got Social Security retirement and Medicare: checks go out no matter what you do with your life. Nobody asks whether a retiree is looking for work, whether they're spending the money wisely, nothing. On the other tier you've got SNAP and TANF: work requirements, sanctions if you miss appointments, lifetime time limits, and every few years another state proposes drug testing recipients. Unemployment insurance sits weirdly in the middle — it's framed as something you paid into, like Social Security, but unlike Social Security it comes with job-search requirements and you can lose it for turning down a suitable offer.

So here's what I want to think through: what does this whole arrangement presuppose about why some benefits carry strings and others don't? The natural reading is that contributions create something like property — you paid in, so attaching conditions to your own money would be a kind of theft — while tax-funded transfers are gifts the giver can condition however they like. But if that's the implicit theory, UI breaks it, since UI is contributory and still conditional. Does the practice need a second theory, maybe about whether unemployment is chosen versus suffered? And which views does the two-tier structure actually rule out — could someone who thinks all unmet need generates equal claims, or a luck egalitarian, still endorse it, or does it presuppose something like a contribution-based theory of desert?

Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where would several rival theories license the same design? And concretely, what would have to change if the system were rebuilt on a needs-based view instead?
