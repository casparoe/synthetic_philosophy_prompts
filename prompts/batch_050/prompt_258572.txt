This past semester, in my graduate seminar on decision theory and the nature of persons, one of my stronger students presented a reconstruction of the now-familiar argument from transformative experience, and I have been unable to shake the suspicion that her version proves far too much. Her argument ran as follows. (1) A choice between options is rational only if the chooser can, at least roughly, assign expected subjective values to the outcomes from a single, stable evaluative standpoint. (2) The subjective value of living out a future depends essentially on two things: what that future is like from the inside, and which preferences one endorses while living it. (3) Certain experiences are epistemically transformative: what they are like cannot be known in advance — not by testimony, not by imagination, not by statistical knowledge of how others have fared — because the relevant knowledge is constitutively tied to undergoing the experience itself. (4) Such experiences are also personally transformative: they replace the chooser's core preferences with new ones, and since it is precisely her current evaluative standpoint that gets replaced, she cannot now assess the preferences she will later act from. (5) Hence, for any such choice, the condition stated in (1) cannot be satisfied: there is no well-defined schedule of expected values, indexed to one standpoint, for the chooser to compute. (6) Therefore such choices cannot be made rationally — they can only be made. (7) But the defining conditions of the class — high stakes, practical irreversibility, the dependence of the outcome's value on presently inaccessible phenomenology, and subsequent preference revision — are satisfied not only by the literature's stock cases (becoming a parent, becoming a vampire) but by emigrating, entering religious life, enlisting, marrying, and undergoing surgery that would restore a sense one has never had. (8) Worse, the argument is symmetric: declining the child, the move, or the vows is equally a choice whose foregone outcome cannot be evaluated from the inside, so remaining as one is enjoys no rational advantage over leaping. (9) It follows that the overwhelming majority of consequential human decisions are, in both directions, rationally undecidable — a result I take to be a reductio, since we manifestly deliberate about such matters and sometimes do so well.

I pressed the student on where the error lies, and her defense was robust enough that I now bring the question to you. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the fault lies with (3), I want to know what account of testimony, imagination, or statistical inference secures the needed access — and here I confess a constraint: I am loath to surrender the claim that there is something one can know only by having an experience, since I take the familiar thought experiments in the philosophy of mind to have established at least that much, so any repair at (3) should tell me precisely what I am giving up. If instead the fault lies with (1) or with the transition from (5) to (6), I want to hear what notion of rational choice survives once expected-value comparison from a fixed standpoint is abandoned — whether appeal to revelation, to second-order preferences, or to values one currently holds about the person one might become can do the work without smuggling the standpoint problem back in. And if one is tempted to accept (6) while blocking (7) through (9), I need a principled boundary between becoming a parent and, say, emigrating, that does more than point at a difference of degree. My own instinct is that something goes quietly wrong in the middle of the argument rather than at its foundations, but I have not been able to name it, and I would rather have the diagnosis than my instinct.
