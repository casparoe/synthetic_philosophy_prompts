I work as a software engineer, mostly on developer tooling and platform governance, and I have no formal training in philosophy, so forgive me if this is well-trodden ground. I am trying to find out whether a pattern I keep observing has an established name, and I have ended up wondering how it relates to something Pascal says in the Pensées.

The pattern, in my own terms: when a rule is enforced reliably and automatically, people's sense of what is right migrates to match whatever the system enforces, and the enforcement itself starts functioning as the justification. Three concrete instances. First, our continuous integration pipeline enforces a set of formatting rules that were chosen essentially arbitrarily years ago; new hires who have only ever known the enforced style now defend it as objectively better engineering practice, and they experience violations as sloppiness rather than as mere non-conformance. Second, in an online game I play, the automated anti-cheat system flags accounts, and the community has slid from asking whether a flagged player actually cheated to treating the flag as constitutive of cheating -- the ban is taken as proof of guilt rather than as a fallible consequence of it. Third, content filters on a platform I worked on changed what users could post, and within a year users' own reported intuitions about what counted as acceptable speech had shifted to track the filter, including on topics the filter had never touched.

What strikes me is the direction of the causal arrow: enforcement comes first, and the conviction that the enforced rule is just arrives afterwards, trailing behind it. This is not people grudgingly complying while privately dissenting -- the belief itself seems to move.

This brought to mind a passage in Pascal's Pensées, in the section on justice and the reason of effects (Section V in the Trotter translation). I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, since I have the text only in translation: Pascal says, roughly, that it is dangerous to let the people see that the laws are unjust, because they obey them only because they believe them to be just; so they must also be told to obey the laws simply because they are laws, just as they obey superiors not because superiors are just but because they are superiors -- and that if this could be made intelligible to them, sedition would be prevented entirely.

My questions:

1. Does the phenomenon I describe -- perceived justice produced by the fact of enforcement, rather than enforcement produced by perceived justice -- have an established name? Please distinguish it carefully from its nearest neighbours: status quo bias, habituation, adaptive preferences or sour grapes, preference falsification (which seems to be almost the mirror image, since there the private belief stays put while public conduct complies), and ordinary procedural legitimacy, where people accept outcomes because they accept the procedure that generated them. If none of these fits exactly, say so, and coin and define a term, making clear what its boundary cases would be -- in particular, when the mechanism fails and people see through the enforcement, and how one could tell it apart empirically from genuine persuasion.

2. Is the mechanism I describe the same one Pascal is relying on in that passage, or its inversion? He seems to assume that belief in the law's justice must come first, because it is what produces obedience, and that where justice is lacking, a substitute belief -- obey because it is the law -- must be installed instead. Does his recommendation presuppose that such beliefs can be manufactured from above, and if so, is that the same causal arrow as mine, or a different one? And does the difference matter morally: is it worse, better, or neither when the belief is produced by automated enforcement rather than by deliberate instruction?

Please answer in British English. I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
