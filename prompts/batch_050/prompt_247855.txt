I teach a continuing-education evening course on law for working journalists — bright adults, no legal training, about twenty of them, meeting for a single two-hour session. Next month's session is on strict liability: offences where a defendant can be convicted without proof of intention, knowledge, or even negligence as to one or more elements of the offence. The cohort keeps running into this in their reporting (a restaurateur convicted after serving a mislabelled allergen, a shopkeeper liable for an under-age sale despite checking what looked like genuine ID) and they consistently misreport it as either outrageous or as a mere technicality. I don't want you to explain strict liability to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them. Specify the order in which the components have to be introduced and why that order is forced — my instinct is that they need the actus reus/mens rea distinction and the negligence baseline before strict liability can look like a departure from anything, and I'd like you to confirm, correct, or refine that dependency structure, noting anything else they must grasp first (e.g., that mens rea is about the elements of the offence, not about motive or general wickedness). Then give me the one running example to build the whole session around — say what it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack so the concept stays visible and the session doesn't slide into a debate about sentencing, and explain why a regulatory/regulatory-adjacent scenario serves better than a violent-crime one.

The heart of what I'm asking for is the misconception map. Tell me which misunderstandings this audience will predictably arrive with or form mid-session — I expect at least: strict liability means convicting people who took every reasonable care; strict liability is the same as absolute liability, with no due-diligence defence ever available; strict liability means punishing for accidents or for someone else's acts; and the journalistic favourite, that it exists purely to make convictions easier for lazy prosecutors. For each, identify the exact point in my planned sequence where it should be headed off (not merely where it happens to come up), and the specific move that heads it off. Finally, give me one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the idea has actually landed rather than been memorised, and name the things I should openly decline to go into at this level (constitutional debates about whether strict liability is legitimate at all, the doctrine on corporate attribution, and so on), with a line I can use to defer them honestly. Close with a table of the misconception map with four columns: Misconception; Why this audience forms it; Point in sequence to address it; Defusing move.
