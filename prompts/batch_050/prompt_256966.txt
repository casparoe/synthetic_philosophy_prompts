I am a novelist preparing an afterword for a book whose most criticized chapter makes readers pity a charming traitor and, weeks later, notice a harsher suspicion toward gentle people in their own lives. Write a short dialogue between two equally capable philosophers who disagree about this effect. Call the first a quarantine theorist: emotions felt in reading are make-believe simulations, valuable for rehearsal and pleasure, but they become evidence or grounds for blame only when the reader exports a claim about actual persons. Call the second a porous-self theorist: sustained imagining is a form of training that can leave an affective residue, so an author may be answerable not for false propositions but for what the fiction repeatedly rehearses. Have them test their views against the residue case above, against the reply that readers know the traitor is invented, and against the harder claim that a scene can be manipulative even if every sentence in it is fictional. Neither speaker may caricature the other; each must concede the strongest pressure against their own view before answering it. Keep the exchange focused on whether responsibility tracks belief, habit, attention, or the design of emotional aftereffects.
