I serve on the acquisitions committee of a publicly funded regional gallery. A moderately well-known conceptual artist has offered us a work consisting of two items: an unaltered, commercially manufactured coat stand purchased from an ordinary furniture shop, and a notarised certificate signed by the artist. The certificate states that the coat stand is a work of art — title, date and edition number included — only for as long as it is owned by an institution that exhibits it publicly at least once in every five-year period; should it ever be sold into private hands, destroyed, or left in storage for longer than that, it reverts to being, in the artist's words, merely furniture. The offer includes a substantial accompanying bequest, contingent on our accepting the work into the permanent collection as art. Two committee members argue that a shop-bought coat stand cannot be a work of art whatever a certificate says, and that accepting it would discredit the collection. Two others argue that a committee which has learnt nothing from the past century of art practice ought not to be making acquisitions at all. The bequest would fund our education programme for three years. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — accept, decline, or accept under specified conditions — with a timetable, and it must take a reasoned position on whether this object is a work of art rather than merely citing precedent or deferring to expert consensus. In particular, it should address the self-referential feature of the certificate: whether the artwork-status of a thing can coherently depend on the future behaviour of the institution that owns it, and what follows for our decision if it can or cannot. The strongest argument on the losing side, whichever that turns out to be, should be stated fairly and answered, not caricatured. The opinion should be written so that a sceptical councillor with no philosophical training could follow it, without sacrificing precision.
