After thirty-eight years as an experimental solid-state physicist, I have spent the first years of my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I have become preoccupied with Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. In three weeks I am to give a talk to the philosophy society at my former university, defending the following thesis: the distinction between progressive and degenerating problemshifts is the best demarcation criterion we possess, but it can only ever deliver its verdicts in retrospect, and I intend to defend this retrospective character as a virtue rather than a failing, on the grounds that it matches how science actually adjudicates its own disputes.

My argument runs in three parts. First, naive falsificationism founders on the Duhem problem: no theory confronts observation alone, and Lakatos's hard core protected by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses describes how working scientists in fact behave when results go against them. Second, the positive heuristic supplies the forward-looking rationality that I find missing in Kuhn's account of normal science under a paradigm. Third, the requirement of novel facts — understood in Zahar's sense of use-novelty rather than mere temporal priority — gives content to progressiveness without demanding that we be able to say, at any given moment, which programme deserves our allegiance.

The audience will be about twenty people, and two of them concern me. One is a historian of early modern astronomy who knows the Lakatos–Zahar reconstruction of the Copernican case well and, I suspect, regards it as Whiggish — history tidied up to fit the methodology. The other is a philosopher who wrote her doctoral thesis on Feyerabend and regards Lakatos as Popper with the teeth removed. The remainder are interested amateurs and a few old-school Popperians.

Prepare me for the question period. I should like the six or seven objections I am most likely to receive, ordered by the damage each would do to my thesis if answered badly, and phrased as the person most likely to raise it would actually phrase it — the historian's objections should sound like a historian's, not a logician's, and the Feyerabend scholar's like hers. For each objection, tell me what I ought to concede, what I ought to hold, and give me a reply deliverable in under two minutes, with a note on where that reply is softest. Finally, tell me the question I am quietly hoping nobody puts to me — I have my suspicions about what it is — and explain why it is the dangerous one.
