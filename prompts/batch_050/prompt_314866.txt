I'm a graduate student in a historiography seminar on the French Revolution, and I need help dissecting a line that keeps doing argumentative work in our discussions: "revolutions devour their own children." It came up again last week when we were debating whether the Terror of 1793–94 was already latent in the principles of 1789 or was instead the contingent product of war, food shortages, and factional struggle in the Convention. One participant invoked the slogan to shut the debate down, as if the fates of the Girondins, Danton, and Robespierre proved by themselves that radicalization was a law of revolutionary dynamics rather than a sequence of avoidable decisions. I suspect the slogan trades on ambiguity, but I can't yet articulate the different claims it might be making.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing. Candidates I half-see include, but are not limited to: a bare empirical generalization (revolutions characteristically end up killing or ruining their own leaders); a structural claim about revolutionary competition (in the absence of settled rules for transferring power, factions must outbid one another in radicalism, and yesterday's moderates become today's traitors); a claim about ideology (revolutionary virtue is defined so as to generate an endless supply of suspects, since purity can always be impugned); a claim about legitimacy (regimes born in rupture cannot distinguish loyal opposition from counter-revolution, so all dissent becomes capital); a psychological or moral claim (the temperament that makes a revolutionary is consumed by the very qualities it unleashes); and a merely definitional point (the slogan says only that revolutionaries die by the Revolution rather than by the old regime, which may be trivially true of any violent upheaval). For each thesis you identify, give a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false — and if false, what is the cleanest counterexample (the American Revolution, the Glorious Revolution of 1688, 1848)?

Then do the two harder things. First, say which of these readings, if any, would actually license the use my classmate made of the slogan — namely, settling in advance the dispute between ideological-determinist and contingency-focused accounts of the Terror without examining the evidence. If none of the defensible readings licenses that move, say exactly why not: what additional premise would have to be smuggled in? Second, address the word "children": the slogan presupposes a distinction between the Revolution's own offspring and its external enemies, yet the Terror's victims included aristocrats, refractory priests, and peasants of the Vendée alongside Hébertists and Dantonists. Does the slogan require a prior theory of who counts as an insider, and does that requirement make it unfalsifiable — immune to counterexamples because whoever survives retrospectively counts as the Revolution's true child? I'd like the output structured so I can bring it to the next session: a numbered list of readings with verdicts, followed by a short section on what the slogan can and cannot legitimately be used to conclude.
