I write a genetics column for a general-interest magazine, and I rely constantly on the analogy that DNA is a code: the genome is a text in a four-letter alphabet, genes are instructions, and the cell is a reader that transcribes and executes them. Recently I used this to argue that genome editing is essentially copy-editing -- if an organism is its genome the way a book is its text, then rewriting a sequence is like fixing a sentence, and traits can be revised as cleanly as prose. A colleague who works in developmental biology pushed back, saying the analogy is doing all the argumentative work and that the cell is nothing like a reader. Please act as a professional philosopher of biology and think carefully before answering; begin with a short brainstorm of the relevant features of codes and of gene expression, then give the audit. Specifically: lay out the correspondence table explicitly -- what plays the role of the alphabet, the words, the sentences, the author, the reader, the meaning, and the act of reading -- and mark the slots where the mapping has no clear occupant. Identify where the analogy breaks down (consider, among other things, whether anything in the cell fixes the "meaning" of a sequence the way convention fixes the meaning of words, the role of cellular and environmental context in what a stretch of DNA actually does, and the many-to-many relation between sequence and trait). Separate the disanalogies that matter for my copy-editing conclusion from the ones that don't. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether my colleague's objection succeeds or overcorrects. Finally, if there is a better analogy for what genome editing accomplishes, propose it and say what it gets right that the code analogy gets wrong.
