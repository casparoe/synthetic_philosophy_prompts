A colleague circulated a summary of the philosophical literature on aesthetic testimony, taken from a recent popular book on everyday aesthetics, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. The summary runs as follows:

"When it comes to aesthetic matters, testimony is useless. Philosophers of aesthetics endorse what is called the acquaintance principle: an aesthetic judgment about a work of art is only legitimate if it is based on direct, first-hand experience of the work itself. If a trusted friend who shares your taste tells you that a film is profound and moving, that testimony cannot give you the belief that the film is profound and moving; you must see the film yourself. The standard explanation is that aesthetic properties, unlike ordinary facts, are not the sort of thing that can be transmitted from one mind to another through assertion -- they can only be apprehended in experience. It follows that aesthetic testimony is unlike testimony about, say, the time of a train: in the train case, hearing it from a reliable source settles the matter; in the aesthetic case, it settles nothing. This is the consensus position in the field. It also has a practical corollary that the book draws out: reading reviews before choosing what to watch or listen to is fundamentally misguided, and deferring to critics is a category mistake, since no amount of reliable testimony can substitute for acquaintance."

Something about this feels off to me, but I cannot put my finger on it. Please audit the summary for accuracy. Specifically: (1) separate what is an accurate statement of the acquaintance principle from what subtly distorts it, including whether the principle is best understood as a claim about what beliefs one can hold, what judgments are legitimate, or what understanding one can possess; (2) say whether the analogy with train-time testimony is fair, or whether it overstates the gap by ignoring a distinction the literature draws; (3) assess the claim of consensus -- are there serious defenders of the opposing view, and if so what roughly do they say; and (4) most importantly, tell me whether the practical corollary actually follows, or whether it rests on a conflation of two claims the debate carefully keeps apart. I am inclined to keep reading reviews, but I want to know whether the corrected version of the view gives me principled grounds for doing so or merely shows that the book overreached.
