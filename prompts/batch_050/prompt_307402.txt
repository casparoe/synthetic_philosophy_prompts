I am a junior in high school and I serve on my school's student-run Honor Council. Roughly once a month we hear a case and have to recommend a consequence to the faculty advisor, who signs off on it. Our school's honor code, like most, is written around actions: cheating, plagiarizing, stealing, lying. But about a third of what actually reaches us is inaction -- a student who watched someone copy during an exam and said nothing, a group member who noticed fabricated data in the shared lab report and let it be submitted, a student who knew a rumor about a classmate was false and let it keep spreading. The code is silent on these, and our deliberations on them are chaos: some council members treat them as equivalent to the underlying offense, others as no violation at all.

Please act as a professional moral philosopher who has experience translating the philosophical literature for non-specialists, and think carefully before writing. First, spend a short section brainstorming: list the candidate distinctions from the philosophical work on doing versus allowing harm (for example, making something bad happen versus letting it happen, redirecting a threat versus creating one, intending versus merely foreseeing, and the idea of a normal baseline against which a harm is measured), and briefly note which of them seem load-bearing and which seem like they would collapse if a tired seventeen-year-old tried to apply them in a forty-five-minute hearing.

Then write a decision procedure my council could actually run in those hearings: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure stops and concludes there is no honor violation even if something morally bad occurred; and the points where the procedure must hand the question to the council as a value judgment rather than a fact-finding, because the doing/allowing distinction itself does not settle it. Be explicit about which distinctions survived your simplification and which you deliberately dropped, and honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases: (1) a student who secretly changes a lab partner's data in a shared spreadsheet before submission; (2) a student who notices the data is wrong, realizes correcting it will delay their own project, and says nothing; (3) a student whose friend confides a plan to plagiarize, who urges the friend not to but does not report it. I suspect at least one of these will produce a recommendation I will not like, and I would rather you follow the procedure honestly than flatter my intuitions -- flag explicitly where that happens.
