I'm a third-year philosophy major taking a seminar on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment. For our final unit, the professor has asked each student to build a map of the debate over what the alignment problem actually is — in particular, whether it is one problem or several only loosely related problems that have been lumped together under a single name. I'd like your help producing a decision-tree taxonomy I can use as a class handout.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in the conceptual landscape of AI safety who also has experience designing teaching materials. Work in two stages. First, in a short brainstorming section (no more than 200 words), sketch the logical space of possible decompositions — the axes along which views about "the alignment problem" can genuinely come apart — and note which axes you think will do the most sorting work. Then build the tree itself.

Requirements for the tree:

- The root question should be whether "alignment" names one unified problem or several; both answers must be reachable as leaves.
- Label each branch with the claim that splits it, and wherever possible phrase that claim as a verdict on a concrete case rather than an abstract thesis, so readers sort themselves by their judgments rather than by which labels they like. Please use these cases somewhere in the tree: (i) a system that does exactly what its operator asks, where the operator's aims are bad; (ii) a system trained on a flawless reward signal that nonetheless internalizes a proxy goal and pursues it competently; (iii) a system that reports its own reasoning transparently, where that reasoning is aimed at an objective nobody chose; (iv) a system that is perfectly corrigible — it lets itself be shut down and redirected — but holds no values of its own beyond that.
- The following positions must appear as leaves, arrived at by answers rather than named up front: the view that the whole problem reduces to specifying the right values; the two-factor decomposition into outer and inner alignment; the view that alignment to an operator's intent is sufficient and that further questions are political rather than technical; the view that mesa-optimization is the irreducible core; a deflationary view on which there is no philosophical problem here, only engineering; a view on which the problem reduces to governance and coordination among humans; and a view on which sufficiently capable systems converge on moral truth, making alignment either trivial or impossible depending on one's metaethics.
- At each leaf, give the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Note any logically available but unoccupied combinations of answers, with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody sits there (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).
- Flag any required positions the tree cannot cleanly separate, rather than papering over the failure.

The audience is classmates with one semester of philosophy of mind and no AI background. Aim for six to nine branch questions. No citations or reading lists are needed — I'll handle those myself.
