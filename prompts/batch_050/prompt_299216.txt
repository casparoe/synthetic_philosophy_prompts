A friend and I have a long-running disagreement about expected value fanaticism — roughly, the thesis that rational agents must maximize expected value even when probabilities are tiny and payoffs enormous. Her credence in the thesis is about 0.9; mine is about 0.25. The disagreement recently became concrete: she declined a secure, high-confidence path to doing good in favor of a speculative research agenda she estimates has perhaps a one-in-a-million chance of success, with a payoff conditional on success she takes to be astronomically large, and she maintains this was the only defensible choice rather than a judgment call.

Give me your credence that she is right — specifically, that fanaticism is the correct decision rule for agents in our epistemic position, not merely for idealized reasoners with well-defined probabilities. Decompose the estimate: the probability that credences in the 10^-6 range about untestable mechanisms are epistemically respectable rather than noise; the probability that the outcomes at stake are unbounded in value, or at least large enough to make her arithmetic go through; the probability that Pascal's-mugging-style counterexamples admit a principled answer short of abandoning maximization; and the probability that the standard non-fanaticist alternatives — bounded utility, risk-weighted expected value, a floor below which small probabilities are simply ignored — are coherent and do not produce worse verdicts elsewhere. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, show how they combine, then tell me which term most plausibly accounts for the gap between 0.9 and 0.25, and what evidence or argument would move your final number the most.
