I lead the ethics review board at a large consumer software company, and my team keeps hitting a version of the same problem. Product changes rarely harm anyone seriously; instead they impose tiny costs on enormous numbers of people -- an extra confirmation dialog for three hundred million users, a notification that briefly annoys two billion, an autoplay default that wastes ten seconds a day each. Reviewers on my board handle these intuitively and inconsistently: some treat any harm below a certain felt intensity as a rounding error no matter how many people it touches, while others multiply everything out and end up concluding that a mildly irritating banner outweighs a feature that would genuinely ruin a handful of users' lives (say, a recommendation system that reliably pushes a few hundred vulnerable teenagers into months of disordered eating). I know philosophers argue about whether arbitrarily many tiny harms can ever aggregate to outweigh a severe one -- the dust-specks-versus-torture style debate -- but I need that dispute turned into something my reviewers can actually run, without requiring them to take a position on the metaphysics.

Please write a decision procedure for proposals of this form: a fixed set of questions, in order, that a reviewer can work through in under an hour. The procedure should operationalize the live positions in the aggregation debate rather than silently picking one -- e.g., it should force the reviewer to check whether the small harm is truly below some threshold of experienced significance or merely below some threshold of salience, whether the harms hit the same people repeatedly (so that "ten seconds a day" is really one person's cumulative burden, not billions of separate specks), and whether the severe harm to the few is something the many would accept for themselves if roles were reversed. It must specify: where the procedure delivers a verdict on its own, where it requires escalation to the full board, and which steps are irreducibly judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle, and where it will misfire.

Then apply the procedure to two cases from our queue: (1) adding a one-time three-screen onboarding flow that internal testing shows mildly frustrates nearly all of our 500 million users, in exchange for eliminating a security flaw that would seriously harm an estimated two thousand users per year; and (2) a default setting that saves each user roughly four seconds per day but, for a small identifiable minority, causes a persistent low-grade compulsion loop they report hating but cannot disable. At least one of these should come out with a verdict I will find uncomfortable -- tell me which, and why the procedure commits to it anyway. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
