I retired three years ago after four decades in molecular biology, and since then I have been making my way through moral and metaphysical philosophy with the zeal of a late convert. My reading group has just finished Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife, and for our next meeting I have proposed pairing it with Alfonso Cuarón's film Children of Men (2006), loosely adapted from P. D. James's novel. Scheffler's afterlife conjecture holds, roughly, that the point and value of much of what we do depends on our confidence that humanity will continue after we die -- his doomsday and infertility thought experiments ask us to imagine a world in which no one dies prematurely but humanity has no future, and to observe how our projects and attachments wilt under that supposition. The infertility scenario is, of course, precisely the film's premise: no child has been born for eighteen years, and the youngest person on earth, the celebrated Baby Diego, is killed in the opening minutes.

I would like a reading of the film through this lens, in preparation for leading the discussion. Specifically: where does the film dramatize the conjecture -- the empty schoolyards, the state-issued suicide kits, the lassitude that settles over ordinary life -- and does it support Scheffler's claim that the badness of extinction outruns the deaths it involves, given that here almost no one is dying of the catastrophe itself? Where does it complicate the conjecture: the film's horror is bound up with authoritarianism, caged refugees, and the Bexhill camp, so how much of the despair on screen is futurelessness and how much is ordinary political evil wearing it as a costume? Take the government's art-salvage project as a probe: what is the point of preserving Michelangelo's David for an audience that will never exist, and does the film treat that effort as noble, absurd, or something in between -- and what follows for the scope of Scheffler's claim? Finally, the ending: does Kee's newborn restore value to the world in a way that vindicates the thought that what we need is confidence in a collective afterlife rather than our own survival, and what work is the ambiguity of the ship Tomorrow doing?

I also want the reading to be honest about strain. Where is the film doing something Scheffler's framework cannot absorb, or vice versa, and what does the film's stubborn refusal to explain the infertility -- a detail my scientific training makes me itch at -- contribute that the bare thought experiment lacks? No citations needed; I want the argument, not the apparatus.
