The student's answer reads as follows:

"Newcomb's problem, first formulated by Robert Nozick: a being with an excellent track record predicts your choice between taking only an opaque box, or taking both the opaque box and a transparent one containing $1,000. If the being predicted you would take only the opaque box, it placed $1,000,000 inside; if it predicted you would take both, it left the opaque box empty. Causal decision theory recommends taking both boxes. Whatever is already in the opaque box, taking both yields exactly $1,000 more, so two-boxing dominates; the contents are fixed and your present action is causally independent of them. Evidential decision theory instead recommends the act whose performance would be the best news. Given the predictor's record, taking one box is strong evidence that $1,000,000 is there, so one-boxing has the higher evidential expected value. This recommendation, however, depends on the predictor being essentially infallible: if the success rate fell to, say, 60 percent, the evidential link would be too weak and evidential decision theory would join causal decision theory in recommending two-boxing. The two-boxer, for her part, holds that one-boxing tacitly assumes backwards causation, as though choosing now could reach back and alter yesterday's prediction. Since nothing can do that -- the money either is or is not in the box before I act -- the observation that the contents are fixed really does settle the disagreement in favor of two-boxing, and one-boxers are guilty of a superstitious confusion."
