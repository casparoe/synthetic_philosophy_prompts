I'm a PhD student in political theory, and next month I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on contested monuments. My thesis is that the standard "retain and explain" compromise -- keeping a contested statue in place but adding a contextualizing plaque -- fails on its own terms, because a monument's expressive force lies in its scale, placement, and material prominence, none of which a plaque can neutralize; contextualization works for artifacts in museums precisely because the museum has already stripped those features. The audience is roughly half historians and public-history practitioners, half moral and political philosophers. The historians will care about erasure, precedent, and how these decisions actually get made; the philosophers will press on the expressivist machinery and whether my argument proves too much. Prepare me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from each camp, phrased the way that camp would phrase them, ordered by how damaging they'd be, with a short reply to each and a flag on where the reply is weakest. Include what I should concede outright, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. No more than 600 words.
