My friend Dana did her phd on the philosophy of emotion and ever since she's been insufferable about what does and doesn't count as an emotion. We got into it again last week over beers, and I want to figure out the actual logic behind her calls, because she swears she's being consistent. Her verdicts so far, as best I remember them:

- The fear she felt when an off-leash dog charged her in the park: obviously an emotion, paradigm case.
- The startle when a pan crashed in the kitchen: not an emotion, just a reflex.
- The vague unease she gets on sunday evenings that isn't about anything in particular: not an emotion, that's a mood.
- Her resentment of a former colleague, which has run for years and involves no racing heart or tension at all, just a settled sense that he treated her badly: still an emotion, she insists.
- Her belief that climate change is terrifying, which she holds in a totally cold-blooded way and which doesn't move her to do much of anything: not an emotion, just an evaluative belief.

Here's the new case I'm planning to spring on her, and I want to predict her answer first. Her sister is phobic about flying. She'll tell you sincerely that flying is safer than driving, she can quote the statistics, she genuinely believes it -- and yet she can't sleep the week before a flight, her hands shake at the gate, and last year she got off a plane before takeoff. So: is the sister's state an emotion (namely fear), and what would Dana say? Please work out at least two different principles that each fit all five of her earlier verdicts but come apart on this case, tell me which one you think is really driving her judgments and why, and flag any earlier verdict that rules out some other obvious theory of emotion outright. Also, if you think her track record genuinely underdetermines the sister case, say so, and give me one more hypothetical case whose verdict would settle which of the two principles is hers.
