I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized company, and over the past year our team has been building features on top of large language models, which has sparked a running debate in our engineering Slack about whether these systems genuinely understand anything. I have no formal philosophy background, but I read a fair amount of commentary on both sides. Last week a colleague of mine, who is generally careful and whose judgment I trust, posted a summary of the "stochastic parrots" critique — the argument associated with the well-known paper by Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, and their collaborators — and concluded that the debate is essentially settled. I've been turning his summary over since and can't decide whether it's a faithful representation or a caricature, so I'd like a second opinion from someone who actually knows this literature.

His summary went roughly as follows: The stochastic parrots argument shows that large language models cannot understand language, and this is now close to a consensus position among serious researchers. The core proof is a thought experiment involving an octopus that taps into an undersea cable between two humans and learns to imitate their conversation purely from statistical patterns; because the octopus would fail the moment the conversation required real-world knowledge it had never encountered, it follows that any system trained on text alone is in the same position — it manipulates linguistic form without ever connecting it to meaning, since meaning requires grounding in embodied experience and communicative intent, both of which a language model definitionally lacks. He then added that "stochastic parrot" is a precise technical description of what these models do: they stitch together fragments of their training data by probabilistic pattern-matching, with no internal representation of what the text is about, which is why they confidently produce fluent nonsense. Finally, he argued that anyone who claims these models understand is guilty of anthropomorphizing — we see coherent text and reflexively project a mind behind it, the same way people were fooled by much simpler chatbots decades ago — and that the main upshot of the paper is that we should stop asking whether LLMs understand, because that question was answered in the negative.

My instinct is that this is mostly right but that something is off, though I can't articulate what. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful proponent of the stochastic parrots critique would endorse, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and which parts misrepresent what the original argument actually claimed? In particular: does the octopus thought experiment establish what he says it establishes, or is it an intuition pump rather than a proof? Is "the model just stitches together training fragments" the same claim as "the model's training on form alone can't ground meaning," and do both sides accept the first? Is it accurate to describe the paper's central aim as settling whether LLMs understand, or was it principally about something else, with the understanding question entering differently? And is he right that the anthropomorphizing charge cuts only one way, or are there serious arguments on the other side — from people who fully grant how these models are trained — that he hasn't mentioned? I'd like to come away knowing what to trust in his summary and how to respond if the debate comes up again.
