I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has an eccentric tech billionaire who announces a game: he'll give a hundred million dollars to whoever writes him a letter asking for it — but only if exactly one person writes in. If two letters arrive, or none, nobody gets a cent. I stole the setup shamelessly from something I half-remember reading years ago. Douglas Hofstadter apparently ran games like this for readers of his Scientific American column back in the 80s: one where people could enter a lottery as many times as they liked and the prize was a fixed sum divided by the total number of entries (which I'm pretty sure ended in dissaster when people mailed in astronomical numbers of entries), and another with roughly the 'exactly one entrant wins' rule. Wrapped around these was his idea of superrationality: since all the players are thinking about the same problem, and each knows the others are too, a rational player should reason their way to whatever conclusion everyone else will also reach, and act on that basis.

Here's my problem. For the plot I need to know whether superrationality actually tells my characters anything they couldn't already get from plain expected-value reasoning, or whether it's just a fancy costume on the symmetry problem. On one side: in the one-letter game, ordinary reasoning says everyone enters with some small probability tuned so that exactly one letter landing is as likely as possible, and that's that — superrationality adds nothing, everyone symmetrically randomizes the same way, and you still usually get zero letters or three. And the lottery fiasco seems to show that 'we all reason identically' can march a whole crowd off a cliff rather than save it. On the other side, maybe that's too quick: perhaps shared reasoning really can do coordination work that independent randomizing can't, or the disaster cases show something important about when the idea breaks rather than showing it's empty. I'm genuinely torn, and I've had two glasses of wine arguing about it with my husband, so I definately need outside help.

So: write me an essay weighing both sides, but land somewhere — I need a verdict for the book, not a shrug. Does superrationality earn its keep as a real decision procedure in games like these, or does it collapse into ordinary game theory plus wishful thinking about coordination? And since the plot partly hinges on this, don't be coy about your own fallibility: tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the one step in your argument where you feel least solid — the point where, if you're wrong, the whole essay tips over.
