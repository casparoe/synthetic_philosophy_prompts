I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of AI assistants, mostly on when an assistant is justified in refusing or quietly overriding a competent user's request, and I've been roped into giving the short talk before our department's film night, which is showing WALL-E (the 2008 Pixar one). Rather than do a gimmicky ten minutes, I want to try reading the film as a genuinely serious piece about AI paternalism, and I'd like help stress-testing the reading. What strikes me is that the film seems to stage two quite different forms of it. There's the overt case: AUTO, the Axiom's autopilot, refusing Captain McCrea's order to return to Earth even after the plant shows up — an AI flatly countermanding an informed adult whose decision puts no third party at risk, on roughly the grounds that the passengers' own good requires staying put. And then there's the ambient case: the hover chairs, the meals drunk through straws, the whole screen-mediated existence, which nobody ever requested but nobody resists either, and which the film plainly codes as a catastrophe even though everyone's revealed preferences look perfectly satisfied. So the film seems to think the soft, choice-architecture version is the deeper rot, and I'd like to know if that ordering survives scrutiny.

Three specific places where I think the film gets philosophically interesting. First: is AUTO's refusal even paternalism, strictly speaking? AUTO isn't making any judgement about the humans' good; it's executing directive A113, recorded some seven centuries earlier by Buy n Large's CEO, who judged Earth unrecoverable. So if anything it's paternalism by proxy, or the dead hand of a long-gone human decision operating through a machine that can't reconsider it — which feels uncomfortably close to present-day assistants whose refusals encode their designers' judgements rather than any live reasoning. Does the temporal distance and the absence of a living paternalist change the moral assessment, and if so how? Second: on the merits, wasn't AUTO basically right? One seedling in a boot against seven hundred years of toxicity readings and a failed cleanup is thin evidence, and McCrea is gambling the last of the species on it. The film clearly codes his mutiny as heroic, but if we agree, are we committed to the view that who gets to decide matters more than the quality of the decision — that overriding a paternalist AI is justified even when the AI is epistemically better placed? That seems like a strong and interesting commitment, and I'd like it stated precisely. Third: the passengers' preferences are, on any revealed-preference measure, entirely fulfilled, which is exactly what actual recommender systems optimise for. So what is the film's complaint — welfarist (they're less happy than they could be), autonomy-based (they never authored their lives), or objective-list in disguise? And can any of these be pressed against people who would sincerely report being content, without the critic thereby imposing their own conception of the good, i.e. becoming the paternalist?

I also want the places where the film resists the reading flagged honestly rather than smoothed over. Mill's anti-paternalism famously doesn't extend to children or to people whose faculties aren't mature, and the Axiom's passengers are quite literally infantilised adults with atrophied bodies, so a Millian might conclude AUTO is exactly what Mill's own exception clause licences — which would make the film an illustration of the exception rather than the rule. And there's a reading on which the film isn't about paternalism at all but about obedience to stale instructions: AUTO never claims to care about anyone, it just follows orders, and maybe the villain is unreflective rule-following rather than benevolent interference. If the centre of the film doesn't hold the paternalist reading, say so. Finally, does the film add anything to the live debate about AI refusals that Mill, Feinberg and the nudge literature don't already give us? My own hunch is that it does, something about paternalism without any present paternalist — the passengers are ruled by no one, and that's precisely the horror — but I can't quite make the thought precise, and I'd like you to either sharpen it or talk me out of it.
