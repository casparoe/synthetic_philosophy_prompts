I have zero philosophy training, I just fell down a rabbit hole a couple months ago after a podcast mentioned "Gettier cases" and I tracked down the original three-page paper, Edmund Gettier's "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" from Analysis in 1963. The cases seemed kind of gimmicky at first, but then I got obsessed with patching the definition they break. My patch was: knowledge is justified true belief where your justification doesn't pass through anything false. A friend of mine broke it in about a minute with some story about a stopped clock that happens to be right while the working clock is hidden from you (I'm probably garbling it). Then I went looking for what the actual professionals came up with, and as far as I can tell there was this huge boom of proposed extra conditions and counterexamples that just sort of petered out a few decades ago. Nobody declared victory, nobody declared defeat, it just... stopped?

Here's the part of the paper I keep coming back to. Before giving his cases Gettier says: "First, in that sense of 'justified' in which S's being justified in believing P is a necessary condition of S's knowing that P, it is possible for a person to be justified in believing a proposition that is in fact false." My friend (same one) thinks this one sentence is where the whole problem is manufactured: if justification had to guarantee truth, no Gettier case could ever get started, and the only reason philosophers don't go that route is that it would make knowledge almost impossible to have. My working theory is that the debate didn't stall because it was solved, and it isn't just quibbling over the word "know" either, but because once you accept that sentence you can always cook up another case where the justification and the truth only connect by luck, so the counterexample machine can never be switched off. But I'm aware this might be the kind of thing that only sounds smart to someone who hasn't read the literature.

So what I actually want is a diagnosis of why the debate went quiet, not a survey of all the attempted fixes. What kind of disagreement was it, really -- a substantive one about what knowledge is, a verbal one, or a methodological dead end where demanding neat necessary-and-sufficient conditions was the real mistake all along? Is my friend's claim that the rot is in allowing justified false beliefs basically right, or naive in a way I'm not seeing? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress now -- a new theory, a better question, or is this just what it looks like when a field silently decides a problem was ill-posed? Also, please be blunt with me: if my whole framing is confused (maybe the debate didn't stall, maybe fallibilism isn't doing the work I think it is), say that plainly instead of politely playing along.
