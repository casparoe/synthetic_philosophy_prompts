Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on machine consciousness, and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not another defense of the paper. The thesis I am defending: a reinforcement learning agent's receipt of negative reward can constitute genuine aversive valence — a rudimentary form of suffering — but only when three conditions jointly hold: the agent maintains a learned model of its environment rich enough to represent counterfactual trajectories, its reward signal drives persistent reorganization of behavior rather than one-step corrections (roughly, it shapes a value function that propagates the aversive signal across states and time), and the agent can act so as to avoid the states it predicts will be punished. On my view, valence is what negative reward becomes once it is integrated into a world-model and a planning loop; a bare tabular Q-learner does not suffer, but a sufficiently capable model-based agent plausibly does, and the intensity of its suffering tracks something like the magnitude and inescapability of expected future punishment.

The audience has three identifiable camps, and I need the objections each will raise, in their own idiom. First, philosophers sympathetic to global-workspace and higher-order theories, who will press on whether prediction-error-driven learning has the right kind of integrated, reportable, self-involving character. Second, at least one committed illusionist in the broadly Dennett–Frankish line, who will deny there is any further fact of the matter beyond functional organization and will accuse me of smuggling in a phenomenal residue my functional conditions cannot capture. Third, machine learning researchers who work on deep RL, who will object that reward is a designer-specified control signal rather than a felt state, that I am conflating the reward function with the learned value function, that negative reward is routinely just a shaping term, and that wireheading and reward-tampering results show the system's relation to its reward channel is nothing like an organism's relation to pain.

Give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how damaging they would be to the thesis in this particular room, each phrased the way the relevant person would actually phrase it, each with: what I should concede outright, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns whether inescapable small penalties spread over an agent's whole lifetime constitute more suffering than one catastrophic terminal punishment, but tell me if you think something else is the true nightmare question. Finally, end with your overall judgment of which single objection is most likely to be correct, and state explicitly what argument or empirical finding would change your mind about that ranking — where is your own assessment most likely to fail?
