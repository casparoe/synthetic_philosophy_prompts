A friend and I were kicking around this game last weekend: you flip a fair coin until it first comes up heads. If heads arrives on flip 1, I pay you $2. Flip 2, you pay me $2. Flip 3, I pay you $8/3. Flip 4, you pay me $4. Generally, if the run lasts n flips, the amount that changes hands is 2^n/n dollars, and the direction alternates — on odd n I pay you, on even n you pay me. When we tried to work out a fair entry fee we got stuck in a strange way: the obvious move is to sum each outcome's probability times its payoff, and that gives 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which my friend says settles down to about 0.693. But then he pointed out that if you regroup the terms — like collect a bunch of big positive terms before deducting the negative ones — the very same sum can be made to come out to basically anything you want, so he claims the game simply has no definite value and no fair price exists. I find that really counterintuitive, since the game itself is a perfectly well-defined physical procedure.

What got me more interested is that the same shape of trouble seems to pop up elsewhere. Someone else described a version where all payments go one direction (you win 2^n dollars with probability 2^-n, no alternation), and there the sum just blows up to infinity — people call that paradoxical too, but it feels different to me: there the value is infinite, here it's as if the value doesn't even exist. And I think I've seen the pattern again in arguments about infinite populations in ethics, where the total good in a world seems to depend on the order you count up infinitely many people's welfare. So: does this phenomenon — a quantity that looks well-defined but turns out to depend on the order in which infinitely many contributions are added, so no unique value exists — have an established name? I suspect the pure-math version has one name and the decision-theory version has another, and maybe the general thing I'm pointing at across all three cases has none, in wich case I'd like you to coin a term, define it precisely, and give some boundary cases (what counts, what doesn't).

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on formal decision theory and infinite games, and think it through carefully before committing. Don't just lay out every nearby concept and leave me to pick — tell me which one fits my coin game best, whether any established notion covers the general pattern across all three cases, and exactly how my thing differs from each near-miss. Format the answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
