**Draft: "The Correlation Budget: Why Deliberative Design Can Make Groups Dumber"**

1. The Condorcet jury theorem is usually stated as a triumph: if each of n voters is independently correct with probability p > 1/2, then majority rule is correct with probability approaching 1 as n grows. The standard gloss treats independence as a technical nuisance — a footnote to be discharged by a sentence about "diverse perspectives." I want to argue that independence is not a footnote but the whole load-bearing structure, and that the way we actually design deliberative bodies trades independence away for competence in a deal that is often arithmetically bad.

2. Consider the two ways correlation enters. First, *common-source correlation*: every voter forms their judgment from a shared body of evidence — the same expert report, the same media environment — and errs only if that shared body is misleading. Model this as a common signal that is correct with probability q, after which voters judge independently with competence p. Then the large-group accuracy of majority rule converges to q, not to 1: once the group is large, private variation washes out and the majority simply ratifies whatever the common signal said. A thousand voters fed the same flawed briefing are, epistemically, one flawed briefing with extra steps.

3. Second, *exchangeable correlation*: suppose any two voters' errors are correlated with coefficient ρ > 0, regardless of how many voters there are. Then the variance of the group's average judgment does not shrink to zero as n grows; it bottoms out at a floor set by ρ. The group behaves like an "effective sample" whose size saturates near 1/ρ. Adding voters past that point is decoration.

4. Now the design claim. Deliberative minipublics, expert panels, and advisory committees are typically engineered to raise p: members receive balanced briefings, hear testimony, and deliberate together. But every one of these interventions also raises correlation. Briefings create common sources; deliberation aligns members on a shared framing of the problem, which is precisely a common signal. The designer faces what I call a *correlation budget*: for a group whose judgments exhibit exchangeable correlation ρ, improvements in individual competence p have sharply diminishing returns once the effective sample size is capped by ρ. The theorem's asymptotic promise is spent entirely within the first 1/ρ members. A design that raises p from 0.6 to 0.7 while raising ρ from 0.02 to 0.10 has cut the effective sample from roughly 50 to roughly 10 — a bad trade even though every member got smarter.

5. The remedy I want to defend is *partitioned deliberation*: divide the body into k subgroups; give each subgroup a deliberately non-identical evidentiary diet — different expert witnesses, differently framed briefings, different background readings; let each subgroup deliberate internally and vote; then aggregate the subgroup majorities by a further majority vote, with no cross-talk between subgroups before the votes are cast. The idea is to purchase independence between subgroups at the price of within-subgroup correlation and some information fragmentation. Since the common signal is now k distinct common signals rather than one, the limiting accuracy of the top-level aggregation is governed by the probability that a majority of independent evidentiary streams are individually non-misleading, rather than by the reliability of a single stream.

6. I concede two costs. The information-pooling argument: siloing subgroups destroys deliberation's main epistemic benefit, the exchange of reasons, and small subgroups have noisy internal majorities. The reply: pooling only helps if it transmits information; when the pool is dominated by one shared framing, pooling is correlation in disguise. And the noise cost is bounded — subgroup size can be chosen so that within-subgroup majorities are reliable, while the independence between subgroups is what the top-level aggregation actually cashes in. Second, the manipulation worry: whoever assigns the evidentiary diets wields enormous power, since they control which signals exist. This I can only answer with institutional safeguards — rotation, transparency, adversarial design of the diets — and I treat it as a political problem rather than an epistemic one.

7. The essay's broader moral: when epistemic democrats cite the jury theorem on behalf of large inclusive bodies, they are citing a theorem about independence, not about numbers. The number of participants is the visible variable; the correlation structure is the operative one. Institutional design should be evaluated by its correlation budget first and its headcount second.
