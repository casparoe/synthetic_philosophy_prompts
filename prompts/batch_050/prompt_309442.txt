I write for a general-interest magazine on science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on collective intelligence -- slime molds, beehives, markets, neural ensembles -- and what, if anything, it would take to say that a group itself thinks. As preparation, I want a serious philosophical essay on the question Hofstadter staged in his Ant Fugue dialogue: can an ant colony have a mind of its own, over and above the minds (if any) of its individual ants?

Please write an essay in American English that takes up this question through the specific device of Aunt Hillary, the conscious colony that converses by steering the movements of its own ants. The essay should not merely summarize the conceit but should use its details as load-bearing pieces of the argument. In particular:

1. Hofstadter's colony contains an intermediate level between ant and colony: small teams of ants that form and dissolve in response to circumstances, carrying meaning the way neural symbols do rather than the way raw neural signals do. The essay should grapple with this middle level and ask whether it is essential to the case for a colony-mind, or decorative -- that is, whether a colony whose information processing ran directly through individual ants, with no stable intermediate structures, could still count as thinking, and what hangs on the answer.

2. Construct the strongest version of the case for Aunt Hillary's mindedness, including the claim that nothing about the *substrate* matters and that what makes a mind is a pattern at a certain level of description. Then construct the two objections I find hardest to dismiss: first, that the colony's apparent mentality is parasitic on the interpreter -- Aunt Hillary "talks" only because an outside observer can read her states as utterances, whereas my brain would go on thinking even if nobody ever decoded it; second, that there is nothing it is like to be a colony, because consciousness requires a causal unity or integration that a swarm of physically separate organisms, communicating by touch and pheromone, cannot achieve. The essay should also consider whether the first objection actually applies to *all* minds or only to colonies, and whether the second is a principled verdict or a guess dressed up as one.

3. Test the position against a hard case of your own construction along the following lines: suppose we begin replacing Aunt Hillary's ants, one at a time, with tiny robots that reproduce the outgoing behavior of the ant each replaces, and then -- the reverse direction -- suppose we begin with a single human brain and gradually delegate its subroutines to physically separated agents scattered across a city. Does the colony stay minded through the first replacement, does the person stay unified through the second, and what do the two answers together reveal about what the real criterion is?

The essay should reach a definite verdict on whether an ant colony, suitably organized, could literally have a mind -- yes, no, or a carefully specified "only under conditions X" -- and should defend that verdict against the strongest consideration on the other side. If the verdict turns out to be yes, the essay should say what follows for the humbler cases I actually cover (real ant colonies, which nobody claims can talk); if no, it should explain what Aunt Hillary is, if not a thinker. Aim for the register of a long-form essay rather than a journal article, but keep the arguments tight.
