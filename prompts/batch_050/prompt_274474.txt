**Exam question:** Explain the Daoist notion of *wu-wei* (often translated "non-action" or "effortless action") as it appears in the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi. Does the notion generate a paradox -- sometimes called the paradox of trying not to try? If so, state the paradox precisely and assess whether the Daoist tradition has the resources to resolve or dissolve it. Support your answer with at least one concrete example from the texts.

**Student's answer:**

Wu-wei is one of the central concepts of the Daodejing, where the Way itself is described as accomplishing everything precisely by not striving to accomplish anything. The sage ruler, on this model, governs best by refraining from deliberate interference, so that the people transform of themselves. The most famous illustration of the idea is the story of Cook Ding in the Daodejing, whose blade stays sharp for nineteen years because he does not cut through the ox but lets the knife glide through the natural spaces in the joints, following the structure that is already there.

The concept does generate a genuine paradox. If wu-wei is the goal, then aiming at it appears self-defeating: deliberately trying to act without deliberation is itself a deliberate act, and effort directed at effortlessness only produces more effort. A person who decides to be spontaneous is, by that very decision, not spontaneous. So it seems the Daoist either must not want wu-wei (in which case the doctrine gives no guidance) or wants it and thereby forfeits it.

I do not think the paradox can be resolved within Daoism, and I take the skill stories to concede this. Any program of training or cultivation would be a form of deliberate striving, and deliberate striving is exactly what wu-wei rules out. It follows that wu-wei cannot be taught, practiced, or approached gradually; it must be a matter of temperament or grace, something that simply happens to some people. The story of Cook Ding, with its implausible nineteen-year blade, is best read as hyperbole rather than as a picture of achievable mastery, since no amount of practice could count as non-action. The Confucian alternative, with its rituals and its cultivation of virtue, is at least honest about the effort involved, and wu-wei remains an ideal foreign to Confucian thought.

In short, wu-wei is a coherent description of how the Way operates in nature, but as an instruction for human beings it collapses into contradiction: the moment it becomes a goal, it becomes unattainable, and the Daoist texts do not offer a way around this.
