Exam question: In section II of "The Use of Knowledge in Society," Hayek writes: "The answer to this question is closely connected with that other question which arises here, that of who is to do the planning. It is about this question that all the dispute about 'economic planning' centers. This is not a dispute about whether planning is to be done or not. It is a dispute as to whether planning is to be done centrally, by one authority for the whole economic system, or is to be divided among many individuals." (a) Explain what Hayek means here and why he thinks framing the dispute this way matters. (b) Contrast the argument Hayek is making with the challenge Mises posed in his 1920 essay on economic calculation -- are these the same argument or different ones? (c) Given your answer to (b), what would Lange's trial-and-error proposal have to show in order to count as an answer to Hayek, as opposed to merely an answer to Mises? Rubric: 3 pts for (a), 3 for (b), 3 for (c), 1 for clarity/organization.

Student's answer: "Hayek's point in this passage is that planning is unavoidable -- every economy plans -- so the real question is who does the planning: one central authority following a unified plan, or millions of separate individuals whose plans get coordinated through competition. Once you see it that way, the debate about socialism isn't about planning versus no planning but about centralization versus decentralization. This reframes the calculation debate. Mises had argued in 1920 that socialist planners lack knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place, knowledge that is dispersed among countless individuals and never given to anyone in its totality, so they cannot allocate resources rationally. Hayek's passage makes essentially the same argument in a different vocabulary: because the relevant knowledge is dispersed, it can never be conveyed to a single planning authority, and this shows rational central planning is impossible in principle rather than merely difficult. For Lange, the implication is that it is not enough to show, as his trial-and-error method tries to, that a Central Planning Board could mimic the market by raising accounting prices where there are shortages and lowering them where there are surpluses. Since Hayek has shown the board's task to be logically impossible, Lange's proposal needs no seperate refutation -- it fails a priori, just as Mises said socialism must. Hayek's contribution is therefore to restate Mises' impossibility argument in terms of knowledge rather than in terms of money prices for the means of production."
