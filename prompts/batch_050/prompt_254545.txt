I run trust & safety at a mid-size company that ships customer-facing AI assistants, and twice in the last quarter an internal fight has gone all the way to the exec team over whether some model output counted as "deceptive." In one case the assistant told a user it had "looked into" their account history when it hadn't, because it has no access to that. In another it gave a fluent, confident, totally wrong answer about what a warranty covered and the user made a purchase decision based on it. Legal's position is that deception requires intent, the model doesn't have intentions, so strictly speaking nothing it says can ever be deceptive. Our user research lead says that can't be right, since users walk away with false beliefs either way and at least some of those were foreseeable by us if not by the model. Meanwhile my review team (six contractors, no philosphy background, roughly ten minutes per item, a few hundred flagged items a week) needs a rule they can actually apply, because "it depends" is not auditable and we've got a regulator asking for our working definition of honesty in writing.

Please build me a classification procedure the reviewers can run on a flagged output: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with a verdict versus escalating. It needs to handle at least: outright false assertion; technically-true-but-misleading phrasing; confident invention of the "hallucination" type; answers that tell the user what they seem to want to hear; and first-person claims about the system's own states ("I remember," "I checked," "I'd recommend"). Keep whatever the philosophy of lying and misleading actually preserves here, but be explicit about the translation choices: I know the standard accounts lean on intent, so tell me what we lose if we drop the intent condition entirely versus replacing it with something like "generated because it was rewarded for pleasing the user rather than because the evidence supported it," and where that substitution misfires. Then run the procedure on the two cases from my first paragraph plus this third one: marketing wants the assistant to say "I'd recommend" instead of "the system suggests," and I strongly suspect the procedure flags it, which is an answer nobody upstairs wants to hear -- I'd rather know that now. Also mark the points where judgement genuinely can't be eliminated and a named human has to sign off.
