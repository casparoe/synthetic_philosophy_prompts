Im a novelist, not a philosopher, and next week I somehow agreed to give our little fiction cohort a 20 minute primer on causation by omission, because everyone keeps writing scenes where somebody merely fails to act and the plot wants that to feel as causal as a pushed knife. Anchor it in John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, specifically the bit where he treats people as able to harm others through inaction as well as action and still be answerable for it. Do not turn this into an ethics lecture on blame only; the hinge is whether not-doing can be a cause at all, and when Mill's move is legit versus when it just lets us moralize any absence.

Plan the teaching, dont give the talk itself. I want the exact order of ideas so each one earns the next: start where their folk physics starts, then counterfactual dependence, then the background conditions problem, then the role of duties, promises, jobs, plans-in-progress, and norms as the things that make an absence more than empty air, and only at the end the difference between cause, contribution, enabling condition, and moral hook. Tell me what has to come before what and what collapses if I reorder it.

Give me one carrying example to build the whole thing around. I am leaning lighthouse keeper on a stormy night who never lights the lamp because he is sulking over a letter, versus a passing tourist who also does not light it and cannot. Say why this pair can carry the load, what details I must strip out so it does not become about maritime law, intoxication, telepathy, or God, and whether I should make the keeper definitely able to save the ship or only probably. If the pair is wrong, propose a better one.

Map the misconceptions I should expect from clever novelists: that every counterfactual difference-maker is a cause; that causation needs motion or touching; that omission means nothing happened so nothing was caused; that if there is a duty then causation is automatic; that if there is no duty there is never causation; that multiple sufficient causes rescue the defendant; that intentions sneak into metaphysics; that but-for and proximate cause are the same beast wearing two hats. For each, say where in the sequence it will pop up and how to head it off without shaming anyone.

Include two quick checks for understanding that would prove it landed: one where they classify a new case, one where they diagnose a deliberately bad sentence from a draft. End with what I should openly postpone at this level, like overdetermination, absence as entity, legal proximate cause, and whether omissions are negative facts, plus one sentence on why novelists should care even if metaphysics stays messy.
