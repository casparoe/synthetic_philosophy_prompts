So I've rashly agreed to give a talk at our business school's behavioural economics workshop next term — I'm in the philosophy department, I mostly do rational choice stuff, and a colleague dared me into it after I grumbled one too many times in the pub that economists are far too quick with the word 'fallacy'. My thesis: the sunk cost fallacy is not always a fallacy. Not because past expenditure magically alters future payoffs — I'm not an idiot — but because there are cases where finishing what you've started does genuine rational work. I'm defending three mechanisms: completion as self-binding (you know your future self will want to bail, so treating the investment as a reason helps you stay the course), what I'm calling 'completion evidence' (sticking things out generates reliable self-knowledge about your own follow-through, which you then use in later decisions), and the mundane point that abandonment itself has costs the standard analysis quietly zeroes out. I'll happily concede that most of the textbook cases — the miserable film you sit through because you paid for the ticket — really are irrational; I want to defend the narrow version.

The audience will be maybe twenty behavioural economists, a handful of decision theorists, and two psychologists who cut their teeth on the escalation-of-commitment literature and, I'm told, regard this as settled science. Prepare me for the Q&A: give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from *this* crowd — not from philosophers, who'd go for counterexamples — phrased the way economists phrase things ('you're just relabelling preferences', 'reference-dependence already explains this', 'what's the falsifiable prediction here?', 'this is one clever model away from justifying Concorde'), ordered roughly by how much damage each would do to the talk. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede without flinching, what I must not concede, and a note on where my reply is softest. Then tell me which objection I'm secretly hoping nobody raises, and sketch how much trouble I'm in if someone does.
