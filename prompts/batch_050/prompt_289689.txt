In The Querist — first published in three parts in 1735–1737, then revised as a single volume in 1750 — George Berkeley pressed a long chain of questions about what money is, writing at a time when paper's claim to be money at all was thought to stand or fall with its convertibility into silver or gold. Two of them (numbered as in the 1750 edition):

"Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?" (Query 26)

"Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?" (Query 21)

Modern monetary practice has run Berkeley's thought experiment. Central banks issue money redeemable for nothing but itself; states fix the unit of account and declare which instruments discharge taxes and debts; commercial banks create most of the money stock as ledger entries when they lend; and the denominations have been retained though the bullion is long gone.

First, what theory of money does this practice presuppose? Set out the candidates — the commodity theory (money must be, or be anchored in, intrinsically valuable stuff), the state theory (money is whatever the state names and accepts), and the credit theory gestured at in Berkeley's queries — and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. Could a convinced metallist operate the very same institutions unchanged, treating unbacked money as counterfeit not yet exposed, or does the practice's stability rule that reading out?

Second, do Berkeley's queries state the theory the practice presupposes, or something stronger than it needs? Modern money is not merely a counter for stirring industry: it is a store of value, an asset on balance sheets, an object of speculative demand. Identify where Berkeley's picture captures the practice and where the practice has outrun the picture.

Third, Berkeley the metaphysician held material substance to be an idle posit — a bearer that did no work. Berkeley the querist treats bullion the same way. Is fiat practice thereby committed to the monetary analogue of his idealism — the claim that the metallic substratum never did any work — or only to the weaker claim that whatever work it did is now done by law, taxation, and convention? Say what would have to change in the practice on each answer.
