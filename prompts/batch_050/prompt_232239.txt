I got into an argument with my brother-in-law last weekend that has been nagging at me since. A high court in our country recently struck down a piece of legislation that had passed the elected legislature by a wide margin, and I was saying that, whatever one thinks of the policy, it seems troubling that a small group of unelected judges can simply veto what elected representatives decided. He cut me off with a line I have heard a hundred times: judges are supposed to interpret the law, not make it. The conversation died right there, because the sentence sounds obviously true -- of course interpretation and legislation are different jobs, that is why they are different jobs. But the more I turn it over, the less I understand what the sentence actually forbids. Every hard case involves some uncertainty about what the law is, so if judges always "just interpret," either interpretation is a mechanical activity that never involves judgment (which nobody who has watched judges disagree seems to believe), or the slogan is smuggling in some view about how much judgment is too much. I am not a lawyer or a philosopher and have no training in either; I have just been chewing on this on my own.

Could you unpack that slogan for me? I would like a menu of the different precise claims it could be making, laid out one by one. I can imagine, for instance, that it might be claiming something nearly trivial (judges should not set out to replace the legislature), something about the nature of legal interpretation itself (that legal texts have determinate meanings waiting to be found), something about institutional roles in a constitution (that law-making power is assigned elsewhere), or something about democratic legitimacy (that unelected officials lack the authority to substitute their judgments for elected ones). There may be other readings I have not thought of. For each reading, I would like your verdict: is it a platitude almost no one disputes, a substantive position that is at least defensible, or something that is actually false? And most importantly, which of these readings would my brother-in-law actually need for the move he made -- that is, for concluding that this court did something illegitimate by striking down a duly passed statute? My suspicion is that the readings that sound obviously true are too weak to deliver that conclusion, and the readings strong enough to deliver it are anything but obvious, but I cannot quite prove this to myself.

Please be candid about your own epistemic position throughout. Where you are confident in a classification, say so; where you are not, say that too, and at the end single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of and explain why it wobbles.
