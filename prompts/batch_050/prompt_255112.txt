Next spring I am presenting a paper at a law school faculty workshop defending a view I call consensualism about culpability. The view adapts Abelard's position in his Ethics: wrongdoing is fully constituted by the will's consent to what the agent takes to be wrong, and neither the outward act nor its consequences add anything to culpability. On my account, the person who consents to poisoning a rival but unknowingly spoons sugar into the coffee is exactly as culpable as the poisoner who succeeds; the coerced or innocently ignorant person who in fact causes a death, without ever consenting to it, is not culpable at all. My argument runs in three steps: blame properly tracks what is up to the agent; outcomes are never fully up to the agent, while consent is; therefore the differential treatment of completed crimes and failed attempts in sentencing is a moral error, an institutionalized confusion of luck with desert.

The audience is roughly twenty legal scholars. Most are criminal law theorists trained in the standard actus reus / mens rea framework; a handful are retributivists, two work specifically on the law of attempts, and there is at least one consequentialist who thinks culpability talk is a dispensable fiction anyway. None of them are medievalists, so nothing may rest on Abelard scholarship; but they know the moral luck literature and the long-running fight between subjectivist and objectivist theories of criminal liability better than I do.
