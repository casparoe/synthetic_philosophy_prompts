So I'm taking this ethics seminar on promissory obligation and I volunteered to run the undergrad Q&A session with this philosopher who's visiting campus next week. She's got a new book out defending a pretty strong version of the normative powers view: basically promises bind because we have a genuine moral power to create obligations just by communicating an intention to be bound, and this works even if the promisee never relies on the promise at all and even if no expectation is ever formed. No practice needed, no reliance needed, just the power itself.

My problem is she's apparently really polished at dodging the classic pressure points. Her standard moves are: when someone brings up a case where the normative power seems to do no work (like a promise the promisee immediately forgot about) she says the obligation exists whether or not anyone notices it, and when someone brings up coercion or manipulation she says those cases just fail the voluntariness conditions for exercising the power, so they're not counterexamples.

Can you write me a sequence of like 7-8 questions that builds up pressure step by step? I want questions that turn on the actual content of her view, not generic stuff. A few angles I'd love covered, though feel free to restructure:

- Start somewhere friendly-ish so she'll engage, then get at how her view differs from Scanlon-style expectation accounts in a case where expectation and communication come apart, and why anyone should prefer hers.
- Press on the "obligation exists whether anyone notices" move: if a promisee can never even know they're owed something (say the promisor dies before the promisee finds out), what work is the obligation actually doing, and isn't that just reliance/expectation theory sneaking back in through the epistemology?
- Something about promises made to people who explicitly don't want the promise ("I promise I'll keep your secret" said to someone who would rather not know). Can you bind someone against their will on her view? Her book apparently says yes via "standing acceptance," whatever that means, so push on that.
- A question about whether an AI assistant could exercise a normative power on my behalf, like if my calendar app auto-accepts invitations, did anyone promise anything? If not, what extra ingredient do humans have; if yes, isn't the "power" getting spread pretty thin?
- And end with something like: what would it take for her to give the view up? Like what case or result would actually count against it, since every apparent counterexample so far gets absorbed as "failed exercise of the power."

Also if you could note for each question what her likely reply would be and give me a one-line follow-up, that would be amazing, because I always freeze up when the guest pushes back. Thanks!!
