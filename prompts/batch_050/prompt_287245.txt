I am about to spend several weeks reading the realist responses to the pessimistic meta-induction, and I would like the specification before I read the theories. What must a defense of scientific realism against the pessimistic meta-induction actually accomplish in order to count as adequate — adequate, specifically, for settling whether it is rational to regard our current best theories in the mature sciences as at least approximately true, rather than merely predictively reliable?

Please write the desiderata as a requirements list. For each requirement, give the consideration or historical case that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as something a defender might reasonably concede and route around. I expect the list to engage with the following requirements, but you should feel free to reorganize, subdivide, or add to them:

- The response must engage the actual historical record of discarded but empirically successful theories — the optical and electromagnetic ether theories, caloric, phlogiston, the crystalline spheres — rather than a sanitized record in which only failed theories were ever abandoned.
- It must fix a criterion of empirical success on which those discarded theories genuinely count as successful, including, in some cases, successful by the realist's own favored standard of novel prediction, as with the ether-based wave optics that yielded the Poisson spot — and it must do so without defining success retrospectively, as whatever current theory happens to retain.
- It must identify which parts of the discarded theories were doing the predictive work and show that those parts survived in successor theories, and it must draw the line between working components and idle components on grounds available to scientists at the time, not by consulting what we now believe.
- It must explain what is disanalogous about our current epistemic situation without simply asserting that we have now finally got things right, since every discarded generation could have said the same.
- If the response is itself an induction over the history of science, it must explain why that induction is permissible while the meta-induction it answers is not.
- It must stop short of concessions a thoroughgoing instrumentalist could happily accept; the defended position must remain a realism about unobservables, not a relabeling of anti-realism.
- It must state whether answering the meta-induction also answers the problem of unconceived alternatives, or whether those are separable challenges requiring separate treatment.

Once the list is complete: identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension; say which single requirement you take to eliminate the most contenders, and why; and indicate which requirements you consider negotiable and which not. Finally, and briefly — no more than a paragraph each — score structural realism, selective realism about a theory's working posits, and entity realism against the full list. I am considerably more interested in the criteria and their motivating cases than in the scorecard.
