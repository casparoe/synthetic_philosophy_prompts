So I'm trying to sort out my own views on "repugnant markets" — the stuff people find icky to buy and sell even when everyone involved genuinely consents. Please take on the role of a philosopher who works on the ethics of markets and commodification, and actually think carefully here rather than giving me a survey of the literature. Maybe brainstorm a few candidate principles first before you commit to one.

Here are my gut verdicts on some cases:

1. Kidney sale: a healthy 28-year-old, fully informed, no debts or coercion, wants to sell a kidney to a stranger for $50k in a well-regulated market. My verdict: repugnant, shouldn't be permitted.
2. Price gouging: after a hurricane, a hardware store triples the price of generators. My verdict: honestly fine, maybe even good — it discourages hoarding and gives suppliers a reason to truck more in.
3. Vote selling: a citizen sells their vote in a mayoral election for $200. Repugnant, rightly illegal.
4. Paid gestational surrogacy: $40k, reputable agency, everyone consenting, kid goes to a couple who can't conceive. A bit queasy-making but I think permissible.
5. Paying someone to stand in line for you (concert tickets, congressional hearings). Totally fine, who cares.
6. Hazard pay: offering extra wages for dangerous work like oil rigs or toxic cleanup. Obviously fine.
7. Selling blood plasma for cash. Fine.
8. Selling the naming rights to your newborn to a corporation. Probably should be legal, but repugnant — I'd judge the parents hard.

The pair I can't reconcile is 1 vs. 4 and 6: kidney sales creep me out but surrogacy and hazard pay don't, even though all three involve people putting their bodies on the line for money, and arguably the kidney is the most medically routine of the three. There's also tension between 2 and 3, since both are cases where a market allocates something important to whoever pays most, and yet I react completely differently.

So here's what I want from you:

(a) What principle (or small set of principles) actually captures these verdicts? Test the obvious candidates against my list — the idea that selling some things corrupts or degrades their meaning, the idea that desperation or inequality taints the consent, the idea that some goods just shouldn't be distributed by willingness to pay — and tell me which one(s) survive contact with all eight cases.

(b) If no principle saves every verdict, tell me which of my verdicts is the weakest link and should be overturned, and wich direction the revision should go. Be honest about the cost.

(c) Give me your best theory's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: paying kids for good grades, a bidding market among prospective adoptive parents, selling citizenship, and paying prisoners to participate in risky medical trials.

(d) Say whether my verdict on 2 can really survive alongside 1, or whether I'm just more sympathetic to economics-flavored arguments when the victim is less vivid.

I'd rather you defend one position cleanly than hedge across all of them.
