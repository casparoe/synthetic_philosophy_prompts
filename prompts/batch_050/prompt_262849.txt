In my philosophy of logic seminar we just covered the liar, Curry's paradox, and Yablo's paradox, and a classmate has produced an argument that seems to me to prove far too much; I want to identify exactly where it breaks down. Consider the infinite sequence Y₁, Y₂, Y₃, …, where each Yₙ says: for every k > n, Yₖ is not true. His argument:

(1) No Yₙ mentions itself or sits in any loop of reference; each speaks only of strictly later sentences. So the sequence involves no self-reference at all.

(2) Suppose some Yₙ is true. Then Yₙ₊₁ is untrue, so some later Yₘ is true; but Yₙ's truth makes every later sentence, Yₘ included, untrue. Contradiction. Suppose instead that every Yₙ is untrue; then Y₁ is untrue, which just means some later sentence is true after all — contradiction again. So no consistent assignment of truth values to the sequence exists.

(3) Steps (1)–(2) use only classical logic and the T-schema, the principle that a sentence is true iff things are as it says.

(4) Therefore the inconsistency has nothing to do with self-reference: it is generated merely by a truth predicate ranging over an infinite chain of sentences with no first, self-standing member to anchor the rest.

(5) But universal generalizations over infinite domains have exactly this structure: ∀xφ(x) owes its truth to infinitely many instances, no finite initial segment of which settles it, and the chain of dependence is bottomless.

(6) So, by the same reasoning, no universal generalization about an infinite domain has a consistent truth value, and arithmetic — which consists largely of such generalizations — is not a truth-apt theory at all.

(7) Since that conclusion is absurd, we must reject either the T-schema wholesale or classical logic wholesale; and since Curry's paradox shows that triviality follows even without negation or a falsity predicate (a sentence saying only "if I am true, then p" yields arbitrary p), the fault must lie in classical logic's conditional and structural rules rather than in the notion of truth.

I would most like to keep both classical logic and the T-schema for ordinary, grounded sentences. Which step should I press him on, why did that step look innocent, and what do the standard responses — Tarskian hierarchy, Kripkean groundedness, dialetheism, substructural restriction — each have to give up in order to resist the argument at that point?
