You're a historian of classical Greece who moonlights as a dialogue writer. Think this through before you start: jot down (silently, don't include it) the single strongest argument each side has and the single biggest weakness in each side's case, then write.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp ancient-history PhD students, Maya and Tomás, sharing a compartment on an overnight train. Maya thinks classical Athens is basically a cautionary tale against direct democracy: the Mytilene decree (vote to kill every adult male in a rebellious allied city), the Sicilian expedition, and the death of Socrates show the demos was a moody mob that demagogues could steer into catastrophe. Tomás thinks this is cherry-picking from nearly two centuries of mostly functional popular government: the assembly reversed the Mytilene decree the very next day and raced a trireme to stop it, the Sicily vote was an information problem more than a character flaw (nobody in the Pnyx had reliable numbers on Syracuse's resources, and the expedition's original co-commander got recalled mid-campaign on religious charges), and Socrates was condemned by a jury court, not the assembly. He also wants to talk about the comparison class: what did Sparta, or the Thirty Tyrants, do with their power?

Ground rules for the dialogue itself: no strawmen — Maya must be able to explain why a next-day reversal isn't obviously a point in the assembly's favor (a body that can be talked out of a massacre was talkable into it 24 hours earlier), and Tomás must grapple with the fact that Athens lost the war and that the amnesty of 403 was arguably imposed by exhaustion rather than wisdom. At some point they should impose a steelmanning rule on each other: each has to state the single strongest piece of evidence for the other's view before advancing their next argument, and the dialogue should show that rule actually changing what they say next, not just being a formality. They should argue about specifics (what the Mytilene episode proves about error-correction vs. error-generation, whether "the jury wasn't the assembly" is a real defense of popular rule or just buck-passing between democratic institutions) rather than trading slogans about "the mob." End without a clean winner — have them at least locate where their remaining disagreement actually lives (e.g., different implicit base rates, different counterfactuals about what a filtered system would have done in 415).

Roughly 900-1300 words. Real back-and-forth, no lecture-monologues.
