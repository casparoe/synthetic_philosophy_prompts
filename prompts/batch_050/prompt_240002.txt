I have no formal education in philosophy — I work in logistics and read on my own in the evenings — and I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the philosophy of logic. Please think carefully and approach this the way you would approach a question from a serious graduate student: begin by brainstorming the full space of relevant considerations before you settle on your answer, and do not soften your verdicts to spare my feelings.

Some background on where I am coming from. I recently encountered Lewis Carroll's old dialogue in which the Tortoise refuses to accept the conclusion of a valid inference even after accepting every premise Achilles offers him, because each time Achilles adds the missing principle as a new premise, the Tortoise simply asks why accepting those premises plus that principle obliges him to accept the conclusion. The regress, as I understand it, is meant to show that the rules of inference cannot themselves be treated as further premises without generating an infinite regress.

From this I have developed my own view, which I will call the Load-Bearing Floor view: deduction neither has nor needs a justification in the way ordinary beliefs do, because any attempt to justify it, to doubt it, or even to state an alternative already employs deductive inference. On my view, the Tortoise is not revealing a gap in logic's foundations; he is revealing that the demand for justification has a natural stopping point, and that demanding a non-circular justification of deduction is simply confused — like demanding to see the bottom of the floor from underneath the floor. I take this to be a stronger claim than merely saying deduction is circularly justified. I am saying the whole question of justification is out of place here.

Here is what I want from you. What would count against the Load-Bearing Floor view? Specifically:

1. Map the defeaters. Which arguments, cases, or results — if they succeeded — would show that the demand for a justification of deduction is not confused after all? I am aware in a vague way that there are logicians who revise or restrict classical inference (people who reject certain classical principles for reasons involving vagueness, contradiction, or the meaning of the logical vocabulary), and I suspect these are the most serious threat to my view, since those people seem to be reasoning their way toward rejecting parts of deduction, which my view implies should be impossible or confused. Please address this head-on rather than treating it as an afterthought.

2. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of my view would concede it as counting against the view or would dismiss it — and on what grounds.

3. Assess the circularity worry about my own position. If no argument could possibly dislodge deduction (because any such argument would presuppose it), does that make my view true, or does it make it unfalsifiable in a way that should worry me? Is there a difference between a belief being unjustifiable and a belief being beyond rational evaluation, and does my view conflate the two?

4. Finally, tell me honestly: if the Tortoise's challenge does not show what I think it shows, what is the best thing a layperson in my position should conclude from the dialogue instead? I would rather abandon the view than keep a comfortable one that cannot survive scrutiny.

Please be explicit about where you are confident and where the professional literature is genuinely unsettled.
