You are a philosopher of science who has spent years teaching modelling and methodology to mixed audiences of economists and non-economists, and who knows how the discussion actually goes wrong in the room rather than merely in print. Before drafting anything, think carefully and privately sketch at least two alternative structures for the session, note why each fails or succeeds, and only then settle on one; I am more interested in the quality of your sequencing decisions than in speed.

Here is the situation. I run a fortnightly evening seminar for mid-career policy analysts, most of whom did some undergraduate economics a decade or more ago and none of whom have any philosophy. I have a single ninety-minute session, a whiteboard, and no slides. My topic is idealisation in scientific models, organised around the position associated with Friedman's essay on the methodology of positive economics — roughly, that a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions rather than the truth of its assumptions — together with the standard rejoinder that this cannot be right wholesale, the rejoinder for which Friedman's view earned the label "F-twist". What I want from you is not the explanation itself but a plan for the explanation. My aim for the session is specific: these people already believe, glibly, that all models simplify, and they will happily nod along to that. I want them to leave able to say precisely what is defensible in the Friedman position and what is not — which kinds of unreality in an assumption are permissible, by what standard, and why predictive success cannot license just anything — rather than leaving either as naive Friedmanites or as people who think the whole thing was a economists' dodge.

The plan should cover the following, and where I have guessed at details below, correct me if your judgement differs. First, the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency structure made explicit. I suspect they cannot even state Friedman's thesis until they can distinguish a model from its target system, and that they cannot evaluate assumptions until they can separate at least three different things an assumption might be doing — ignoring a factor judged negligible, marking out the domain in which the model is meant to apply, and asserting something known to be false for the sake of tractability — because the permissibility question comes out differently for each. Tell me whether that is the right carving-up, what else has to be in place before the "as if" idiom will make sense rather than sound like a confidence trick, and where in the sequence the F-twist objection should land so that it arrives as a genuine problem rather than as the lecturer's verdict.

Second, the one concrete example that should carry the whole session. Specify what it must have — a genuinely false assumption, predictive success within a bounded domain, conspicuous failure outside it, no mathematics beyond arithmetic — and what it must lack; in particular it must not be something the audience can wave away as a pathology unique to economics, since half the room's instinct will be that this is a story about economists' bad habits rather than about science. Then explain why I should not simply build the session on Friedman's own illustrations — the expert billiards player who plays as if he were solving the equations, and the tree whose leaves arrange themselves as if deliberately maximising their exposure to sunlight — given that the first invites a digression about skill and the second is widely regarded as biologically contentious, and say where, if anywhere, those two examples should appear once the carrying example has done its work.

Third, the misconceptions this audience will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. My provisional list is: the caricature that Friedman says assumptions simply do not matter, which makes him too easy a target and guarantees a superficial rebuttal; the converse error that successful prediction retrospectively vindicates the assumptions; the conflation of idealisation with approximation, that is, treating neglecting air resistance and asserting that firms possess perfect information as the same kind of move; a slide into pure instrumentalism in which models are nothing but prediction engines and talk of mechanism or structure is dispensable; and the lazy use of "unrealistic" to mean "omits detail", a complaint no model could survive. Tell me whether I have the order right, which of these will actually bite hardest given the audience, and whether there is a sixth I have missed.

Fourth, one question or brief written exercise for the final ten minutes that would reveal whether they have grasped the distinction between permissible and impermissible unreality — not merely that models simplify, which they knew on arrival. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, saying so to the room: my candidates are the formal machinery of confirmation, the full-blown debate about explanation versus prediction, and anything about the semantics of theories, but tell me what belongs on that list and how to flag the exclusions without making the session feel truncated.
