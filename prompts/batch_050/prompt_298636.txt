I'm prepping for my qualifying exams and my advisor suggested I write up a short "statement of decision-theoretic faith" to check whether my views actually cohere, since I keep citing Rawls and Harsanyi against each other in seminar and it's getting embarassing. I've been going back and forth on this for awhile and here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a Rawlsian about justice: the difference principle is the right standard for the basic structure of society, and I think its best defense is his argument from the original position — rational parties behind the veil of ignorance would choose to maximize the position of the worst off. (2) In ordinary life, though, I'm an expected utility maximizer: whenever you have decent probabilities, EU is the right standard, and maximin as a general-purpose decision rule is a joke — literally applied it would tell you never to fly, never to invest, probably never to leave the house. (3) That said, sometimes probabilities just aren't there — deep uncertainty cases like some climate policy choices where the models disagree about the structure of the problem, not just the parameter values — and there I think minimax regret is the right rule: pick the option whose worst-case regret (the gap between what you'd end up getting and the best you could have gotten in that scenario) is smallest. (4) I'm also pretty committed to menu-independence: adding an option you wouldn't have chosen anyway can't make a previously rational choice irrational. If it was rational to order the steak over the fish, the arrival of a snails special you have zero interest in shouldn't suddenly make the fish the rational choice. (5) Finally, I think Harsanyi's move of assigning equal probabilities to being each person behind the veil is illegitimate — there's no basis for a uniform prior over who you'll turn out to be, which is exactly why his average-utilitarian conclusion doesn't follow and why Rawls's setup isn't utilitarianism in disguise.

My roommate (a Harsanyi guy) insists this is a grab-bag of incompatible rules I picked because they give answers I like. I'm sure he's at least partly right, but I can't tell where. Can you audit the set? Which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are but dissolve once you're careful about what each view actually says? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. Don't end on "it depends" — I want a verdict on which single commitment, if any, I'd be best off dropping or weakening. And flag any place where the package stays consistent only at the price of an extra assumption I haven't stated, so I can decide whether I'm willing to own it.
