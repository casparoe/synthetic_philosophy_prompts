I work for a regional planning authority that reviews land-conversion proposals — mostly farmland-to-development, drainage of small wetlands, and clearing of scrubland for solar arrays — and several times a year I have to write the ecological-impact section of our assessments. A colleague who also reads EA and rationalist-adjacent writing recently started arguing, in our internal reviews, that we should stop treating habitat preservation as an unambiguous good: most wild animals live short lives dominated by starvation, predation, and parasitism, so a hectare of highly productive habitat may generate more suffering than it contains welfare, and destroying it might be net-positive for animals. I am not convinced the argument is right, but I'm more convinced that our current methodology (count the species present, flag protected ones, done) has no way to even engage it. I want to build a defensible, reusable procedure I can run on each proposal, in maybe two to four hours per site, that translates the "net welfare of an ecosystem" question into something a planning officer can actually execute and a skeptical council member can audit.

The procedure should be a sequence of tests or estimation steps, in order, and it has to grapple with the actual conceptual difficulties rather than gesturing at them: how to weight preventing future births against causing near-term deaths of current occupants; how to handle compensatory dynamics, where removing some animals just means the survivors reproduce more and the population returns to carrying capacity; how to think about turnover, since a site with few standing animals but enormous reproductive churn (many offspring, nearly all dying young) may matter more than a site with many long-lived residents; what to do about succession, since cleared land often regrows into something with its own animal community within decades; how far down the size spectrum the analysis should extend, given that insects dominate the numbers and any verdict plausibly hinges on how much weight they get; and how to handle trophic knock-on effects where removing habitat in one place shifts populations elsewhere. Please mark explicitly which steps are genuine estimation and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, state what to do when I cannot get a number for a step (which will be most of the time), and be honest about the points where the procedure will misfire or where two reasonable officers running it would reach opposite conclusions.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases, showing the working at each step: (a) draining a half-hectare seasonal pond dense with amphibian larvae, almost none of which survive to adulthood; (b) converting twenty hectares of low-diversity intensive cropland into a solar farm whose margins get reseeded as wildflower meadow; and (c) clearing a small mature woodland with a modest population of long-lived birds and mammals. I'd like at least one of these to come out at a verdict that a conventional environmental assessment would find uncomfortable, and if none do, explain why the procedure is structurally incapable of reaching such verdicts, since that would itself be an important finding. Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each substantive claim the procedure depends on, and at the end, single out the single point in the whole analysis — an empirical premise, a weighting choice, a step in the procedure — that you are least sure of and explain what would change your mind about it.
