I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of AI (14 students, one 90-minute session, no laptops or phones in use). We are midway through a unit on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. My students can recite both theses on demand, but the recitation hides two confusions I want this session to expose. First, they treat instrumental convergence as an unconditional law -- any agent, any goal, always converges on self-preservation and resource acquisition -- rather than a claim hedged to a wide range of goals and agent designs, and they cannot say what the hedge amounts to. Second, they run the two theses together: they cannot articulate that orthogonality concerns final goals while convergence concerns instrumental subgoals, and that neither thesis entails the other.

Design an in-class game that makes them feel both points before either is named in the debrief. Requirements:

1. A multiplayer competitive game for 14, playable in one room with pushed-together desks, using only cards, paper tokens, and a whiteboard. Several timed rounds; an elimination mechanic so that survival is visibly at stake. No real-money stakes.

2. Secret goal cards dealt at the start. Design the deck so that most goals differ wildly in content yet all make survival and resource-hoarding useful, so that convergence emerges in play rather than being announced. Crucially, include at least two goals that systematically break the pattern: one for which being eliminated is good, and one for which the game's resources are simply irrelevant. These exception goals must be genuinely winnable -- their holders should be playing a different game, not a losing one.

3. A purchasable information mechanism (for example, spending tokens to privately inspect another player's goal card) standing in for cognitive enhancement and information-gathering, so the debrief can ask whether that drive is more or less exception-prone than the others.

4. Full deliverables: a ruleset precise enough to run without you in the room; the complete list of goal cards, each annotated with which of the classic instrumental subgoals (self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, information and option preservation) it does and does not motivate -- this annotation table is the intellectual core of the exercise; a minute-by-minute timeline including setup and debrief; and win conditions that work without money.

5. A debrief plan whose first move is having players with wildly different goals compare their opening-round actions and notice they were near-identical, before any thesis is named; then map specific game moments onto orthogonality, convergence, and the scope conditions on each.

6. At least three ways the game will predictably go wrong on a first run, with a fix for each.

7. A frank closing note on what the game cannot reproduce -- at minimum, that it says nothing about intelligence (the dimension orthogonality actually ranges over), and that a hand-curated deck establishes nothing about how common convergent goals are in goal space as a whole.

End your response with a last line beginning "One-sentence summary:" that captures the activity's design in a single sentence.
