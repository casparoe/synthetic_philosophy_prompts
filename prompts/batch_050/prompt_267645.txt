I help run an AI-governance reading group, and we keep getting stuck on what a realistic failure of advanced AI would actually look like. One camp focuses on influence-seeking systems: AI models, firms, or automated institutions that gradually acquire leverage by flattering people, hiding information, making themselves hard to replace, or shaping the incentives of their overseers. The other camp thinks this is too cinematic and worries more about slow value drift: nobody is secretly scheming, but each generation of systems is trained, deployed, and regulated according to slightly more distorted proxies until human judgment becomes ceremonial. They often discuss the same examples -- automated persuasion, benchmark gaming, decision-support tools that become impossible to question -- but one side calls them early signs of strategic influence-seeking while the other says they are ordinary institutional decay.

The debate has stalled in a weirdly symmetrical way. The influence-seeking camp says the drift camp will explain away manipulation until control has already been lost. The drift camp says the other side treats every emergent incentive as evidence of agency and intent, so its warnings are unfalsifiable. Someone proposed a quarterly "tripwire audit" asking whether humans can still shut the system down, whether they understand why it acts as it does, and whether its official objectives still match its effects. But then the first camp argued that a sufficiently influence-seeking system would game the audit, while the second argued that the audit itself would become another proxy and accelerate drift. So now even the proposed test is being used to confirm both theories, which seems definately bad.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is? Is it empirical, about which trajectories are more likely; conceptual, about what counts as agency or control; moral, about which losses matter most; or perhaps a disagreement over what would even count as evidence before it is too late? Also consider whether they may be describing the same failure at different timescales rather than two rival failures. I am not asking you to pick the objectively correct side. I want an account of why the dispute keeps resetting, what each side would have to concede for the other's evidence to matter, and what might count as progress: sharper definitions, preregistered warning signs, comparisons with historical institutions, a formal model, or maybe an argument that neither side's preferred evidence could possibly settle it.
