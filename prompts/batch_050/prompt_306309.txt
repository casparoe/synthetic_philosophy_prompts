I teach a first-year undergrad seminar, about 20 students, 75 minutes. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of spectrum arguments against the transitivity of "better than" -- the style of move where a week of a mild headache is clearly better than an hour of agony, but then you chain tiny pairwise comparisons and end up somewhere nobody wants to be. I don't want to present the argument to them. I want the students to generate the chain themselves through a sequence of small pairwise choices, ideally with real tiny stakes so the comparisons feel real (waiting time, holding something cold, whatever works), and only realise at the end that they've either endorsed something absurd or have to point to a step where it broke.

So: design the game -- rules, the sequence of choices, and crucially how to structure it so the chain comes from them and not from me steering them. Then a debrief section: where each step of the chain can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there (a sharp cutoff in what looks like a continuum? giving up on comparing some pairs at all?), what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle that, and one thing the activity structurally cannot reproduce that I'll have to supply in discussion myself. No lecture up front, and the vocabulary only comes out in the debrief.
