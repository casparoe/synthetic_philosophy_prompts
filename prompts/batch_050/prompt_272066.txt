So I'm a retired chemist (polymers, mostly) who's gotten into philosophy embarrassingly late in life, and I'm currently stuck on the dispositions literature. I keep tripping over two gadgets people call finks and masks, and before I embarrass myself at the reading group I want to get them properly straight rather than just nodding along.

Here's my current understanding, correct me where it's wrong: a fink is something that would remove or flip the disposition at the very moment it's triggered — the classic example being a live wire hooked up to a device that would cut the current the instant anyone touched it. A mask, on the other hand, leaves the disposition fully in place but blocks the manifestation from happening — the fragile glass wrapped in bubble wrap that survives the fall.

Two things I want explained. First, why exactly do finks cause trouble for the naive analysis "x is fragile iff it would break if struck"? I keep getting confused about which direction the counterexample cuts — does the finkish case break the sufficiency of the conditional, the necessity, or both, and do masks cut the same way or the opposite way? Second, as an ex-lab person it seems to me that basically every real disposition can be masked (packaging, shielding, inert atmospheres — that's just Tuesday in a lab) whereas finks are these baroque contraptions nobody would ever build. So is the conditional analysis really killed by finks while masks get handled by some "under ideal conditions" clause, or does that clause smuggle in problems of its own? And honestly: does any of this matter for how a working scientist should think about things like solubility or conductivity, or is it purely a metaphysical parlour game?

Concrete examples please, ideally ones a bench scientist would find intuitive rather than vases and sorcerers.
