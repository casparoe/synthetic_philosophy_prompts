I spent my career in experimental science and have taken up philosophy in retirement; I now lead a monthly reading group at our local library, most of whose members are educated laypeople with no philosophical training but plenty of patience for a hard argument. Our next session concerns a debate I have been turning over for some time: whether dreams are experiences had during sleep, with the Wittgensteinian denial associated with Norman Malcolm on one side and Daniel Dennett's rival picture on the other.

What I want from you is not an explanation of the debate but a plan for teaching it in a single ninety-minute session. Specifically:

First, the order of ideas. Which pieces of the ordinary concept of dreaming must be secured before either position can even be stated — for instance, the distinction between having a dream and telling a dream, and the assumption, invisible to most people, that the waking report is evidence of something that happened hours earlier. Tell me what depends on what, and which commitments my listeners must be made to notice in themselves before the disagreement will strike them as real rather than verbal.

Second, the carrying example. I am inclined to build the session around the familiar case in which a sleeper dreams a long, elaborate story that ends with the sound of church bells, and wakes to find the alarm clock ringing — the case that makes it look as though the dream must either have anticipated the noise or been assembled at the moment of waking. Tell me whether this is the right example to carry the whole session, what features an example must have to put pressure on both positions at once, and what tempting features it must lack so as not to smuggle in the conclusion.

Third, the misconception map, in the order my listeners will fall into the misconceptions. I anticipate, among other things: that Malcolm will be heard as claiming dreams are fictions or that we simply cannot know what happens in sleep; that Dennett will be heard as denying that anything is ever experienced; and that several members will assume the physiology of sleep — REM and so forth — settles the matter empirically and makes the philosophy otiose. Tell me where in the sequence each error is likely to form, and at what point the session's structure should head it off rather than leave it to be corrected afterward.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Propose a closing question or two whose answers would show that someone has grasped the disagreement — for example, whether they can say what observation, if any, could discriminate between the two views, and what each side is committed to saying about the bells case — rather than merely acquired a taste for one conclusion.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to flag as deliberately omitted: the verificationist machinery underneath Malcolm's argument, the subsequent empirical work on dream incorporation and lucid dreaming, and whatever else you judge would muddy the session rather than deepen it.
