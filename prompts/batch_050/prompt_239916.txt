I'm in a reading group on multi-agent AI safety and one member keeps deploying an argument that I'm convinced proves too much, but I can't find the loose brick in it. His version, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Tacit collusion requires no communication at all; it only requires that each agent can observe the others' behavior well enough to notice deviations from a jointly profitable arrangement and punish them. (2) Any agent that learns from repeated interaction with other agents must, pretty much by definition, be observing their behavior -- that observation is the learning signal. (3) So any population of capable learning agents in a repeated game already has everything needed to sustain tacit collusion. (4) In any domain where the agents' joint profits come partly at the expense of outsiders (buyers, users, principals), the jointly profitable arrangement is one that transfers welfare away from those outsiders. (5) Therefore, deploying multiple capable AI agents into markets, negotiation, or other repeated economic settings guarantees collusion at the expense of whoever sits on the other side of the transaction, wich makes multi-agent deployment unsafe full stop unless we strictly isolate the agents or heavily handicap them.

The reason I think this proves too much: swap "AI agents" for "human firms" and the argument seems to go through unchanged at every single step, wich would imply that every market with repeated interaction among firms should have collapsed into a cartel long ago, antitrust enforcement or not. Some industries do cartelize, but most don't, and the variation across industries seems to track factors his premises never even mention. So something in the chain has to be wrong. But when I push on step (3) he says I'm just denying that AI systems are capable learners, and when I push on (4) he asks whether I think pricing algorithms are charities. For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since those seem like the careful, defensible claims, and I'd be happiest if the flaw lives in (3) or in the move from (4) to (5).

So: which step do you actually reject, and why does that step look so plausible on first pass? And whatever you reject, I want the honest price tag. For instance, if the argument fails for humans only because of limitations humans happen to have -- forgetting, slow deliberation, misreading each other's intentions, difficulty coordinating on one arrangement among many -- does accepting that diagnosis cost us any right to be reassured about AI agents that lack exactly those limitations (perfect memory, fast iteration, possibly correlated training)? Or is there a premise whose rejection blocks the argument for both humans and machines at once?
