I'm in Year 11 and our school's philosophy club has spent this term on dispositions — fragility, solubility, elasticity — and on why the tempting analysis that a thing has a disposition just in case it would produce the manifestation if the stimulus occurred runs into trouble from finks and masks. I've volunteered to close our end-of-term evening with a short poem, and I want it to do real philosophical work rather than just gesture at breakable glasses. A friend in the club keeps insisting that a wine glass wrapped in cotton wool is "not really fragile anymore, since it won't break," and I'd love the poem to show, without a single prose sentence of argument, why he is confusing a mask with a fink.

Please write a poem from the point of view of the glass itself, in three stanzas of equal length. The first stanza should set out the naive picture: the glass, the blow, the shattering — the straightforward link between being struck and breaking. The second and third stanzas should each replay that same scene with the blow landing but no shattering following — except that in one stanza the glass remains fragile throughout because something external absorbs the impact (a mask), while in the other a watchful sorcerer whisks the fragility away at the very instant the blow arrives, so the glass survives only because it is no longer fragile (a fink). Make stanzas two and three as close to mirror images of each other as the material allows — nearly the same words, the same blow, the same intact glass at the end — so that the entire difference between a mask and a fink is forced into the few lines where the stanzas diverge. A reader who knows this debate should be able to say which stanza is which purely from whether the poem presents the fragility as still present but thwarted or as genuinely gone, and my skeptical friend should feel the sting of the mask stanza.

Please format your reply as follows: first the poem itself, with a title and its three stanzas of roughly four to six lines each; then a single section called "Which stanza is which, and why", about 100–150 words long, in which you identify the mask stanza and the fink stanza and explain your poetic choices — above all what you changed between the two stanzas and why that change captures exactly the difference between a blocked manifestation and a removed disposition.
