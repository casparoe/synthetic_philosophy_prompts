I'm a TA for an upper-division phil of mind course and I'm grading short essays tonight. Below is the question I set and one student's answer. Grade it out of 100 and give me the comments I'd hand back with it.

The question: (a) Explain Searle's distinction between original (intrinsic) and derived intentionality, and the role the Chinese Room plays in supporting it. (b) Reconstruct Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment -- the setup, the question it raises, and the conclusion Dennett draws about that distinction. (c) Does the two-bitser undercut Searle's distinction? Defend your verdict. (~400 words)

The student's answer:

Searle distinguishes original or intrinsic intentionality from derived intentionality. My belief that it's raining is about rain just by being the state it is; it doesn't need anyone to interpret it. The word "rain," a map, a painting, or the state of a thermostat are about things only because we take them to be -- their intentionality is derived from ours. The Chinese Room is meant to show why computers fall on the derived side. A man who knows no Chinese sits in a room manipulating Chinese symbols according to a rule book, and from the outside he passes for a Chinese speaker, but he understands nothing. Since he carries out the whole program without understanding, Searle concludes that syntax by itself is never sufficient for semantics, which shows that no computer, whatever it's made of and however convincingly it behaves, could genuinely understand anything.

Dennett's two-bitser is supposed to put pressure on the distinction. Imagine a vending-machine coin detector built to accept U.S. quarters: when a coin of the right size and weight drops in, it enters a state that intuitively says quarter. Now suppose the machine is shipped to Panama and put into service there, where people feed it quarter-balboas, which the machine cannot tell apart from quarters. What is its state about now -- quarters or quarter-balboas? Dennett's answer is that this is settled by the machine's current environment: since it now reliably responds to quarter-balboas, that's what it detects, and its meaning shifted without anyone redesigning or reinterpreting it. That already shows that meaning can come and go without any act of interpretation, which is hard to square with Searle's claim that derived intentionality always depends on a mind conferring it.

Dennett then turns the point on us. Our own intentionality is the product of a design process too -- natural selection rather than an engineer -- so by Searle's standards it is just as derived as the two-bitser's. The conclusion is not that nothing means anything, but that derived intentionality is the only intentionality there is, and it is entirely real. Searle's supposedly privileged category of original intentionality is empty.

Does the two-bitser undercut Searle? I think it does. It shows that original intentionality is impossible in principle, because every content-fixing fact about any system traces back to a prior process of design or selection, so there is no room left for content that is original. Searle could reply that natural selection is not really a designer and so confers nothing, but then he owes us an account of why brains manage to mean things while artifacts don't, and appealing to the special causal powers of neurons just restates the difference instead of explaining it.
