I teach a free will unit in my intro philosophy course (community college, about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and every year the same thing happens: I present Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility on the slides, the students nod along to each premise -- you do what you do because of the way you are, so to be responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, and for that you'd have had to have chosen it, which just kicks the problem back -- and then half the class shrugs and says "well it's still my choice, isn't it." The regress stays abstract for them, like a logic puzzle, not something that bites. Last semester a student said the argument felt rigged, and honestly I think that's partly because they've never *done* anything that would let the regress grab them.

So here's what I want from you: design an in-class activity or game that makes them *feel* the regress before I ever name it. Act as someone who's designed a lot of seminar activities and knows where they fall apart. Brainstorm a couple of possible mechanics first and tell me why you're picking the one you pick. My rough idea (feel free to improve or scrap it) is something like a "why-ladder": students pick a real recent choice of theirs, and the game forces them to keep answering why-questions, with some token or payoff system so that they *run out* of self-authored answers and are pushed to attribute their motives to stuff they plainly didn't choose -- temperament, upbringing, whatever mood they woke up in that morning. I want the moment where they hit the bottom of the ladder to be something the rules produce, not something I announce.

Give me: concrete rules and materials (no prep beyond index cards and maybe tokens, and no real money stakes), the payoff or constraint structure that generates the regress, a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the steps of Strawson's argument -- but tell me how to run the debrief so it lands on the students who stayed stubborn. Also, be honest with me: what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this (I'm guessing the smart ones will try to game it, and the compatibilists will cry foul about the rules being stacked?), and which part of the Basic Argument the activity structurally *can't* reproduce, so I know what I still have to supply with plain old lecturing.
