I have settled views about when a biological trait has a function -- or at least, I have verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know whether any coherent theory of function stands behind them. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(1) A finch population colonizes an island with hard-shelled seeds; a beak-shape variant that cracks the shells more efficiently spreads to fixation under selection. The variant's function is to crack hard seeds. Obvious.

(2) The very first finch born with that variant, before it has done its bearer any good in reproductive terms and before selection has touched it: its beak shape has no function yet, even though it will later acquire one. It's just a lucky shape, the way a randomly generated key that happens to open a lock isn't *for* the lock.

(3) A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me is assembled by freak accident -- a lightning strike in a swamp, say. His heart has the function of pumping blood, full stop. If you opened him up and found his heart, you could say what it's for, and if it stopped pumping you'd say it was broken.

(4) My appendix once had a function in my ancestors (housing gut flora, or whatever the historical story is) but does nothing for me now. My verdict: my appendix currently has no function, even though it did in the lineage. The function lapsed with the usefulness.

(5) A neutral mutation drifts to fixation in a small population; ten thousand years later the environment shifts and the trait turns out to be essential for survival, though it was never selected for. The trait has no function. Sheer luck, however beneficial, doesn't confer function.

(6) My congenitally malformed heart pumps weakly and erratically. It nonetheless has the function of pumping blood -- that's precisely why we can call it defective. A heart's function survives its total failure to perform.

(7) Rain clouds droplet-sort in a way that reliably waters crops below. The clouds have no function. Contributing systematically to an outcome isn't enough.

(8) A prosthetic valve has the function of regulating blood flow because its designers intended it so. I take this to be a genuine function, not a metaphor -- and I suspect it's genuinely the same *kind* of fact as the biological cases, not a merely analogous one.

My problem is that (2), (3), and (5) seem to be at war with each other. In (2) and (5) I denied function to traits lacking the right history; in (3) I granted function to a heart with no history at all. And (6) and (8) pull in further directions: (6) suggests function is independent of what a thing actually does, while (8) suggests function can be grounded in intentions rather than in selection or use.

So here is what I want. First, state precisely the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible -- with each clause explicitly tied to the case that motivates it. I'm familiar with the broad families on offer (selected-effects or etiological accounts, causal-role accounts, and accounts that ground function in contribution to some goal or capacity of a containing system), so don't just summarize those views at me; build or hybridize whatever actually fits the verdicts, and say which family, if any, your construction belongs to. Second, be honest about which of my verdicts cannot be saved jointly, and tell me which one you would overturn and why -- I expect at least one of (2), (3), (5) has to go, but tell me which. Third, apply your resulting account to cases I haven't listed: a gene drive engineered in a lab and released into a wild population; a protein in a virus that facilitates infection; the human appendix considered across the species rather than in one individual; and a trait that is currently being selected for but will be extinguished next generation before selection can complete its work. Fourth, say whether your account treats "function" in artifacts and in organisms as one concept or two, and what follows for (8) either way.
