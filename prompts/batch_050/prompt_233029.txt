You are a professional moral philosopher with particular expertise in the ethics of our treatment of animals. Think carefully and methodically: begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before settling on one, and stress-test each against my cases rather than retrofitting a principle to them.

I just spent an evening arguing about speciesism with a friend, and the argument ended in a stalemate because neither of us could say what principle actually governs my own verdicts. Here they are. Buying and eating factory-farmed chicken: clearly wrong. Eating a deer my neighbour hunted and would have starved of natural causes otherwise: permissible. Deliberately swatting a mosquito biting me: permissible. Killing a mouse in my kitchen with a snap trap: permissible, but I feel it requires some justification in a way the mosquito does not. Testing a promising cancer drug on mice: permissible, reluctantly. Testing mascara on rabbits: clearly wrong. Eating oysters: permissible. Eating shrimp: wrong, or at least uncomfortable — and this is the pair I keep stumbling over, since I cannot articulate why the oyster and the shrimp differ if the mouse and the mosquito do. Finally, catch-and-release fly fishing for sport: wrong, even though the fish usually survives, and my friend found this verdict bizarre given that I accept the snap trap.

Please state, as precisely as you can, the principle (or minimal set of principles) that best captures these verdicts — for instance, one based on sentience, on the purpose for which harm is imposed, on necessity versus pleasure, or some combination — and indicate which clause of it is motivated by which of my cases. Then tell me honestly: does any coherent principle recover all nine verdicts, or must at least one be revised, and if so which one is the weakest? Finally, apply your favoured principle to two cases I did not mention: using elephants for tourist rides, and feeding a rescue cat a vegan diet.
