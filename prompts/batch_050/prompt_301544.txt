A few months ago, in the course of an otherwise ordinary conversation about memory, I discovered that my wife's experience of "picturing" something is radically different from mine: she describes a detailed, almost photograph-like scene that she can rotate and inspect, whereas what I call picturing is more like knowing facts about a scene without anything being visibly present. This sent me into the literature on the mental imagery debate, and I have been struck by something that puzzles me more than the phenomenon itself. The dispute between the pictorialist camp associated with Stephen Kosslyn and the propositionalist camp associated with Zenon Pylyshyn has been running for roughly half a century. Enormously sophisticated experiments have been run on both sides -- mental rotation, image scanning, interference tasks, and more recently neuroimaging work purporting to show that visual cortex activation during imagery tasks mirrors perception in a map-like way. And yet my impression is that nobody has been converted. Pylyshyn's side seems to concede each new result while denying it shows what the other side claims, and Kosslyn's side seems to treat each concession as evasive.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical disagreement at all. Both sides accept the same behavioral data and, by now, largely the same neural data; what they disagree about is what would even count as a depiction inside a head -- whether "picture-like" is a claim about the physical format of a representation, about its functional role in processing, or merely about the phenomenology it produces, and whether those are even separable questions. On this reading, the experiments are doing no work because each side interprets every result through its own prior definition of what an image is.

Is that diagnosis right? I would like you to lay out what each side is actually claiming -- taking care to distinguish the claim that imagery is depictive in format from the weaker claim that imagery tasks draw on spatial processing or on visual cortex -- and then assess what kind of disagreement this is. Is there a hidden empirical question that genuinely divides them, such that some conceivable experiment could settle it? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "image," with both sides describing the same mechanisms in different vocabularies? Or are they answering different questions under one heading -- one side explaining the conscious character of imagining, the other explaining performance on imagery tasks? If the debate is stalled because the key term is ill-defined, say what distinction would need to be drawn to make the competing claims testable, and whether anyone's actual position survives that clarification. And if you think the dispute really is substantive and empirical after all, tell me what the decisive evidence would look like and why fifty years of clever experiments have failed to produce it. I would prefer a diagnosis with reasons over a survey of the literature, and an honest verdict on whether anything could end this debate or whether it is the kind of question that simply gets abandoned rather than answered.
