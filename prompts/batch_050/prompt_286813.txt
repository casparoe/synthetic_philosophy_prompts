I'm drafting a dissertation prospectus in early modern philosophy and need a two-page proposal for one chapter. The chapter is on Reid's brave officer objection to Locke's memory criterion of personal identity. The replies I already know fall into three families: make the criterion transitive by counting indirect memory chains (the officer remembers the boyhood, the general remembers the officer's deeds), give up the transitivity of identity, or replace memory with a broader notion of psychological continuity. My half-formed thought: all three treat Reid's case as an objection to be answered, but it might be an objection to be dissolved. Locke introduces the term 'person' for forensic purposes — accountability, reward, punishment — and accountability, unlike numerical identity, need not be transitive. So perhaps the brave officer shows only that memory is a bad criterion of numerical identity, which Locke arguably never needed it to be, while remaining a serviceable criterion of the accountability-relation he actually argued for; on that reading the case marks a point where the two notions come apart rather than a counterexample. Turn this into a proper proposal: state the chapter's question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the three standard reply-families leave it open (including why the continuity move arguably inherits its own version of the transitivity problem); say what I would actually do — which parts of Locke's Essay chapter on identity and diversity and of Reid's discussion of memory in the Essays on the Intellectual Powers need close reading, what reconstructing the objection as two distinct objections would involve, and how I would test the dissolution against Lockean commitments that seem to require numerical identity after all, such as judgment and resurrection; and state what the chapter concludes in each case and what would count as the project failing.
