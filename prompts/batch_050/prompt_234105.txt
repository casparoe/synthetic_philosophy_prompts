So a bunch of US jurisdictions now use actuarial risk scores in bail and sentencing decisions -- the tool takes things like age at first arrest, employment history, prior record, and spits out a number like "this defendant has a 45% chance of reoffending within two years." Judges then use that number to deny bail or lengthen sentences, and the whole thing is sold as more objective than a judge's gut.

Here's what bugs me: that 45% is just the reoffense rate of some group the defendant got sorted into. Sort him into a different group -- same age and record, but also employed, or also from this county, or also with a GED -- and you get a different number, and there's no tool-independent fact about which grouping is the right one. So what does the practice actually presuppose about individual risk? That a single person has a definite propensity to reoffend that the stats merely estimate? That "his risk" just means the frequency in the correct reference class, with the correctness coming from... what, predictive accuracy? Or is the practice really just consequentialist sorting dressed up in the language of individual assessment?

Reconstruct the best version of the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs it or could run on a more deflationary view (it's just a policy of acting on group frequencies, full stop), and tell me what would have to change -- in how the tools are built, or in what judges are told the numbers mean -- under each reading.
