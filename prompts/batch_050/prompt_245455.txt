Design a classroom activity for a first-year university econ/history course (about 20 students, 75 minutes) that makes them feel why the "why are some nations rich" debate is so hard to settle. The idea I want them to bump into: even when we have loads of data about countries, institutions, geography, and culture all get tangled up together, so you can't cleanly say which one caused the wealth -- and even smart people with the same numbers will disagree. I'd like it to be an actual game or simulation, not just a discussion: e.g. groups run little fictional "countries" over a few rounds under rules that secretly bake in different advantages (starting resources, a rule-change halfway through that mimics a conquest or imposed institution, random shocks), and afterwards each group has to argue from the game's history which factor made countries rich. The catch should be that the facilitator knows what actually drove the outcomes and it won't match what the students infer -- ideally the game is rigged so the true cause is something hard to see (like the one-time intervention) while the visible stuff (geography, "national character" that emerged during play) is a red herring. Please include the setup and rules, a rough timeline, how the debrief should connect the game to the real-world literature on institutions vs. geography vs. culture (Acemoglu/Robinson, Jared Diamond, etc. can be named in the debrief, not the game), what will predictably go wrong when running it, and an honest note on what the activity can't capture -- like the fact that in the real world there's no omniscient facilitator who knows the true data-generating process.
