I am assembling materials for an upper-level undergraduate unit on the paradox of blackmail, and I want a satirical piece to distribute alongside the readings. Please write, as a course handout, the written testimony of a fictional trade association — call it the Society for the Ethical Withholding of Information — delivered before a legislative committee considering whether to decriminalize blackmail. Roughly 850 words, in the blandly confident register of real industry-group testimony: an opening flattery of the committee, numbered points, and a closing offer to assist with drafting.

The target of the satire is the libertarian defense of blackmail — the position that since it is legal to keep silent and legal to request money, offering silence in exchange for money cannot be a crime. The testimony should take that position completely seriously and follow it wherever it goes, functioning as a reductio: the Society is not embarrassed by the argument but professionally organized around it. The humor should come from watching the logic run in corporate form, not from invented absurdities foreign to the view. For instance, the Society might describe its member code of conduct (threats delivered courteously and in writing, a formal appeals process for those who feel their secret was mispriced), its tiered subscription model for ongoing silence, its nondiscrimination pledge regarding whose secrets it will acquire, its consumer-protection commitment to honoring a secret even when a rival withholder would pay more for it, and its proposed licensing board to weed out the amateurs who give the profession a bad name.

The piece must also do real philosophical work, because I will be asking students to diagnose it. The Society should anticipate and dismiss, in deadpan, the standard attempts to solve the paradox — that the threat is coercive in a way the component acts are not, that the wrongness lies in how the information was gathered, that criminalization is justified by its deterrent effects on snooping — and each dismissal should be just plausible enough on the position's own terms that students have to work to say where the testimony goes wrong. The irony must stay straight-faced throughout: no winking, no moment where the Society admits what it is. A reader who knows the blackmail literature should recognize the position being faithfully extended rather than caricatured; a reader who doesn't should still be able to tell, from the mounting bureaucratic serenity, exactly what is being satirized. Do not name any actual philosopher in the testimony itself.
