I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last year I reported a long feature on a conservation consortium's proposal to release a CRISPR gene drive to suppress invasive mice on a Pacific archipelago. What stayed with me from that reporting was an argument I kept hearing from the drive's engineers: that their design is equipped with a molecular "self-limiting" mechanism — the drive allele is built to degrade and wash out of the population after roughly twenty generations — and that, because of this, releasing the drive is morally on a par with erecting a fence or laying poison bait, interventions we can always discontinue. I've since been invited to set the essay question for the final exam of an upper-level undergraduate course in environmental ethics and the philosophy of biology at a university near me, because the instructor wants an outsider's scenario-driven question rather than a recitation prompt. I'd like you to draft that question, built around the engineers' parity claim: the claim that once an intervention is engineered to be temporally self-limiting, its release is morally no different from any other reversible conservation measure.

The question should force students to actually interrogate what "reversible" must mean for the parity claim to go through, rather than reward them for summarizing the standard pro- and anti-gene-drive talking points. I want a question that pushes them to distinguish things that the public debate runs together: the ability to stop an intervention from continuing versus the ability to restore the world to its pre-intervention state; practical versus in-principle reversibility; and the possibility that a single release is epistemically irreversible even where it is biologically self-limiting — that is, that you cannot unlearn what the release teaches you about escape mutation, selection pressure, or unintended spread, and you cannot undo having crossed a threshold that other actors will cite as precedent. It should also ask students to consider whether the de-extinction case differs: if a revived species is introduced into a niche that has since been occupied by others, is the intervention's self-limiting design even coherent, given that the effects on the incumbent community don't degrade on a twenty-generation timer? The students will have 90 minutes. The question should be answerable from course concepts alone — no specialized technical knowledge — but unanswerable by anyone who has merely memorized the vocabulary.

Alongside the question, please provide: (1) a model answer of roughly 700 words, written at the level of a strong student rather than a professor, and (2) a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each point carries, a one-line description of what separates full marks from partial marks on each, and a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker answers go wrong on each — for instance, treating self-limitation as settling the reversibility question without argument, or conflating the irreversibility of an intervention with its riskiness, as though the objection to gene drives were just that they are dangerous.
