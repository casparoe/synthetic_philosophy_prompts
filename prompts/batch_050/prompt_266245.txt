I sit on the operations committee of a manufacturing-and-retail group with roughly forty thousand employees, and we will soon vote on a proposal from our new chief operating officer that has split the room. At present our divisions negotiate transfer prices with one another and are evaluated as profit centers; the COO wants to abolish all of that, convert the divisions into cost centers that execute instructions, and let a central team allocate inventory, freight, machine time, and capital by algorithm, using the real-time sales and logistics data we already collect. Everything we buy and everything we sell would continue to trade on ordinary external markets; only our internal economy would cease to have prices. I keep hearing this argument in a second register, because my uncle — who ran logistics for a two-thousand-store retailer for thirty years and has spent his retirement reading Mises and Hayek and relitigating Lange's side of the socialist calculation debate at family dinners — has a standing habit of delivering instant verdicts on whether some scheme of deliberate allocation can work, and I would like to anticipate what he will say about ours before I have to advise the committee.

His track record, as I have compiled it: the Soviet planning apparatus was doomed from the outset and only ever limped along by cribbing prices from world markets; his own employer's distribution network, centrally scheduled down to the truckload with no internal prices anywhere, was obviously sound — that is just a business, he says; a startup pitch he heard for replacing a national price system with machine learning over point-of-sale data was Lange with better hardware, still doomed, because it mistakes the problem for a computational one; a vertically integrated competitor that owns everything from mines to storefronts, so that its intermediate goods never touch a market, is perfectly fine; a conglomerate larger than some national economies, spanning unrelated industries with each division run as a profit center at market prices, is also fine, which tells me sheer scale is not his criterion; and military logistics — feeding and moving a million soldiers with no prices anywhere in the chain — works, he says, because the objective has already been fixed by someone else, making it an engineering problem rather than an economic one.

So: what is his verdict on the COO's proposal, and what reasoning is most likely driving it? I want three things. First, a best guess at his answer, since I cannot consult him before the vote — including whether his own employer's network or the startup pitch is the operative precedent here, given that his record seems to pull in both directions. Second, the rule that best unifies his six verdicts, together with at least two rival rules that fit the same record equally well, and an account of what each of the three predicts about our company, since I gather they diverge on exactly this kind of case. Third, the single follow-up case I could put to him at dinner that would most efficiently reveal which rule he is actually running. And since I must eventually advise the committee myself: if his record genuinely underdetermines the proposal, tell me what our company would be betting on, concretely, under each candidate rule.
