I'm a retired chemist and have only been reading moral/political philosophy for about a year, so bear with me. I keep running into the same move in discussions of patriotism, and I heard it again at my reading group last week: patriotism is like love of your family -- you didn't choose your mother, you love her anyway, and preferring her over strangers isn't some sinister bias, it's just what love is. The guy making the argument used it to say patriotism is straightforwardly a virtue, and that cosmopolitans who treat it as an arbitrary accident of birth are demanding an inhuman kind of impartiality. It has real intuitive pull, I'll grant. But my scientist's instinct says an analogy this load-bearing deserves an audit before anyone builds a conclusion on it.

So: how far does the country-as-family analogy actually hold? Give me the response as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. I want (1) the explicit mapping -- what plays the role of the mother, the siblings, the shared household, the obligations to kin; (2) the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the virtue conclusion, e.g. that a state is an institution with interests and coercive demands rather than a person who can love you back, and the sheer difference in scale; (3) the disanalogies that look damaging but actually aren't, or vice versa; (4) what the analogy quietly smuggles in, like the assumption that unchosen attachments automatically justify preferential treatment, and whether the abusive-parent case (where we'd say cutting ties is fine) implies the same for a rotten country; and (5) a verdict on whether the patriotism-as-virtue conclusion survives once the mapping is on the table, plus a better analogy if you have one.
