I've been thinking about a question ever since I fell down a rabbit hole about the philosophy of photography, and I'd like you to work through it with me. Here's the background as I understand it: some philosophers of art argue that photographs are "transparent" in a special sense -- that when you look at a photograph of something, you are literally seeing that thing, just through a mechanical aid, the way you see through a telescope or a mirror. The rough motivation is that a photograph is counterfactually dependent on the scene in front of the lens in a way a painting or a written description never is: the painter could have painted the scene wrongly on purpose, but the camera just records whatever was there. Now consider the Rodney King case from 1991. A bystander, George Holliday, filmed Los Angeles police officers beating King with batons from his balcony; the footage played on television constantly; virtually everyone who saw it reacted with outrage, as if they had witnessed the beating itself. Yet at the 1992 state trial, the defense's whole strategy was built on that same video: they slowed it down, froze it frame by frame, replayed isolated seconds, and walked the jury through an expert interpretation in which King's movements looked like aggression and the baton strikes looked like controlled technique. The jury acquitted on almost all counts. The city erupted in riots, driven partly by the sense that everyone had seen the beating with their own eyes and the jury had somehow refused to believe it.

Here's what I want you to do, and please approach it as a philosopher of perception who also knows this case well: think carefully, and before giving your verdict, spend some time brainstorming the exact mapping between the transparency thesis and the events. If photographs are transparent, did the millions of television viewers literally see King being beaten? Did the jurors see the very same event and simply draw different conclusions from it -- or did the defense's techniques change what was available to be seen? I'm especially interested in the hard middle cases the thesis seems unprepared for: a video rather than a still photograph; slow motion and freeze frames, which preserve the mechanical, counterfactual dependence on the scene but destroy its temporal structure; and expert narration that tells you what to see while you are looking. Does freezing a frame keep you "seeing through" the camera, or does it turn the footage into something more like a drawing -- a constructed representation whose meaning an interpreter has to supply? Be disciplined about this: tell me precisely which features of the episode the transparency thesis explains beautifully (for instance, why the public's outrage felt so direct and non-inferential), which features it misdescribes or simply has nothing to say about (why two groups could watch the same footage and reach opposite verdicts), and resist the temptation to make the theory speak where it's actually silent.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the case do to the thesis? Is the acquittal evidence against transparency -- proof that what the camera gives us can be reframed so thoroughly that "just seeing" is a myth? Or does the episode, especially the public reaction and the later federal civil-rights convictions of two of the officers, actually vindicate the thesis by showing that the jurors' eventual interpretation had to fight against what they had plainly seen? And one more wrinkle I keep circling: if the defense could use the video's mechanical reliability to erode trust in what it showed, does that suggest transparency was never really the source of the photograph's evidentiary power in the first place -- or that the thesis describes something real but fragile, which courtroom technique can exploit precisely because it is real? Give me your honest assessment of which reading the episode supports, and flag explicitly any point where you'd be guessing rather than reasoning.
