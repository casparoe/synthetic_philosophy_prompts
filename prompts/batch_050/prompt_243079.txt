I spent my career in experimental physics and have only lately taken up metaphysics, so I may be missing something elementary; but a member of my reading group set me an argument I cannot defuse, and I would like a proper diagnosis. (1) Two things are numerically identical only if they share all of their properties. (2) The statue on my mantel and the lump of clay composing it differ in their modal properties: the lump would survive being rolled into a ball, while the statue would not. (3) Therefore the statue and the lump are numerically distinct things, occupying the same place. (4) Yet the very same reasoning applies to the statue and to the statue-minus-one-grain-of-clay, which differ in their persistence conditions just as surely. (5) Iterating, the mantel holds not one statue but an enormous multitude of them, differing grain by grain, each with an apparently equal claim to being the object I purchased at auction. (6) That conclusion is absurd, so some earlier step must fail; but each looked unimpeachable in isolation. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I should most like to retain (1), which strikes me as the one non-negotiable principle in the chain — though I would settle for learning precisely why that preference is mistaken.
