Six months ago I was appointed to the five-person steering committee of a philanthropic foundation with a substantial endowment, and we are now weighing a structural proposal: replacing discretionary grantmaking by program officers with a futarchy-style arrangement, in which the board fixes a small set of measurable objectives (say, health-adjusted life years per dollar across the portfolio) and grant decisions are made by prediction markets estimating each candidate grant's contribution to those objectives. I have absorbed the standard case for this arrangement — the idea that we should vote on values but bet on beliefs — and I have come to accept it, at least for organizations like ours. Before I cast my vote, I want the strongest possible map of what would count against the position. Not the generic observation that markets can be wrong, but: which specific things would have to be true for governance by prediction markets to underperform the discretionary system it replaces, and how would we detect them in time to matter? In particular: first, is the objection from thin or manipulable markets a contingent engineering problem or a structural one, and what observation would settle that? Second, the markets estimate what happens to the metric given a grant; if the metric is imperfectly correlated with what we actually value, does the scheme merely inherit the metric-specification problem that any bureaucracy has, or does it amplify metric capture in a way discretionary judgment does not, and what evidence would distinguish the two? Third, there is a reflexivity worry — that a market asked to price a policy's effects is pricing a world in which decisions are already being made by that very pricing mechanism, so the conditional is not the one we need; is this a genuine incoherence or a pseudo-problem, and what would tell them apart? Fourth, what would the position's own defenders concede as a decisive defeater — a competently run pilot that underperforms, say — versus what they would explain away, and does the position as currently stated risk anything at all, or has it been hedged into unfalsifiability? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which single piece of evidence, if I encountered it within the year, should most move me to vote no.
