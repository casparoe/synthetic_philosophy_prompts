My brother-in-law farms wheat and has a standing rant about Malthus, and he finally wrote the argument down for me. It's been bugging me for weeks because I can't find the wrong step, but the conclusion has to be wrong somewhere, or at least it proves way too much. Roughly as he put it:

(1) Malthus said population tends to grow faster than food production can, so in the long run living standards get ground back down toward subsistence, with famine and misery doing the grinding.

(2) Two centuries of rising wages and calories per head don't refute this, because the prediction never had a date attached. Any finite stretch of good times is compatible with the crash coming later.

(3) The underlying mechanism -- exponential growth running into roughly linear resource growth -- is still in place. Technology has pushed the ceiling up and out, not removed it. (He says topsoil loss and aquifer depletion are just the modern version of the same squeeze.)

(4) So Malthus was right, just early, and the smart money is still on him.

Now here's the part that convinces me this proves too much:

(5) If (2) and (3) are right, the same rescue works for every failed long-run prediction ever made: the apocalypse sects that kept moving the date, the people who said we'd run out of copper by 1990, anyone still waiting on a revolution that was due a hundred years ago. None of them were wrong either. They were all just early.

(6) But a defense that makes failure impossible for everyone makes success meaningless for anyone. If nothing could ever count against Malthus, then nothing ever counted for him in the first place.

(7) So something in (1)-(4) has to go.

Here's my problem. I actually want to keep (2) in some form. It seems obviously true that you can't refute "the crash is coming" by pointing at a few calm decades -- stock markets teach us that much. And I sort of want to keep the thought behind (3), that a mechanism can be real even while its effects are delayed or masked. But if I keep both, I'm stuck with (5), and (6) is definately right.

So: which step do I actually reject, what does rejecting it cost me, and why did the rejected step look so good in the first place? And if your answer is that "right but early" is sometimes a legitimate defense and sometimes just a rescue, then give me the test that seperates the two cases -- a test I could apply before the crash, not after -- rather than just a name for the fallacy.
