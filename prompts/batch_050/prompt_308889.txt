I'm in a casual reading group (mix of grad students, mostly not philosophers) and we just read a chapter on Zeno in a popular math book -- I won't name it, but it's the kind that sells well at airports. The author's take, roughly, goes like this: "Zeno's paradoxes were genuinely baffling in antiquity only because the Greeks lacked the mathematics of infinite series. Once you see that 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converges to exactly 1, the Dichotomy and Achilles evaporate: Achilles covers the infinitely many shrinking distances in a finite time, end of story. The Arrow is the same trick -- motion is just an object's position being a continuous function of time, and calculus gives you velocity at each instant as a derivative, so there's no mystery about how the arrow moves. Aristotle basically anticipated all this with his distinction between potential and actual infinity, and the only reason philosophers keep writing about Zeno is that the philosophy literature moves slower than the math."

Now, this smelled off to me, partly because I dimly remember a philosophy friend once insisting the calculus answer "misses the point" -- but when I pressed her she couldn't articulate why, and honestly the book's version is very seductive. So what I'd like is an audit. Which parts of this summary would a philosopher who works on Zeno accept as basically right? Which bits conflate things that come apart -- I'm suspicious, for instance, that "you can sum the series" and "someone can complete infinitely many tasks" might not be the same claim, and that the Arrow might raise a different issue than the Dichotomy rather than being 'the same trick.' Is the Aristotle attribution fair, or is his potential/actual infinity distinction more contested than the book lets on? And the thing I most want adjudicated: does the book's conclusion -- that continued philosophical interest in Zeno is just disciplinary lag -- actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does correcting the summary undercut the sneer? If there are live positions on which the math is all granted and the paradoxes still bite, sketch at least one charitably, because the book certainly doesn't.
