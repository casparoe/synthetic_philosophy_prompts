I teach an undergraduate seminar in ethical theory, and this term a recurring move has begun to grate on me. We were discussing whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible -- cases like Sartre's student, torn between caring for his mother and joining the Resistance, where it seems the agent ought to do each of two things and cannot do both. Whenever the class starts taking such cases seriously, at least one student deploys the slogan "ought implies can" as though it dissolved the problem on the spot: if the student cannot do both, then it cannot be true that he ought to do both, end of discussion. When I press, the students are rarely able to say what exactly the slogan asserts, and I have come to suspect it is doing several different jobs at once -- sometimes it seems to be a constraint on when blame is appropriate, sometimes a claim about the point of moral language, sometimes something close to a logical thesis about the concept of obligation itself.

Here is what I would like. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "ought implies can" could be expressing, ordered from the near-platitudinous to the philosophically loaded, and for each one say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or actually false. Then -- and this is the part where I want you to commit rather than hand me a menu -- identify which reading the anti-dilemma argument actually requires, and take a definite position on whether that reading is true and whether, even granting it, it really dissolves the dilemmas or merely relocates them (for instance, whether the residue of regret and the sense of having failed someone, which my students treat as evidence for dilemmas, survives the dissolution). I would rather you defend a view I can argue against in class than present a balanced survey I have to adjudicate myself.
