I am a PhD student in formal epistemology preparing a two-page prospectus for my dissertation committee, and I need it drafted from scratch on the following idea.

Van Fraassen's Reflection principle is normally stated and evaluated under a presupposition that is rarely examined in its own right: that the propositions over which I now have credences are the same propositions over which my future self will have credences. My working conjecture is that Reflection behaves very differently once you drop that presupposition. If my future self may assign credences to propositions I cannot currently even formulate — because I will learn a new concept, encounter a new theoretical possibility, or simply become aware of a hypothesis that was never on my radar — then the principle as standardly stated is either inapplicable or quietly restricted to the fragment of my future credences that happens to live in my present algebra of propositions. I suspect that a suitably generalized version of Reflection for expanding algebras can be formulated precisely, and that it will still be violated by some perfectly rational agents, but for different and more interesting reasons than the standard counterexamples involving foreseeable irrationality or self-locating uncertainty.

Write the prospectus. It should state the central question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing debates about Reflection — the Dutch book defenses, the counterexamples from anticipated memory loss and foreseeably diminished rationality, and the attempts to rescue it by restricting it to "epistemically accessible" future selves — do not settle the expanding-algebra case one way or the other; and describe what I would actually do, roughly term by term over a year (e.g., formalizing awareness growth in a Bayesian setting, deriving what Reflection must say about coarse-grained projections of richer future credences, and checking whether the generalized principle retains the arguments that made the original philosophically attractive rather than merely being a trivial restatement). Include a section on what a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing or collapsing into an already-known result, and an honest assessment of the main technical risk.

Two further constraints. First, keep the epistemic calibration explicit: where you present background, distinguish what is genuinely settled in this literature from what remains contested among formal epistemologists, and do not present a contested position as the received view. Second, do not include a bibliography or any citations — I will supply those myself; refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by author-year. Maximum 900 words.
