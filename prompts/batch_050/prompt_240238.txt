I taught Being and Nothingness in my existentialism seminar last week and two of my best grad students, Priya and Marcus, got into it after class and dragged me in. I stayed out of it, partly because I genuinely wasn't sure how it should come out. Here's the gist. Priya's line was that bad faith, for Sartre, is just a fact about how consciousness relates to itself: since consciousness is always free, any time I treat myself as a fixed thing with a settled nature ("I'm just not a brave person," "I have to stay at this job, I have a mortgage"), I'm lying to myself, full stop. She used a case I hadn't heard before: a guy who's stayed in a miserable career for twenty years and tells everyone, sincerely, that he'd leave in a heartbeat if only his circumstances were different. Textbook bad faith, she said -- he's turning his choices into facts about the world so he doesn't have to own them. Marcus pushed back with what seemed to me a decent point: Sartre himself says freedom is always freedom-in-situation, and he even says the slave is as free as the master, but that the slave's freedom isn't freedom to *become* a master -- so on Sartre's own view there's a real difference between what you're free *from* and what you're free *to do*. So when the miserable career guy says his circumstances constrain him, Marcus argued, he's not necessarily lying; he might be accurately reporting that his options for change are objectively thin, and calling that bad faith turns Sartre into someone who blames people for their material conditions. Priya replied that this conflates two claims: saying your options are thin is fine, but saying you *couldn't* choose otherwise is bad faith regardless of how thin the options are -- bad faith is about the structure of the self-ascription, not the content. Marcus said that can't be right either, because then Sartre would have to say that a prisoner who sincerely believes he can't walk out of his locked cell is in bad faith, which is absurd. Priya said no, the prisoner case is different because the constraint there is external and absolute, whereas the career guy's constraint is one he's constituting as absolute through his projects. At which point Marcus said that's exactly the question -- whether the mortgage is more like the locked door or more like a value the guy is projecting -- and Priya said that's not a question Sartre leaves open, because facticity only ever shows up as this-or-that *within* a freely chosen project.

They went another round but that's the core of it. Can you adjudicate this for me? I want a point-by-point verdict: who is right about (1) whether bad faith is a matter of how you relate to yourself regardless of your actual options, (2) whether Marcus's prisoner case is a genuine counterexample or a misread of what bad faith is, and (3) whether Sartre's slave/master claim actually supports Marcus's reading or Priya's. And if there's something both of them are missing -- some wrinkle in Sartre's account that dissolves or relocates the dispute -- flag that too. I'd also like your own bottom line on the original case of the miserable career guy, since that's what started it. Please write in American English, and don't just summarize both sides -- actually take positions and defend them.
