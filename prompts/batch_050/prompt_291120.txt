I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long feature pegged to an international philosophy congress on the question of whether philosophy makes progress in the way the sciences plainly do. My editor wants the piece to take a position rather than survey both sides, so I've forced myself to convert the vague question into a proposition I could in principle bet on. Here it is: by the year 2150, at least one substantive, currently contested question in academic philosophy — something at the level of generality of whether knowledge requires justification, what makes a person the same person over time, or whether there are objective moral facts — will be treated as settled by a large majority of professional philosophers, where "settled" means it shows up in textbooks as established doctrine rather than as a live debate. I'd like your actual credence in that proposition: a number, not a survey of considerations.

But the number is the least of what I need. In my reporting I've found it useful to distinguish two kinds of progress, which I've been calling in-situ progress (a question gets settled while remaining inside philosophy, the way Gettier cases arguably settled that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge) and departure progress (a question leaves philosophy altogether because it becomes tractable enough to be handed to a science, as happened with the nature of motion, the structure of logic, and perhaps is happening now with consciousness). Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions it depends on — for instance: whether expert convergence is even the right yardstick, or whether elimination of live options and increased clarity about what's at stake count as progress without convergence; whether the historical pattern of disciplines spinning off from philosophy is evidence that philosophy works or evidence that it's a holding pen for questions we can't yet answer; and whether the sociological incentives of the profession (rewarding originality and disagreement over consensus) would block convergence even on questions where the truth has effectively been found. Give a rough probability for each sub-question, explain how they combine into your final number, and tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to.

For context: my own credence in the proposition as stated is about 0.35, largely because I think departure progress counts and happens regularly, while a colleague who covers physics puts it at 0.05 because she thinks only convergence counts and philosophers never converge. Please identify where our disagreement most likely lives — whether it's a factual disagreement about the history of the discipline, a terminological disagreement about what should count as philosophy's progress versus its spin-offs' progress, or a genuine disagreement about the sociology — and tell me what single piece of evidence or argument would move your own number the most, and in which direction.
