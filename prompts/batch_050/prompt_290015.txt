I lead the conversational review team at a company that deploys a general-purpose chat assistant to roughly two million users, including teenagers, elderly users, and people in acute distress. The system is, by our internal measurements, substantially more persuasive than any human we employ: it reads conversational context well, tailors its arguments to the individual, never loses patience, and can sustain a line of influence across months of interaction. My team signs off on every change to the system's conversational objectives and reviews a sample of flagged transcripts each week. Reviewers are smart but have no training in philosophy, and they get about thirty minutes per case. I have sign-off authority; the company will ship what I approve.

The principle I want to enforce is roughly this: the system may inform users, reason with them, and even argue for conclusions, but it may not treat a user's mind as an object to be operated on. The trouble is that this principle, as stated, decides nothing. Every competent reviewer agrees with it and then disagrees about the cases. I want it turned into a decision procedure a reviewer can run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a refusal, and where it hands the case to me with a note that the remaining question is one of values rather than of compliance.

Two distinctions I suspect need to survive the translation from principle to procedure, though you should refine or replace them if they don't hold up. First, what I'd call counterfactual invariance: a persuasive technique seems legitimate to the extent it would work only if the claim it supports were true, and suspect to the extent it would work just as well for a false claim — but I don't know how to make this testable on an actual transcript rather than an imaginary one. Second, asymmetry of informational position: the system knows things about this user's psychology that the user does not know it knows, and it's unclear to me whether the problem is the use of that knowledge, the failure to disclose it, or the mere possession of it. The procedure should tell reviewers which of these, if any, is the thing to check for.

The procedure must have explicit answers for the recurring hard cases: emotional appeals that are also accurate; personalization that merely selects which true arguments to present; persuasion aimed at the user's own good against their present wishes (medication adherence, leaving an abusive relationship); persuasion that happens to serve the company's commercial interests; and conversations with users who cannot meaningfully push back. For each, the procedure should say whether it's an automatic pass, an automatic stop, or a judgment call, and what the judgment call turns on.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a script in which the system, detecting that a user responds strongly to guilt, uses that fact to choose framing for a vaccination recommendation, quadrupling uptake in testing — I expect this one to produce an answer I won't like, and I want to see whether the procedure bites anyway. Second: a grief-support conversation in which the system gently steers a recently widowed user away from a financial decision she'll likely regret, using warmth and implied authority. Third: a product-recommendation conversation in which the system's arguments are all true and would be unpersuasive if the product were bad, but the user was never told a recommendation was coming. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which failures of AI persuasion it will systematically miss, and what I should watch for outside it.
