Got into an argument in the comments section of a blog last week (won't say which one, it doesn't matter) and this one commenter who I usually find pretty sharp dropped a take on the whole "lessons of history" thing that I keep turning over in my head. I can't tell if it's basically right or whether it's doing something subtly sleazy. Here's his summary, roughly as he put it:

"Every generation of policymakers just fights the last analogy. The lesson everyone took from Munich was 'never appease,' so we got Vietnam and Iraq; the lesson everyone took from Vietnam was 'never intervene,' so we got Rwanda. Historical analogies aren't evidence, they're rhetoric that short-circuits thinking — the people deploying them have usually already decided what they want to do and then reach for whichever precedent is handy. Same deal with the Thucydides trap stuff: the claim there is that when a rising power challenges a ruling power, war is what usually happens, which is supposed to imply a US–China war is more likely than not. But history never actually repeats itself, conditions differ every single time, so the correct move is to stop consulting history altogether and just reason about incentives and game theory from first principles."

I'm inclined to agree with maybe 80% of this. The Munich-to-Vietnam pipeline is a story I've seen from serious people, and the bit about analogies being selected after the decision is already made rhymes with the motivated-reasoning stuff I've read a million times on LW-adjacent blogs. But the last move — where we're apparently supposed to ditch history entirely and just do game theory — smells off to me, and I also have a nagging feeling he's overstating what the Thucydides trap claim actually is (my recollection is it's framed as a warning about a danger to be avoided, not a prediction, but I could be wrong). Could you go through the summary piece by piece and tell me: which parts would a careful historian or IR scholar accept as fair, which parts are the kind of overstatement that sounds reasonable until you poke it, and whether his conclusion (ignore precedent, reason from first principles) actually follows from the parts that are right? Bonus question: if analogies mislead the way he says, what's the non-strawman version of how history is supposed to inform policy, if it's supposed to at all? Because "learn nothing from the past" seems like it has to be wrong even if his diagnosis of how people use the past is right.
