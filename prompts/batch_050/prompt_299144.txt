I'm in a havruta that's been crawling through the Guide for the Perplexed all year, and last week we hit III.32, the chapter where Maimonides explains why the Torah commands animal sacrifice at all. Our group split hard. I came away defending the standard "concession" reading: sacrifices aren't worship God actually wants, they're a pedagogical detour. The Israelites couldn't imagine religion without altars and slaughter, so God redirected the pagan form toward Himself rather than abolishing it, and the real aim all along is knowledge of God and prayer, with sacrifice as scaffolding. A friend in the group says this reading is too clever by half and only survives because nobody ever pressures it. So here's my request: what would actually count AGAINST the concession reading? What evidence or arguments should genuinely move me?

Part of what made me dig in is that Maimonides himself seems to anticipate the strongest objection and basically dares you to press it. After laying out the accommodation story he writes: "How can we suppose that Divine commandments, prohibitions, and important acts, which are fully explained, and for which certain seasons are fixed, should not have been commanded for their own sake, but only for the sake of some other thing: as if they were only the means which He employed for His primary object?" (Guide III.32, Friedländer translation). So he clearly knows the worry: if sacrifices are mere means, their binding status looks weird. And yet he still piles up detailed halakhic treatments of sacrifice in the Mishneh Torah, and apparently holds that the Temple service returns in messianic times. Doesn't that sink the "temporary scaffolding" picture? Or can the reading absorb it?

I'd like a map of how exposed the concession reading really is: which considerations would be decisive (textual, from Maimonides' own halakhic writing, or comparative like Nachmanides' rival account), which would merely be awkward, and what defenders of the reading themselves concede vs. what only critics say. I also worry the reading gets protected by esotericism, i.e., whenever a passage contradicts it, someone says "that's just the exoteric layer," which starts to feel unfalsifiable. Tell me honestly if that's a fair charge. And please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled among people who study Maimonides (e.g., that the accommodation argument is really there in the text) from what's contested (whether it was his sincere view, whether it implies sacrifices lapse permanently) -- don't present a contested scholarly take as the consensus, becuase I'll be quoting you back to my study group and I'd rather not get burned.
