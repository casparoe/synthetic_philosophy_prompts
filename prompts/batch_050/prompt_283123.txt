Ok, slightly unhinged request: I want a poem about gauge freedom in electromagnetism — basically the question of what's actually real in a gauge theory — and I want the poem's form to enact the problem, not just describe it.

The physics it has to get right (please don't fudge this):

In classical electromagnetism you can work with the vector potential A, but A is only fixed up to adding the gradient of any smooth function. Change that function and A changes, while the electric and magnetic fields — and apparently everything measurable — stay exactly the same. So you get infinitely many mathematically distinct descriptions of one and the same physical situation, and the textbook move is to dismiss the potential as bookkeeping: surplus structure, not ontology.

The wrinkle is the Aharonov–Bohm effect. Send electrons around both sides of a perfectly shielded solenoid. They only ever travel through regions where the fields are zero, and yet the interference fringes shift, by an amount set by the magnetic flux trapped inside. So the lazy conclusion that A is pure bookkeeping and only the fields are real can't be right either. The careful conclusion: what's real is the gauge-invariant content — the fields plus the phase picked up around closed loops (the holonomies). More than just E and B, less than the full A. The redundancy was in our description, not in the world.

Formal constraints:

- Rhymed iambic pentameter, somewhere between 24 and 36 lines, in quatrains.
- Early on, state one quatrain. Later, that same quatrain must reappear in completely different words — same content, new wording — and the poem itself has to insist the two versions are one and the same stanza, the same fact in two gauges. The reader should feel the redundancy rather than be told about it.
- The turn of the poem is the fringe shift: the moment "mere bookkeeping" starts pushing electrons around.
- The ending must say precisely what survives every regauging — not the raw potential, not only the local fields, but the loop phases, what's invariant under all redescriptions. If the closing couplet answers "so what is real?" with anything mushier than that, the poem has failed.
- Accurate enough that a philosopher of physics wouldn't wince. In particular: don't claim Aharonov–Bohm proves the potential itself is physically real (thats the standard overclaim), and don't retreat to claiming it's really all just fields either.
