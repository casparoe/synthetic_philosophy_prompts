I run a decision theory reading group for philosophy graduate students, and next month I'm leading the session on acausal trade. They all know the prisoner's dilemma and the basic setup of Newcomb's problem, but none of them have worked through functional or timeless decision theory, and most are instinctively suspicious of anything that smells like causation running backwards. Plan the session for me: the order in which the pieces have to go so the idea lands as a natural extension of reasoning they already accept rather than as a sci-fi leap -- in particular, what has to be established about Newcomb's problem, and about agents choosing under the knowledge that their deliberation is mirrored or predicted elsewhere, before 'trade between agents who have never interacted and may not even both exist' can be made intelligible; the one example to build the whole session around (I'm torn between two copies of the same program playing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma and two civilizations in causally isolated regions of the universe coordinating on which costly projects to fund -- tell me which carries the idea better and what the other one wrongly suggests); the misconceptions I should expect, such as that acausal trade is just ordinary cooperation with extra steps, that it requires each party to literally simulate the other, or that it commits you to backwards causation, and the exact points in the sequence where I should head each one off; what I should openly decline to cover at this level; and one closing question that would tell me whether they've grasped the difference between an acausal agreement and a merely hoped-for coincidence of choices.
