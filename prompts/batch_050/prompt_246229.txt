Here's my rough understanding so you don't have to guess what I know: in Nomic, players take turns proposing changes to the rules, the rules themselves govern how rule changes get adopted, and the rules start out divided into mutable and immutable tiers, with mechanisms for "transmuting" one into the other. Suber's puzzle is about a legal system where even the amendment clause can itself be amended -- nothing is bedrock, so what (if anything) holds still, and can the system authorise its own ultimate change of authorisation procedure without circularity?

Restate this whole setup and the paradox in the vocabulary I actually use: self-modifying code, hot-swapping modules, meta-circular interpreters, self-hosting compilers, quines, immutable infrastructure, Git history and code review as governance, etc. Be precise about which Nomic concepts map onto which of these.

Then give me the residue report: what didn't survive the translation. My suspicion is that the big casualty is the difference between a rule being *valid* and it being *followed* -- in code there's no gap between the amendment rule and its execution, the interpreter just does whatever the bytes say, so "can this rule legitimately amend itself?" collapses into "does the runtime let it?" Tell me whether I'm right about that, which other parts of the paradox have no counterpart in software, and whether the residue shows that legal self-reference involves something computation genuinely lacks, or whether it shows Suber's paradox was partly an artifact of legal vocabulary all along.
