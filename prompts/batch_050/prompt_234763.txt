I run a small philosophy reading group at my local library, and we're currently slogging through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments -- about ten of us, mix of retired folks, a couple of grad students, one very opinionated plumber. We meet for two hours. The sticking point is the impartial spectator: everyone nods along at the idea that we judge our own behaviour by imaginig how a fair-minded bystander would see it, but it stays completely bloodless for them. They treat it as a bit of 18th-century politeness. What I want is an activity, maybe 60-75 minutes, that makes them actually *feel* the thing Smith is on about -- specifically the way our passions get 'cooled down' when we have to present our case to someone who has no stake in it, and how that cooling isn't hypocrisy but is partly constitutive of getting along with other people at all.

Here's roughly what I'm imagining, but please improve on it or replace it entirely: some structure where participants bring or are given a real-ish grievance (a neighbour's fence, being passed over at work, a family inheritance row -- small-stakes stuff people genuinely feel), first get to vent about it to a sympathetic ally, and then have to re-present the same case to someone playing the disinterested onlooker, with rules that stop the onlooker from just being a pushover or a contrarian. The point should land in what happens to their description and their feelings during the retelling, not in anything I say afterwards. I want the moment where they notice their own account quietly changing -- details getting dropped, the other person's motives getting conceded, their certainty deflating a bit -- before anyone mentions Smith's name for it. So I need concrete roles, rules, a timeline, and some mechanism to keep it from turning into either group therapy or a mock trial where 'winning' is the goal.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto Smith's actual machinery (the spectator we internalise, the lowering of passion to a pitch the onlooker can go along with, the difference between being praised and being praiseworthy if that's reachable), plus an honest account of what will predictably go wrong when I run it with this particular group and what to do about it, and one frank paragraph on which feature of Smith's view an exercise like this simply cannot reproduce -- I suspect something about the *internalised* spectator operating when nobody is watching, but tell me what you think. Please write the whole thing in British English.
