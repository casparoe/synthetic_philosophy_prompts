Three weeks ago, my local council fined me because refuse from an overflowing communal bin outside my building was attributed to my household. I had not put it there and, as far as I can tell, could not reasonably have prevented it, but the enforcement officer informed me that this is a strict liability offence: my intentions, knowledge, and level of care are simply irrelevant to my guilt. Coincidentally, I have just been asked to help draft the disciplinary code for my allotment association, and several committee members want to import this very model — making certain rule breaches punishable regardless of fault — on the grounds that it would spare them the messy business of adjudicating what a member knew or intended.

Please describe the most important implications of strict liability, understood as punishment without mens rea, for how I should think about both situations. In particular, I want to understand what the doctrine presupposes about the purposes of punishment, when dispensing with fault is defensible and when it is not, what it does to deterrence and to people's willingness to participate in a rule-governed activity, and which design features — due-diligence defences, rebuttable presumptions, penalties scaled to harm rather than culpability, and the like — might preserve its administrative advantages while blunting its unfairness. I am especially interested in whether the justifications standardly offered for strict liability in state law survive transplantation into a small voluntary association, where exit is cheap and the rules' legitimacy rests on members' continued consent.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
