Two features of how randomized clinical trials are regulated seem to pull against each other. An ethics board will approve a trial only if the expert medical community is genuinely undecided about which arm is superior; yet a physician who was merely undecided would not be permitted to flip a coin in her own practice, while a physician with a settled view is permitted — indeed encouraged — to enroll her patient in the very trial whose premise is uncertainty. And once the trial is running, it is halted not when individual investigators become convinced but only when pre-specified statistical thresholds are crossed, however strong the hunches in the meantime. What does this arrangement presuppose about whose uncertainty counts: the clinician's, the profession's, or the trial's? Reconstruct the implicit view of how medical knowledge relates to the duty to patients under which all three features hang together. Say which rival views the practice rules out — for instance, that a doctor's obligations are fixed by her own best judgment, or that experimenting on patients requires a justification different in kind from treating them — and mark where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories, including a merely pragmatic compromise, would license the same rules. Finally, if the individual clinician's settled judgment were made authoritative instead, which features would have to change, and would anything of value be lost?
