I am a novelist, and this spring I am to deliver the inaugural memorial lecture at the literary festival where my late mentor, a comic novelist of some ferocity, taught for thirty years. The lectureship is funded jointly by the festival and by a university centre for humour research, so the room will be split roughly in half: scholars of comedy on one side, his widow, children, former students, and loyal readers on the other. I have eight minutes.

Here is the difficulty I want the speech built around. My mentor did not merely write comedy; he preached a theory of it, an uncompromising version of the superiority account: every laugh, he held, has a loser, and the affectionate joke is only aggression wearing gloves. He applied this to himself without mercy, including at the podium, which is why the festival asked a novelist rather than a cleric to speak at his memorial. I have spent twenty years quietly dissenting. I believe laughter is what a violated expectation feels like when it resolves into safety, and that in a grieving room it is also simply the release of pressure — which is to say I think he was wrong in at least two of the standard ways, and I would like his memorial lecture to demonstrate it rather than assert it.

So the constraint is dialectical as much as tonal. Every laugh the speech gets at his expense is evidence for his theory; a speech too reverent to be funny is evidence for it too, since he would have called the reverence fear. The comedy therefore has to be built almost entirely out of incongruity — the small absurdities of mourning, of academic ceremony, of being asked to commemorate a man who despised commemoration — and out of the room's need for relief, with no target. One exception: I want to make myself the butt of exactly one joke, and to pre-empt, inside the speech itself, his predictable reply that self-mockery merely makes the audience superior to me.

Please write the speech, roughly a thousand words. It must land for the scholars, who will catch the theoretical moves, without losing the family, who must hear only love and laughter. Begin with the speech itself — no preface, no commentary, no explanation of the choices afterwards.
