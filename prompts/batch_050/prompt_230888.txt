You are a moral philosopher with expertise in Scanlonian contractualism, asked to reason carefully about a colleague's decision pattern. Begin by brainstorming the candidate principles before committing to an answer.

My colleague, who serves on a hospital's scarce-resource ethics committee and describes herself as a contractualist, has ruled on five past cases, each a forced choice about allocating a single indivisible treatment:

1. Save one patient's life versus cure 500 patients of a chronic, moderate, non-lethal pain condition: she chose the one life.
2. Save one patient's life versus save two other patients' lives: she chose the two.
3. Save one patient's life versus save one other patient's life and, as a side effect of the same treatment, cure 10,000 patients of tension headaches: she chose the second option, citing the headaches as a legitimate tiebreaker.
4. Save one patient's arm versus cure 10,000 patients of a week-long migraine: she chose the arm.
5. Prevent one patient from going blind versus prevent two other patients from going blind: she chose the two.

She now faces a sixth case: the treatment can either save one patient's life or save sixty other patients each from permanent total paralysis, with no other differences between the parties.

Her verdicts so far strike me as jointly consistent with at least two distinct rules: a strict complaint-comparison rule on which only the strongest individual complaint on each side counts and lesser complaints never aggregate against a stronger one (numbers entering only as tiebreakers), and some form of weighted or threshold aggregation on which sufficiently many serious-enough complaints can outweigh a stronger one. Identify the strongest candidate rules that fit all five verdicts, state what each predicts about the sixth case, give your best guess as to how she will rule and why, and specify a further case whose outcome would best discriminate between the rules you take most seriously. Where two of your candidate rules turn out to be empirically equivalent across every case you can construct, say so rather than inventing a spurious difference.
