I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working broadly in philosophy of language with a sideline in philosophy of AI, and I'm putting together a proposal for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship. For the past year our department reading group has been chewing over the now-familiar question of how to classify LLM 'hallucinations': some people call them lies, some call them bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, and some insist they're just errors and that the other two labels are category mistakes. The debate keeps circling the same drain, and I think I can see why. All three classifications are normally defined by the speaker's mental states -- lying requires an intent to deceive, Frankfurt-style bullshit requires indifference to whether what one says is true, and error presupposes a genuine attempt to get things right -- and so the whole dispute collapses into the prior, murkier dispute about whether LLMs have the relevant mental states at all. My half-formed idea is to cut through this by relocating the analysis from the producer's mind to the production process: roughly, an utterance counts as bullshit when it issues from a process that is not oriented toward tracking the truth, whatever attitudes (if any) accompany it. The test cases I'd lean on are ones where the mental-state approach already struggles even with humans -- institutional statements drafted by committees, press releases assembled from templates, the 'organisational bullshit' that no individual quite intends -- since these give us a foothold for a mind-independent account without presupposing anything about machine consciousness.

Please turn this into a two-page fellowship proposal. It should state the central question precisely (something like: can the lying/bullshit/error distinction be reconstructed in terms of the truth-directedness of generative processes rather than speaker psychology, and does the reconstruction classify LLM outputs more informatively than the standard accounts?); explain why the existing classifications leave the question open rather than merely being applied uncertainly; and lay out the method concretely -- I'd envisage the first months on developing the process-based account against the human institutional cases, the middle months on applying it to distinguish different kinds of LLM output (confabulation under training-data gaps versus fluent fabrication under optimisation pressure), and the final months on the objection that the account simply changes the subject. I also need the proposal to say what would count as the project failing -- for instance, if the process-based account turns out either to classify all committee statements as bullshit (too permissive) or to smuggle mental states back in through the notion of a process being 'oriented' toward truth (not genuinely novel) -- since fellowship panels here rightly distrust projects with no failure conditions. Please don't include a bibliography or cite specific works beyond naming positions and arguments in general terms; I'll supply the references myself.

One further constraint on the framing: I want the proposal to argue, without grandstanding, that this is a philosophy project rather than an empirical AI-safety project -- the contribution would be a conceptual analysis with test cases, not benchmarks -- and it should end with a short paragraph on what the deliverable would look like for people outside philosophy (AI policy folk and journalists reach for 'hallucination', 'lying', and 'bullshit' interchangeably, and I'd want the proposal to promise something usable by them). Please write the proposal itself in British English, and keep the tone appropriate for a fellowship application: confident but honest about scope.
