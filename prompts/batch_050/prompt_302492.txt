For a reading group on fatalism I had to write down my positions on future contingents, and I'd like a consistency check before the others tear into it. Here's where I've landed, prompted by the sea battle argument in ch. 9 of Aristotle's De Interpretatione (the bit where he reasons, roughly, that if something is the case now, then it was true earlier to say it *would* be the case, and so it looks like everything that happens was always going to happen):

(1) Aristotle's argument is a real problem that a theory has to block somehow, not a mere sophism you can wave off.
(2) All the same, "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is either true or false right now — bivalence holds even for contingents about the future.
(3) The future is genuinely open: nothing about how things stand right now settles whether the battle happens, and both outcomes are still real possibilities.
(4) A sentence is true now only if present reality makes it true; truth supervenes on what is the case.
(5) If someone predicts the battle and it happens, then what they said was true all along — truth doesn't change when the event does.

Some of this feels off to me but I can't locate the problem.
