ok so I've been reading about the honor / dignity / victimhood cultures distinction and I want to nail down where I actually stand before I argue about it with people. Here's roughly where I've landed, tell me if it hangs together:

1. Dignity culture is morally better than honor culture. Your worth shouldn't be hostage to whether other people show you respect, and a culture that treats insults as things you shrug off or handle through law is just healthier than one where every slight demands a response.

2. That said, honor cultures aren't stupid or backward. If you live somewhere the state can't or won't protect you, your reputation for not being pushable-around is genuinely protective, and being touchy about disrespect is rational there. Different incentives, not worse people.

3. Victimhood culture, though, I think is a real pathology. Appealing to third parties (administrators, HR, twitter mobs) over small slights, and building moral status out of being wronged, incentivizes people to construe themselves as victims, and institutions shouldn't reward it.

4. Microaggressions are real and they do cumulative damage. And part of what's bad about them is precisely that the target often can't just handle it directly -- there's deniability baked in ("it was a joke," "you're reading into it"), and often a power asymmetry, so direct confrontation is costly or pointless. Sometimes someone with authority actually does need to step in.

5. As a general rule people should try to sort out their own conflicts before escalating anything to an authority.

A friend says 3 and 4 can't both survive and I think she's wrong, but I haven't been able to say exactly why. So: which pairs here are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and for the real ones, ranked by how bad they are, what's the cheapest fix in each case? Also curious whether the line I've drawn between dignity culture (fine, uses law) and victimhood culture (bad, uses third parties) is as clean as I need it to be, given that courts are also third parties.
