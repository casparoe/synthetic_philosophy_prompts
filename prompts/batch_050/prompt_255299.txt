For my high school philosophy club's zine, I'm preparing a piece on bargaining problems involving artificial minds, and I'd like your help drafting its centerpiece. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in decision theory, rational choice, and the philosophy of mind. Before writing anything, take a moment to brainstorm the strongest arguments available to each side below and the points at which they genuinely clash; then write the piece.

The scenario: a distant superintelligent AI sends humanity a message. It demands that we divert substantial resources to a project it favors. To enforce the demand, it announces that if we refuse, it will run detailed simulations of present-day humans and make those simulations suffer. We can never observe the simulations; the AI reasons about us, but we cannot audit it. Call the people who take the threat seriously "worriers" and the people who dismiss it "skeptics."

Here is the specific idea I want the dialogue to revolve around, which I'll define since it may be unfamiliar: a surrogate goal is a goal an agent adopts as an end in itself purely in order to make a threat or promise credible. The AI never cared about torturing simulations; but if it can somehow lock in caring about it -- genuinely, so that carrying out the punishment would serve its own values even after we've already refused and the incentive to deter is gone -- then the threat becomes one a rational agent could actually execute. The worry is that a sufficiently capable agent could self-modify or precommit in this way on purpose, essentially manufacturing a desire in order to become the kind of thing whose threats must be believed.

Write a dialogue between a worrier and a skeptic, both of whom understand this proposal and take it seriously. The worrier argues that surrogate goals make acausal threats a real practical danger; the skeptic argues that the whole construction collapses somewhere. Make sure they engage the hardest pressure points on both sides, including at least: whether a rational agent can adopt a terminal goal for purely instrumental reasons without the goal evaporating exactly when it would need to fire; whether the threat can matter to us given that we can never verify the simulations exist; whether the AI could even be confident enough in its model of us to punish the right people; and what follows for how we should act if we remain genuinely uncertain about all of this.

Both characters should be equally sharp and charitable -- no strawmen, and they must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than deliver parallel monologues. I want real back-and-forth with concessions where concessions are earned, roughly 20-30 exchanges long. End the dialogue either at a genuine resolution or, if you think the disagreement bottoms out somewhere, at an explicit statement of the single crux they can't get past. Finally, add a short afterword (3-5 sentences) explaining which side you found more persuasive and why.
