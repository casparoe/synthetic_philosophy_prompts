I'm in the second term of an undergraduate metaethics module, and this week we've been discussing evolutionary debunking arguments — the worry, associated with writers like Sharon Street, that if Darwinian forces shaped our moral beliefs, then realists about objective moral truth face a dilemma. A friend of mine who hasn't taken the course but read a popular science book on the evolution of morality gave me his summary of the argument over coffee, and I wanted to check it before our seminar, since something about it felt off to me but I couldn't put my finger on exactly where.

His version ran roughly as follows. Natural selection, he said, has been shown to have shaped our moral beliefs: we think co-operation is good and cruelty is bad because ancestors who believed such things left more descendants, not because those beliefs are true. Since evolution selects for reproductive success rather than truth, he concluded, our moral intuitions are no more likely to be true than false, and so morality itself is a kind of useful illusion — a fiction our genes tell us. He then added that this amounts to a scientific refutation of moral realism, and that, strictly speaking, we ought to stop making moral claims altogether, or at least to stop treating them as anything more than expressions of taste. I suspect he's right about the basic evolutionary premise, but the leap from "our beliefs were shaped by selection pressures" to "morality is an illusion" struck me as suspicious, and I have a feeling the philosophers we've read were making a more careful point than the one he ended up with.

Could you audit his summary for me? I'd like you to go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a proponent of evolutionary debunking arguments would accept as accurate, which parts are defensible simplifications, and which parts misrepresent the argument — in particular, whether debunkers actually conclude that moral claims are false rather than merely unjustified, whether the argument targets all moral thought or only certain metaethical positions, and whether his final practical recommendation follows from anything in the argument itself. If you could also sketch the strongest reply available to the moral realist, so I can see whether the argument survives it, that would be a great help. Please write your answer in British English.
