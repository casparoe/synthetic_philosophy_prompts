I am a PhD student in comparative philosophy, and next month I am presenting a paper in our department colloquium series. I would like help preparing for the question period.

The paper defends a reconstruction of Yi Hwang's (Toegye's) position in the Korean Four–Seven debate. Briefly: Mencius singles out four incipient moral responses — the heart of compassion, of shame and dislike, of deference, and of approval and disapproval — as the sprouts of the four cardinal virtues, while the classical ritual corpus lists seven ordinary emotions (joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hate, desire). Since all ten are feelings, the question arose why Mencius treats the four as a distinct class. Yi Hwang proposed that the four issue from principle (li) whereas the seven issue from material force (qi); his critic Ki Daeseung objected that principle is inert and cannot generate anything, so both classes must issue from qi, with principle merely patterning them — a line later systematized by Yi I in the slogan that qi issues and principle rides. My thesis is that Yi's talk of principle "issuing" should not be read as a causal claim positing a second generative source of emotion, but as a structural claim: the four beginnings are the seven emotions themselves when their intentional structure tracks the moral pattern, so "issuance by principle" marks a difference in how a response is directed and grounded, not in where it comes from. On this reading the inertness objection dissolves.

The audience will be roughly a dozen faculty and graduate students: three analytic philosophers of emotion who work on intentionality and on whether emotions can be assessed as fitting or unfitting; two specialists in early Chinese philosophy (Mencius and Xunzi) who do not work on Korean Neo-Confucianism; a metaphysician with interests in causal powers and the causal exclusion problem; and no one with expertise in the Yi Hwang–Ki Daeseung correspondence itself.

Please draft my prep sheet: the eight objections this particular audience is most likely to raise — not the objections a room of Korean philosophy specialists would raise — ordered by how damaging each would be to my thesis if left unanswered. Phrase each objection the way the relevant audience member would actually put it, identify who is likely to raise it, and give me a reply of roughly two minutes for each, flagging where each reply is weakest and what I should concede versus hold. Also tell me which objections I can convert into support for the reading, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks.
