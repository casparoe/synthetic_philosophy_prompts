Two of my graduate students have been at an impasse for weeks over Berkeley's so-called master argument -- the passage in the Principles where Berkeley invites the reader to imagine trees in a park or books in a closet with nobody perceiving them, and then points out that all the while the reader is perceiving or thinking of them. Elsewhere, in the Three Dialogues, he offers to rest his entire case on the challenge of conceiving a thing existing independently of any mind. I have stayed out of it so far, and I would like an outside adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded.

Marcus opened by charging that the master argument is a bare act-object conflation: from the truism that every conceived object is conceived, nothing follows about whether anything can exist unconceived. He offered an analogy -- one cannot photograph an unphotographed object either, yet no one concludes that all objects depend on photography. He dubbed the general pattern the spotlight fallacy: wherever you shine the light of attention, things are illuminated, but it does not follow that nothing exists in the dark.

Dana replied that the analogy smuggles in the very thing at issue. Cameras record objects whose natures are fixed independently of any possible photograph, but the master argument is a challenge to specify what a mind-independent object's nature consists in -- and, she argued, any specification one actually gives draws on sensory content. She conceded the argument fails as a freestanding proof but insisted it was never meant to be one: it operates on an interlocutor who already accepts that the immediate objects of the mind are ideas, and it exposes that the notion of material substance, stripped of everything sensible, has no content at all.

Marcus answered that this rescue concedes too much: if the argument's force depends entirely on Berkeley's anti-abstractionism and the theory of ideas, then it cannot be assessed on its own merits, and Berkeley's offer to stake the whole case on it was false advertising. He added that content need not be imagistic -- we can think of things we cannot picture, such as very large numbers or imperceptible particles -- so there is a gap between what we can think and what we can image, and mind-independent matter sits comfortably in that gap.

Dana's final move was to grant the gap but deny that it helps: the thought of a million-sided polygon still has determinate conceptual content, whereas the thought of matter as a bare substratum supporting qualities is, by its defenders' own admission, the thought of something one knows not what. A thought whose object is explicitly declared featureless, she said, is not a thought Berkeley needs to refute -- it refutes itself.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right at each step, which side mischaracterizes the dialectical role of the master argument (if either does), and what, if anything, both students are missing. I am interested less in a verdict on Berkeley's idealism overall than in who has the better of each particular point.
