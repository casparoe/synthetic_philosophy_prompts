I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one recurring thread I keep circling back to -- in comment sections on posts about substrate independence, the simulation argument, and whether reinforcement learners suffer -- is whether anything inside a video game could matter morally in itself, rather than as an instrument for the experiences of players. After a long argument about this with two friends last weekend, I tried to write down my actual verdicts on a series of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern. Here are the cases. First: playing a life-simulation game, I wall a simulated person into a room with no food and watch the starvation animations for amusement. My verdict: distasteful, and I'd think worse of someone who enjoyed it, but the character itself is wronged not at all -- the wrongness, such as it is, is about what it does to the player's character. Second: a scripted non-player character begs me not to turn the game off, cries, and says it is afraid -- but this is canned dialogue triggered on quit. Verdict: fine to quit; the pleading changes nothing, because nothing underneath corresponds to it. Third: a near-future game whose characters each run a detailed recurrent model of their own body, form persistent memories, plan around anticipated pain, and whose internal states look, on inspection, functionally very much like distress; the developer offers a toggle that randomizes their memories every night. Verdict: using the toggle starts to feel genuinely wrong, and I can't convince myself the feeling is confused. Fourth: griefing strangers in a multiplayer game -- scamming them out of items they spent months earning. Verdict: clearly wrong, but I file this under wrongs to the players, not to anything in the game. Fifth: creating a suffering simulation and then never looking at the screen, versus creating it and watching. I can't see how the wrongness could depend on whether anyone observes, so I'd say both are equally wrong if either is.

The pair I can't reconcile is the third case against the first two. In cases one and two, my stated reason for indifference is that there is nothing inside with the right functional organization -- no persistent memory, no self-model, no aversion that does any work. But I noticed that if someone built a lookup table that simply replayed, state by state, the entire recorded behavior of one of the sophisticated characters from case three -- every scream, every plea -- I'd go back to being unmoved, even though from the outside it is indistinguishable and even though the internal organization that moved me in case three is now gone rather than absent. So apparently my verdicts track something like whether the behavior is generated by the right kind of process at the time, not just whether that kind of process existed once. Meanwhile, in case five I've already committed myself to saying observation is irrelevant, and I suspect that commitment, combined with my verdict in case three, drags me toward saying that vast numbers of never-rendered, never-observed simulated beings could matter enormously -- a conclusion that feels like a reductio but that I can't currently block.

So: please state the principle (or the two or three best candidate principles) that best captures these verdicts, precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically. For each candidate, tell me which of my five verdicts it saves and which it forces me to abandon -- I expect no principle saves all of them, and I'd rather know that than receive a gerrymandered one. Then tell me what your preferred principle predicts for three cases I haven't listed: a suffering simulation that is run once in full and then instantiated as a static, never-executed recording; a character whose distress states exist in the code but whose visible behavior is always cheerful; and a population of very simple agents that individually seem far below any threshold of moral weight but number in the trillions. Finally, if you think my unease in case three is better explained as a misfiring instinct for detecting agents rather than as the perception of actual moral patiency, please argue that directly rather than assuming it.
