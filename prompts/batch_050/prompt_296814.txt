My reading group is working through Jaspers on German guilt this month, and one member, Tom, rolled out an argument last week that I'm convinced proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the step where it breaks. Here's my reconstruction of it. (1) You can only be guilty of what was up to you -- guilt requires control. (2) You can only be guilty of acts performed by an agent who is you. (3) Nobody alive today had any control over what their nation did generations ago, and none of them is the agent who did any of it. (4) So no living person bears any guilt for the Holocaust, or for slavery, or for colonial expropriation, whatever Jaspers wants to say about a people's political liability. (5) A nation is just a name for a succession of persons; once the membership has completely turned over, no agent that did the deed remains, so (1) and (2) rule out collective guilt across generations too. (6) Therefore talk of a nation's guilt is either reducible to the guilt of individuals or it's empty.

Up to (6) I was nodding along. But Tom then pointed out, with some glee, that the same machinery dissolves cases nobody at the table wanted dissolved: (7) a corporation can't be liable for asbestos installed under a board whose members have all since retired; (8) a state's treaty obligations lapse every time the electorate turns over; (9) an old man whose character, memories and even cells have wholly turned over since age twenty-five isn't guilty of what that young man did. We all agreed (7) through (9) are absurd. So one of (1)-(6) has to go, but honestly each one looked like common sense when Tom first said it.

So: which step should we actually reject, why did it look so plausible, and what's the bill for rejecting it? If I get to keep something, I'd most like to keep (1) -- it feels like the very core of fairness -- and I'd also like to keep (4), since I really do think my German-born neigbor doesn't carry anyone else's personal guilt around with her. What I can't see is how to keep those two without also swallowing (7)-(9). Walk me through what I'm paying on each option, including the option of just biting one of the absurd bullets if that's secretly the cheapest move.
