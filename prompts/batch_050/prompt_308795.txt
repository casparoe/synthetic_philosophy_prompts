Last weekend I got into a dumb but weirdly heated argument with a friend of mine who trades crypto and small-cap stocks for a living. I told him about the no-trade theorem results in economics -- the idea that if everyone is rational, and everyone knows everyone is rational (and knows that everyone knows, all the way up), and people share the same prior beliefs, then purely speculative trades basically shouldn't happen, because anyone willing to trade with you is telling you something, and once you update on their willingness the deal falls apart. He laughed and said real markets have trades happening every millisecond, so the theorem must be irrelevant academic nonsense. I think he's wrong -- the theorem tells you exactly which assumptions real markets must be violating -- but I want to be able to show him, concretely, *where* the escape hatch is.

So: construct a thought experiment in which two agents who are both perfectly rational, and who both know (and know that they know, and know that they know that they know...) that the other is rational, nevertheless genuinely complete a trade with each other -- and the no-trade theorem does not block it. Constraints: the trade has to be purely about who ends up holding the asset (no hedging motive, no insurance angle, no liquidity needs, nobody forced to sell to pay rent); the agents have identical risk tolerances; there are no noise traders, market makers, or third parties in the background; and the case has to work because of the information/belief structure, not because I've smuggled in some non-informational reason to swap positions. Both agents should walk away feeling like they got the better end of the deal, ex ante, not just ex post.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping for me: list the assumptions the no-trade result needs, one by one, and show that my case satisfies all of them except the one that fails, and that the trade goes through precisely *because* that one fails. Then two stress tests. First: if I patch the case so that the failing assumption now holds too, show that the trade unravels -- walk through how the deal dies once you fix it. Second: tell me which single feature of the original case is doing all the work, and how my trader friend would attack the example -- e.g., if he'd claim the trade is secretly just insurance, or that no real human's beliefs have the structure the case needs, or whatever his best move is. I want to be able to hand this to him and have him actually have to concede something specific.
