A colleague in my reading group has an argument she says shows scientific realism is not merely false but self-undermining, and I can't find the step to refuse. (1) Consider the theories that were, at some past time, our best-confirmed accounts of the unobservable world: phlogiston chemistry, caloric thermodynamics, the luminiferous ether, Newtonian gravitation. Each was mature, each enjoyed striking empirical successes including novel predictions, and the central unobservable posits of each were eventually discarded as non-referring. (2) Our current best theories are accepted for the same kind of reasons those were — empirical success, explanatory power, novel prediction — so there is no epistemic marker that distinguishes our situation from theirs. (3) Therefore, by straightforward induction, the unobservable posits of our current best theories will probably be discarded too. (4) It is irrational to believe what you expect will be discarded as false, so we should not believe what current theories say about unobservables. (5) But the historical record in (1) is not special to the past: the same reasoning will be available to every future generation, so no theory at any time could ever warrant belief in its unobservables. (6) And here is the twist: the evidence for (1) — our knowledge of what past theories posited and predicted — itself comes partly through instruments and measurement theories, so if (4) and (5) are right, we are not entitled to (1) either, and the argument consumes its own foundation. She concludes realism is a position no one can rationally hold or rationally reject. That proves too much for me; I would rather keep trust in current physics than keep the inductive form of (3) if forced, but I want to know exactly where the chain breaks — which step you reject, why it looked fine, and what the standard exits (restricting the inductive base, selective retention of working posits, structural realism) each have to give up.
