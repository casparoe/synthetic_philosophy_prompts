I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of science with a unit on mechanisms, and our discussion section keeps getting stuck on a deceptively simple question: when a monitoring system (or a scientist) calls something an anomaly, it's implicitly judging that the event didn't arise from "the same mechanism" as the normal cases -- but nobody in my section could say what makes two token processes instances of the same mechanism rather than different ones. The readings give us several plausible criteria -- same variables and same causal-graph structure, same component entities and activities at a fixed level of description, same invariance profile under interventions, sameness of the underlying physical interactions -- but they were presented as friendly elaborations of one idea, and I suspect they can actually come apart.

So here is my request: construct a thought experiment, set in something mundane like a water treatment plant, a hospital pharmacy, or a commercial bakery (no fictional physics, no demons, no idealized super-computers), in which two failures occur that at least three of those criteria sort differently -- e.g., the causal-graph criterion counts them as the same mechanism while the entities-and-activities criterion counts them as different, and the intervention-invariance criterion is either split or delivers a third verdict. Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph, realistic enough that a plant manager would find it familiar; the divergence must be generated by the details of the case, not stipulated ("suppose the criteria disagree" is cheating); and the case should not depend on either failure being weird or exotic -- both should be the kind of thing that actually happens.

After the scenario, walk through each criterion's verdict and why it lands there, then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work -- what minimal change to the scenario would make all the criteria agree again. Also sketch how a defender of each criterion would argue that the case is not really a counterexample to their criterion but rather a misapplication of it. Finally, I'd like you to be explicit about the state of play in the literature: which parts of this (e.g., that mechanisms can be described at multiple grains, or that there is no single settled criterion of mechanism identity) are broadly agreed among philosophers who work on mechanisms, and which parts remain genuinely contested -- please don't present any one criterion as the consensus view if there isn't one.
