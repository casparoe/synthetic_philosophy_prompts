I run a rationalist reading group that meets monthly -- about ten regulars, mostly software people, all fluent in expected value, scope insensitivity, and the usual decision-theory vocabulary. We've been circling the ethics of imposing risks on people without their consent: driving, gain-of-function research, shipping an AI system a few percent faster. What I've noticed is that the group's EV-native instincts actually flatten the topic. If a 0.01% chance of killing a stranger is just "0.0001 expected deaths," then consent, thresholds, and the ex post victim all look like rounding errors, and the discussion dies in twenty minutes because everyone agrees to shut up and multiply.

I want a 90-minute session built around a game or simulation that makes them feel the structure of the problem before anyone names it -- no lecture up front, no terminology until the debrief. Specifically, I want participants to run into three things themselves: first, that each individual risk imposition can be genuinely negligible while the aggregate everyone imposes on everyone is not (a harmless-torturers dynamic, though I don't want it called that during play); second, that there's a felt difference between being harmed and being subjected to a risk that doesn't materialize, and that this difference is harder to cash out than it first seems; third, that adding a consent requirement isn't a clean fix -- vetoes get gamed, holdouts extract rents, and the person who'd consent ex ante to the whole scheme still feels wronged when they're the one who draws the short straw.

Concretely, I'm asking for: rules that fit on one page; a payoff scheme using points or candy, no money; a phase structure with a timeline, including at least one mid-game rule change that introduces consent or compensation; and ideally one asymmetric role -- a player whose project pays out much more than everyone else's and imposes correspondingly more risk, the AI-lab-shaped chair at the table. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the distinctions I want them to leave with (harm vs. risk exposure, ex ante agreement vs. ex post grievance, per-act permissibility vs. aggregate effects), and that pushes on the group's EV instincts rather than endorsing them.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong with this particular crowd -- I suspect probability neglect in both directions and someone trying to collude -- and be honest about what the format can't reproduce: the cases I care about most involve people who aren't in the room and can't be consulted at all, like future generations, and every player in a game can at least in principle be asked.
