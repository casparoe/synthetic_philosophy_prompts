So I write novels for a living, and the book I'm working on has a character who makes a kind of crazy pre-commitment -- think burning the boats so she can't back out of something. At dinner last week a friend of mine who did a couple years of grad school in philosophy told me I'd basically reinvented this thing called Kavka's toxin puzzle, and he explained it like this:

"A rich eccentric offers you a million dollars if you can form the sincere intention, by midnight tonight, to drink a toxin tomorrow that'll make you miserably ill for a day or so. You don't actually have to drink it -- you get the money the moment a brain scanner confirms the intention is genuine, and after that you're free to do whatever. Kavka's point was that a rational person simply can't form the intention, because you know in advance that come tomorrow morning you'll already have the money and drinking would be pointless, so you already know you'll back out, and you can't sincerely intend to do something you know you won't do. His conclusion was basically that rationality is self-defeating here: the rational agent loses the money, and the only winning move is to somehow make yourself irrational."

My friend then said this proves that commitment devices like my character's only work if the person is fundamentally irrational about something, and that no purely rational person could ever bind their future self, which he thought was kind of devastating for my book's whole premise.

Here's the thing though -- my friend is smart but he was also on his third beer, and something about the way he jumped from "you might not drink it" to "rationality is self-defeating" felt too neat. Can you audit his summary for me? Which parts would Kavka himself have signed off on, which parts are him editorializing or overstating, and is there anything he's gotten subtly wrong about what the puzzle is even supposed to show? And seperately: does his conclusion about rational people being unable to bind their future selves actually follow, or did he skip a step? Because if he's right I need to rethink my third act, and if he's wrong I'd love to know exactly where before I let this ruin a chapter I've already written.
