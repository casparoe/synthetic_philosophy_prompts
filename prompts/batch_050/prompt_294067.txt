I run a small philosophy reading group, and a few weeks ago we spent a session on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility -- roughly, the line of reasoning that since the way you are is not ultimately up to you, and since what you do flows from the way you are, you cannot be ultimately responsible for what you do, on pain of having to be somehow self-caused. What struck me was not the argument itself but the shape the discussion took. One member treats it as close to a proof: short, nearly undeniable premises, and a conclusion people resist only because it is uncomfortable. Another member treats the very same argument as a reductio -- she says that if your premises rule out responsibility that cleanly, that simply shows you have built an impossible standard of responsibility into the premises, and no one was ever committed to that standard in the first place. What fascinates me is that both of them fully understand the argument, neither disputes any step of the reasoning, and after an hour they had each simply restated their opening reaction with more confidence.

My working hypothesis is that the whole debate is doing less work than it appears to. Specifically, I suspect the two sides agree on everything Strawson's argument actually establishes -- that a certain very demanding notion of ultimate, buck-stopping responsibility is incoherent -- and disagree only about whether that notion was ever the one at stake in ordinary moral practice, blame, and desert. On this reading, the impasse is not really about the argument at all but about a prior, largely unargued question: what ordinary responsibility-talk commits us to, and who gets to say. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, because the 'it's just a reductio' response also seems to conceal a substantive bet about which intuitions are load-bearing -- the intuition that responsibility requires ultimacy versus the intuition that we obviously are responsible -- and maybe those bets are the real disagreement and could in principle be argued about.

So: diagnose this for me. Is the Basic Argument debate a substantive disagreement, a verbal one trading on different senses of responsibility, or two parties answering different questions -- one about what ultimate responsibility would require, one about what our actual practices presuppose -- under a single heading? Please engage with what each side characteristically says rather than surveying compatibilism and incompatibilism in general, tell me whether my hypothesis above is right or whether the disagreement lives somewhere deeper, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a piece of empirical work on people's responsibility judgments, a conceptual result, or an argument that this is bedrock and no progress should be expected.
