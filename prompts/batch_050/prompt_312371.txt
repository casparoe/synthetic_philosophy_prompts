Pretend you're a moral philosopher who knows the effective altruism / longtermism literature well but isn't a cheerleader for either side. Think this through carefully, and brainstorm the candidate commitments first before sorting them.

Here's the practice I want the implicit philosophy of. Longtermist grantmakers (Open Philanthropy-style) routinely fund things like AI safety research and biosecurity over interventions with well-measured near-term benefits -- even when the near-term option's cost per life saved is solid and measurable, and the longtermist option's case rests on shifting a tiny probability of an astronomically bad far-future outcome by an amount nobody can actually measure. What does that pattern of grantmaking presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: about the value of future people (no pure time discount?), about whether minuscule probability shifts on enormous stakes should dominate large measurable medium-stakes benefits (fanaticism?), about whether expected value reasoning stays action-guiding under deep uncertainty and not just ordinary risk, and about whether "cluelessness" -- the fact that we can't trace the far-future effects of our actions at all -- undermines the whole project or is just a complication.

For each commitment: does the practice strictly need it, or does it merely fit well with it (i.e., could a weaker or different view license the same allocation)? And concretely, what would the grantmaking have to look like if you dropped that one commitment?

Also be honest about what's basically settled vs. genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this. My sense is the moral status of futrue people is mostly not contested but the expected-value-under-cluelessness stuff is a real fight -- correct me if that's wrong, and don't present any camp's minority position as the consensus view.
