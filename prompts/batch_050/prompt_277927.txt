I am drafting a political thriller in which three characters — an intelligence officer, a human-rights lawyer, and a retired judge — must argue, at length and in good faith, about whether to torture a detainee believed to know the location of a concealed explosive. For the scene to work, each character must occupy a genuinely distinct position rather than a caricature of one. Please build me a decision tree that sorts the positions on the ethics of torture in extremis, constructed from the logical space rather than from a roster of famous names: each branch should be labeled by a concrete claim or case on which the positions actually diverge — for instance, whether the wrong of torture admits of a threshold under catastrophic stakes; whether legal prohibition might rightly license extralegal action followed by submission to punishment, rather than forbid the act outright; whether the ticking-bomb scenario's stipulated certainties could ever obtain in practice; and whether what may be done to a culpable captive differs from what may be done to his innocent child to leverage him. Phrase every diagnostic so that a reader can answer it without knowing any labels, use six to eight questions, and give each leaf a name, an indication of which of my three characters could plausibly hold it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark any unoccupied combinations and say briefly whether they stand empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any positions your tree cannot separate.
