I direct the review process at a medical diagnostics firm. Several times a year I convene panels of five to nine staff — engineers, clinicians, statisticians — to make go/no-go calls: whether a model is ready for clinical validation, whether to adopt a supplier's component, whether an anomaly warrants a recall review. I've been reading about the Condorcet jury theorem and want it turned into a written decision policy I can actually run: for a given question, should I take a majority vote, defer to the strongest individual, restructure the process before any vote, or decline to convene the panel at all?

The procedure should be usable by a manager who has never heard the theorem's name. It should give the conditions that must hold before majority voting is trustworthy (better-than-chance individual competence, independent judgments, sincere rather than strategic votes, a well-posed binary question), state what evidence would count as each condition being met or violated in a workplace setting, and specify what to do when a condition fails only partially — for instance, when panelists were all trained on the same materials, when they have all read the same test report before voting, or when the most senior person habitually speaks first and I have noticed the later votes cluster around her view. It should also say how to handle a panel where one member is a recognized expert and the rest are competent generalists, and a decision with more than two options on the table.

Include the steps in order, the stopping rules, and the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to my judgment because the question is about values or stakes rather than accuracy. Then run it on the three cases above — the shared test report, the one-expert panel, and the four-option supplier choice — including at least one where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do even when run correctly.
