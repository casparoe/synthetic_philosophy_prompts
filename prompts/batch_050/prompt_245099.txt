I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the legitimacy of direct-democratic decisions, and I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind my verdicts on cases. Here they are.

The Swiss minaret initiative of 2009 followed the rules impeccably -- correct number of signatures, proper wording, orderly vote -- and yet I think the outcome was illegitimate, because whether a religious minority may build its houses of worship is not the kind of question a majority should get to decide at all. The Brexit referendum of 2016 I regard as fully binding despite the 52-48 margin, because the rule was set in advance and everyone campaigned under it; complaining about the threshold afterward is moving the goalposts. Yet I also think Switzerland's requirement of a double majority -- of the people and of the cantons -- for constitutional amendments is a genuinely good design, and that a country would be wise to require more than 50 percent plus one for changes that are deep and hard to reverse. California-style initiatives drafted and bankrolled by a single industry, with signature-gatherers paid per name and a ballot summary written to mislead, strike me as producing outcomes with a real procedural taint even if every formal requirement was met. A non-binding referendum that comes out 60-40 and is then quietly ignored by the parliament that called it strikes me as a genuine wrong done to the voters, even though nothing was legally promised. A ballot measure that simultaneously cuts a tax and protects the spending it funds strikes me as one whose passage should not count as a mandate for anything, because you cannot obey a contradiction. A requirement that tax increases need two-thirds while tax cuts need a simple majority strikes me as unfair asymmetry rigged in one direction. And a neighborhood-level vote on whether to site a homeless shelter there, where only immediate neighbors vote and the rest of the city does not, strikes me as illegitimate even if the neighbors vote unanimously, because the people who would bear the benefits of the shelter never got a say.

The pair I keep worrying at is the first and the fifth: if the reason the minaret vote was illegitimate is that majorities cannot settle certain questions, then parliament ignoring the advisory vote looks like the same move -- treating the popular verdict as less than decisive when one dislikes it -- and I cannot easily say why one is principled and the other is a betrayal. And the second and third pull against each other too: if hard-to-reverse changes should require supermajorities, why was it right to treat the 52 percent as binding?

Is there a principle -- or a small set of principles -- that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates? Please say what the resulting account implies for cases I have not considered: a recall election of a judge after an unpopular but legally correct ruling; a referendum that passes on 15 percent turnout; a citizen initiative to lower the voting age, where the electorate is voting on the composition of the electorate itself. And if no reasonably simple principle saves all of my verdicts, tell me honestly which ones it forces me to drop, and which you would drop.
