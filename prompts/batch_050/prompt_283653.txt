You are an experienced instructor of classical Chinese philosophy who has taught early Mohist and Confucian ethics to undergraduates for many years; think carefully about the dependency structure of these ideas before answering. I am a TA in an introductory comparative ethics course, and next week I lead a fifty-minute discussion section on the dispute between Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai) and Confucian graded love for about twenty first-year students who have read only short excerpts: a portion of the Mozi chapters on impartial care and a handful of passages from the Analects and Mencius. I do not want you to explain the debate to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single most important structural decision the plan turns on — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.

Here is what the plan must contain. First, the order of ideas and why: my current guess is that the Confucian picture of the self as constituted by roles (son, minister, friend) has to be in place before graded love is intelligible as a thesis rather than a description, and that graded love has to be secure before impartial care can be posed as a challenge to it rather than a strange proposal — but tell me if I have the dependency backwards, and where the Mohist consequentialist justification for impartial care (it produces order and mutual benefit) belongs relative to the statement of the doctrine itself. Second, the one example that should carry the whole session: it must be a case where the two positions genuinely diverge, so explain why Mencius's child about to fall into a well cannot do this work — both sides save the child, and the example risks making the disagreement look smaller than it is — and assess my candidate, a famine-relief case with grain enough for one village, your own or a stranger's, or propose something better and say what the carrying example must have and must lack. Third, the misconception map: I expect at least three — that impartial care means treating a stranger's parent identically to your own in every respect, that Confucian graded love is dressed-up nepotism with no principled stopping rule, and that Mozi is simply an early utilitarian so the whole debate collapses into consequentialism versus role ethics — and for each one, say at what point in my sequence it will predictably form, exactly where I should head it off, and what to say there rather than letting it harden and correcting it later. Fourth, one check-for-understanding question for the final five minutes that would reveal whether students grasp that the dispute is about the structure and justification of moral concern, not merely about how much to care about strangers. Fifth, what to deliberately leave out at this level — the later Mohist dialectical defenses, the Mencian attack on Mohism, the translation controversy around jian'ai — with a one-line justification for each exclusion.
