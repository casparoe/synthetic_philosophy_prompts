I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course in social and cultural psychology, and I also serve as the vice-president of my university's largest student society, which runs cultural events and a weekly language exchange. We have about two hundred members from a wide range of backgrounds, and this year I rewrote our constitution to include fairly detailed rules: firm deadlines for event proposals, attendance requirements for committee members, a formal procedure for approving expenditures, and penalties for repeated lateness to shifts members signed up for. My reasoning was that past chaos came from everything being negotiable. But the new system has produced a rift. Several members, mostly those who grew up here, think the rules are still too loose and want stricter enforcement; a number of international members have told me they find the atmosphere policing and joyless, that small infractions like arriving ten minutes late to a volunteer shift are being treated as moral failings, and that people are leaving because of it. One of them said something that stuck with me: that in her home country, rules like ours would be understood as guidelines to be interpreted with judgment, and that treating them as absolute says something bad about how we see each other.

My instinct is that both sides are partly right, but I can't articulate the principle that would tell me when strictness is justified and when it becomes oppressive. The course has me thinking about how groups calibrate the strength of their norms, but I want to apply it to an actual decision: our annual general meeting is in three weeks, and I have to propose either keeping the constitution as is, loosening it, or making enforcement stricter. So my question is: what should determine how much rule-following a group like ours demands? Does the right level of strictness depend on the stakes of the activities (a casual social club versus, say, handling money or safety), on how much members depend on each other's reliability, on the diversity of the membership, or on something else entirely? And is there a coherent middle position -- strict about the rules that protect coordination and fairness, relaxed about the rest -- or does that distinction collapse in practice, because members can't tell which rules are the serious ones?

I'd also appreciate advice on the harder question underneath: when a member from a background with different norms about punctuality and procedure breaks one of our rules, is strict enforcement a neutral application of standards everyone agreed to, or is it smuggling in one culture's assumptions under the guise of fairness? I don't want to run the society in a way that quietly excludes people, but I also don't want a group where nothing can be relied on. Please give me concrete guidance for what to propose at the meeting, and the reasoning behind it.
