So the Fermi paradox has been chewed over for seventy-plus years and as far as I can tell the state of play in 2025 is basically the state of play in 1990, just with more exoplanets. Everyone has their favorite solution (rare earth, great filter ahead of us, great filter behind us, they're hiding, we're early, we're boring, zoo hypothesis, etc.) and the arguments seem to go in circles.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want you to stress-test it rather than just agree with it: the debate is stalled because it isn't actually one disagreement but at least two wearing the same clothes. One is empirical -- what are the actual values of the Drake equation terms -- and that part is slowly getting less embarrassing as we get real data on planet frequencies and maybe eventually biosignatures. The other is methodological, maybe even temperamental: how do you reason responsibly from a sample of one, and what does "absence of evidence" entitle you to conclude when the observation protocol is as pathetic as ours is? People who think the silence is deeply informative and people who think we've barely looked are not disagreeing about any number either of them can cite -- they're disagreeing about what kind of inference is legitimate here, and that kind of disagreement doesn't dissolve when new data comes in, it just gets re-dressed in the new data.

So: is that the right diagnosis, or is there a real empirical core to the dispute that I'm dismissing? What kind of disagreement is each of the main standoffs (e.g., "the Great Filter is probably behind us" vs "it's probably ahead of us") actually made of -- prior choice, anthropic reasoning, disputed biology? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would genuinely count as progress: a specific observation, a theorem about self-locating belief, a conceptual cleanup of what would even count as detecting someone? Or is the honest answer that nothing moves this debate except the one observation nobody knows how to schedule?
