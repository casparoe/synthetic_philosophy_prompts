At a dinner party last week, two friends of mine got into a long argument about aesthetic judgment that they eventually asked me to referee, and I'd like a proper adjudication. Some context: another guest, who has not read it, described a certain much-praised novel as "clearly one of the best novels of the decade," citing a critic whose taste she trusts and who had written a detailed review explaining the book's virtues. Here is the exchange, roughly as it went:

(1) Dan objected that she wasn't entitled to the claim at all: whatever else testimony can do, it cannot ground aesthetic judgment, because judging a work requires experiencing it firsthand. Priya (who was defending the guest, not the guest herself) replied that this is special pleading. Testimony transmits knowledge in nearly every domain — she knows the bridge she drove over is safe without being an engineer — and the burden is on Dan to explain why aesthetics should be quarantined.

(2) Dan conceded the engineer case but distinguished two things: knowing that a work is good, and appreciating or understanding it. Testimony might deliver the former but cannot deliver the latter, and the latter is what actually matters in aesthetics — a person whose entire aesthetic life consisted of true secondhand beliefs would be missing the point of art entirely.

(3) Priya answered that the retreat from knowledge to appreciation concedes most of what she wanted: the guest claimed only that the novel is excellent, not that she appreciates it. She added that this wasn't bare deference anyway — the review explained why the novel is good, describing its structure, pacing, and prose — and testimony that transmits reasons transmits more than testimony that merely transmits a verdict.

(4) Dan replied that reason-laden testimony changes less than Priya thinks. Descriptions like "tightly paced" or "lyrical but controlled" are generic: two novels could satisfy exactly the same description, one a masterpiece and one kitsch, and whether the described features are actually pulled off is precisely what only reading the book reveals. So the reasons don't travel through testimony; only the verdict does, and the verdict unaccompanied by the reasons is exactly what pessimists about aesthetic testimony find wanting.

(5) Priya said this proves too much. Moral judgment and even ordinary empirical judgment often depend on how considerations are realized in detail, yet we accept moral and empirical testimony all the time; and on Dan's principle, most art-historical scholarship would be ungrounded, since scholars routinely assess works they cannot experience — lost plays known only from reports, performances no living person attended.

(6) Dan bit both bullets: secondhand moral conviction is indeed missing something even when correct, and scholarship about lost works is genuinely provisional in a way firsthand judgment is not. He said the fact that these implications are surprising shows how much we lean on testimony, not that leaning on it is legitimate.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, partially sound, or mistaken, and justify the verdict. Then assess who is more right overall, and — just as important — identify anything both parties are missing. In particular, I'd like you to consider whether the disagreement looks different once you separate the epistemic question (can testimony justify aesthetic belief?) from the normative question (is there something improper or second-rate about resting an aesthetic judgment on testimony even when it does), and whether Dan's concession in (2) was larger or smaller than it appeared.
