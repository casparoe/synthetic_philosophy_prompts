I'm a high school senior, and I run the fact-check desk of our school newspaper. Roughly twice a month, a submitted opinion piece leans on some old long-run prediction that didn't come true on schedule -- Malthus on food supply and population, the late-twentieth-century warnings about mass famine or resource exhaustion, predictions about oil running out -- and the writer draws one of two conclusions: either "the theory was wrong, stop listening to people like this" or "the theory was right, just early, so the warning still stands." I have to rule on whether the reasoning is acceptable within about a day, with no access to outside experts, and my ruling goes to the editor-in-chief, who signs off.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure my desk can run on any claim of this kind, grounded in whatever the philosophy of science actually has to say about falsification, auxiliary assumptions, and ad hoc rescue -- but translated into questions a student with no philosophy background can ask in order. A framing I've found useful, which I'd like the procedure to build on: split any long-run prediction into its mechanism (the causal story about why the thing should happen) and its schedule (the claim about when, or under what conditions). My suspicion is that "right but early" is sometimes a legitimate defense of the mechanism and sometimes a way of making the theory unfalsifiable, and I want a procedure that tells these apart rather than relying on my gut.

Concretely, please give me:

1. The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each -- including at least: Did the original prediction actually carry a date or condition, or has one been attached after the fact? When the prediction failed, did the theory's defenders explain the failure using resources already in the theory, or did they bolt on something new whose only job is to absorb the failure? If the prediction later "came true," did it come true for the mechanism's reasons or for an unrelated reason? Would any possible outcome have counted against the theory as currently stated?
2. The points where the procedure stops with a definite ruling, and the points where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment, not checklist -- please be honest about where those are, rather than disguising judgment calls as steps.
3. Three worked applications: Malthus's population-and-food-supply prediction; a resource-scarcity prediction of the kind made famous by the wager between an ecologist and an economist over commodity prices; and one case of your choosing where the procedure delivers a verdict that a typical student environmentalist (like me) won't like -- flag that case explicitly.
4. A short statement of what the procedure cannot do: which disputes about old predictions it will never settle, and what I'd need beyond a checklist to settle them.

For each worked application, end with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: falsified, Verdict: right-but-early upheld, or Verdict: undecidable as stated. Then end the whole response with a final machine-readable line, \answer{...}, containing the number and one-sentence wording of the single question in your procedure that does the most work in separating legitimate defense from unfalsifiable retreat.
