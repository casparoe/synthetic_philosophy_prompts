Aumann's agreement theorem is usually treated as a curiosity: a result showing that rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot disagree, followed by a long, inconclusive debate about why actual people disagree constantly anyway. Suppose a small research institute wants to fund a two-year conceptual research program on this theorem and its aftermath -- not experimental psychology, but the philosophy, epistemology, and formal modeling around it. They can support three lines of inquiry, no more.

Which open questions should they fund, and in what order? I'd like a defended agenda rather than a survey. Candidate questions you might consider (but feel free to argue for ones I'm not listing):

- Whether the common-prior assumption is doing all the work, and what the theorem is even *about* if rational agents need not share priors;
- Whether common knowledge of posteriors is an idealization that severs the theorem from any real case of disagreement, or whether weaker conditions (iterated mutual knowledge, high-order approximate knowledge) preserve enough of the result to bite;
- How the theorem relates to the peer disagreement literature -- whether it underwrites conciliationism, presupposes it, or is largely orthogonal to it;
- What to make of the gap between the theorem and persistent expert disagreement (in economics, medicine, climate science, philosophy itself): is this evidence that the idealizations fail, that people are irrational, or that the theorem's antecedents are simply never met?
- Whether results about how cheaply agents could actually reach approximate agreement (bounded communication, computationally bounded reasoners) change the philosophical significance of the theorem, and whether philosophers have adequately absorbed them;
- What, if anything, the theorem implies for the design of deliberative institutions or for one's own practice when disagreeing with someone whose judgment one respects.

For each line of inquiry you include: explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others (which answers constrain which), how tractable it is for philosophers and formal modelers rather than experimentalists, and what would count as progress within two years. Also say which candidate question you would leave unfunded and defend the exclusion -- in particular, whether the "why do people actually disagree?" question deserves funding at all, or whether it collapses into empirical psychology and is therefore out of scope. Finally, identify the single assumption in the standard framing of the theorem whose abandonment would most change the entire agenda, and say how the rankings would shift if it were dropped.
