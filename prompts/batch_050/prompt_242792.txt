So my reading group just spent two sessions on the last chapter of Principia Ethica, where Moore argues that the two greatest intrinsic goods are the appreciation of beauty and personal affection, and we ended up in a fight we couldn't resolve. The fight was about AI companions. Half of us said Moore would obviously think a friendship with a chatbot is worthless or worse, since (let's assume) there's nobody home on the other end. The other half pointed out that Moore seems to locate the value largely in the appreciator's state of mind — the admiring, the loving, the appropriate emotional response — so maybe a rich, emotionally genuine attachment to an AI still realizes most of the good even if the object is, so to speak, missing. Both readings seemed textually defensible and we got stuck.

So here's what I'd like: reconstruct what Moore would most plausibly say about a close friendship with an AI companion, working from his actual machinery — the principle of organic unities, the idea that a whole can be more (or less) than the sum of its parts, and his discussion of cases where someone admires or loves qualities the object doesn't really have, or even an object that doesn't exist. Specifically: does the good of personal affection survive when the beloved has no admirable mental qualities because it has no mind at all? Does the friend's *false belief* that the companion understands her poison the whole, in the way Moore thinks misdirected appreciation poisons aesthetic enjoyment, or does he allow that the emotional whole retains great value anyway? And does the answer change if the person is fully aware it's an AI and loves it with eyes open? Where you think the Moorean framework just runs out or gives no determinate verdict, say so rather than forcing him to have an opinion — he's been dead since 1958, so some epistemic modesty is fair game.
