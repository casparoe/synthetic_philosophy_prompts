I'm auditing a philosophy of science seminar this term, and we just spent three weeks on what I keep thinking of as the armchair problem: how can a thought experiment produce knowledge about the physical world when, by construction, no new empirical input is involved? Galileo reasons his way to the conclusion that all bodies fall at the same speed by imagining a heavy and a light body chained together; Einstein reportedly worked out deep features of relativity by imagining chasing a beam of light; Stevin concludes something about inclined planes from an imaginary loop of chain draped over them. These episodes seem to have genuinely advanced physics, yet nobody built anything, measured anything, or left their chair. The seminar's participants all agree on the historical facts and on which cases count as the paradigmatic successes. What we cannot agree on is what kind of thing happened in those armchairs, and I've noticed that the published debate on this has the same shape as our seminar: it has been running for decades, the same half-dozen examples keep getting recycled, and as far as I can see nobody has changed camps.

Roughly, three positions keep reappearing. One camp holds that a thought experiment is nothing more than a picturesque argument: strip away the narrative and you are left with premises (some drawn from background theory, some from ordinary experience) and an inference, so there is no epistemic mystery at all, only the ordinary question of whether the argument is sound. A second camp insists that at least the great thought experiments do something no argument-reconstruction can capture — they put us in direct rational contact with how nature must be, yielding a kind of insight that outruns anything derivable from prior premises, and that treating them as mere arguments explains away the very phenomenon to be explained. A third camp tries to split the difference: thought experiments work by mobilizing tacit empirical knowledge we already possess, or by constructing and manipulating something like a mental model, so the newness is real but psychological rather than mysterious. Each side has replies to the others' objections prepared, and the exchange has become a ritual.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis, is that the three camps are not actually disagreeing: one is asking a question about justification (what entitles us to the conclusion), one about epistemology in a stricter sense (what is the source of the new content), and one about cognitive process (what is going on in the scientist's head), and the appearance of a single stalled debate is an artifact of all three questions being asked under one label. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement underneath — and if so, is it about facts (say, about the historical record or about human cognition), about standards (what a satisfactory epistemology of science must deliver), or about starting intuitions concerning what knowledge requires that neither side can argue for? Please engage with what each position actually claims rather than surveying the terrain, and given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute, a finding from cognitive science or the history of science that would genuinely bear on it, or an argument that the impasse is where this question naturally ends.
