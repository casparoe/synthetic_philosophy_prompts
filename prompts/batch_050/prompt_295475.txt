Next month I'm presenting at a two-day workshop on longtermism and existential risk, and I want to be properly prepared for the question period rather than ambushed by it. My talk defends the claim that the simulation argument should dampen our fanaticism about the far future. The rough skeleton of my argument: the case for prioritizing the far future derives most of its force from the sheer number of beings who could exist if our civilization survives and expands; but if we are living in a simulation, the resources available to our civilization are plausibly capped by whatever compute budget our simulators have allocated, so the astronomically large futures that longtermist arguments trade on are largely off the table; and given the standard three-way disjunction (nearly all civilizations go extinct first, nearly all lose interest in running ancestor simulations, or we're almost certainly in one), the probability that we're simulated is high enough that this ceiling should substantially shrink the expected value of far-future-oriented interventions. My conclusion is not that simulated lives matter less -- I explicitly concede substrate independence on that point -- but that the ceiling on numbers means we should be much less willing to trade away solid near-term goods for tiny probabilities of vast far-future payoffs.

The audience is mixed, which is what worries me. There will be philosophers working on population ethics and decision theory under deep uncertainty, two or three people who are sympathetic to Bostrom-style reasoning and will know the trilemma and the self-sampling assumptions better than I do, a few skeptics of longtermism who I suspect will attack me from the opposite direction (arguing that my reasoning proves too much and undermines practical reasoning generally, since the same logic could be used to shrug off any low-probability high-stakes consideration), and at least one person with a physics background who I expect will go straight for the compute-cap premise. Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this particular audience, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that kind of person would actually phrase it. For each, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes.
