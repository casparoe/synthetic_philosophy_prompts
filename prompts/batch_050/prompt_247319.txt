I am a PhD student in Islamic philosophy preparing a short essay for a workshop on kalām and metaethics. I want to use a passage from Plato’s Euthyphro that is not the usual Euthyphro dilemma but still captures the pressure that moral demands exert against authority and kinship: “The real question is whether the murdered man has been justly slain. If justly, then your duty is to let the matter alone; but if unjustly, then even if the murderer lives under the same roof with you and eats at the same table, proceed against him.” My working idea is that this passage dramatizes a conflict between moral reasons that bind independently of social position and commands whose force depends on the status of the commander, and that this conflict can be used to illuminate the Mu‘tazilite–Ash‘arite dispute without reducing it to a simple Islamic replay of Plato.

Here is the draft essay: Socrates’ exchange with Euthyphro is often compressed into the question of whether the pious is loved because it is pious, but the passage above reveals another dimension: Euthyphro treats justice as a reason-giving fact capable of overriding household loyalty, reputation, and even paternal authority. My essay argues that the Mu‘tazila generalize this stance into a theology: God necessarily commands what is just because justice is intelligible independently of command, and rational agents can know at least some of it prior to revelation. The Ash‘arite objection is not merely that this subjects God to an external law; it is that injustice is conceptually tied to wronging another by usurping what is theirs, whereas nothing lies outside God’s dominion, so God cannot wrong creatures in the way creatures wrong one another. I contend that this reply avoids crude arbitrariness but creates a subtler problem: if divine justice is secured by total ownership, then justice no longer supplies an independent reason for command, and the believer’s moral vocabulary risks becoming honorific rather than explanatory. Yet the Mu‘tazilite alternative faces its own version of the difficulty, since saying that God cannot command injustice may either place a standard above God or merely redescribe God’s necessarily just nature. The essay concludes that the debate is best framed not as objectivity versus voluntarism but as a dispute over where necessity is located: in moral truths reason discovers, in God’s nature, or in the relation between creator and owned creation.

I would also appreciate concrete suggestions for restructuring the essay, cutting redundancies, sharpening the thesis, and choosing a more precise title. Please do not supply citations or a reading list; I am looking for philosophical criticism of the argument as drafted.
