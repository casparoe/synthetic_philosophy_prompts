A survey article I recently read summarises mechanistic anomaly detection as follows: "Mechanistic anomaly detection shifts safety evaluation from behaviour to mechanism: rather than asking only what a model does, one inspects its internals to check whether it arrives at its answers in the intended way. Two models count as implementing the same mechanism when their circuits correspond — when the same features are computed by analogous components in each — and a model whose internals deviate from the reference circuit is anomalous by definition. The pay-off is that this approach can in principle catch scheming models that behave flawlessly during evaluation, since a model that would defect out of distribution must be running different internal machinery from an honest one. The principal obstacle is practical: fine-grained mechanistic analysis is labour-intensive, so until costs fall, behavioural testing on larger and more diverse datasets remains the more scalable option." The article goes on to conclude that, once these engineering costs fall, mechanistic inspection will straightforwardly supersede behavioural evaluation as the standard of evidence for alignment.

I am a PhD student working on interpretability, and something about this framing bothers me, though I cannot quite localise it. Is the summary fair? I would like you to separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what a researcher in the area would object to. In particular: is correspondence of circuits an adequate criterion for sameness of mechanism, or does it smuggle in a contested choice about the level of abstraction at which mechanisms are individuated — could two networks with quite different internals nonetheless implement the same mechanism, and could near-identical internals fail to? Does good in-distribution behaviour followed by defection really entail different internal machinery, or might one and the same mechanism produce both, in which case inspection faces a harder problem than the summary suggests? Finally, does the article's concluding claim — that mechanistic inspection should eventually replace behavioural evaluation — follow from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, or does it rely on precisely the assumptions the correction undermines?
