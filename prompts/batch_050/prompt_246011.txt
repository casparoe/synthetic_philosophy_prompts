So I'm teaching a grad seminar on moral responsibility next term and I keep making the same mistake: we read Strawson, everyone nods along to the reactive attitudes stuff, and then every theory after that gets judged by vibes -- students like whichever one handles their favorite case. Before we read anything this year I want the spec first.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of moral responsibility has to deliver if it takes Strawson's starting point seriously -- that holding responsible just is (or is grounded in) the web of reactive attitudes like resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, forgiveness, rather than something those attitudes merely track. The purpose I'll hold the list to: a theory good enough to tell us when it's actually fitting to keep resenting someone versus when we're wrong to, including in the messy cases where everyone involved disagrees.

The list needs, for each desideratum, the case or consideration behind it, not just the label. Things I suspect belong on it, though push back if some are really just camp prejudices:

- it has to make sense of Strawson's two ways we let people off -- "he didn't mean to, he tripped" type excuses versus "he's six, he's schizophrenic, he's not really one of us" type exemptions -- and explain why those feel different, not just that they both cancel blame
- it has to handle self-directed attitudes: why guilt and shame aren't just resentment pointed inward, or why maybe they are
- it has to say something about standing: why the hypocrite's blame feels defective even when its content is true
- it has to deal with moral luck (identical choices, different outcomes) without just stipulating one answer
- it has to handle cases where the attitude seems fitting but the target is odd: resentment at your own past self, indignation at a crowd no one member of which did much, anger at the delivery app, at a friend's brain tumor
- it can't just presuppose libertarian free will and call it a day, but it also can't be so compatibilist that it makes the attitudes into mere social tools with no claim to be deserved
- it has to explain what an apology is supposed to change and why forgiveness is sometimes owed and sometimes a gift
- it has to handle proportionality -- when is the anger itself too much, and is that a moral failure or just bad manners

Three extra asks. First, mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which look like preferences smuggled in by one side of the literature. Second, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- my guess is something in the vicinity of "must vindicate our actual practices" and "must tell us when our actual practices are wrong" is in trouble, but work it out properly. Third, tell me which single desideratum knocks out the most actual contenders.

Then briefly, like a page max, score the main contenders against the list: Strawson's own account, the communicative theories that grew out of it, deep-self/attributionist views, and the hard-line skeptic who says dump the whole practice. Keep the scoring short, the requirements are the deliverable. If a contender does well on the list only by quietly dropping a desideratum, say which one it dropped -- thats the part I'll actually use in class.
