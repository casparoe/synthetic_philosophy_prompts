I’m a novelist working on a story about a small town that discovers a “reset machine”: whenever anything sufficiently bad happens, the town goes back to the previous morning, but a handful of people remember what was erased. While trying to work out the mechanics I’ve been rewatching Groundhog Day, and I’d like a philosophical reading of the film through money pumps and diachronic rationality.

Phil can learn from each repeated day while almost everyone else forgets, so his later selves inherit information but not the costs paid by his earlier selves. That seems to create what I’m calling a reset pump: over a sequence of loops, choices that might look individually sensible to the current Phil can collectively push suffering, risk, or humiliation onto temporal versions of himself who are no longer around to object. But maybe that’s not a money pump in the standard sense, since there isn’t necessarily an exploiter, stable preference cycle, or series of trades making him predictably worse off.

Please use Groundhog Day to sort this out. Pay attention to his hedonistic phase, his despair and suicide attempts, and his later self-improvement and care for other people. Does the film portray irrational preference change being repaired, or a rational transformation in what he values? Does his eventual “good” life vindicate the earlier exploitative loops as a kind of costly education, or would that excuse anything? I’m especially interested in whether diachronic rationality should govern a sequence of remembered selves, even when the external world resets, and whether the loop actually strengthens or destroys the ordinary idea of one continuing agent.

Be concrete about major scenes, but don’t quote the script. Also, please be candid about how confident you are in each part of the reading, and single out the claim or interpretation you’re least sure of. I want the ambiguities, not a fake tidyness.
