I am drafting a survey article on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I want to make sure I am not missing a structurally distinct family of solutions. To fix the setup: in the lottery case it seems rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose, yet irrational to believe that all of them will lose; in the preface case an author rationally believes each claim in her carefully researched book while also rationally believing, on grounds of her own fallibility, that the book contains at least one error. The solution space I already have covered, and which I do not want repeated, reworded, or combined, is as follows:

1. Threshold-plus-restriction accounts: outright belief is rational once credence passes some threshold, but lottery beliefs are excluded by an additional condition, such as a sensitivity requirement or a ban on belief grounded in purely statistical evidence.
2. Stability accounts in the style of Leitgeb: rational belief corresponds to credences that remain high under suitable conditionalization, which rules out lottery beliefs while tolerating ordinary fallible ones.
3. Denials of agglomeration: one may rationally believe each conjunct without rationally believing their conjunction.
4. Toleration of inconsistency: a rational set of outright beliefs need not be consistent, so the preface author is rational throughout and the lottery believer may simply hold the inconsistent set.
5. Replacement or eliminativist views: the lesson of both paradoxes is that binary belief talk should be abandoned in favor of graded credences, so the paradoxes rest on a mistaken demand.
6. Contextualist or pragmatic-encroachment moves: what rational belief requires varies with stakes or conversational context, and the appearance of paradox comes from shifting standards mid-argument.
7. Higher-order dissolutions of the preface: the author's belief that her book contains an error is a second-order judgment about her fallibility, not a first-order claim on a par with the book's contents, so there is no genuine inconsistency at a single level.

Give me one further solution that is genuinely different in structure from everything on this list -- not a relabeling of one entry, a special case of one, or a hybrid of two. State it precisely enough that I can tell which element of the standard framing it rejects or revises (closure under conjunction, agglomeration, the link between high credence and rational belief, the requirement of consistency, the assumption that both paradoxes demand a uniform treatment). Say whether it resolves both paradoxes by a single mechanism or treats them separately, and whether you take that to be a virtue or a defect. Then assess it candidly: does the proposal actually work, or does it merely relocate the problem? Finally, state explicitly what would change your verdict -- for example, a particular counterexample or desideratum that, if your solution failed it, would lead you to abandon the proposal -- or, if you cannot name such a test, identify the step in your own reasoning where a mistake is most likely to be hiding.
