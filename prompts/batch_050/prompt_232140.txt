Here's a thought experiment I've been chewing on — it's a riff on Quine's gavagai, but I changed the setup to make the usual quick answers harder.

Mara is a field linguist studying the Ssithra, an aquatic species who perceive the world almost entirely through echolocation; their eyesight is vestigial. Whenever a click returns from a fish, the nearest Ssithra utters "tok." Mara has months of field data and notes the following:

- The Ssithra behave identically toward real returning echoes and toward recordings of echoes Mara plays back to them. They say "tok" either way, and they show no surprise or self-correction when theres no fish behind the recording.
- Every inference they draw from "tok" (where to swim, when to feed, which waters to avoid) is one they could equally well draw from information about the echo-pattern itself. Nothing in their behavior ever requires the fish, as opposed to the echo, to exist.
- They do correct each others "tok"s sometimes, but only ever on acoustic grounds — roughly, "that wasn't a tok-shaped sound" — never on grounds like "turned out there was no fish there."

Candidate translations of "tok": (a) fish, (b) fish-shaped echo / echo-pattern of type F, (c) the region of water currently returning an F-pattern, (d) undetached fish-parts, (e) a pure stimulus-meaning reading like "lo, fishiness."

Here's what I want from you. Take the five standard resources people appeal to for pinning down translation —

1. the principle of charity (translate so that their beliefs mostly come out true),
2. naturalness/eligibility (prefer the translation whose referents carve nature closer to its joints, e.g. fish over fish-echo-fusions),
3. behavioral and ostensive evidence (what they do, pursue, avoid, point at),
4. causal-informational links (the word means whatever standardly causes its utterance),
5. the speakers' own error-practices (what they themselves count as a mistake worth correcting)

— and rank them from the one that removes the most indeterminacy in this specific case to the one that removes the least. Give each placement a sentence or two of justification tied to the details above, not generic textbook talking points.

Then commit to answers: pick the single best translation of "tok" from the five candidates and defend it against the runner-up. Also give me a number from 0 to 100 for how much genuine indeterminacy you think remains in the Ssithra case after all five resources are fully exhausted (100 = still completely indeterminate, 0 = completely pinned down). Don't hedge with "it depends on your theory of meaning" — tell me which theory of meaning the case pushes you toward, and say whether that should worry us about translating ordinary humans, too.
