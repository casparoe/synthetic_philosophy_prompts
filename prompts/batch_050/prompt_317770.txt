In an argument with a colleague about whether a social-media company is responsible for the political effects of its recommendation algorithm, I said the design of the feed is itself a political choice, and she replied with what she treated as a decisive one-liner: technology is neutral -- tools don't have politics, only people do. I've also heard the opposing slogan, artifacts have politics, repeated in philosophy-of-technology contexts as if it were a settled result rather than a claim needing argument. I'm left suspecting that each slogan is doing several different jobs at once and that my colleague and I were probably talking past each other without either of us noticing.

Can you disambiguate both slogans into the distinct precise theses they could be expressing, and give a verdict on each? For "technology is neutral," I'd like you to separate, at minimum: the claim that artifacts have no moral agency or intentions of their own; the claim that an artifact's effects are determined entirely by its users rather than its design; the claim that the same design is value-symmetric across contexts, so it can serve any political end equally well; and any stronger or weaker variants worth distinguishing. For "artifacts have politics," separate the claim that design decisions settle contested questions within a community (who gets access, who bears costs, whose behavior is constrained); the stronger claim that some technologies are inherently political, in that they can only function under particular social arrangements regardless of how they are used; and the claim that artifacts can embody values even when no designer intended them to.

For each thesis on both lists, tell me whether it is close to a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and say what kind of consideration or example would settle the contested ones. Then apply the menus to my argument: which reading of "technology is neutral" would my colleague need for her dismissal to work, is that reading among the plausible ones, and which reading of "artifacts have politics" is the weakest claim on my side that still defeats her dismissal? Finally, say whether there is a reading of each slogan on which both are true at once -- and if so, whether that means our original disagreement about the company's responsibility was really about something else, and what.
