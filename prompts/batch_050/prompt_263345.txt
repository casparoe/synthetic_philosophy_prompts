I've been reading LessWrong and its neighboring blogs for well over a decade, so I'm familiar with the family of decision theories discussed there that recommend choosing a policy rather than an individual act — the ones on which you act as the output of a procedure you'd want all relevantly similar agents to instantiate, and on which, at least in some versions, the right object of choice is a complete mapping from possible observations to actions, selected as if before you knew which observations you'd in fact receive. I'm deliberately not naming those theories, because my question is precisely whether the pattern I'm about to describe belongs to that family or is a corruption of it.

The pattern I keep noticing — in my own reasoning and in forum arguments — runs as follows. Someone adopts a policy that was, ex ante, the best mapping from evidence to action they could find. Later, they receive information of a kind the original policy never priced in, or that would have changed the mapping had it been foreseeable. But instead of treating the new information as an input to the policy, they treat fidelity to the policy as itself the point. Two instances. A friend resolved years ago to honor long-dated bets made at odds favorable relative to his priors at the time of the resolution; he is still paying out on one whose odds have since moved sharply against him, even though the counterparty has offered to release him and no one is watching. He says that reneging would be updating on the bet's prospects after the fact, which is exactly what the commitment was meant to foreclose. My own case: I give to whichever charity an annual evaluation selects, and I kept doing so last year even after the evaluation process changed in ways I now consider unreliable — telling myself that the change should feed into next year's policy felt, in the moment, like precisely the kind of motivated re-derivation that policy-level decision theories exist to prevent.

What strikes me is that the policy-choosing theories, as I understand them, don't actually license this: the mapping takes observations as inputs, so refusing to let genuinely new information function as an input is not fidelity to the policy but something cruder — treating a cached output as binding. Yet it doesn't seem to be the sunk cost fallacy either, since nothing is being thrown after irrecoverable losses; nor ordinary akrasia or status quo bias, since the person often endorses the stickiness on reflection; nor the resolute choice of the literature on dynamic consistency, since there the point is to avoid exploitation across time by one's own future preferences, whereas here the future self's preferences haven't changed — only its information has.

Does this configuration — a principled-seeming refusal to let new evidence enter an adopted policy, justified by appeal to ex ante optimality — have an established name in decision theory or the adjacent informal literature? If it sits between named concepts, which is nearest, and where exactly does the described thing diverge from it? And if nothing quite carves it, could you coin a term, define it, and mark its boundary against genuine policy-fidelity on the one side and plain stubbornness on the other? A bare label won't satisfy me; what I want is the carving.
