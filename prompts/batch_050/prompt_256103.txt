I co-host a philosophy podcast aimed at graduate students, and our upcoming guest is a philosopher who has built much of her career defending the buck-passing, fitting-attitude account of value: to call something good, admirable, or shameful is, on her view, just to say that there is reason of the right kind to favor it, admire it, or be ashamed of it. Her standard answer to the wrong kind of reasons problem — the familiar demon who credibly threatens catastrophe unless you admire him, and who thus seems to give you an excellent reason to admire him without being even slightly admirable — is the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons: the threat bears on the value of your being in the state of admiring him, she says, but supplies no reason for the admiration itself, since nothing about the demon merits it. She has delivered this answer for twenty years and can produce it in her sleep, which is exactly the problem for an interviewer.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, designed so the distinction has to do real work on air rather than merely be recited. The sequence should include: a question on whether the object-given/state-given line is principled or whether "object-given" just gets defined, circularly, as whatever kind of reason supports fittingness; a question built around a case I have devised and plan to spring on her — a lonely, utterly sincere man who will admire you genuinely and permanently, but only if you first admire him, so that your admiration is the sole key to the deepest admiration you will ever receive — where the reason seems to be a fact about him (his sincerity, his dispositions) yet functions like a reward for occupying the attitude, and where I want her pressed on whether the classification tracks the reason's content or its motivational source; a question about a higher-order demon who threatens you unless you *judge that there are* object-given reasons to admire him, asking whether her account then needs fittingness facts about judgments about reasons, and whether a regress of such facts is looming or harmless; and at least one question on cases where an attitude seems fitting but held for the wrong reasons, or unfitting though the reasons look like the right kind — envy at a colleague's fully deserved success, or genuine amusement at a joke that is funny precisely because it is cruel. Close the sequence with a question about what the view is committed to that could turn out to be false — what she would accept as a genuine counterexample rather than a puzzle to be re-described.

Keep the questions pointed but askable aloud by a host who likes her work; no warm-up biographical questions, and nothing she can answer by simply restating the distinction in fresh vocabulary.
