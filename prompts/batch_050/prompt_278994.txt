I cover science for a general-interest magazine and I keep getting pulled into taxonomy fights: lumpers vs. splitters, the red wolf saga, whether polar bears and grizzlies "really" count as seperate species now that they hybridize. Every expert I interview talks as if there's a fact of the matter out there and the only question is whether we have the evidence yet. Then I stumbled on this in Chapter II of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) and it kind of undercuts the whole frame:

Nor shall I here discuss the various definitions which have been given of the term species. No one definition has as yet satisfied all naturalists; yet every naturalist knows vaguely what he means when he speaks of a species. Generally the term includes the unknown element of a distinct act of creation.

Here's what I want: argue that Darwin's "knows vaguely" is not a temporary embarrassment that some future species concept (biological, phylogenetic, ecological, whatever) will finally fix, but the correct permanent verdict -- that there is no deeper fact about species boundaries waiting to be discovered, only more or less useful conventions, and that the modern multiplication of competing species concepts vindicates him rather than refuting him. You can use the hybridization and endangered-species-policy stuff if it helps. Give me the argument as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
