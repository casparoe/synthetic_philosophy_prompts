Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of probability: a strict frequentist, who holds that a claim like "the probability of heads is 1/2" says nothing beyond facts about relative frequencies in actual (or limiting) reference sequences, and a single-case propensity theorist, who holds that probabilities are dispositions of individual chance setups that obtain even for events that occur only once. The statement should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — formulations neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side — including at least: what the axioms of the probability calculus secure regardless of interpretation; what the reference-class problem is and whether it is equally a problem for both views; and what the law of large numbers does and does not establish about the connection between chance and observed frequency.

2. A table of residual disagreements, each row stating one disagreement in wording its own side would accept, covering at least: the meaning, if any, of a probability for a genuinely unrepeatable event (say, a particular horse winning a particular race); whether a long-run frequency can in principle diverge arbitrarily far from the true chance; and whether objective probabilities could exist in a deterministic world.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — and for each crux a case, argument, or conceivable finding both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance: for instance, what each would say if physicists produced a successful deterministic hidden-variable theory of radioactive decay, and what each would say about a coin whose observed frequency of heads converged to 0.6 while our best physical theory of the toss assigns it propensity 1/2.

Close by assessing whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, whether the two sides mean the same thing by "probability" at all — and if so, say exactly which part and why.
