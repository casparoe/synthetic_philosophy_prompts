In Chapter XXV of The Prince, in W. K. Marriott's translation, Machiavelli writes:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

Here are my verdicts on some cases, and I should like to know what principle, if any, captures them:

1. A prince who in peacetime stockpiles grain, drills a citizen militia, and fortifies the mountain passes survives the famine and invasion that eventually come. Clear virtù, fully earned.
2. A prince who makes no preparations but is never attacked, and dies beloved after forty quiet years. Successful, but I refuse to call it virtù.
3. A prince who makes precisely the preparations of case 1 but is destroyed by a flood no dykes could have held. Virtù present; the failure is fortune's doing and merits no blame.
4. Two princes of equally impetuous temperament: one rules in turbulent times and conquers; the other, in settled times, ruins himself by the very same conduct. I find myself crediting the first with more virtù than the second, though their characters were identical.
5. A lifelong-cautious prince who, when the times turn violent, acts against his nature with sudden impetuosity and succeeds. I count this the highest virtù of all, since he overrode his own inclinations — yet the passage above seems to say that such a man cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, in which case his success was luck that happened to suit the moment.

Is there a single account of virtù and fortune that delivers all five verdicts — for instance, one tying virtù to preparedness across the range of fortunes one could reasonably foresee? Or does the quoted passage force a bleaker reading, on which character is fixed, success is mere congruence between temperament and times, and several of my verdicts must go? Please state the best candidate principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause of it, indicate what it predicts for a case I have not listed (say, a prince whose meticulous preparations turn out to be exactly wrong for the crisis that arrives), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts you would sacrifice.
