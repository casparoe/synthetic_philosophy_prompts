The Vaiśeṣika and Buddhist attempts to fold testimony into inference fail; knowing something from a trustworthy speaker is its own instrument, not a disguised anumāna about the speaker's reliability.

(2) I'm a fallibilist across the board: any particular cognition a pramāṇa delivers can in principle be overturned by a stronger contrary one, no matter which instrument produced it.

(3) Vyāpti (pervasion, like wherever there's smoke there's fire) is known through observation of co-presence, with tarka brought in to knock out lingering doubts about counterinstances. I don't buy anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) as a fifth, seperate pramāṇa — I'm with the Naiyāyikas against the Bhāṭṭas on that.

(4) Testimonial knowledge is immediate. When a reliable person tells me p, I just know p; I don't first run an inference like "she's been right before, so probably right now too." You only start checking a speaker's reliability when some doubt has already come up.

(5) Universals are real, mind-independent, and perceived — you literally see cowness when you see a cow. And this is what makes inference work: pervasion holds between real universals (smokiness, fireness), not between concepts we've cooked up, which is why anumāna latches onto the world instead of onto our psychology.

(6) When two pramāṇas conflict — say a trusted report clashes with what I seem to be seeing — the conflict gets settled by weighing the cognitions' relative strength, and in practice the strength of a piece of testimony is a function of the speaker's track record, which I know by perception and inference.
