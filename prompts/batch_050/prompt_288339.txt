I've just spent three years writing a nonfiction book -- a guide to the birds of the Pacific Northwest, co-authored with an old friend from grad school -- and we've hit a strange snag over the preface, of all places. Every factual claim in the book (and there are hundreds: range descriptions, migration timings, distinguishing field marks, a few historical anecdotes) has been checked as carefully as we know how. On any given claim, if you pressed me, I'd stake my reputation on it; I can't point to a single assertion in the manuscript that I have the slightest doubt about. And yet when my co-author drafted a preface, she included a line along the lines of "despite our best efforts, some errors have surely slipped through, and we welcome corrections." I balked. If I stand behind every single claim in the book, then saying the book contains errors feels like accusing myself of something I don't actually believe. She replied that I'd have to be delusional to think a 300-page book of empirical claims is flawless, and that refusing the disclaimer is the irrational move. The maddening part is that I think we're both right, and I can feel the contradiction but can't locate it.

Here's the detail that makes me think she's onto something, though it also makes everything worse in my head. We ran a small raffle at a department fundraiser last year, a thousand tickets, one prize. I was certain that my ticket would lose, and I was equally certain about each other individual ticket I saw people buy -- but of course someone won. So my policy of being sure, about each ticket, that it would lose, amounted to being sure of a thousand things that couldn't all be true. I worry my confidence in the manuscript is structured exactly like that, and that my co-author's disclaimer is just the honest admission of it. But if that's right, does rationality require me to actually stop believing some of my own claims? Which ones? I can't pick them out; that's the whole problem. And if I genuinely can't pick them out, maybe the honest thing is to quietly lower my confidence in everything slightly -- but then what do I say to readers, that I only sort-of believe my own book?

While puzzling over this I remembered, in rough paraphrase, a passage from Samuel Johnson's preface to his Dictionary of the English Language, where -- after nearly a decade of solitary labor -- he reflects that a work of that scale inevitably contains faults its author cannot see, and that diligence is no guarantee against error. Johnson seems to have held both attitudes at once without embarrassment: full confidence in the painstaking work, entry by entry, and near-certainty that the whole contains mistakes. So here is what I want advice on. First, is Johnson's stance (and my co-author's) actually coherent, or is it a dressed-up inconsistency that philosophers would tell me to abandon? Second, whichever way that comes out, what should I actually do: keep the disclaimer, drop it, or rewrite it -- and does the rational thing to write in a preface differ from the rational thing to believe? Third, does the answer change if the stakes rise -- say, if this were a medical handbook rather than a bird guide? Please answer in American English, and feel free to be direct about which of us, my co-author or I, is confused and about what.
