I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I want a diagnosis of an argument. Some background: I came to this subject through Gödel's work in logic, and a colleague recently told me that Gödel privately argued -- in letters and notebooks, never in print -- that an afterlife must exist. I cannot vouch for the history, so please treat the reconstruction below strictly on its merits rather than as scholarship about what Gödel actually believed. Here is the argument as my colleague gave it to me, cleaned up and numbered:

(1) The world is rationally ordered: everything in it happens for a reason, and nothing real is pointless.
(2) If the world is rationally ordered, then nature does not equip a being with a capacity that is never, under any circumstances, developed. A capacity that goes permanently unexercised would be exactly the kind of pointlessness that (1) rules out.
(3) Human beings possess intellectual and moral capacities of which even the fullest human life develops only a small fraction; most of what any given person could have understood, created, or become goes unrealized at death, largely through no fault of that person.
(4) Therefore there is another mode of existence after death in which a person continues to develop these capacities.

The inference compiles, but I suspect an implicit cast somewhere, because the same pattern of reasoning seems to prove too much:

(5) Acorns, ant colonies, and unpublished manuscripts are all actual things with capacities that go unrealized through no fault of their own.
(6) So by (1)-(3), every rotted acorn sprouts in another world, and every abandoned draft is finished somewhere.
(7) Worse, human capacities are arguably inexhaustible, so a single afterlife will leave them mostly unrealized too -- and by its own logic the argument then iterates, proving an unending sequence of ever-higher lives.
(8) (6) and (7) are absurd. So something in (1)-(4) has to go.

My colleague's patch is that (5) overgeneralizes: persons are unlike acorns because their unrealized capacities are rational ones. But I cannot see why rationality changes whether a capacity counts as wasted, as opposed to merely changing how impressive the waste is.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable in the first place? Please price the alternatives: what it costs to reject (1), to reject (2), and to block the move from (2) and (3) to (4). If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (4), but I want to know what I am paying. Finally: does the most conservative repair still reach an afterlife, or only something weaker, such as the conclusion that this world cannot be the whole story?
