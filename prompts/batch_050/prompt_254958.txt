I handle procurement and risk review for a mid-sized logistics company, and we're being pitched a lot of "autonomous" systems lately -- routing software that reassigns drivers on its own, warehouse robots that decide pick order, a claims tool that auto-denies certain damage reports. Our legal team keeps raising the responsibility gap worry: if one of these systems causes harm and no human specifically chose that outcome, nobody may be answerable for it, and "the algorithm did it" isn't an answer we can give a customer or a regulator. I've read enough about this to know philosophers distinguish a few different gaps -- who answers, who's blameworthy, who's liable -- but I can't get a room of engineers and vendors to care about the distinctions, I need them to care about the contract.

So what I want is a procedure we can actually run during vendor review, something like 45 minutes per system: the questions to ask in order, what a sufficient answer looks like at each step, which answers should be hard stops (no sign-off until fixed), and which are yellow flags that get escalated to a human because they're really value judgments rather than checkable facts. Please be explicit about where judgment calls are hiding inside what looks like procedure, because I'd rather know that than pretend we've mechanized it. A few things I specifically want the procedure to handle: systems whose behavior changes after deployment via updates, cases where the vendor's docs are vague about failure modes, and the difference between a system that recommends and a system that acts (vendors blur this constantly).

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical products: (1) a routing system that reassigns delivery drivers in real time and occasionally routes someone through a flooded road it was told to avoid, and (2) a hiring-screen tool that ranks applicants and that we can't fully audit because the vendor treats the model as a trade secret. Ideally one of them should come out as "sign anyway with conditions" and one as a no, but if your procedure says otherwise, go with what it actually says. Also: tell me where this procedure is most likely to be wrong or gamed by a savvy vendor, and what you'd have to see -- in practice or in the literature on responsibility gaps -- that would make you redesign it.
