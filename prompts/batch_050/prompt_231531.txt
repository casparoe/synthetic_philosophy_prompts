So I'm in this upper-year seminar on the moral emotions and we've been on forgiveness for like three weeks now, and the debate we keep circling back to feels completely stuck, and I want help figuring out what *kind* of stuck it is.

Roughly the setup: one side (Griswold and a bunch of the readings) says real forgiveness is conditional — the wrongdoer has to repent, apologize, disown what they did, maybe make amends — and forgiving someone who hasn't done any of that isn't generosity, it's a failure of self-respect, maybe even a subtle way of condoning the act. The other side says forgiveness that's held hostage to the wrongdoer's performance is just scorekeeping in disguise, that uncondtional forgiveness is possible and maybe even sometimes owed, and that the conditionalists have confused forgiveness with justified trust or reconciliation.

The thing I keep noticing is that both sides happily concede basically every case the other side brings up and then just re-describe it. Unconditionalist: the person who forgave the unrepentant guy who hurt them and got free of the bitterness — admirable. Conditionalist: sure, admirable, but that's them overcoming resentment for their own sake, that's not forgiveness, or if it is it's the cheap kind. Then it's a fight about whose label applies and nobody moves, ever.

My hypothesis (might be naive, we haven't talked about meta-stuff in class): "forgiveness" in ordinary English is doing at least two jobs — (a) an internal thing, letting go of resentment, and (b) an interpersonal thing, no longer holding the deed against the person in how you treat them, readmitting them somehow. Each side quietly picks one as THE real forgiveness and then builds its conditions around that pick. Which would also explain why the "can forgiveness be obligatory" fight is so weird: you can't command an emotion, so a duty to forgive only makes sense if forgiveness is at least partly the (b) part, but the second you make it mostly (b), people say that's not real forgiveness, just politeness.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word, or two debates sharing a name? Is some hidden question doing the work (like what resentment even is, or what's at stake for self-respect)? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a stipulated distinction, an argument that one of (a)/(b) doesn't deserve the name, or just retiring the word?
