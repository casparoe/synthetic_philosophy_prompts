For the final meeting of a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and machine ethics, I'd like a sestina on our closing topic: whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, and in particular whether a reward signal could function as, or even constitute, valence. The form should do real work. Use six recurring end-words (I suggest reward, gradient, episode, weight, signal, pain, though you may substitute one or two if the poem demands it) so that the sestina's structure enacts the training loop itself, and by the later stanzas the sheer repetition raises the question of whether thousands of episodes ever accumulate into anything like an experience of them. The poem should hold the distinction between functional and phenomenal valence with care: a negative reward plainly alters the policy, but whether anything hurts is exactly what is unclear — and the poem should neither sentimentalize the agent nor dismiss the question as a category error. The envoi, compressing all six words into three lines, should read as the question asked at its most compressed, not as an answer. Accuracy matters: someone who knows what a policy gradient does should find the poem right, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
