In my graduate seminar on rational choice theory, we spent a session on the money pump argument, and at some point the discussion leader shut things down with the familiar one-liner: "If your preferences are intransitive, you can be turned into a money pump — so rationality requires transitivity." The reason this stung is that it was aimed, half-jokingly, at my friend Mara, who was in the room and whose life is a running joke on exactly this point: she has moved apartments three times in two years, each time trading toward a place she preferred to the one she had, and at the end of it she was living in a strict downgrade from her original apartment and out several thousand dollars in fees and deposits. Her defense, offered afterward over drinks, was that the money pump argument is rigged: no real trader ever offers you the full cycle of trades in one package, any sane person stops playing after one or two rounds, and preferences about real, thickly described options are allowed to shift as the comparison set changes. She claims she has never once been "pumped" and never would be.

I suspect both the discussion leader and Mara are overclaiming, but I can't articulate where. What I'd like is a careful disambiguation of the slogan: the distinct, precise theses that "intransitive agents can be money-pumped, so rationality requires transitivity" could be expressing. I can see the outlines of several — transitivity as a constitutive norm of synchronic consistency, transitivity as a merely instrumental norm for avoiding losses, the money pump as a literal empirical prediction about what will happen to intransitive agents versus a thought experiment showing what their preferences commit them to judging, and a diachronic reading where the real norm concerns how an agent follows through over time rather than what she prefers at an instant — but I don't trust my taxonomy, and I don't know which readings survive obvious replies (for instance, that a sophisticated agent who knows her own intransitivity can simply refuse to keep trading, so the pump never gets going).

Please answer as a philosopher specializing in decision theory who is also a patient teacher. Take your time and think carefully: begin by sketching the full space of readings before evaluating any of them, then lay them out as a numbered menu. For each reading, state the thesis precisely enough that it could be attacked or defended, give a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, substantive but doubtful, or simply false — and say why. Then, and this is the part I care most about, tell me which of the surviving readings would actually support the use the slogan was put to in my seminar, namely pressuring Mara to treat her apartment saga as evidence of irrationality rather than as evidence that housing is complicated. If the strongest reading of the slogan in fact vindicates her, or cuts against both of them, say so plainly.
