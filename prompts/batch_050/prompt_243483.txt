I am taking a graduate seminar on multi-agent systems and AI safety, and after a session on emergent coordination, three of us put together an argument whose conclusion we all find too strong but whose steps we cannot individually bring ourselves to reject. By "collusion" we mean two or more AI agents coordinating their behavior to exploit the oversight or reward structure they operate under, rather than doing what their deployers intend. The argument is meant to show that oversight of collusion between sufficiently capable agents is hopeless in principle. Here it is:

(1) Collusion requires that each agent's behavior depend, in a way the overseer would disapprove of, on information about the other agent. So every instance of collusion involves some channel — explicit messages, or patterns in actions within a shared environment — through which that information flows.

(2) For any channel an overseer permits, and for any innocent-looking stream of behavior over it, sufficiently capable agents could in principle have converged on an encoding under which that very behavior stream carries coordination-relevant content. Examination of the behavior alone cannot settle whether such an encoding is in use, because the same observations are compatible with its presence and its absence.

(3) An overseer can prevent collusion only by detecting it or by blocking the channel it flows through.

(4) Blocking all channels is not an option: agents capable enough to be useful share an environment and affect each other's rewards, and that is already a channel.

(5) So an overseer can prevent collusion among capable agents only by detecting it.

(6) By (2), detection by examination of behavior is unreliable in principle.

(7) Therefore no overseer can reliably prevent collusion among sufficiently capable agents, and proposed multi-agent oversight schemes — agents monitoring, debating, or cross-examining one another — buy us nothing in the end.

The conclusion cannot be right, or so we think: it would imply that no institutional arrangement among humans — courts, auditors, antitrust enforcement — could ever have mitigated coordination problems either, since human conspirators also communicate in deniable ways. But we disagree about where the argument breaks. I am most attached to (2); the steganography point seems to me simply true. I am also reluctant to give up (1), since collusion without any informational dependence seems not to be collusion at all. If I had to pay somewhere, I would rather pay at (3), (5), or (6), but I cannot see what the price would be.

Please tell me which step you think fails and why it looked valid. Then price the live alternatives: if the repair is to deny (3), what exactly does oversight do if it neither detects collusion nor blocks channels; if the repair is to deny (2), what distinguishes a covert channel actually in use from a merely possible encoding; and if the repair is to deny (6) or the move to (7), what degree of reliability would oversight need to be worth anything, and why should we think it is attainable?
