I don't have any background in philosophy or engineering, but I've been going down a rabbit hole lately about whether we can actually build ethics into machines, and I keep coming back to the Boeing 737 MAX crashes (Lion Air in 2018, Ethiopian Airlines in 2019). From what I understand: Boeing added a system called MCAS that automatically pushed the plane's nose down when it thought the plane was stalling, it relied on readings from a single sensor, the pilots weren't properly told about it or trained on it, and in both crashes the system kept fighting the pilots until the planes went down. Almost 350 people died.

Here's what's bugging me. The stuff I find about "machine ethics" — building moral reasoning into AI and robots — seems obsessed with dramatic dilemmas, like should a self-driving car swerve to kill one person instead of five. But MCAS never faced a dilemma. It didn't weigh anything. It just did the wrong thing over and over while the humans who were suppose to be in charge didn't understand what it was doing or why.

So analyze the MAX crashes as a test case for machine ethics. Three things I specifically want:

1. Take the main approaches people propose for making machines behave ethically — top-down (program in rules or principles), bottom-up (let the machine learn from examples), and hybrid versions — and say what each one would actually have amounted to for a system like MCAS. Would any of them have caught this, or is the whole toolbox aimed at the wrong kind of failure?

2. The responsibility question. Boeing, the FAA (who apparently let Boeing certify alot of its own work), the software itself, the pilots. Does machine ethics have anything real to say about who was responsible, or does it just wave at "the human in the loop" in a way that falls apart the moment the human doesn't even know the loop exists?

3. Run it both ways. What does machine ethics help you see about the crashes that ordinary engineering-safety talk (redundancy, failure modes, human factors) misses? And what do the crashes show about machine ethics — are they a counterexample to something the field assumes, or do they show the field mostly thinks about flashy robot dilemmas while boring automation quietly kills people?

Please answer in American English, and since I'm not an academic, keep the jargon light and explain any technical terms you use.
