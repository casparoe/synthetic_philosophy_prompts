A few weeks ago my fiancée asked me, half-teasing, why I love her. I answered honestly: her precision with language, the patience she shows my difficult mother, the way she can make a delayed train feel like an adventure. She went quiet. That night she told me the answer had hurt her, and she explained why with an analogy she had heard from a couples therapist. Love that rests on a person's qualities, she said, is like an appraisal report on a house. The appraiser's esteem tracks the specifications and the comparable listings; should a better-appointed house come on the market, the appraisal shifts without any disloyalty to the old one. Real love, she said, runs on the model of bestowal: like a gift that becomes the recipient's regardless of its appraised value, or the way parents love a newborn who has no qualities yet to appraise. Her conclusion was that my answer showed I love her the way an appraiser esteems a property, and that what she wants — what love is — admits no reasons of the kind I gave.

I have been turning this over ever since, and I keep half-agreeing, which is why I am writing. I would like you to audit both analogies rather than take sides on instinct. For the appraisal comparison, lay out in prose what is meant to correspond to what — the qualities I listed to the specifications, me to the appraiser, her to the property, the hypothetical rival to the new listing — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I suspect, for instance, that an appraiser's stance is third-personal, interchangeable, and aims at accuracy about a value that exists independently of him, and I am not sure any of that is true of a lover; but I also cannot shake her point that if my reasons are her qualities, then anyone sharing those qualities seems equally owed my love, and she seems owed it only for as long as she keeps them. For the bestowal comparison, do the same work: what corresponds to the gift, to the giver, to the transfer of ownership; where the mapping breaks; and what it quietly assumes. I notice, for example, that gifts are unilateral and normally irrevocable, that a newborn will soon have qualities aplenty, and that parental love may be the exception smuggled in as the rule — so the analogy may be importing a one-way, causeless picture of love that no adult relationship could actually live up to, and perhaps should not.

Then I want the verdict. Does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? She moved from "your love has reasons" to "your love is an appraisal" to "an appraisal is not love." Tell me which step the analogy was carrying and which can stand on its own — including whether there is a stable middle position, the sort philosophers gesture at when they say love might respond to reasons without being grounded in qualities, or might be grounded in the history of the relationship rather than in the person appraised at a moment. And if neither analogy can bear the weight it is being asked to bear, tell me what better one you would offer in its place, and to which of us, and why.

Please write your answer in American English, and write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. I want it to read like a letter I could actually send her.
