A few years ago my family stumbled onto something odd about a story from my grandfather's childhood. Both he and his younger sister tell the story of the day their house flooded, and both tell it from the inside: the cold water around the ankles, the smell of the soaked carpet, their mother carrying the mantel clock upstairs. But his sister was born two years after the flood. She grew up hearing the story at every family gathering, and at some point it quietly converted into a memory, subjectively indistinguishable from her genuine ones. Once I started looking for this pattern I found it everywhere: a friend who "remembers" his brother's training accident in the army right down to the sound of it, a colleague who realized that the holiday she had pictured vividly for years was in fact taken by her cousin. In each case the content of the memory is broadly accurate — these are not invented episodes — and yet the person remembering was simply not there.

I mention this because I have been reading about personal identity on my own, working through Locke's proposal that what makes me the same person as some past individual is my capacity to remember that individual's experiences, together with Reid's well-known counterexample of the boy flogged at school, the officer who later remembers the flogging, and the elderly general who remembers the officer's exploits but no longer the flogging. As I understand it, Reid's case attacks the memory criterion through forgetting: memory links can die out along a chain, while identity cannot. My family's case seems to threaten the criterion from the opposite direction — not a link dying between one person's stages, but a link springing up between two different people. If remembering an experience from the inside is what makes me the person who underwent it, then my great-aunt apparently is, to some small degree, my grandfather, which is absurd; but if we add the usual rider that the memory must be caused by the experience "in the right way," it is far from obvious that her case fails, since the experience really did cause her memory — it merely traveled through his storytelling rather than through her own brain tissue. And presumably many of our own childhood memories are, at bottom, memories of having been told about them.

So here is my question, and I would not be satisfied with a bare label. Does this phenomenon — a first-personal memory of an episode one never lived through, acquired through testimony rather than through suggestion, fantasy, or forgetting — have an established name, in philosophy or in psychology? I am aware of confabulation and of the false-memory literature, but those seem aimed at cases of fabrication or therapeutic suggestion, whereas what I am describing can arise naturally, with accurate content and no error of detail beyond the misattributed subject. If nothing established quite fits, please say so and coin a term with a definition sharp enough to settle the boundary cases: does it cover remembering your own third birthday party via your parents' retellings, or only episodes belonging entirely to someone else? And finally, how does the phenomenon bear on the Locke–Reid dispute — do the standard repairs of Locke against Reid (appeals to overlapping chains of memory connections, for instance) also handle this branching variant, or does the branching variant actually get worse under those repairs, so that a memory theorist must say something extra here?
