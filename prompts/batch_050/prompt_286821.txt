I've spent enough years around rationalist blogs and forums to be at home with credences, Dutch books, and bets that get called off, which is part of why the following argument — put to me by a friend from that corner of the internet — has been eating at me. Its conclusion seems to make nonsense of ordinary speech, yet I cannot locate the false step. It concerns indicative conditionals: plain if-then claims like "if the shipment clears customs, we will be profitable by spring."

(1) For a rational agent, the credence appropriate to "if A, then C" is the conditional probability P(C | A), whenever P(A) > 0. This is just the thought that to evaluate the conditional you suppose A and ask how confident you are of C on that supposition — the same thought that sets the fair price of a conditional bet, one that pays on A-and-C, loses on A-and-not-C, and is called off if A is false.

(2) If indicative conditionals express propositions — if they say something that can be flat-out true or false — then an agent's credence in a conditional is simply the probability they assign to its proposition being true.

(3) But the triviality results of the 1970s show that no proposition can play this role: for any candidate proposition a conditional might express, there are perfectly coherent credence functions in which the probability of that proposition comes apart from P(C | A). The equation holds across the board only in degenerate cases. (The proof turns on how a rational agent's credences must evolve upon learning the conditional itself.)

(4) So indicative conditionals do not express propositions. Uttering one is not making a truth-evaluable claim; it is doing something else — expressing a high conditional credence, issuing an inference license, or the like.

(5) Now consider compounds: "Either the alarm was a drill, or, if it was real, the night guard ignored it." "It is false that if he knew, he would have told us." "If you leave now, then if the bridge is closed, you will miss the ferry." Negation, disjunction, and conditionalization are operations on truth-valued contents; if conditionals lack truth values, these compounds lack truth values too.

(6) Yet we manifestly treat such compounds as truth-evaluable: we bet on them, retract them under pressure, and catch one another contradicting them.

(7) So the argument proves too much. (4) renders unintelligible something we transparently do, and one of the earlier premises or inferences must give.

What I would most like to keep is (1); the tie between the conditional and conditional credence is the one piece of this territory I took for bedrock, and (2) strikes me as close to a definition. Tell me which step you reject, why it managed to look innocent, and what rejecting it costs — including what becomes of the embedded conditionals in (5) and (6) on your preferred diagnosis.
