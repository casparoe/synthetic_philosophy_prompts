I'm a high school senior and I co-run my school's philosophy club, which meets once a week for about an hour with roughly fifteen students, most of whom have no background beyond an informal logic unit from English class. For an upcoming session I want to design a game that makes the club feel the force of common knowledge of rationality -- the way that piling up levels of "I know that you know that I know that everyone is rational" drags a group's behavior toward an equilibrium nobody actually wants -- instead of just lecturing about it. Here's the twist I want built in: the same underlying game should be played several times in a row under different public announcements about rationality (first nothing is said, then it's announced that everyone is rational, then that everyone knows everyone is rational, then one level higher), so the group watches its own behavior shift as the epistemic ladder gets taller, and only afterwards learns that what they were climbing was the difference between finite levels of mutual knowledge and genuine common knowledge.

Please design the full activity: the base game and its exact rules (something where iterated elimination of dominated strategies is intuitive, like a number-guessing contest with a shrinking target, though I'm open to a better vehicle if you have one), the precise announcement script for each round, a payoff scheme using points or small prizes rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps what happened in each round onto k-level reasoning, the mutual-versus-common-knowledge distinction, and why full common knowledge of rationality forces the equilibrium outcome. The concept should emerge from what they did, not from anything I say up front -- they shouldn't hear the terms until the debrief.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the student who jumps straight to the equilibrium in round one and flattens the whole demonstration, friends quietly coordinating, kids who don't track the announcement wording, and anyone who reasons irrationally on purpose because they think it's funny. How do I handle each? Finally, be honest about which feature of the formal idea a room full of non-ideal teenage reasoners cannot reproduce, and what I should say about that gap in the debrief rather than pretending the results will come out clean.
