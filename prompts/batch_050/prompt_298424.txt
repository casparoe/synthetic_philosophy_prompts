So I had this massive argument with my friend last night over beers about the "dogmatism paradox" and I want to keep hashing it out, but he's busy, so I'm recruiting you to play his side. Quick background in case the name doesn't ring a bell: the paradox goes like this. Suppose I genuinely know that p — say, that my bike is locked up outside where I left it. Now some apparent counterevidence comes along: my roommate texts me "pretty sure I just saw someone ride off on your bike." Well, if I know p, then I know any evidence against p must be misleading. So I can reason: p is true, therefore the text is misleading (mistaken bike, prank, whatever), therefore I'm rationally fine to just dismiss it. Knowledge seems to license ignoring all future counterevidence, forever. Which feels deeply wrong — that's not what knowers are supposed to do, that's what cult members do.

My friend bit the bullet completely. His position: the reasoning is totally valid and the "paradox" isn't a paradox at all. If you really do know p, then apparent evidence against p really is misleading, and there's nothing irrational about discounting evidence you know to be misleading — that's just what knowing entitles you to. He says the whole thing only feels icky because in real life we claim knowledge way more often than we actually have it, so the dismissal move looks terrible in cases where we shouldn't have claimed knowledge in the first place. He's happy to concede that we should be humble about what we take ourselves to know, but he insists that conditional on genuinely knowing, dismissal is the rational response, full stop.
