Last night I spent about three hours arguing with a friend who works on host–microbe symbiosis, and we made no progress at all, which is why I'm writing this. The question was whether a holobiont — say, a coral together with its algal symbionts and associated microbial community — counts as a single organism. Her case rested on integration: the partners exchange metabolites, the whole assemblage develops in a coordinated way, the host's immune system actively regulates its microbial members rather than attacking them, and in some systems selection demonstrably acts on the collective as a unit. My case rested on heterogeneity: the partners come from separate evolutionary lineages, community membership shifts across environments and life stages, transmission of symbionts is often leaky and horizontal rather than vertical, and the "whole" can survive wholesale replacement of its parts in a way no ordinary animal can. The frustrating part is that we agreed on essentially every empirical claim either of us made. She conceded the lineage and transmission points; I conceded the integration and selection points. And yet she still concluded "organism" and I still concluded "something more like an ecosystem or an association," and neither of us could say what further fact would settle it.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute isn't about holobionts at all but about which theoretical job the concept of an organism is supposed to do. On one side there's a physiological or metabolic notion, where individuality is a matter of internal integration, boundary maintenance, and mutual dependence; on the other there's an evolutionary notion, where individuality is a matter of being a unit of selection with sufficiently faithful reproduction and heritable fitness. Holobionts sit near the maximum of the first scale and somewhere in the murky middle of the second, so whichever notion you privilege delivers a different verdict, and the apparent disagreement about the coral is really a disagreement about which scale deserves the word. A stronger version of the hypothesis — which I'm less sure about — is that "organism" simply isn't a natural kind, that the world contains several respectable kinds of individuality that cross-classify actual cases, and that asking whether the holobiont "really is" an organism is therefore ill-posed rather than merely hard.

What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on the coral. Is the stalled debate best understood as two camps answering different questions under one name, as my hypothesis says, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath — for instance, about whether immunological regulation is the right criterion of integration, or about whether collective-level heritability in these systems is empirically adequate to sustain evolutionary individuality? If it's the former, what would count as progress: a stipulated distinction between kinds of individuality that both camps can accept, an empirical result that would force one notion to dominate, or an admission that the question should simply be retired? If it's the latter, please identify where the real disagreement lives and what observation or argument could resolve it. I'd also like an honest assessment of my stronger claim: is "organism is not a natural kind" a conclusion the debate's history supports, or is it the kind of thing people say when a dispute is hard and they're tired of it?
