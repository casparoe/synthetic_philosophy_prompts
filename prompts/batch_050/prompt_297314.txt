I chair the editorial standards committee of a mid-sized regional newspaper, a role I took on three months ago after fifteen years as a reporter, and I need help drafting the written opinion I will circulate to the full board before our vote next month.

The immediate background is painful. In the spring, an anonymous comment thread under a story about a fatal traffic accident named a local schoolteacher as the driver. It was wrong — the actual driver was someone else with a similar name — but by the time we corrected the story, the thread had been screenshotted and circulated, and the woman has since taken leave from her job. The publisher has now proposed that we abolish anonymous commenting altogether and require every commenter to register under a verified legal name, and the committee is split down the middle.

One of my colleagues, a former philosophy lecturer, has been pressing a Kierkegaardian argument on us: that the press manufactures something Kierkegaard called the public, an abstraction that lets everyone hold and broadcast opinions without ever standing behind them as a concrete person, and that anonymity is not a bug in this arrangement but its perfection — opinion with the responsibility boiled off, idle chatter replacing genuine communication, a leveling of everyone into spectators and commentators on everything. On his reading, requiring real names is not a mere civility measure but a partial undoing of the public itself, since it forces the commenter back into the position of someone who can be answered, reproached, and held to account. Another colleague counters that Kierkegaard is a poor witness for our side, since he published much of his own work under invented names, so that writing under something other than one's own name can apparently serve serious purposes even on his own showing — and we will have to say what distinguishes his case from our commenters'.

The strongest argument on the other side, as I see it, is not abstract at all: we cover a small region dominated by a handful of employers, and some of our most valuable reader input — about working conditions at the hospital group, about contracting practices at the school district — has come from people who could not safely have written under their own names. A real-name rule would not merely silence trolls; it would silence the nurse and the teacher's aide, and our reporting would be poorer for it.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should: (1) recommend a concrete policy — adopt the publisher's proposal, reject it, or replace it with a specified alternative — and state plainly what we will actually do; (2) take the Kierkegaardian diagnosis seriously enough to say whether it genuinely supports the recommendation, including an honest answer to the pseudonym objection; and (3) give the losing side's strongest consideration its full due, in a form that colleagues who vote the other way would recognize as a fair statement of their view. Around 800–1,000 words, addressed to the full board.
