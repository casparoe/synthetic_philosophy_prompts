Last night a friend and I argued for nearly three hours about a puzzle in epistemology, and we ended the evening exactly where we started, which has left me wondering what our disagreement actually consisted in. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of dispute were we having, and what, if anything, would resolve it?

The puzzle is the one sometimes called the dogmatism paradox, usually credited to Kripke. It runs as follows. Suppose I know some proposition p — say, that my colleague did not take the missing money, on the strength of solid evidence. If I know p, then I know that p is true, and hence I know that any future evidence appearing to tell against p must be misleading: it points towards a falsehood. But if I already know that any such evidence will be misleading, then I seem to be in a position to know in advance that I may disregard it. Knowledge, the argument concludes, licenses a kind of dogmatism: the rational knower may dismiss future counterevidence sight unseen.

My position was that the paradox is dissolved by a distinction. Knowing that future counterevidence will be misleading is one thing; being entitled to avert one's eyes from it is another. The argument equivocates between dismissing evidence one has encountered (which may indeed be rational, conditional on one's still knowing p) and refusing to gather or consider evidence at all (which is not rational, because encountering genuine counterevidence might destroy one's knowledge, and one cannot know in advance that this will not happen). My friend resisted this on the ground that the distinction does no real work: if I truly know that the evidence is misleading, then exposing myself to it can only put me at risk of being irrationally swayed by it, so the cautious, knowledge-preserving policy is precisely to look away. I replied that this confuses what is rational relative to retained knowledge with what is rational for an agent whose knowledge is fragile; he replied that the conditionality I kept invoking ('conditional on still knowing p') is a philosopher's dodge, since agents must act on what they now take themselves to know, not on what they might cease to know. Round and round we went.

What struck me afterwards is that we never disagreed about the logic of the argument, nor about any case. We disagreed about what the argument shows. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only, is that the dispute is really about the normativity of epistemic rationality: my friend treats rationality as a matter of what follows from one's current epistemic position, whereas I treat it as a matter of policies that serve one well across the ways one's position might change. But I am not confident of this, and I can imagine other diagnoses — that the dispute is verbal, trading on different senses of 'disregard'; that it is a substantive dispute about whether knowledge can be lost through the acquisition of true evidence; or that it is a stand-off between two starting intuitions (that known falsehoods cannot be supported by evidence, and that a knower may rationally investigate) which neither of us can argue for without circularity.

Please tell me, with reasons: which diagnosis best fits the exchange as I have described it? If the disagreement is verbal, identify the ambiguous term and show how each of us was using it. If it is substantive, state the underlying proposition we actually disagree about. And in either case, say what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a test case we would both have to read the same way, a formal result about evidence and knowledge — or, if you think no progress is possible because the impasse reflects irreconcilable starting intuitions, argue for that instead. Please answer in British English.
