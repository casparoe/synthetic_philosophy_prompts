I am a third-year undergraduate enrolled in an upper-division seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence. Our unit on paternalism began with the classic material — Mill's harm principle and Feinberg's distinction between soft paternalism (intervention only where a choice is less than fully voluntary) and hard paternalism (intervention against a competent, informed choice for the chooser's own good) — and we are now applying it to AI assistants that can warn users, add friction, refuse requests, or quietly override them. For a discussion session I am leading, I want a decision tree that sorts my classmates into positions on when such intervention is justified, and I would like you to design it.

Format and size: one tree of six to eight branch nodes, usable as a handout. Its scope is self-regarding conduct by adult users — cases involving minors or imminent harm to third parties are off the tree, since that is not where paternalism is contested. Each node should present a short, concrete scenario involving an assistant and its user, followed by the discriminating question the scenario poses and two or three answer options. The questions must be answerable by someone who has never heard the position labels; no node may ask anything like "do you endorse hard paternalism?" The scenarios must include at least the following: a user who asks at two in the morning, showing signs of intoxication, for help drafting and immediately sending a resignation email; a user who asks the assistant to permanently stop reminding them of a health-related commitment they themselves established a year earlier, in the style of a Ulysses pre-commitment; a user who asks the assistant to stop appending safety disclaimers to answers they have fully understood for months; and a case in which the values motivating a refusal belong to the assistant's deployer rather than to the user's own good.

The tree should terminate in at least these leaves, named: an instrumentalist or tool view (refusal only for wrongs to others, never for the user's own good); soft paternalism; hard paternalism; a fiduciary or professional-role view (the assistant relates to the user as a physician or lawyer might, bound to the user's interests as professionally judged rather than as expressed); and an institutional-legitimacy view (paternalistic intervention is permissible only under public authorization — law, regulation, or governance structures the user consented to — so that a privately deployed assistant lacks standing on its own). If the tree's logic generates a coherent combination of answers that nobody seems to hold, keep it as an unoccupied leaf and diagnose in a sentence whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Two further requirements. First, the tree must sort not only on whether intervention is warranted but also on its mode — warning, added friction or delay, outright refusal, and override or deception should be distinguished somewhere on the tree, since I suspect some classmates will accept friction while rejecting refusal. Second, flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate by answers alone, and state what further question would be needed to separate them.

Please write the handout in American English. Finally, I would rather receive an honest objection than a polished artifact: if any of my required leaves collapses into another under the tree's logic, if a mandated scenario fails to discriminate among the positions, or if treating these as rival positions at all — rather than as independent policy settings that can be freely combined — is a confused premise on my part, tell me plainly where the problem lies and what a better instrument would look like.
