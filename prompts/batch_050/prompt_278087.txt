So I'm trying to write an essay on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day (I read the novel years ago, just rewatched the Anthony Hopkins film, and now I can't stop thinking about it), and I want to read it through the philosophy of hard choices and incommensurable values. The standard line from people like Ruth Chang is that some choices are hard because the options are "on a par": neither one better, but not exactly equal either, and the right response is to commit yourself, to make reasons rather than find them. But the more I think about Stevens, the less sure I am that this framework even applies to him -- and that mismatch seems philosophically interesting in itself, not just a failed application.

Here's my thought. Stevens never actually deliberates. He doesn't weigh a life with Miss Kenton against his ideal of professional dignity; he just keeps polishing the silver while she cries behind a door he doesn't open. He doesn't weigh his father's dying moments upstairs against the conference downstairs. He doesn't weigh anything at all when Darlington tells him to dismiss the two Jewish maids -- Miss Kenton does the agonizing for him, threatening to quit, and he treats it as a puzzle about her mood rather than a question about what he's doing. So is the novel really about a hard choice gone wrong, or about a man who has arranged his entire life precisely so that he'll never have to make one? If values genuinely can be incommensurable, is submerging yourself in a role (the "great butler," where dignity means never taking off the costume) a way of refusing that condition -- and is the refusal itself a choice that can be judged, or a kind of moral self-amputation?

And where I'd like the essay to land: what does the pier scene at the end show about regret? Suppose the choice between dignity and love was genuinely hard in Chang's sense, the options on a par. Then what exactly does Stevens have to regret, given that on her view you can't go wrong in such cases -- you just fail to commit? Is his heartbreak evidence that the values weren't on a par after all, that he simply got it wrong? Or is it something more like agent-regret in Bernard Williams' sense, where you can appropriately regret what you were justified in doing? Or -- third option -- is the regret about something else entirely, not the choice but the self-deception: the fact that he can only narrate his life through Darlington's greatness, so that admitting the truth about Darlington would collapse the whole structure? I'd like a reading that takes both the book and the philosophy seriously -- don't flatten Stevens into an illustration of a theory, and don't wave the theory away because he's a fictional character. And be honest about where the novel resists the hard-choices framing, because I'm pretty sure it does, and naming that resistance might be the most interesting part.
