Before I commit to a framework for a comparative project on societal breakdown, I want the specification first: what must an adequate general theory of societal collapse deliver? Take the purpose as settled — the theory should let us say when a collapse has actually occurred and why that one happened, not predict future ones — and give me the desiderata in full: which cases it must get right (the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic-period Maya southern lowlands, the Western Roman Empire, Norse Greenland, Rapa Nui), which apparent counter-cases it must classify correctly (the Eastern Roman continuation, Egypt's recovery after each Intermediate Period, the Soviet dissolution), which distinctions it cannot collapse (decline vs. collapse vs. transformation; loss of state capacity vs. population loss vs. elite turnover; a polity failing vs. a people dispersing), and which established findings it cannot contradict — for instance, the linguistic and demographic continuity of Maya populations after the abandonment of the southern lowland cities, and the evidence that Rapa Nui's population catastrophe postdates European contact rather than preceding it. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it.

Then the harder questions. I suspect some of my desiderata are prejudices of particular camps — the marginal-returns-on-complexity school, the ecological-overshoot school, and the resilience-theory school — so mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are factional preferences wearing the costume of adequacy conditions. Tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable; I have in mind a possible conflict between demanding that collapse be objectively identifiable without normative input and demanding that the theory explain why post-collapse populations were sometimes better off (the skeletal evidence for improved health in post-Roman Britain). And which single requirement excludes the most existing contenders? Score the main families briefly against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria, since I will do the fuller scoring myself.

Finally, fold in two requirements of my own and tell me whether they are legitimate desiderata or idiosyncrasies: (i) the theory must solve the unit-of-analysis problem — specify what the collapsing entity is, given that in the Late Bronze Age palace economies vanished while interregional exchange networks merely restructured; and (ii) it must supply a principled criterion for when a stressor such as drought or invasion counts as the cause of a collapse rather than a trigger or a coincidence, given that comparably stressed societies have responded differently with different outcomes.
