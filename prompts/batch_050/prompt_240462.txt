I'm a PhD student in political philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on historical theories of distributive justice, and I keep running into the same wall with the Wilt Chamberlain argument. The standard case moves from a distribution everyone in the story accepts (D1) to a greatly unequal distribution (D2) through a long chain of fully voluntary transfers, and then challenges the egalitarian to explain on what grounds anyone could object to D2 without endorsing constant interference in people's lives. My advisor thinks the inference embedded here — that whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just — is the real load-bearing claim of Nozick's entitlement theory, and she wants me to stress-test it with a carefully engineered counterexample rather than the usual gesturing at exploitation. That's what I'd like your help with.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Begin from a distribution that is just by the entitlement theory's own lights: holdings acquired in ways that satisfy the principle of justice in acquisition, including whatever proviso on appropriation you take Nozick to require. Then describe a sequence of transactions, each one individually voluntary, with no coercion, no fraud, no deception, and no violation of anyone's property rights at any step, such that the endpoint distribution is one that even a reader sympathetic to Nozick should balk at calling just — not merely very unequal, since Wilt Chamberlain already delivers that comfortably, but objectionable in some further respect that you identify. Constraints: the scenario should be economically realistic — a labor market, a housing market, or a platform economy, not a demon, a utility monster, or an all-seeing predictor; the setup should take no more than a paragraph or two; and the troubling feature must emerge from the aggregation or interaction of the voluntary transfers themselves, not from an unjust acquisition or a rights violation smuggled in at some step. A case that stipulates its verdict rather than generating it from the theory's own principles is useless to me.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: walk through the chain and confirm that justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and (if it comes up) rectification are satisfied at each stage, and explain precisely where the intuition of injustice comes from and why it cannot be pinned on any single transaction. Then identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work — for instance, erosion of the proviso across many individually permissible appropriations, market-mediated effects on third parties that the transfer principle simply does not register, or the gradual foreclosure of an option that no individual trader was responsible for preserving — and say what happens to your case if that feature is removed: does it collapse back into a standard Wilt Chamberlain scenario that Nozick handles without strain? Finally, give the strongest response available to a defender of the entitlement theory, whether that is biting the bullet, invoking the proviso, or appealing to rectification, and say whether you think the response succeeds. One request about format: please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
