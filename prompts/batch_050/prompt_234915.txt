Okay so I sit on a hospital's advance-directive review committee. A few times a month we get cases where a patient signed an advance directive years ago (refusing dialysis, say, or a feeding tube), the patient's condition has now changed, and the question comes up: does this document still speak for this patient? Sometimes the issue is dementia and the patient can't remember signing anything. Sometimes the family says the person had "become someone else" after a religious conversion or a stroke, so the old directive shouldn't count. Sometimes the patient remembers signing it perfectly but the family claims the person who signed it was coerced or depressed and "wasn't really them." We have maybe two hours per case, none of us are philosophers, and right now we just argue in circles.

You're a philosopher who specializes in personal identity and who's unusually good at translating theory into things committees can actually use. Think carefully about this before answering, and feel free to brainstorm about what the theory can and can't carry before you commit to a design.

Here's the philosophical backdrop I want you to work from. Locke's view, roughly, is that a person at one time is the same person as someone at an earlier time just in case the later person can remember the earlier person's experiences and actions -- consciousness is what makes personal identity. Thomas Reid, in his Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, raised a famous objection with the case of the brave officer: imagine a boy who is flogged at school for robbing an orchard, who grows up to be a junior officer who takes an enemy standard in battle, and who eventually becomes a general. The grown officer remembers the flogging; the general remembers taking the standard but has completely forgotten the flogging. On Locke's account the officer is the same person as the boy, and the general is the same person as the officer, but the general is *not* the same person as the boy -- and Reid points out that this makes a hash of identity, since identity is supposed to be transitive: if the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, the general just is the boy, memory or no memory.

What I want from you:

1. A decision procedure our committee can run in our two-hour window. Not an essay -- an ordered set of questions, what counts as an adequate answer to each, and at which points we stop deliberating and the directive stands or falls. The procedure should tell us which questions are actually about identity (does this patient stand in the right relation to the signer?) versus which ones are sneaky substitutes (is this patient still competent? was the signer competent? whose values should win when identity is unclear?) -- because I suspect half our circular arguments come from conflating these.

2. The translation has to make a call about Reid's objection, and this is the crux. If we run a strict Lockean memory test, we get Reid's transitivity problem: a chain where each link is fine but the endpoints aren't connected. If we accept linked-chain or overlapping-memory fixes (the general counts as the boy because each stage remembers the previous one), we seem to rescue Locke but at the cost of letting the document bind someone with zero memory of making it. If we drop memory as a criterion entirely, what's left -- bodily continuity? psychological continuity of some broader kind? Tell us which criterion the procedure should actually operationalize, and be explicit about what we're giving up with each option. I want you to recommend one, not just lay out a menu.

3. Run the procedure on three cases: (a) a patient with moderate dementia who cannot remember signing a dialysis refusal five years ago but is otherwise clearly continuous with the signer in every biographical respect; (b) a patient who had a major stroke, underwent a dramatic personality change the family describes as becoming a different person, but clearly remembers signing the directive; (c) a patient who remembers signing but whose signature is on a document executed during a severe depressive episode that later resolved. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer our committee probably won't like, and say so.

4. Mark clearly where the procedure can't decide and a human value judgment has to be made -- don't dress up a judgment call as a mechanical step.

One more thing on calibration: please distinguish carefully between what's settled ground here and what's genuinely contested. My understanding is that Reid's objection is widely treated as a real problem for a naive memory criterion, but that whether Locke himself is actually vulnerable to it (or was saying something subtler about "person" as a forensic term) is disputed among scholars, and the linked-chain repair has defenders and critics. Don't present any of these as consensus if they aren't -- I'd rather have honest uncertainty than a tidy story, since we're going to be making real decisions off this.
