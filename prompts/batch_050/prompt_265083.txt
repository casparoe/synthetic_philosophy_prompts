I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and a rereading of Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach has pushed me toward a position I now want to stress-test. In the Ant Fugue, Hofstadter gives us Aunt Hillary, a conscious colony composed of ants that individually know nothing of the whole, the way neurons know nothing of thoughts. I hold the strong version of this: colony-level minds are possible in principle, with no lower bound on how stupid the parts may be, provided the pattern of interactions is right. But I also hold a further thesis that I haven't seen defended anywhere: our refusal to ascribe minds to actual ant colonies, corporations, or ecosystems has nothing to do with their internal organization and everything to do with timescale. These systems compute their colony-level responses too slowly for us to converse with; if the signaling among an ant colony's members were sped up a millionfold, so that colony-level answers to colony-level questions arrived in seconds, we would ascribe beliefs and desires to it without hesitation. My claim is that this timescale dependence is doing illegitimate work in our intuitions about which systems are minded, and that mind attribution is in this respect more about the observer's tempo than the system's structure.

Acting as a careful philosopher of mind, tell me what would count against this position. I'd like you to begin by brainstorming the full space of objections -- functionalist, biological-naturalist, panpsychist, pattern-realist, and any others -- and then select only those with real bite, including considerations my own side (functionalists and Dennett-style pattern realists) would concede as threatening, not merely what critics of functionalism already say. Distinguish what would be genuinely decisive from what would merely be awkward, say what a rival view predicts that mine doesn't, and address whether my timescale thesis risks anything at all or has been insulated against refutation by its own framing -- and if it has been, whether that is a vice for a thesis of this kind. Present your answer as exactly seven numbered items, each one or two sentences: the first six ordered from most to least decisive, and the seventh devoted to the question of unfalsifiability, with no introduction and no conclusion.
