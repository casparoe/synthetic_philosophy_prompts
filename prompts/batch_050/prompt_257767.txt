A friend of mine who reads a lot of decision theory recently gave me an account of the absent-minded driver problem, and before I repeat any of it I would like to know how much to trust. First, the setup as I understand it: a driver is traveling along a highway that has two exits. The exits look identical, and the driver is absent-minded in a specific way — when she arrives at an exit, she cannot remember whether she has already passed the other one, so her situation at either exit appears exactly the same to her. Getting off at the first exit yields a payoff of 0; getting off at the second yields 4; staying on the highway past both exits yields 1. She has no way to leave herself reminders and no device for making an advance commitment she cannot later undo.

Here is his summary, as best I remember it:

"The puzzle was constructed to show that a perfectly rational agent can be forced to act against her own best plan. Before the trip, the driver can work out that the best she can do is adopt a mixed strategy: exit at any given exit with probability one half. But once she is actually sitting at an exit, deliberation tells her something different. Since she cannot tell the exits apart, continuing is what eventually gets her to the second exit, where the big payoff is, so at the moment of choice she should stay on the highway. Whatever she planned, rationality at the moment of action overrules it. The lesson of the problem is that ideal rationality requires perfect memory: forgetfulness doesn't merely cost you information, it makes rational planning and rational acting come apart. It is essentially the same puzzle as the Sleeping Beauty case, just with exits instead of awakenings."

Something in this feels off to me, though I cannot say where. I have a vague sense that the probability is not one half, and that the Sleeping Beauty comparison papers over a real difference. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts would a decision theorist accept as accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent the problem or the standard results about it? In particular, tell me whether his account of what the driver should do at the exit, and his moral that rationality requires memory, follow from what is actually true about the case — and whether the tension he describes is a genuine inconsistency in rationality or something the literature regards differently.

When you correct him, please indicate how confident you are in each correction, and single out the one point where you are least sure you have the standard treatment right.
