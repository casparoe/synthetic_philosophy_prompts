I sit on the research ethics committee of a regional hospital network, and we have a proposal on the table I need to write our opinion on. The background: when a patient here gets newly diagnosed hypertension and needs a first-line med, doctors in our system pick between two standard drugs essentially by habit -- where they trained, what the drug reps pushed ten years ago, whatever's in the sample cabinet. There's no good evidence that either is better than the other for the average patient.

A research team wants the EHR to randomly default each new hypertension patient to one of the two drugs, with the doctor able to override in one click if they have a patient-specific reason, and then track outcomes (blood pressure control, side effects, adherence) across the network for two years. Their pitch: both arms are bog-standard care, no patient loses anything they were owed, randomization is more principled than the coin flip of physician habit, and if we require written consent from every patient the study takes a decade, the consenters won't look like the refusers, and the knowledge just never gets generated. They propose a general notice at intake ("your care may contribute to system learning") and no study-specific consent.

The committee is split. One member says this is experimenting on patients without consent, full stop: randomizing a treatment choice in order to generate generalizable knowledge is research, the fact that each option is individually acceptable doesn't make choosing between them by lottery for science acceptable, and the SUPPORT trial uproar a while back showed where that reasoning leads. Another member says it's backwards to demand consent for a rigorous comparison of two things doctors do anyway while demanding nothing at all for the status quo of prescribing by whim.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before next week's meeting. It should: land on approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with the conditions stated concretely (including a specific trigger for pausing the trial if the accumulating data starts favoring one drug); examine whether the concept of clinical equipoise actually does the work the researchers think it does, and whether it matters that the choice here is random-by-design rather than random-by-habit; and steelman the strongest argument on the losing side rather than brushing past it. Around 500-700 words.
