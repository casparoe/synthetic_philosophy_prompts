Last night, over several hours, a friend and I argued to a standstill about interpersonal comparisons of welfare, and I would like a referee who will commit to a number. The disagreement began with a trivial case: our host offered the last slice of cake to whichever of us wanted it more, and my friend claimed that this question, taken literally, has no answer -- not merely that we cannot know the answer, but that there is no fact of the matter as to which of us would enjoy the slice more, because there is no shared scale on which her enjoyment and mine could both be measured. I maintained that such comparisons are ordinarily meaningful and often true. We eventually stipulated away the easy evasions: assume that each of us can consistently rank and even quantify our own preferences, that neither of us is lying or self-deceived, and that we are not asking about any practical decision procedure. The question is purely metaphysical: does the proposition "she would derive more welfare from the slice than I would" have a determinate truth value, independent of any convention we might adopt for measuring it?

At one point I proposed a test that I thought would settle it, and this is where the argument became genuinely interesting rather than merely repetitive. Imagine a device that lets a third party live through my hour with the cake exactly as I experience it, and then my friend's hour with it exactly as she experiences it, with full fidelity. My friend replied that this settles nothing: the observer still has to compare the two experiences, and the comparison will be made on the observer's scale, which reintroduces the very problem -- whose unit of welfare governs the comparison? I replied that if the observer's verdict is forced on any rational subject who undergoes both experiences, that is enough objectivity for the comparison to be truth-apt. She said a forced verdict shows at most a shared convention wired into our psychology, not a fact about us.

Please give me your own credence that interpersonal welfare comparisons, understood as above, are truth-apt -- that at least some claims of the form "A would benefit more from X than B would" are determinately true or false. But I do not want the number alone; I want the derivation. Decompose the question into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on: for instance, whether within-person welfare is itself a determinate magnitude (as opposed to a mere ordering); whether, granting that, there is a privileged or natural mapping between two persons' scales rather than indefinitely many equally legitimate rescalings; whether my device would identify such a mapping or merely relocate the problem to the observer; and whether the obvious epistemic inaccessibility of other minds is merely epistemic or is evidence that there is nothing there to access. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question with a sentence of justification, state how you take them to combine into your final number, and identify the term to which your overall credence is most sensitive. Then, since my friend sits near 0.1 and I sit near 0.75, tell me which of the sub-questions in your decomposition most plausibly houses our disagreement, and what argument or consideration would move your own number furthest in either direction.
