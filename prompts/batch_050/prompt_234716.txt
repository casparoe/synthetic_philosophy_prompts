I am a software engineer by training and trade, and I moonlight as the organizer of a small science-outreach nonprofit in my city. Every February we host a Darwin Day dinner, and this year the board asked me to deliver the after-dinner address, partly because nobody else wanted it. Our programming this year has centered on The Descent of Man, which makes the assignment delicate. Half the room will be working evolutionary biologists and graduate students who know the book well and will notice every evasion; the other half will be members of local congregations who co-sponsor the dinner because they see no conflict between their faith and evolutionary science, and who will notice every sneer. The book itself is the problem: it is the one where Darwin extended his theory to the human mind, the moral sense, and religious belief, and it also contains passages on race and on women that no one at a celebratory dinner would defend. I found my way to what I want the speech to be about while reading the preface to the second edition of 1874, where Darwin writes, immediately after insisting his conviction about sexual selection remains unshaken: "It is probable, or almost certain, that several of my conclusions will hereafter be found erroneous; this can hardly fail to be the case in the first treatment of a subject."

That sentence struck me because my profession produces postmortems and deprecation notices, but nothing like a preface announcing, before the reader has begun, that the author believes his own conclusions are probably in part wrong. I want a speech of about six minutes, roughly eight hundred words, built around this passage rather than merely mentioning it. The speech should open with the quotation and take seriously a genuine interpretive question it raises: whether "several of my conclusions" is meant to cover the whole book or only the sexual selection theory Darwin had just been defending, and what difference each reading makes to how we should receive his chapters on morality and religion. I see two ways this speech can fail, and I want the speech to steer between them using the passage as its rudder. The first failure is triumphalism: treating Darwin Day as a victory lap over the religious guests, which would misread a man who wrote that sentence. The second is hollow diplomacy: praising Darwin's "humility" in a way that quietly drains his claims of content, which the scientists in the room will hear as a dodge. The harder question the speech must answer is whether celebrating a thinker for his method and intellectual honesty, rather than for the truth of his conclusions, is a genuine form of honor or an elegant way of saying nothing; the speech should take a position on this, not gesture at it.

A few constraints. The passage should do structural work, meaning the speech's central argument should be one that could not survive its removal. Do not quote any other passages from Darwin verbatim, since I cannot verify them before dinner; paraphrase his arguments about the moral sense and the natural history of religious belief as needed. The speech should include one moment that plainly names something in the book Darwin got wrong, using the preface passage to explain why that admission is not an embarrassment at an event like this but rather the point of it. It should be warm enough for an after-dinner setting, may include one joke, and should not require the audience to have read the book. Along with the speech, give me a short note explaining how the passage shaped your choices, one paragraph on which of the two failure modes you found harder to avoid and why, and a flag on any line you nearly cut as too risky for one half of the room.
