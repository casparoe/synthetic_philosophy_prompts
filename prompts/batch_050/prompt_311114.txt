I coordinate a monthly philosophy evening at our town's public library. None of us has any formal training in philosophy — we are readers, not scholars — and next month it falls to me to lead a session on a passage I have been turning over on my own for some weeks. It is the thought experiment known as the "flying man," from Avicenna's discussion of the soul in his Book of Healing. Here is the passage, in the English rendering I have been working from:

"One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence."

I do not want the argument explained to me in full — I want a plan for how I should lead a room of about fifteen curious adults through it in seventy-five minutes, so that they grasp what the passage is actually doing rather than leaving with a slogan. Specifically, I would like your advice on the following.

First, the order of ideas. My sense is that before anyone reads a word of the passage, the room has to be comfortable with a distinction the whole thing turns on: the difference between not being aware of something and that thing not existing. Tell me what has to be in place before the passage can do its work, in what sequence, and why the sequence matters — which piece depends on which.

Second, the carrying example. The floating man himself is the example, but I suspect he cannot carry the crucial step alone, because the argument seems to move from "he would affirm his self without affirming his body" to "his self is therefore something other than his body," and that move is exactly where a careful reader should press. Give me one ordinary, non-philosophical case — I have been thinking along the lines of affirming that the person at the door is my neighbour while not affirming that the person at the door is the tax inspector, though they are one and the same — that would let the room feel the difference between "I do not affirm that X" and "I affirm that not-X," and tell me at what point in the session to introduce it so that it sharpens the passage rather than deflating it prematurely.

Third, the misconceptions. I can already predict some of what the room will bring: that this is simply a proof that the soul survives death; that it is Descartes' "I think, therefore I am" six centuries early and so nothing new; that the floating man would in fact experience nothing at all; and that being able to imagine yourself without a body proves you could exist without one. Tell me which of these I should head off in advance and which I should let arise naturally and then correct, and exactly where in the sequence each correction belongs.

Fourth, where the argumentative weight actually falls. I would like the session to end with the room able to say, in their own words, precisely which sentence of the passage does the real work and which step a sceptic could refuse without refusing the thought experiment itself. Suggest the closing question I should put to them to test whether that has landed — ideally one that makes them apply the distinction to a case we have not discussed, so I can tell understanding apart from agreement.

Finally, tell me what I should openly set aside at this level — I assume the Arabic terminology, the question of what Avicenna meant by "self" across his different works, and the later Islamic commentary tradition are all beyond us for one evening — and how to say so without making the room feel they have been given a watered-down version.
