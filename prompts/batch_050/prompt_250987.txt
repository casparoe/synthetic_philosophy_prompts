I'm a high school senior, and our school runs a small philosophy-and-economics society that meets after class. Last week our faculty advisor introduced us to the Ellsberg paradox: you face two urns, the first containing exactly 50 red and 50 black balls, the second containing 100 red and black balls in an unknown proportion, and most people strictly prefer to bet on a color drawn from the first urn — even though, by symmetry, a draw from the second urn seems to give each color probability one-half as well. My friend and I stayed late trying to turn that observation into an argument, and we ended up somewhere neither of us wants to be. Here is where we landed: (1) Rational preferences over bets are preferences that can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some single probability assignment over the relevant events. (2) The usual Ellsberg pattern — preferring a bet on red from the known urn to a bet on red from the unknown urn, while also preferring a bet on black from the known urn to a bet on black from the unknown urn — cannot be so represented, since the first preference requires assigning red a higher probability than black and the second requires the reverse. (3) So the Ellsberg pattern is irrational, and rationality requires holding precise credences — one definite number — for every event you might bet on. (4) But the events of real life — whether a particular friendship will last, whether a particular career will suit me, whether it will rain on graduation day — are all "unknown urn" events: nothing in the world fixes their frequencies the way a composed urn does. (5) By (3), then, I am rationally required to assign each of these a precise number and to be exactly as willing to stake my future on them at the implied odds as I would be to stake it on a coin toss at matching odds; anyone who hesitates, who would rather take the "known" gamble than the life gamble, is simply irrational in the way the Ellsberg choosers are. (6) That conclusion seems to flatten a distinction any sane decision-maker must respect, and it also demands something psychologically impossible, since I cannot summon a precise credence for most of these events by an act of will. (7) Something in the chain must therefore be rejected — but my friend and I disagree about which link, and every candidate we test seems to cost more than we expected.

Which premise or step should we give up, and what does giving it up actually cost? If we have to keep something, I would most like to keep (2) and (6); my friend would most like to keep (1). Please explain why the step you reject looked innocent in the first place, and what the main positions in this neighborhood — strict probabilism on one side, and the models that permit agents to be averse to ambiguity on the other — each have to pay for their way out. End your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the premise or step you reject.
