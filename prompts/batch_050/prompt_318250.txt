I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and one recurring fault line in discussions of the ergodicity economics program — the work associated with Ole Peters and collaborators, arguing that agents should maximize the time-average growth rate of their wealth rather than its expected utility — is that participants talk past each other. Some treat the program as a refutation of expected utility theory; others dismiss it as logarithmic utility wearing a new coat; others regard it as a narrow result about repeated multiplicative bets that has been inflated into a manifesto. Our local meetup keeps re-litigating this, and I'd like to settle the geography of the debate once and for all.

Please construct a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on this dispute, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Requirements:

- The tree begins from a shared starting commitment: the reader accepts the arithmetic facts that, for a multiplicative process, the time-average growth rate equals the expectation of the logarithm of the growth factor, and that the ensemble average of wealth can grow while almost every individual trajectory decays. The tree's job is to find out what the reader makes of these facts.
- Every branch node should be a question the reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Candidates include: whether the St. Petersburg paradox is dissolved by attending to the dynamics of repeated play or only by curving a utility function; whether a genuinely one-shot gamble falls within the theory's scope and, if not, whether that silence is a defect; whether risk aversion is best understood as a property of preferences or as a rational response to the payoff structure; whether the practical wisdom of the program (don't take bets where ruin is on the table, even at favorable odds) is really driven by ruin and absorbing states rather than by the time/ensemble distinction as such; and whether growth-rate maximization should be read as a normative principle, a descriptive hypothesis about actual agents, or merely one objective among several a rational agent might adopt. Use these or better ones of your own devising, but phrase each as a concrete case or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart — never as "do you accept ergodicity?"
- The leaves should include at least: the "nothing new" view (ergodicity economics is expected utility with logarithmic utility, rederived); the "new foundation" view (time-average growth is more fundamental than utility, which should be derived from dynamics rather than posited); the "restricted tool" view (correct and genuinely useful for repeated multiplicative settings, silent or misleading elsewhere); the "descriptively false" view (people demonstrably do not maximize growth rates, so the program fails as behavioral economics whatever its normative merits); and the "wrong diagnosis" view (the practical advice is sound, but irreversibility and ruin, not non-ergodicity per se, are doing the real work). Add further leaves if the logical space demands them.
- For each leaf, give the position a name (or mark it "unnamed" if nobody defends it), state its single most awkward commitment in one sentence, and indicate in general terms — no citations — which strands of the rationalist-adjacent and econophysics discussion roughly occupy it.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and identify any combination of answers that is logically available but apparently unoccupied, with a sentence on whether the vacancy looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for roughly eight to twelve branch nodes. The intended reader is mathematically literate and familiar with the Kelly criterion and the expected-value-versus-expected-utility discourse, but has not picked a side.
