Two of my grad students have spent the whole semster going at each other about the Meno problem -- why knowledge is supposed to be better than mere true belief -- and I've finally told them to stop arguing and run an adversarial collaboration instead. Draft the joint statement they should come back with.

Here's where they stand. Anna is a swamping-problem skeptic: once a belief is true, she thinks, the reliability or competence of whatever produced it adds no further value to the belief itself -- her favorite analogy is that a perfect espresso isn't made any better by having come from a reliable machine -- so whatever extra value knowledge has must be derivative: knowledge is more stable under new evidence, safer to act on, less likely to collapse when you reflect on it. Ben is a performance-normativity guy, roughly in the mold of Sosa and Greco: knowledge is an achievement, a belief that's accurate because competent (apt, in the lingo), and achievements have final value that lucky successes lack, so knowledge is non-derivatively better than mere true belief in the way a bullseye you actually shot is better than one the wind gave you.

The document I want from you has four parts:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list both would sign. Make them substantive, not platitudes like "knowledge is valuable." Anna has to concede that the intuition in achievement cases is real and needs explaining away, and Ben has to concede that lucky true belief is often just as action-guiding as knowledge, maybe even equally valuable for a one-shot decision.

2. Stated disagreements. The remaining disputes, each phrased neutrally enough that both would accept the wording of their own position. Flag anything that turned out to be merely verbal once pinned down -- I strongly suspect they use "epistemic value" in different senses, and that half their fights were really about whether the Meno question asks for epistemic value specifically or just any non-derivative value.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. My guess: one crux is whether the achievement view overgenerates, i.e. whether every competent true belief, however trivial or however lucky in its content, gets final value; another is whether Anna's swamping argument proves too much, since the same logic says a skilled archer's bullseye is worth no more than a gust-assisted one.

4. Tests. For each crux, one concrete case or comparison they'd both accept as bearing on it, with each one's prediction recorded in advance so nobody can retrofit after hearing the intuition pump. For the archery crux, for instance: two archers hit identical bullseyes, one by skill, one by freak gust. Ben predicts a robust intuition that the skilled shot is better as an achievement, surviving full stipulation that the shooter will never shoot again. Anna predicts the intuition evaporates once you strip out every practical consideration (who you'd bet on next time, who deserves the trophy), and that what's left over is aesthetic or moral, not epistemic -- and she'd say that even if she loses on archery, belief isn't a performance in the relevant sense anyway.

Write it in their voices as co-authors, not in mine as referee, and don't adjudicate: the whole point is that both of them can put their names on it, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved.
