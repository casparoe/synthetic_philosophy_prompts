So I'm a journalist, mostly covering science and ideas for a mid-tier magazine, and I've been commissioned to write a feature on futarchy -- Robin Hanson's proposal that we vote on values but bet on beliefs, i.e. keep electing what to aim at but let prediction markets decide which policies will actually get us there. My editor loves the pitch but keeps pressing me on whether it's actually any good or just a fun thought experiment, and honestly I've realised I can't answer her. Here's the concrete scenario I've been using as the spine of the piece: suppose a mid-sized, reasonably well-governed country (think Estonia-ish) agrees to run a ten-year pilot where major infrastructure decisions -- whether to build the flood barrier, the motorway, the nuclear plant -- are settled by conditional prediction markets. A contract pays out on national GDP per capita in year ten *if the project goes ahead*, another pays out on the same measure *if it doesn't*, and the project gets built only if the go-ahead market prices higher. My question: what is your actual credence, as a percentage, that this pilot produces demonstrably better decisions than the ordinary ministerial-committee-plus-consultants process it replaces?

I really don't want "it depends on the details" -- of course it does, but I want a number and, more importantly, the working. Break it down for me: how much of your credence rides on whether conditional markets like this can ever be liquid enough to be informative, given that the losing branch never resolves and so traders can't be paid on it (which I gather guts the incentive to bet on the "don't build" side)? How much rides on whether punters in a market can actually out-forecast domain experts on ten-year GDP effects, which seems like a heroic claim about the epistemics of crowds? How much on the manipulation problem -- a contractor with billions at stake surely tries to move the market, and the question is whether that backfires by attracting informed money, or just works? And how much on the deeper worry that GDP per capita is doing all the normative lifting here, so futarchy quietly presupposes we can agree on a welfare metric, when the whole point was supposed to be that we only fight about values and let markets handle facts -- except choosing the metric turns out to be the biggest value fight of all?

Finally: which of these sub-questions is your final number most sensitive to -- i.e. if I handed you decisive evidence on one of them, which one moves your credence the most and in which direction? For calibration, my editor reckons the pilot succeeds with probability around 60% ("markets beat committees, simple as"), I'm sitting nearer 15%, and I'd like to know which of the sub-questions our disagreement most likely lives in, because that's the one I should spend my remaining wordcount on.
