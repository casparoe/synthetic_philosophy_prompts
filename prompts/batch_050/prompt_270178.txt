The question was: "Hedonism about value says only pleasure is intrinsically good. But pleasures seem wildly diverse -- the pleasure of solving a proof, of a hot bath, of seeing an old friend. State the 'heterogeneity problem' this raises for hedonism, and explain the two main strategies hedonists use to answer it (the felt-quality strategy and the attitudinal strategy). Does either succeed?"

Student's answer:

The heterogeneity problem is that if all these different experiences count as good in the same way, namely as pleasures, hedonism owes us an account of what they have in common. It can't just be that we call them all 'pleasure' -- that's the word, not the thing. If there's no shared feature, then hedonism isn't really one theory of the good, it's a disguised pluralism.

The felt-quality strategy says there is a common phenomenological element, some kind of pleasant feeling-tone that bath-pleasure and proof-pleasure both contain, even if its hard to introspect. The attitudinal strategy, defended by Fred Feldman, says pleasure is a sensation with a characteristic feel that is intrinsically good, so the unity is phenomenological after all.

Mill anticipated this problem and solved it with his distinction between higher and lower pleasures: pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, and the higher ones are more valuable. This handles heterogeneity by ranking the pleasures instead of unifying them.

I think the felt-quality strategy succeeds, since introspection does reveal a shared glow across pleasures, so the problem doesn't actually threaten hedonism.
