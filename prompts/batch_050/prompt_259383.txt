Overheard two classmates going at it after our phil of mind seminar and they roped me in, but I genuinely can't tell who's winning. The question was whether phenomenal consciousness outruns access -- whether you can experience more than you can ever report or use. Alex cited the classic partial-report finding (whole grid of letters flashed briefly, you feel like you saw all of them but can only name a few until a cue tells you which row) as proof that experience is rich and access is a thin bottleneck. Priya said that only shows the letters were stored in some fragile short-term form, not that there was a detailed *experience* of each one -- maybe you just had a vague gist plus confidence, and the cue works because the raw signal is still around, not because some fully specific letter-experience was being read out. Alex then said: fine, but even grant that, you can know exactly what you saw in one row and it still feels like the others were there too, and that feeling has to be *something*, and it's not access. Priya's comeback was that the feeling might just be the access of generic information ("letters everywhere") and Alex is confusing seeing that there were specific letters with having specific experiences of them. Alex then accused her of making phenomenal consciousness definitionally impossible to detect, so her position wins by design. Priya said no, her view makes a prediction Alex's doesn't: if you could get people to bet on uncued rows at better than chance with real confidence, that'd embarrass her; Alex's view can absorb any result. Alex muttered something about unfalsifiability cutting both ways. Neither budged. Who's actually right about which moves here -- and is there anything both of them are missing about what would even settle this?
