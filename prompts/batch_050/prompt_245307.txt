I'm a staff engineer at a mid-sized SaaS company (~400 employees), and I've convinced our CEO to let me pitch a six-month pilot to the executive team: running a slice of our product-prioritization decisions through internal prediction markets, futarchy-style. The proposal: when leadership is split between two candidate features, we open two conditional markets — "if we ship feature A by June, what will net revenue retention be in Q4?" and the same for B — let any employee trade with a real-money stake capped at a few hundred dollars per person, subsidize liquidity so thin markets still move, and ship whichever feature the markets price higher. My planned framing is that this separates values from beliefs: leadership still owns the metric, but the market, not the loudest voice in the room, estimates which option best serves it.

The audience: our CEO (ex-consultant), CFO, VP of Sales, and VP of Engineering. None of them has encountered prediction markets beyond election odds. The CEO is intrigued but allergic to anything that looks like abdicating judgment. The CFO will zero in on gaming and legal exposure. The VP of Sales distrusts any quantitative process he can't interrogate face to face. The VP of Engineering is quietly worried that engineers who dislike a feature will short it and then sandbag the build, making the market a self-fulfilling veto.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get, phrased the way each of these four people would phrase them in their own vocabulary, ordered by likelihood and by how much damage each does if it lands badly. For each: a reply I can deliver in under a minute, an honest note on where that reply is weakest, and whether I should concede the point up front or hold it. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody raises, and what I do if it comes up.

Be committed rather than even-handed. Tell me which single objection is genuinely dangerous to the pilot, as opposed to merely uncomfortable. And make definite design calls instead of listing options — in particular, I want a verdict on the problem that the losing branch never gets tested: if we ship A, market B never resolves, so traders had no real incentive to price B honestly. Tell me whether that's fatal to my design or manageable, and exactly how you'd manage it.
