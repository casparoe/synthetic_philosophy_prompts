Here is an argument a colleague pressed on me after a reading group on Hume's "Of Miracles," and I cannot decide whether it exposes a flaw in Hume or merely states Hume's conclusion in an unflattering way. It runs as follows. (1) A rational person proportions belief to the evidence, and testimony is only one kind of evidence, to be weighed against everything else one knows. (2) Testimony to an event justifies belief in that event only when the testimony's being false would be more improbable than the event itself. (3) A miracle, as Hume characterizes it, is a violation of a regularity established by uniform human experience, so its improbability is as high as anything's can be. (4) Therefore no quantity of human testimony can make belief in a miracle rational. (5) But notice that the reasoning nowhere essentially uses the concept of the supernatural. It applies with equal force to any event that would be maximally surprising relative to the recipient's prior experience. (6) History supplies many such cases: educated Europeans long dismissed reports of stones falling from the sky, which we now call meteorites, precisely because such events contradicted their settled experience, and travelers' reports of unknown animals and unfamiliar natural phenomena met similar receptions. (7) So the argument implies that these dismissals were rational, and more generally that dismissing first reports of genuinely unprecedented phenomena is always the rational response. (8) That conclusion is false: sometimes the rational response to highly surprising testimony is to revise one's picture of what is possible, and the meteorite skeptics were wrong in the end. (9) Therefore the argument against testimony to miracles goes wrong somewhere, since valid reasoning from the same premises reaches an absurd result.

My suspicion is that the argument proves too much, but I cannot locate the step that does the damage, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Specifically: which premise or transition should be rejected, and why did it look innocent? Hume himself appears to have resources for distinguishing (4) from (7) — he separates events contrary to uniform experience from events merely foreign to a particular observer's experience, as in his discussion of the Indian prince who reasonably refused to believe reports of ice. Can that distinction be drawn without begging the question, given that whether a regularity counts as a law of nature or a local accident is exactly what surprising testimony sometimes reveals? If the distinction fails, does the argument show Hume was wrong, or does it show his maxim was always about degrees of belief rather than outright dismissal, in which case (7) misstates what (1)–(3) commit him to? Please also price the alternatives: what it costs to give up (2), what it costs to accept a qualified version of (7) in which initial dismissal is rational but cumulative testimony must eventually win, and what it costs to hold that miracle reports are epistemically special because they compete with the very evidence that established the regularities they violate. If forced to choose, I would rather preserve (2) than deny (8), but I want to know what I am paying.
