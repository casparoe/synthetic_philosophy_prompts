I sit on the clinical priorities committee of a mid-sized regional hospital network, and we're stuck on something that's turned weirdly theoretical. The question on the table: whether to spend about $400k on a one-year pilot of a new sepsis screening pathway in our emergency departments before deciding whether to roll it out network-wide, which would cost roughly $2M and a lot of staff retraining. Data from two other hospital systems suggest the pathway cuts time-to-antibiotics, but their patient mix isn't ours, and our internal review puts it at maybe a 60-65% chance the benefit replicates here. The pilot would give us a much sharper read, though it'd be underpowered for rare outcomes and there's a real chance it comes back inconclusive.

Here's where it got philosophical. One of our younger members, who apparently took a decision theory course in college and has never recovered, circulated a note invoking something called Good's theorem -- roughly, that for a rational Bayesian decision-maker the expected value of acquiring information before a decision is never negative, so the only live question is whether the pilot's cost exceeds the expected savings from avoiding a bad rollout (or a bad refusal). His conclusion: fund the pilot, basically automatically. Our most senior member pushed back hard, and not just on cost. Her argument, as I understood it over a very long meeting, is that the theorem assumes the decision-maker will actually use the information optimally, and a hospital network is not a Bayesian agent: in her experience, an inconclusive or weakly-negative pilot doesn't get read as "insufficient evidence," it gets read as "failure," and the board kills the program even when the prior case for it was strong. So the information could, in practice, make us *less* likely to adopt a pathway we currently think is probably good. She also pointed out there are pilot results under which nothing changes -- if it's strongly positive we were leaning toward rollout anyway -- so we'd have paid $400k for a coin flip we ignore half the time.

I have to write the committee's decision memo before our next meeting, and I want it to actually engage this rather than just count votes. Please draft the memo: a clear recommendation (fund the pilot, decline it, or fund it with conditions -- your call, but commit to one), with reasoning both sides could respond to. Specifically: take the Good's theorem argument seriously and identify which of its assumptions might genuinely fail when "the decision-maker" is a committee plus a board with its own politics, rather than a single expected-utility maximizer; give the strongest honest version of whichever side loses; and if you recommend conditions on the pilot (e.g., pre-committing to how different results would be interpreted), say what they are and why they'd fix the problem rather than just papering over it. Somewhere around 600-800 words is plenty.
