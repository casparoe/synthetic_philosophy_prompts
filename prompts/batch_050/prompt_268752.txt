So my schools philosophy club is doing Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments this term, and the teacher running it wants everyone to write down their "position" on Smith before the next meeting so the others can poke holes in it. Below is mine. Before I hand it in I want a proper stress test, so here are my commitments:

(1) Sympathy, for Smith, is imaginative projection: you put yourself in the other persons situation and feel what you would feel there. It doesn't require the other person to actually have the feeling — that's why we can sympathize with the dead, or cringe for someone who doesn't even realize they're being embarrassing.

(2) Moral approval is just this sympathy succeeding. To approve of someone's gratitude or resentment is to find, when I imagine myself in their shoes, that I'd feel the same thing. Moral judgment = a match between the spectator's sentiments and the agent's.

(3) The impartial spectator is a device inside your own head, so the opinions of actual people are irrelevant to whether your moral judgments are right. Someone raised alone on a desert island could in principle make perfectly good moral judgments.

(4) Since it's sentiment all the way down, there's no objective moral truth underneath. So when Smith says custom can corrupt moral sentiments — like the ancient Greeks being fine with infanticide — all he can really mean is that we disapprove of them, not that they were actually wrong.

(5) What actually gets people to behave morally is wanting praise and fearing blame from the people around them, wich is also why everyone chases wealth and status even though, as Smith himself points out, those things don't really make you happier.

(6) Conscience, i.e. the impartial spectator, gives you a standard that can overrule both your own immediate feelings and the crowd's opinion.
