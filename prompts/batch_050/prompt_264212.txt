In 1973, the University of California, Berkeley's graduate admissions figures showed a striking gap: aggregated across departments, roughly 44% of male applicants were admitted, compared with roughly 35% of female applicants — a difference far too large to attribute to chance. When the data were later disaggregated by department, the pattern essentially reversed: department by department, women were admitted at rates roughly equal to, and in several cases slightly higher than, those of men. The aggregate disparity arose largely because women disproportionately applied to highly competitive departments with low admission rates for everyone, while men disproportionately applied to less competitive departments with high admission rates.

Analyze this episode as a case study in Simpson's paradox. Your analysis should do four things:

1. Explain, as formally as is useful, why the reversal occurs — what the lurking variable is, and how the differing sizes and baseline admission rates of the strata produce an aggregate disparity that no individual department exhibits.
2. Address the harder causal question of whether conditioning on department is legitimate at all. An applicant's choice of department might itself be downstream of earlier discrimination — steering by advisors, self-selection away from hostile fields, or barriers encountered before the application stage — in which case department is a mediator of discrimination rather than a confounder of it, and conditioning on it would conceal precisely the bias one is looking for. Say what would be needed to settle which role department plays, and what follows for the analysis if the answer is "both."
3. Say what the episode shows about the general epistemology of aggregate statistics: when a disaggregated analysis should defeat an aggregate one, and whether there are cases in which the aggregate number is the right one to look at after all.
4. Draw the lesson for Simpson's paradox itself: whether the phenomenon is best understood as a statistical curiosity, as a failure mode of causal inference from observational data, or as a demonstration that "the" effect is relative to a choice of conditioning that the data alone cannot make for us.

Close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, whether the Berkeley figures, properly interpreted, support a finding of bias against women in admissions decisions, and which of the two analyses — aggregate or disaggregated — deserves priority given your answer to point 2.
