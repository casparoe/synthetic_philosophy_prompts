I'd like a consistency check on a position I've arrived at about the concept of disease. I have some background in medicine and philosophy of biology, and I think my views are each fairly standard, but I suspect they don't all fit together. Here they are:

(1) Whether a condition is a disease is a purely descriptive matter. A condition is a disease if and only if some bodily or psychological part or system fails to perform its statistically typical function, assessed relative to an appropriate reference class (same species, sex, and age group). Value judgments — how much we dislike the condition, how socially inconvenient it is, whether it violates norms — have no role in the classification itself. Views that build harm or social disvaluement into the definition of disease are, in my opinion, confusing diagnosis with ethics.

(2) The removal of homosexuality from the DSM in the 1970s was the correction of a scientific error, not a political capitulation. There was never a dysfunction there to classify, and the earlier listing simply reflected the prejudices of the time.

(3) Hypertension, as currently diagnosed, is a genuine disease. The disagreement over whether the treatment cutoff should sit at 130/80 or 140/90 is a technical dispute about the medical facts, and whichever threshold is right, it is right because of the physiology and epidemiology — not because of anyone's judgment about acceptable risk.

(4) Major depressive disorder is a genuine disease, and we are entitled to call it one now, on the basis of the symptom-and-impairment criteria clinicians actually use, without waiting for the underlying biological dysfunction to be identified. Demanding that level of evidence before applying the label would be an unfair double standard.

(5) Alzheimer's disease in an 85-year-old is a disease; the presbyopia that nearly everyone has by their mid-fifties is just aging. The difference between them is real, not conventional.

(6) Whether something is a disease should be entirely independent of whether we can treat it, and independent of whether insurers will pay for treating it.

My questions: Are these six jointly tenable? Which pairs are in genuine conflict, and which only appear to be? Please order any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one tell me specifically which commitment is cheapest to revise and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. I'd also like to know whether the reference-class move I'm relying on in (1) and (5) is doing more work than it can bear — that is, whether the choice of reference class is itself the descriptive matter I take it to be, or whether it quietly reintroduces exactly what (1) claims to exclude. I'm not attached to any particular commitment, so don't assume I already know which one should go.
