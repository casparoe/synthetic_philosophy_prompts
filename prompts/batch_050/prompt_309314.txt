Read the following passage from P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), from the section where he is setting out the dispute between his "optimists" and "pessimists" about determinism:

"But what 'freedom' means here is nothing but the absence of certain conditions the presence of which would make moral condemnation or punishment inappropriate. They have in mind conditions like compulsion by another, or innate incapacity, or insanity, or other less extreme forms of psychological disorder, or the existence of circumstances in which the making of any other choice would be morally inadmissible or would be too much to expect of any man."

Later in the same section Strawson describes the pessimist as holding that "the condition of his really deserving blame is something that goes beyond the negative freedoms that the optimist concedes. It is, say, a genuinely free identification of the will with the act."

(a) Whose account of freedom is being given in the first passage — is this Strawson stating his own view, or someone else's? Explain.
(b) What does the pessimist think is missing, and why does the pessimist take this to be incompatible with determinism?
(c) Strawson says he wants to "reconcile" the two parties. In light of these passages, what is his reconciling move? Does he accept the pessimist's extra condition on desert?

And here's the student's answer:

(a) The sentence about freedom is Strawson stating his own definition: to be free is simply to be acting without compulsion, incapacity, insanity, or similar conditions, or in circumstances where no other choice could reasonably be expected. Freedom in this sense is plainly compatible with determinism, since none of these conditions have anything to do with whether the act was causally determined. So Strawson builds his whole position on a compatibilist, "negative" notion of freedom.

(b) The pessimist accepts that freedom in Strawson's negative sense exists but thinks it isn't enough for genuine desert. For blame to be really deserved, the agent's will has to be freely identified with the act in some stronger sense, and this stronger condition is incompatible with determinism being true. So the pessimist's view is that if determinism holds, nobody ever truly deserves blame, whatever the optimist says.

(c) Strawson's reconciling move is to say that the pessimist is right that something vital is missing from the optimist's picture, but wrong about where it is. What the optimist's story about the social efficacy of punishment and blame leaves out is the whole fabric of human attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness — in which our practices of holding each other responsible actually consist. These attitudes are not a policy we adopted for reasons and could abandon if determinism turned out to be true; they are given with human life, and the demand that they be justified from outside, by some metaphysical fact about the will, is a demand the pessimist wrongly thinks must be met. Strawson therefore rejects the pessimist's extra condition on desert.

So the overall conclusion is that determinism is simply irrelevant to moral responsibility: whether someone is responsible is settled entirely by whether the negative freedoms obtain plus the facts about our practices of blame, exactly as the optimist maintained.
