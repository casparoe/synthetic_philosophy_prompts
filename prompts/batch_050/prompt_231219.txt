I work in economics, mostly on mechanism design and institutional questions, and my field has a habit I had never examined until recently: we speak constantly of reasons and knowledge that no one has. Prices are said to aggregate information dispersed across millions of agents, none of whom possesses more than a fragment of it. A well-designed incentive scheme is said to give a firm reasons to do what the planner wants, even when no one in the firm can articulate the planner's objective. Markets are said to learn, disciplines are said to punish, and reputations are said to do the reasoning that litigation would otherwise require.

Reading philosophy recently, I came across what looks like the same habit, but taken seriously as a problem. Dennett's notion of free-floating rationales holds that natural selection generates genuine reasons — the reason the eagle's wing is shaped as it is, the reason the cuckoo's egg mimics its host's — without there being any reasoner who has, represents, or could state those reasons. Competence without comprehension. The critics divide roughly into those who think this is a mere façon de parler (the rationale is ours, projected onto a mindless process), those who press Fodor-style selection-for worries (selection cannot distinguish between a trait and any coextensive correlate of it, so it cannot select for the right description of the trait, so there is no fact of the matter about what the reason was), and teleosemanticists like Millikan who try to ground function- and content-talk in selection history so that the reasons are real whether or not anyone thinks them.

Here is what I want from you. Restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually work in — utility functions and revealed preference, incentives and incentive compatibility, information aggregation, principal–agent relations, equilibrium selection — as faithfully as you can. Take each of the main positions above and state it in economic terms, telling me where in my conceptual machinery it would live.

Then give me the residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which seem to have no counterpart in economics at all. I am especially interested in two pressure points. First, the philosophers' central question — who, if anyone, has the reason? — does not obviously map onto anything we ask; we are content to say the market knew without asking whether that knowledge was anybody's. Is there an economic question lurking there that we have learned not to ask, or is the philosophers' question itself the artifact of a framework that insists reasons need owners? Second, the selection-for problem: does it have a genuine economic analogue (say, in whether an equilibrium can be said to have been selected for its efficiency rather than for some correlated property of the allocation), or does the analogue collapse, and if so, why?

Conclude with your honest assessment of the total residue: does what fails to translate point to something my discipline is missing, or to something less clear in the philosophical debate than it appears?
