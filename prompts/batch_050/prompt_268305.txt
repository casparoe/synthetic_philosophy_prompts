I'm working on a piece about the experience machine and keep going back and forth on what the thought experiment actually shows, so I want you to compare two versions of the case and tell me what the difference between them teaches us.

Version one is the classic: you're offered permanent connection to a machine that will give you whatever experiences you'd most want, a life that feels from the inside richer and happier than your actual one in every respect, and once plugged in you won't know it. Most people decline. Version two is flipped around: you're informed that your whole life up to now has in fact been lived inside such a machine, that your real body has been floating in a tank the whole time, and you're offered the choice to be unplugged into a reality that is genuinely real but considerably poorer and less pleasant than the simulated life you've been having. The odd thing is that many of the same people who refuse to plug in also refuse to unplug.

Two readings of this asymmetry interest me. On the first, the flip shows that the original case was never really tracking well-being at all: what drives the intuition is just status quo bias plus attachment to whatever one currently happens to have, so the experience machine fails as an argument against hedonism. On the second, the two cases are not symmetric in the way that matters — in one case you'd be abandoning real projects and real relationships for fake ones, in the other you'd be abandoning the attachments your actual psychological life is made of (simulated in origin, but yours) for the sake of contact with a reality you have no connection to — and the intuitions are tracking something genuinely normative in both cases.

Which reading do you find more convincing, and why? Then the part I most want your take on: suppose the second reading is right, and the upshot of the pair of cases is that judgements about how well a life goes are path-dependent — they care about the history that produced the life, not just the stream of experiences stretching ahead. Can any of the standard theories of well-being (hedonism, desire-fulfilment theory, objective list theories) actually accomodate that, or does taking the intuition seriously push you somewhere none of them sit comfortably? If instead you think the first reading is right, answer the mirror question: what would an argument against hedonism have to look like in order to dodge the status-quo-bias objection, and do the desire-fulfilment or objective-list approaches actually supply one, or do they just change the subject to something other than well-being?
