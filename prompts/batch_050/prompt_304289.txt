Okay so here's a variant on Hofstadter's Luring Lottery that's been bugging me and a friend for weeks, and we keep talking past each other. Quick refresher for anyone who needs it: in the original Luring Lottery, anyone can enter for free (as many times as they want), the prize is a fixed jackpot divided by the total number of entries, and Hofstadter's point was that "superrational" reasoners -- people who assume others like them will reach the same conclusion they do -- should enter only with some small probability, keeping expected turnout low. In the actual event, people mailed in avalanches of entries, some of them thousands or millions of entries each, and the prize collapsed to essentially nothing.

Our variant changes two things. First, entries are limited to one per person, but they're public: after the drawing, the organizer posts the full list of everyone who entered, with names attached. Second, the lottery runs every year, indefinitely, and the organizer publishes each year's total entry count and the per-person payout.

Now suppose it's year one, prize capped at $1,000,000. You could genuinely use the money. If turnout stays moderate -- say a few thousand entries -- the expected value of entering is clearly positive. But your name will be on that public list, visible to your friends, your employer, your judgy superrationalist book club, everyone. Do you enter in year one? And then: does your answer change in year five, if it turns out that in years one through four, millions of people entered each time and the per-person payout was pocket change?

Write an essay defending a verdict on both questions. Along the way, grapple with at least these: whether Hofstadter-style superrationality actually recommends not entering, or rather entering with some calibrated probability (and whether that's even a stable recommendation once everyone applies it); whether the publicity rule changes the game in a way that pure expected-value reasoning misses -- e.g., does entering now signal something about you, and should that matter; and what a would-be superrational agent should do once the population has demonstrably, repeatedly failed to coordinate. Does the failure of others let you off the hook, or does it just make the math easier?

Don't end on "it's complicated" or "reasonable people disagree." Take a position on both the year-one and year-five questions and argue for it.
