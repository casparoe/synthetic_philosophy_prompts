So there's a real case I want analyzed: in the early 2000s, a deaf lesbian couple in the US (both deaf from birth, active in deaf culture) deliberately chose a sperm donor with hereditary deafness going back several generations, specifically in order to raise their chances of having a deaf child. They succeeded, twice. They were pretty open about their reasoning: deafness to them was a culture and an identity, not a disability, and they wanted a child who'd share it. This got a lot of press at the time and lots of people were furious.

Analyze this through Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence -- the idea that when prospective parents can choose between possible future children, they have a moral reason to select the child expected to have the best life. I want the mapping done carefully, not just "PB condemns them, discussion over":

- What exactly does PB say here, given that the choice wasn't between embryos via screening but between donors? Does the mechanism of selection matter to the principle, or only the choiceworthiness of the outcome?
- The non-identity problem is obviously lurking. Spell out where it bites and where it doesn't, because PB was designed partly to sidestep non-identity (it's about which child to bring into existence, not about harming a particular child). Does the couple's actual choice have the structure PB assumes?
- The couple's core claim is that deafness isn't a harm -- social model of disability, deafness as linguistic minority, the works. Does PB have the resources to take that claim seriously? It seems like the whole case turns on what theory of wellbeing you plug into the principle (objective list vs. preference satisfaction vs. hedonic), so work out what PB says under each and whether the principle itself is neutral on that or secretly smuggles one in.
- Here's the part I find genuinely interesting: this is a case of prospective parents using reproductive freedom to select FOR a trait the majority considers a disadvantage. Does the episode show that liberal, choice-based eugenics cuts in both directions -- i.e., that you can't defend parental selection freedom and then complain when parents select against what you count as the good? Or does PB have a principled way to block that conclusion?
- And run it the other direction too: what does the episode show about PB? Is it a straightforward confirmation (the principle gives a clear verdict and the verdict seems right), a case PB never anticipated, or evidence that "best life" is doing all the work and the principle is close to empty without a defended account of welfare?
