Construct a thought experiment in which a maximally morally good person is defective as a person precisely because of their maximal goodness. The setup is the familiar critique of moral sainthood associated with Susan Wolf: a moral saint is someone whose every deliberation is governed by impartial concern for the well-being of others, and the worry is that such a life, however admirable, is not one we should want for ourselves or for those we love. Your task is to build the best possible case for that worry.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. The setting must be ordinary and small-scale — a household, a long friendship, a small workplace. No emergencies, no lifeboats, no triage, no situations where one person's sacrifice saves many lives. The point is to test sainthood as a way of living, not as an emergency disposition.

2. The saint in your scenario must be competent and clear-eyed. Their deficiency may not be explained by naivety, fanaticism, poor judgment, bad luck, or commitment to a mistaken moral theory. Whatever goes wrong must trace specifically to the completeness of their moral devotion — to the fact that no corner of their deliberation is reserved for non-moral projects, tastes, or attachments.

3. Include a counterpart: a person of ordinary, substantial-but-incomplete morality in the same scenario, and show that this person possesses a good the saint lacks.

After presenting the scenario (one or two paragraphs), do the following:

(a) Identify the specific good or capacity the saint lacks — e.g., an aesthetic sensibility, a personal project, a sense of humor, a partial attachment — and argue that its absence is a defect in the saint rather than merely a cost borne by the saint. This distinction is the crux: a sacrifice is something a good person pays; a defect is something that makes the person less good as a specimen of human flourishing. Show your case lands on the right side of that line.

(b) Distinguish two readings of the moral saint — one on which the saint maximizes others' welfare, and one on which the saint perfectly instantiates the virtues — and state whether your case succeeds against both, or only one, and why.

(c) Identify the single feature of your scenario that does the argumentative work, and describe what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed (for instance, if the saint is permitted one small non-moral passion).

(d) Give the strongest available defense of sainthood against your case — for example, that a sufficiently rich impartial love of others is itself fulfilling, or that the supposed defect is just virtue's price and no argument against the ideal — and explain how the case should be modified, if at all, to withstand it.
