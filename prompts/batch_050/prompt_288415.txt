You are a professional philosopher of mind with substantial experience designing assessments for upper-level undergraduates, and I would like your help drafting the final exam for my third-year seminar on consciousness. The deliverable is one essay question on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

First, some context on what the question should test. Any student who did the reading can recite the slogan that consciousness is an illusion and summarize Frankish or Dennett; I do not want to reward that. I want a question that forces candidates to engage with the view's central epistemic pressure point: illusionism needs our introspective judgments as its data, since it is ultimately a theory of why we judge that we are phenomenally conscious, yet it also holds that introspection systematically misrepresents what our experiences are like. To that end, I want the exam question to present candidates with the following short passage, which I have written myself in the voice of an unnamed critic, and ask them to evaluate it:

"Illusionism refutes itself. The theory's only evidence is that our experiences seem to us to have phenomenal character. But if introspection is as unreliable as the illusionist claims, then we have no reason to trust that seeming, and the theory has no data left to explain. If, on the other hand, introspection is reliable enough to be trusted here, then phenomenal character is real and the theory is false. Either way, illusionism loses."

A strong answer, as I see it, must do at least the following five things, and I want the marking scheme built around exactly these points: (1) state illusionism precisely, making clear that it denies phenomenal properties, not experiences or seemings as such; (2) diagnose the equivocation in the critic's phrase "trust introspection," distinguishing reliability about the fact that we make phenomenal judgments from reliability about phenomenal character; (3) connect the illusionist's reply to the meta-problem of consciousness, that is, the project of explaining our phenomenal judgments in functional or representational terms; (4) take seriously the residual worry that the alleged illusion of phenomenality must itself be phenomenal, and evaluate at least one illusionist response to it; and (5) reach a defended verdict on whether dissolving the equivocation fully answers the critic or leaves the illusionist with a residual explanatory burden.

Please deliver the following:

1. The exam question itself, phrased exactly as it would appear on the paper and answerable in 75 minutes by a well-prepared student. It should reproduce the critic's passage verbatim and include explicit instructions about what candidates must do with it, so that merely summarizing the illusionist position earns little credit.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words that would receive full marks.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, organized around the five must-hit points above: for each, state how many marks it is worth, what a top-scoring treatment must contain, and give a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically get it wrong.
4. A short paragraph stating what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer, naming the argumentative moves each must contain rather than just the topics each must mention.

Before drafting, work through the problem carefully. Begin with a clearly labeled brainstorming section in which you consider two or three alternative cruxes you might have built the question around (for instance, whether illusionism is empirically tractable, or whether it collapses into behaviorism) and explain briefly why you set each aside in favor of the one above. Then draft the deliverables, and before finishing, check that your model answer actually exhibits each of the five must-hit points rather than merely gesturing at them, revising if it does not. Write in American English throughout, and do not pad the model answer with historical or biographical summary that the marking scheme does not reward.
