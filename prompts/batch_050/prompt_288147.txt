So our reading group has been doing Dennett's later stuff (From Bacteria to Bach mostly) and we got into a long argument about free-floating rationales -- the idea that there can be reasons "out there" in nature that nobody has ever thought, like the reason the arctic fox is white or the reason the cuckoo chick ejects its nestmates. For next week everyone has to submit a one-page position statement, and before I hand mine in I want to know if my commitments actually cohere or whether I've stitched together things that can't all be true. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Free-floating rationales are real: natural selection genuinely tracks reasons, so there's a fact of the matter about what a trait is *for* even in organisms with no minds at all. (2) That said, a reason only counts as a reason *for someone* -- the action-guiding, justificatory kind -- if the agent could in principle be moved by recognizing it, so only representational creatures act for reasons in the full sense. (3) I'm basically a teleosemanticist about mental content: what an inner state is about is fixed by what it was selected to do. (4) Plants, thermostats and bacteria don't have beliefs or represent anything; mindless systems merely behave as if they had reasons. (5) The normativity in all of this is no deep mystery -- talk of what a trait "ought" to do is just shorthand for facts about selection history, nothing spooky.

My friend in the group says (1) and (4) are obviously at odds but I suspect the real problem is somewhere else and she's just pattern-matching.
