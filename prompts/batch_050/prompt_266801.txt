I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of science program, and my reading group on formal epistemology recently spent a session on the no-free-lunch theorems from the machine learning literature. During the discussion I put together an argument that was meant to vindicate the theorems' philosophical importance, but by the end it seemed to prove far too much, and now I can't locate the misstep. I would like you to respond as a philosopher of science with real expertise in formal learning theory: begin by briefly reconstructing the argument in its strongest form, then diagnose it. Here is the argument. (1) A method of ampliative inference is justified only if it can be shown, in advance of applying it, to outperform rival methods at predicting unobserved cases. (2) Since we do not know which possible world is actual, "outperform" has to mean outperform on average across all possible worlds consistent with our evidence; any narrower claim would simply presuppose that the actual world is of a particular kind. (3) The no-free-lunch theorems establish that, averaged over all possible data-generating processes, every learning method has exactly the same expected predictive accuracy -- induction, Occam-style curve fitting, random guessing, and even anti-induction, the method that expects past regularities to break. (4) So induction does not outperform anti-induction even on average. (5) The only way to restore induction's advantage is to weight induction-friendly worlds more heavily in the average, but that weighting just is the assumption that nature is uniform, which is what a justification of induction was supposed to deliver; the repair is therefore circular. (6) Hence induction cannot be justified -- and this is the part that proves too much -- neither can anything else, so the epidemiologist running a clinical trial and the haruspex reading entrails are equally rational, since both employ methods with identical average-case performance and no non-circular edge. (7) Worse, the theorems themselves are proved using probabilistic reasoning about averages over possibilities, so the argument's own machinery rests on the very statistical inference it discredits, making the whole thing self-undermining.

Step (6) cannot be right -- I am not prepared to accept that forecasting and entrail-reading stand or fall together -- but each premise looks defensible in isolation, and the group split three ways on where the argument breaks. One member said the trouble is in (2) and (3): that "averaged over all possible worlds" is doing illegitimate work, because averaging over an infinite space of possibilities requires a measure, there is no canonical uniform measure over worlds, and the technical averaging in the theorems is being quietly substituted for a metaphysical average they say nothing about. Another said the flaw is already in (1): the demand that a justified method demonstrably dominate its rivals across all possibilities is a foundationalist requirement we reject everywhere else, and dropping it is cheap. A third wanted to concede (5)'s circularity and treat it as harmless. I'd like you to identify which step you take to fail, explain why that step looked compelling in the first place, and say what rejecting it costs. If the real culprit is the slide from the theorems' formal result to the claim about worlds in (3), please make the equivocation precise -- state exactly what the theorems quantify over and what my argument needs them to quantify over. I'd most like to preserve (3) as a genuine theorem and the negation of (6), so if those two are in tension, tell me which one has to give and what I'm paying.
