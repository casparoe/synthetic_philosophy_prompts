I am preparing a 90-minute examination for an upper-level master’s seminar on AI governance and the philosophy of supervision. Draft one essay question based on the scenario below, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Scenario: A laboratory deploys AI agents to prepare policy recommendations on questions whose consequences may not become clear for years. Under outcome supervision, reviewers reward recommendations according to their eventual real-world success. Under process supervision, reviewers instead assess the acceptability of each intermediate step: the evidence considered, the assumptions made, the alternatives compared, and the reasons given for rejecting them. The laboratory proposes a hybrid called retrospective process auditing. A separate AI predicts whether each intermediate step is likely to produce a successful final recommendation; after some outcomes become known, those outcomes are used to retrain the step-level evaluator. Human reviewers then reward agents for following processes that this evaluator certifies as reliable.

The intended crux is whether this method genuinely combines process and outcome supervision or merely evaluates the outcome twice: once directly and once through a predictive proxy. Candidates should have to distinguish outcomes used as evidence that a process is reliable from outcomes used as the sole criterion of a good process. They should also consider what changes when the true outcome is delayed or contested, when agents can optimize for legible reasoning rather than sound reasoning, and when some process requirements are non-instrumental constraints rather than merely predicted causes of success.

Requirements:

1. The exam question should be self-contained, suitable for a 90-minute essay, and difficult to answer by reciting generic claims about transparency, alignment, or Goodhart’s law. It should require candidates to take a position on whether retrospective process auditing avoids the principal defects of pure outcome supervision.
2. Provide a model answer of roughly 1,000–1,400 words. It should present the strongest version of both sides before reaching a qualified verdict. It must distinguish instrumental process standards from independently justified constraints such as prohibitions on deception, manipulation, or unauthorized action.
3. Provide a marking scheme out of 30 marks, divided into six components. For each component, state the mark allocation, the precise intellectual move required, and a common way candidates might appear to address it while missing the crux. Include at least one component on which either an affirmative or a negative answer can receive full credit if adequately defended.
4. The model answer should explicitly identify the argument or empirical discovery that would most plausibly change its verdict—for example, evidence about whether process evaluators remain reliable under optimization pressure, or a conceptual argument showing that every allegedly independent process standard must ultimately be validated by outcomes.
