I'm compiling a list of objections to Hempel's covering-law model of historical explanation for a seminar handout. So far I have: (1) the no-laws objection — the generalizations historians actually invoke are either truisms or rough tendencies with acknowledged exceptions, and neither can serve as the law-like premise the model requires; (2) Dray's rational-explanation objection — explaining an action means showing it was the reasonable thing to do given the agent's beliefs and aims, which appeals to a norm of rationality rather than to an empirical regularity; (3) the narrative objection — a historical account explains by selecting and connecting events into a coherent story, and nothing in that activity looks like deriving the explanandum from premises; and (4) the description-dependence objection — events instantiate laws only under certain descriptions, and the descriptions historians care about (revolution, decline, betrayal) don't line up with the predicates of any law-supplying science. Give me one more objection that is genuinely different from these — not a rewording, special case, or combination of them — and say honestly whether it survives the obvious replies, including how it interacts with the ones already listed. In answering, please distinguish what is settled in this debate (for instance, whether anyone still defends the strict covering-law model as Hempel stated it) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of history, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
