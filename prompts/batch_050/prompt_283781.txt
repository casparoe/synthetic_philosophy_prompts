I chair the residents' welfare committee of a housing cooperative with about 180 units, and we are deadlocked over a pest-control decision I need help thinking through. A German cockroach infestation has established itself in the shared basement and is spreading into ground-floor flats. Our contractor's standing proposal is quarterly building-wide spraying with a pyrethroid insecticide, supplemented by glue traps in the corridors and bin rooms. Three of our residents have forced this onto the agenda as an ethical question rather than a purely practical one. One is a doctoral researcher in invertebrate neuroscience who argues that the evidence for pain-like states in insects is now strong enough that glue traps -- on which a cockroach may struggle for days before dying of dehydration or starvation -- are indefensible when alternatives exist, and that even fast-acting poisons warrant scrutiny. A second resident has a child whose asthma is significantly aggravated by cockroach allergens and wants the most aggressive treatment available, immediately. A third objects to the spraying itself on grounds that it mostly doesn't work: the basement's damp and clutter mean the population rebounds within weeks, and we are killing large numbers of animals in a way that accomplishes little, when habitat modification (sealing, moisture control, waste management), targeted gel baits, insect growth regulators, and possibly desiccant dusts in wall voids would do more with fewer deaths.

The committee has asked me to bring a decision memo to our next meeting, and I'd like you to draft it. Take the role of an applied ethicist with working literacy in animal welfare science and integrated pest management, and think the case through carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points on either side. The memo should recommend a concrete policy: an ordered preference schedule of control methods (which we permit, which we forbid, which we use only after others have failed), the conditions under which escalation to more lethal or more aversive methods is justified, and a fair procedure for the dissenting residents. It must engage honestly with the moral status of the insects themselves: what follows for our policy if cockroaches can suffer in ways that matter, what follows if they can't, and -- hardest of all -- what follows given that we genuinely don't know, including how to weigh very large numbers of possibly-small harms against concrete, well-documented harms to the humans involved, such as the child's asthma.

One discipline I'd like the memo to observe throughout: keep clear what is well established and what is genuinely contested, and don't let a contested claim masquerade as consensus in either direction. The evidence on insect sentience and the comparative aversiveness of different killing methods is uncertain and actively debated; so, in places, is the effectiveness ranking of the control options. Where you lean on a contested premise, flag it as such and say what the recommendation would be if the premise turned out false. End with an actionable bottom line the committee could vote on, followed by the strongest argument against your own recommendation and an honest account of why it should or shouldn't carry the day.
