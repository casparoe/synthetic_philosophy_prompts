My philosophy of language professor has been ruling, all term, on whether various speech acts actually came off. Her verdicts so far: a priest who has secretly been defrocked baptizes an infant -- no baptism took place. A guest says I bet you ten dollars it rains tomorrow to a companion wearing noise-canceling headphones who never hears it -- no bet was made. A man says I promise to repay you while having no intention whatsoever of doing so -- a promise was made, albeit an insincere one. A judge, laughing, at a costume party, says I sentence you to ten years -- no sentence was passed. A couple exchanges complete wedding vows before a licensed officiant who, unbeknownst to them, never signs or files the license -- they are not married. Next week's case: during the run of a play, two cast members who are a real couple and genuinely intend to marry have the scripted wedding scene performed by a fellow actor who, unknown to the audience, is a legally authorized officiant; afterward the couple signs a valid license and insists the marriage is real. Predict her verdict and say which rule she appears to be running -- Austin-style felicity conditions with a carve-out for utterances spoken on stage, a pure institutional-fact account on which the act succeeds whenever the relevant conventional conditions are objectively satisfied, an uptake-based account keyed to what participants and audience recognize, or something else. Which rival rules fit her track record equally well, where do those rules diverge on this case, and what further case would best tell them apart?
