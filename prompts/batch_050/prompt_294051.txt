I am a philosophy student drafting a paper on transcendental arguments — arguments that try to establish some contested conclusion by showing it to be a condition of the possibility of experience, thought, or meaningful discourse. A supervisor told me that the ancestor of the whole genre is Aristotle's defence of the principle of non-contradiction in Book IV of the Metaphysics, where instead of proving the principle he offers what he calls a demonstration "by refutation" against anyone who denies it. I have been stuck for days on the central passage (Metaphysics IV.4, 1006a, in W. D. Ross's translation), and I would like help understanding it. The passage:

"Even in the case of this law, however, we can demonstrate the impossibility by refutation, if only our opponent makes some statement. If he makes none, it is absurd to seek for an argument against one who has no arguments of his own about anything, in so far as he has none; for such a person, in so far as he is such, is really no better than a vegetable. And I say that proof by refutation differs from simple proof in that he who attempts to prove might seem to beg the fundamental question, whereas if the discussion is provoked thus by someone else, refutation and not proof will result. The starting-point for all such discussions is not the claim that he should state that something is or is not so (because this might be supposed to be a begging of the question), but that he should say something significant both to himself and to another (this is essential if any argument is to follow; for otherwise such a person cannot reason either with himself or with another); and if this is granted, demonstration will be possible, for there will be something already defined. But the person responsible is not he who demonstrates but he who acquiesces; for though he disowns reason he acquiesces to reason. Moreover, he who makes such an admission as this has admitted the truth of something apart from demonstration [so that not everything will be "so and not so"]."

Here is where I need help, in order of how badly it is blocking me:

1. I cannot parse the sentence "the person responsible is not he who demonstrates but he who acquiesces; for though he disowns reason he acquiesces to reason." Responsible for what, exactly — for the success of the demonstration, or for his own refutation? And why is shifting "responsibility" onto the opponent supposed to matter? I suspect this sentence holds the key to the whole passage, but every paraphrase I attempt either makes it trivial or makes it circular.

2. Aristotle insists that a proof by refutation avoids begging the question in a way a "simple proof" would not. What is the actual mechanism of this? He says the opponent need only "say something significant both to himself and to another" — but how does the mere act of saying something significant commit one to non-contradiction, without already presupposing that words have fixed meanings, which is itself part of what is in dispute? Walk me through the argument step by step, in your own reconstruction, and flag wherever you have to add a premise Aristotle does not state.

3. The question my paper actually turns on: even if this argument succeeds completely, what has it established? It looks as though it shows at most that an opponent cannot coherently deny the principle, or cannot engage in significant speech while denying it — not that the principle is true of things. This seems to be an ancient version of the standing modern complaint against transcendental arguments (associated in the recent literature with Barry Stroud's criticism of Kant): that they deliver conclusions about what we must believe or presuppose, not about how the world is, unless an extra bridge premise is smuggled in. Does Aristotle's refutation-based strategy escape this complaint — perhaps because the opponent convicts himself rather than being argued at — or does it merely make the gap more visible? And would Aristotle himself have accepted the distinction between "true of our thought" and "true of things" that the complaint relies on?
