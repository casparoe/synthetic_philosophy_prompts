I got into a dumb late-night argument with a friend last week after we watched some pop-science video about the Libet experiments. The friend's position was basically: the readiness potential shows up hundreds of milliseconds before you report feeling the urge to move, and in those later fMRI studies researchers could apparently decode which button you'd press like ten seconds early, so neuroscience has empirically disproven free will and the philosophers still arguing about it are just coping. I want to say he's wrong but I honestly couldn't articulate why, because the intuitive gloss -- "your brain makes the decision before 'you' do" -- seems pretty damning.

Then I went and read around a bit and got more confused, not less. There's this critique (I think associated with Schurger?) that the readiness potential might just be an artifact of averaging together lots of noisy neural fluctuations that happen to cross some threshold, so it isn't a decision being prepared at all -- it's more like random drift with a floor on it. Fine, but here's where I'm stuck: even if that's right, it doesn't obviously rescue anything. If my action comes out of stochastic noise accumulating, that's not freedom either, it's just randomness wearing a trenchcoat. And it also doesn't seem to touch the decoding studies, which don't rely on the readiness potential at all. Meanwhile people also talk about a "veto" interpretation, where consciousness can't initiate the movement but can cancel it up to some point of no return, and about how the reported time of the urge is reconstructed after the fact and might be systematically biased. I can't tell whether these are five different answers to the same question or five answers to five different questions that people keep conflating.

So what I'd like: an intuitive, gut-level explanation of what exactly the accumulator critique does and doesn't undermine about the "your brain decided first" inference, and then a map of how the readiness potential stuff, the early decoding results, the veto experiments, and the urge-timing worries fit together -- do they stack into one case, or answer different sub-questions? End with your actual verdict on whether this whole line of research tells us anything deep about free will, not a both-sides-y summary. And tell me what would change your verdict: which experimental result or philosophical argument, if it came out differently, would flip your assessment, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to be on shaky ground.
