So I've been rereading Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) and I got stuck pretty early on, in section 1, where he splits analytic statements into two kinds:

Statements which are analytic by general philosophical acclaim are not, indeed, far to seek. They fall into two classes. Those of the first class, which may be called logically true, are typified by: (1) No unmarried man is married. But there is also a second class of analytic statements, typified by: (2) No bachelor is married.

Compare the two classes Quine distinguishes here. A few things I'm trying to sort out:

(a) Why is Quine willing to grant the first class but not the second? What extra ingredient does reducing a second-class statement to a first-class one require, and why does he think that ingredient is every bit as murky as analyticity itself?

(b) Here's the part that really bugs me: given the confirmation holism Quine defends at the end of the paper, does the first class actually stay safe? If our beliefs only ever confront experience as one big system rather than statement by statement, then wouldn't even "No unmarried man is married" be up for revision in principle? Is his early concession to the first class consistent with his later claim that anything at all can be held onto or given up if we're willing to adjust the rest of the system — or does the concession secretly rely on the very distinction he's busy attacking?

(c) Overall, which of the two classes does more work in the argument of the paper? I keep seeing people treat the bachelor example as the real heart of "Two Dogmas", but the more I look at it the less sure I am.
