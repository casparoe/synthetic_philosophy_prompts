You're a philosopher of social science who's watched this debate from the inside for years. Think it through carefully before answering.

The fight over whether there can be laws of history has been running since at least Popper's attack on historicism, and honestly it feels stuck. Popper's lot say you can't have unconditional predictions about historical development, only conditional trends that could reverse tomorrow; the other side (old-school Marxists originally, and now people doing things like cliodynamics who build mathematical models of state collapse and inequality cycles) say that if a regularity holds across enough cases and survives prediction, calling it a law is fine. Meanwhile everybody seems to agree on the boring middle: you can make decent short-range, hedged forecasts and sometimes they work. So what's actually left in dispute? Is this a real disagreement about whether the social world is law-governed, or a verbal one about how strict the word 'law' has to be, or is each side really answering a different question -- one about what history is like, the other about what explanation must look like? My hunch, offered as a hunch: the hidden work is being done by different standards of confirmation, since one side counts a trend with stated scope conditions as confirmed and the other won't count anything with exceptions. Diagnose the impasse using what the positions actually commit to, and tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result, a case both sides must read the same way -- or argue that none is comming and why.
