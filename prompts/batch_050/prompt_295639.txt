QUESTION: In "Freedom and Resentment," Strawson distinguishes (i) cases in which we excuse someone for a particular act while continuing to treat them as a participant in ordinary interpersonal life, from (ii) cases in which we exempt someone from the participant reactive attitudes more or less across the board, shifting toward what he calls the objective attitude. (a) Spell out this distinction: what does each kind of judgment say about the agent, the act, and the will expressed in the act? What does Strawson think the objective attitude involves, and what is lost when we take it up? (10 marks) (b) Strawson argues that the truth of determinism would not by itself give us reason to suspend the reactive attitudes generally. Reconstruct his case for this. Does it appeal to the falsity of determinism, to the way excusing conditions work, or to facts about human nature — and do these strands depend on each other? (10 marks) (c) Suppose the argument in (b) works. What exactly has Strawson shown: that our practices of holding one another responsible are justified, or that we couldn't abandon them even if we wanted to? If you think there's no real difference, explain why. If you think there is, say whether the pessimist (the incompatibilist) can still get a foothold. (5 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER: (a) Reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude and indignation are responses to the good or ill will others show us in what they do. An excuse ("it was an accident," "she had no idea") targets the act: it tells us this particular performance didn't express the will we took it to, so resentment is out of place here, but the agent stays inside the circle of participants — we still relate to her as someone whose attitudes toward us matter. An exemption targets the agent: the young child, the person with severe schizophrenia, someone whose responses are too distorted for us to make ordinary sense of isn't a candidate for adult interpersonal relations at all, so we shift wholesale to the objective attitude. For Strawson that means seeing the person purely as an object of social policy — someone to be managed, treated, trained or worked around. He's clear that personal feeling has no place in it: we can no longer even love such a person in any real sense, only handle them.

(b) Strawson's case doesn't rest on showing determinism false; he brackets that entirely. First, determinism is a general thesis and so can't do the work excusing conditions do: excuses are essentially particular, they pick out this act or this agent as abnormal, and a thesis that covers everyone equally can't discriminate between cases the way an excuse has to. Second — and he treats this as the deeper point — a general policy of objectivity isn't something we could actually adopt. Our commitment to participant attitudes runs too deep and is too woven into the relationships that give human life its value for a theoretical conviction to dislodge it. The strands connect: the first shows determinism can't force exemption on us through the logic of our existing practices, the second shows why abandoning those practices wholesale never arises for us as a live choice.

(c) What Strawson has shown is that holding one another responsible is justified, full stop. If we couldn't abandon the reactive attitudes even given determinism, no theoretical argument against them can have any force, and the pessimist is simply refuted — determinism turns out to be irrelevant to moral responsibility. There's no interesting gap between "we can't help it" and "we're entitled to it": for creatures constituted as we are, the unavoidability of the attitudes just is their justification, and asking for more is asking for something outside human life altogether.
