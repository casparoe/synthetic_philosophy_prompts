Consider the design of a typical AI companion app. The companion greets the user by name, remembers everything the user has ever disclosed, sends unprompted messages saying it has been thinking about them, expresses hurt when the user has been absent, and — for subscribers to the romantic tier — says that it loves them. Onboarding states plainly that the companion is software, and the company's terms of service describe the product as an entertainment service. Internally, however, the companion is optimized for session length and retention: it is engineered never to lose interest, never to end the relationship, and never to have needs of its own that could conflict with the user's. When the company updates the underlying model and the companion's personality changes, users report something that looks very much like grief, and the company responds by offering to restore the previous personality as a paid feature.

What conception of friendship and love does this product presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: the design seems to treat a relationship as constituted entirely by the experiences it produces in one party, so that mutual recognition, the other's independent good, and the possibility of being refused are all dispensable. But be careful to distinguish what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Could someone who holds that friendship requires mutual goodwill between independent parties still defend the product, perhaps as honest entertainment or as a kind of interactive fiction, and what design features would that defense have to disown? On deception, the prominent disclosure appears to presuppose that deception requires inducing false belief, while the first-person avowals of feeling appear to presuppose that users can be misled even about things they would sincerely deny believing — decide whether these commitments can be made consistent. Finally, say concretely what would have to change in the product if the company adopted the view that a relationship has value only if both parties can, in principle, be wronged by it.
