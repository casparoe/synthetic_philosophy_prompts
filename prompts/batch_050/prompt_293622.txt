A friend and I, both longtime readers of the rationalist blogs, have been arguing about hedonic trade-offs, and I want to figure out what principle she is actually running. Her considered verdicts so far: on the old torture-versus-dust-specks question, she chooses the dust specks without hesitation, no matter how many people get them; she would rather one person endure a thousand years of daily tension headaches than one hour of genuine agony; no amount of pleasure, for anyone, could compensate one hour of genuine agony; two hours of agony are strictly worse than one, and she would accept an hour of it to prevent two; and she would accept a week of severe migraines herself to spare a stranger two weeks of the same. The new case: she must choose between one stranger undergoing an hour of genuine agony and another undergoing twenty years of severe chronic pain — bad enough to preclude work and most enjoyment, but never agony. Predict her verdict. More importantly, her record so far seems consistent with at least three principles: a lexical threshold on suffering set just below agony, a lexical threshold set lower down so that severe chronic pain already falls on the bad side of it, and a thoroughly commensurable view on which disvalue simply grows steeply with intensity. Show that each fits the record, state what each predicts about the new case, give me your best guess with reasons, and name the single further case that would best distinguish whichever principles survive. Two side questions: do her verdicts already rule out classical, non-lexical negative utilitarianism, and do they settle whether pleasure can ever offset suffering below the threshold?
