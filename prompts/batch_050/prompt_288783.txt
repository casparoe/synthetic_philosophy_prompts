I co-run a monthly philosophy reading group in my department's common room, and next semester we're doing a short arc on time. The group is mostly graduate students and a few undergrads trained in analytic metaphysics: they know the A-series/B-series dispute, presentism versus eternalism, and McTaggart, but none of them has read any Buddhist or East Asian philosophy. For the final session I've been assigned Dōgen's being-time (uji), as developed in the fascicle of that name in the Shōbōgenzō, and I've been given forty minutes to get the idea across before discussion. I am not asking you to explain uji to me; I'm asking you to plan how I should explain it to them.

What I want is the architecture of the explanation. Spell out the order in which the pieces have to be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit: my strong suspicion is that I can't start with the doctrine itself, because this audience will immediately assimilate it to something they already know -- probably presentism with some poetry on top -- so tell me what has to be dismantled first (I assume the picture of time as a container or dimension in which beings sit, and of moments as slices of a persisting self) and how to do that dismantling using their existing toolkit rather than against it. Then identify the single carrying example the whole explanation should hang on. Candidates I've considered: the firewood-and-ash passage (which is actually from Genjōkōan, not Uji -- so say whether borrowing it is legitimate clarification or a cheat that distorts the doctrine), the pine and bamboo, or the progression from winter to spring. Say which one you'd build around, what it must exhibit to do the job -- in particular, how it shows that each particular being just is its time, that every being-time comprises the whole of time without moments bleeding into one another, and that "passage" here is not a flow from past to future -- and what a bad example would smuggle in.

Finally, give me the misconception map: the four or five ways this specific audience will predictably mishear the doctrine (reducing it to an impermanence platitude, to a "live in the present" mindfulness slogan, to presentism, to the claim that time is unreal, or to Bergsonian duration), and the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. Include one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show whether they've grasped that being and time are identical rather than merely inseparable, and tell me what to leave out entirely at this level -- I'm inclined to skip the comparisons with Heidegger and the Abhidharma background on momentariness -- and how to say so honestly without making the omissions sound like the interesting part.
