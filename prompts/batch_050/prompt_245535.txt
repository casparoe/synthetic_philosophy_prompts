In my philosophy of language seminar, the deflationary approach to truth was introduced through an analogy: the expression "it is true that" is said to function like a pronoun. Just as "she" lets us pick up a noun without repeating it and lets us generalize over people, "it is true that" supposedly lets us reuse and generalize over things said — when I say "everything the witness claimed is true," I endorse the claims without restating them. On this picture, truth is a grammatical convenience for anaphora and generalization, not a property some sentences have and others lack. My lecturer uses the analogy to argue that no substantive theory of truth is needed and that metaphysical debates about the nature of truth rest on a confusion about grammar. Please audit the analogy: give the explicit correspondence mapping (what plays the role of the antecedent, of anaphoric reference, of abbreviation, of generalization), then work through where the mapping strains — for instance, that ordinary English does not straightforwardly quantify into sentence position, that "true" can endorse content the speaker does not even know, that truth generates paradoxes pronouns never do, and that truth appears to carry normative weight ("belief aims at truth") that no mere pronoun could bear. Which of these disanalogies actually threaten the deflationary conclusion the analogy is used to support, and which are harmless to it? Finally, does the conclusion stand once the analogy is set aside, or was the analogy doing the argumentative work all along?
