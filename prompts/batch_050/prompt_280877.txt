I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. For a piece on who can legitimately claim ownership of things found in nature, I moderated a conversation between two guests: a legal scholar who defends strong intellectual-property rights in biological materials, and a philosopher who is skeptical of them. The case they argued over was a hypothetical I gave them: a mycologist collects a wild yeast strain from a public forest, spends six years isolating, culturing, and characterizing it in her lab, and then patents it, licensing it to commercial bakeries. The strain itself remains abundant in the wild. Their exchange ran as follows, and I’d like it adjudicated move by move.

(1) The legal scholar argued that the patent is a straightforward case of acquiring property by mixing one’s labour with an unowned resource. The mycologist took something from the commons that nobody owned and, through years of skilled, costly work, turned a wild organism into a characterized, reproducible, industrially useful product. This, he said, is precisely the structure of Locke’s account of original acquisition, and the practical argument reinforces the philosophical one: without exclusive rights, nobody would invest the six years.

(2) The philosopher replied that the labour-mixing argument conflates finding with making. Locke’s canonical cases involve transforming a thing — tilling the soil, gathering the acorns — in ways that change what the thing is. But everything commercially valuable about this strain was already present in the forest; the mycologist’s labour produced knowledge of the strain, not the strain. If labour of this kind grounds ownership, then the first person to map a coastline would own the coast, and the first to describe a mineral deposit would own the deposit — a conclusion the legal scholar presumably won’t accept, which means he needs a principled distinction he hasn’t provided.

(3) The legal scholar conceded that the discovery-versus-creation line needs work but said the real test in Locke is the proviso — that enough and as good be left for others — and here it is clearly satisfied. Wild yeast of this kind is effectively inexhaustible; her collecting did not diminish anyone else’s opportunities in the slightest. Indeed, he argued, her patent enlarges the commons rather than depleting it, because patent law requires public disclosure: the characterization she produced is now on the record for everyone.

(4) The philosopher responded that this reads the proviso too narrowly. The patent does not merely cover her particular cultured stock; it excludes anyone else who independently isolates the same strain from the same forest from using their own isolate. So it removes from the commons something she never put into it — the opportunity to do exactly what she did — and “enough and as good” has to be measured in opportunities, not just in remaining physical organisms. The disclosure point, she added, is a policy argument for the patent system, not a defence of the Lockean claim, since Locke’s proviso is not satisfied by compensation of a different kind.

Please go through the four moves in turn. For each, say whether it succeeds as stated, partly succeeds, or fails, with a brief justification; identify any place where a guest mischaracterizes Locke’s actual machinery rather than merely applying it controversially; and then tell me which single issue the whole exchange really turns on. Finally, is there a consideration bearing on the case that both guests overlooked — for instance, about what exactly the patent covers, or about what the proviso is meant to do in a world where the resource in question is information as much as matter?
