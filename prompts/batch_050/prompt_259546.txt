Since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have joined a philosophy-of-biology reading group, and we have spent the past year on the adaptationism debate — the spandrels critique and the literature it provoked. As an exercise, the group has been issuing verdicts on whether particular traits discussed in published studies should properly be described as adaptations. I have kept a record, and I would like you to predict the group's verdict on a forthcoming case by working out what standard they are implicitly applying.

Their verdicts so far:

1. Male nipples in mammals — not an adaptation; a developmental by-product of a shared body plan.
2. The human chin — not an adaptation; a by-product of the growth fields of the jaw, not something selected for in its own right.
3. The panda's false thumb — an adaptation, though one improvised from a modified wrist bone rather than built from scratch.
4. The honeybee's waggle dance — an adaptation; complex, functional, and well evidenced.
5. The red colour of vertebrate blood — not an adaptation; a chemical by-product of haemoglobin, serving no function as colour.
6. The giraffe's long neck — an adaptation; the comparative and biomechanical evidence for selection on feeding reach satisfied them.

Next month we discuss a colleague's study system: a genus of rock-dwelling lizards in which the males possess an enlarged, vividly coloured dewlap used in courtship displays. Behavioural work shows that dewlap size and colour strongly affect mating success, and the structure looks well engineered for display. However, phylogenetic reconstruction indicates that the enlarged dewlap originated in ancestral lineages whose members did not display with it — it appears to have served some other role, or none in particular, before being recruited into courtship. No measurements of selection or heritability in the wild have yet been made, and no comparative analysis across the genus has been done.

Please do the following. First, set out the two or three implicit standards that are each consistent with the six recorded verdicts — for instance a current-utility standard, a strict historical standard turning on the adaptation–exaptation distinction, and an evidential standard that withholds the label where the adaptive story has not been genuinely tested — or whatever better formulations you find. Second, state what each candidate standard predicts for the dewlap case. Third, commit to a prediction of the group's actual verdict: tell me which standard the track record best supports and why, taking a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose between. Fourth, describe the single further case, not among the six, whose verdict would best discriminate between your favoured standard and its strongest rival. Finally, note briefly where the spandrels critique bites hardest in your reasoning and where you think it overreaches.

Please write in British English throughout.
