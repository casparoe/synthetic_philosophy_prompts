Exam question: Hofstadter argues in I Am a Strange Loop that a human "I" is not an extra ingredient in the brain but a self-referential symbolic pattern, modeled loosely on Gödel's self-referential sentences. (a) What makes such a loop "strange" rather than an ordinary circular process? (b) Why does he insist the self lives at the level of symbols rather than at the level of neurons? (c) Does this account explain the self, or explain it away? Defend a reading.

Student's answer: A strange loop is what you get when a hierarchical system, after climbing up through its levels, arrives back at its starting point, like Escher's endless staircase. Hofstadter thinks the brain is packed with these: neurons monitor other neurons, and this literal self-monitoring circuitry, looping back on itself at the hardware level, is what the "I" consists in. The strangeness comes from the loop crossing levels, so that the high-level symbol ends up causally steering the low-level material that built it. The Gödel analogy is that any sufficiently rich system can talk about itself, and Hofstadter's point is that this kind of self-reference can never be fully captured by a mechanism, which is why the self feels irreducible and why a purely physicalist account of the mind can't work. On (c), I'd say it doesn't explain the self away: patterns are real even when they're perspectival. The "I" is a kind of mirage, but a mirage the system itself falls for, and that's real enough to do causal work.
