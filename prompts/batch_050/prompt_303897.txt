I'm a high school junior, and something about my school's morning routine has been bothering me in a way I can't quite articulate, so I'm hoping for help sorting it out philosophically.

Every morning the school plays the Pledge of Allegiance over the loudspeaker. Nobody is required to say it — we're told we may sit quietly if we prefer — but the expectation is clearly that we stand and recite it, and teachers occasionally single out students who don't. At the same time, in history and civics classes we're taught that the best kind of love of country involves criticizing it when it goes wrong, and that people who protested wars or segregation were patriots, not traitors. Yet when professional athletes knelt during the national anthem a few years ago, many adults around me, including some of the same teachers, described that as disrespectful and unpatriotic. So the actual practice around me seems to run on two different ideas at once: patriotism as affirming the country's symbols, and patriotism as holding the country to standards it fails to meet.

My question is: what view of patriotism does the pledge practice itself presuppose? Does making the recitation optional but expected only make sense if patriotism is a virtue — something like gratitude or loyalty that we owe — and if so, can a virtue really be something you're nudged into performing daily? Or does the practice actually presuppose that patriotism is more like a social habit that needs reinforcement, which would sit badly with the idea that it's praiseworthy? I'm also curious whether the practice is even coherent under a view on which patriotism is a vice or a danger — would a convinced cosmopolitan, who thinks we owe equal concern to everyone regardless of nationality, have to see the pledge as something more than just an empty ritual?

Please reconstruct what the practice implicitly assumes, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and say whether a single coherent account of patriotism can make sense of both the pledge and the lesson that criticism can be patriotic — or whether my school is teaching two incompatible things. Where philosophers who have thought about this genuinely disagree, please say so rather than presenting one side as the settled view; I'd like to know which parts of your answer are widely shared conclusions and which are your own best judgment on a contested question.
