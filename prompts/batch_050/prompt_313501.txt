I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been asked to give the twelve-minute keynote at the reopening of a small city museum, housed in a decommissioned rail terminus and now called the Museum of Unfinished Reforms. The timing is awkward in a way that must not be smoothed over: last month the city finally implemented a long-resisted housing inspection ordinance after decades of tenant organizing, and the same week the budget committee stripped out most of the enforcement money. The audience will be unusually mixed. There will be historians and a few philosophers who notice teleology in every sentence, evolutionary biologists and control-systems engineers who resent sloppy progress metaphors, descendants of tenants whose names are on the first case folders, teenage volunteers who want hope, council members who want absolution, and activists who have asked me in advance not to turn their compromise into a sunrise. The curator has suggested two artifacts as anchors: a brass rail from the old station that was bent by a collision and later straightened enough to use again, and a ledger in which each reform is entered twice, once when celebrated and once when quietly defunded, repealed, evaded, or forgotten.

Write the speech so the idea of history bending toward justice does real structural work rather than serving as decoration. It should reject what the curators call retrospective providence, our habit of narrating whatever survived as if it had been inevitable, without collapsing into the opposite claim that events are merely random noise. Draw carefully on my beat: evolution as descent with modification rather than a ladder, feedback systems as capable of correction without a guaranteed setpoint, and measurement as vulnerable to survivorship bias, but mark the limits of each metaphor before it starts to console. Organize the address around three operations I want the audience to be able to perform afterward: distinguishing direction from destination, telling a ratchet from a mood, and recognizing backsliding that arrives wearing the costume of maturity. Do not quote famous lines, do not cite authors or works, and do not reassure the room that the arc is on their side. End not with prediction or benediction but with a concrete discipline for people who intend to keep bending things after the applause.
