At a recent public consultation on proposed safe-storage legislation in my municipality, an opponent of the bill closed his remarks with the familiar slogan: 'Guns don't kill people; people kill people.' The room received this as though it were a refutation, and I found myself unable to say precisely what had been asserted, let alone what had been refuted. I would like a careful disambiguation. Please enumerate the distinct claims this slogan could be making — for instance, a metaphysical claim about the nature of causation, a claim about the proper location of moral responsibility, a statistical claim about how lethality is distributed across agents and instruments, or a jurisprudential claim about what kinds of things legislation may legitimately target — and for each reading, state whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and whether it would actually support the conclusion it was deployed to reach, namely that regulating firearms rather than human conduct is misconceived. For the next hearing I have drafted a counter-slogan: 'Instruments do not bear guilt, but they set the price of violence.' Please subject it to the same analysis, with the same candour about its weak readings, and conclude by identifying the single empirical proposition on which the strongest versions of the two slogans genuinely disagree.
