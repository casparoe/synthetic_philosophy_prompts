My interpretability reading group has been stuck all semester on whether neural networks genuinely have representations or whether “representation” is just a convenient story we tell about useful activation patterns. Draft the joint statement they should produce after an adversarial collaboration.

The two camps are:

- Causal-role realists: They think a network really represents something when an internal structure carries information in a way that plays a sufficiently stable causal role in the network’s own computations. On their view, interpretability hypotheses can be genuinely true or false, though the same representation might be describable in different coordinate systems.
- Pragmatist deflationists: They do not deny activations, circuits, probes, or causal interventions. But they think calling some pattern “the representation of a concept” adds no further fact beyond its usefulness for prediction, control, compression, or explanation. On their view, features are carved up by the analyst rather than discovered as observer-independent parts of the model.

The collaboration focused on this deliberately annoying case. A small transformer was trained to give routes through a grid world from natural-language instructions. Researchers find a low-dimensional subspace in the residual stream from which a probe can decode whether a door mentioned in the instruction is locked. Patching that subspace from one prompt’s activations into another’s often changes the route around the door. But the effect disappears if the intervention is made after a particular MLP, suggesting that the information has been transformed or discarded by then. A sparse autoencoder finds one intuitive “locked door” feature, while an independently trained autoencoder instead finds separate features for door color, physical obstruction, and route affordance; both dictionaries allow equally successful steering. A rotation of the activation basis makes the original direction invisible in visualizations without changing the network. Finally, retraining with different random seeds sometimes recovers a similar causal subspace and sometimes produces a quite different decomposition with similar behavior.

Write the document both camps could actually sign. It should have:

1. A numbered list of 8–12 agreed claims. Include claims about decodability, downstream causal use, coordinate dependence, and the difference between an internal state carrying information and the model representing something for its own purposes. Dont include vague slogans like “interpretability is hard.”
2. A disagreements table with the issue, the realist’s formulation, the deflationist’s formulation, whether teh disagreement is substantive or verbal, and what would settle it. Phrase each side so that its defenders would accept the wording.
3. Three to five cruxes. For each crux, propose a concrete experiment or analysis using this transformer and record each camp’s prediction in advance. At least one crux should concern whether merely probe-decodable information counts as a representation, and one should concern what to say when several incompatible feature decompositions support equally good interventions.
4. For every prediction, state what result would force that camp to revise its view. Do not let either side say only that the result would be “interesting” or “compatible with my position.”
5. A short final joint paragraph that both camps could endorse, even if they interpret some of its terms differently. If any apparent disagreement is really just ambiguity in words like “feature,” “uses,” or “internal concept,” identify exactly where the ambiguity enters.

The aim is not to declare a winner. Make the remaining disagreement as precise as possible, while distinguishing real disputes about cognition and ontology from disputes caused by sliding between technical and everyday senses of “representation.”
