I’m in a political phil seminar and we’re stuck on Hobbes. My prof keeps saying the state of nature in Leviathan is “just a model,” but that feels like it dodges whether the social contract is supposed to be binding because of what people would rationally fear or because an actual sovereign actually showed up. Use this bit from Hobbes’s Introduction to Leviathan as the hinge: “For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life?” Don’t treat it as a cute metaphor only.

Here’s the case I invented for office hours: a city lets residents form “micro-commonwealths” by signing a smart-contract charter; if 60% opt in, the charter instantly gets police powers over the opt-ins, and the old city can’t intervene. Nobody was ever in a literal war-of-all-against-all, but the signup screen shows each person a simulation of what happens to them under no charter, under a charter they help write, and under a charter imposed by the biggest bloc. Is the charter legitimate only for people whose simulated no-charter life is bad enough that consent is rational, or is consent contaminated because the simulation itself scares them into authorizing power?

Write an essay, max 600 words, leading with a one-sentence verdict. Defend whether Hobbes’s argument needs the state of nature to be historical, merely possible, or just a device for making authority look artificiall rather than natural. Apply it to the micro-commonwealth case, say whether consent there is valid or coerced, and take a side: no “it depends” mush. Include one objection a Hobbes fan would press and answer it.
