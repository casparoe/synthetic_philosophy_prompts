I'm a high school senior taking an elective in bioethics, and for my capstone project I've spent the semester moderating a running dispute between two classmates about advance directives for dementia. One defends what she calls the priority of precedent autonomy: when a competent adult signs a directive refusing certain treatments in the event of severe dementia, that directive should govern even decades later, because the competent self is the only self ever in a position to decide how her life as a whole should go, and ignoring the directive treats her deepest commitments as revocable by the mere passage of time. The other defends the priority of the present patient: once a person can no longer remember, understand, or endorse the values behind her directive, what matters morally is the welfare of the person actually in front of us, who may be calm, affectionate, and clearly enjoying things, and whose earlier self is in no position to be harmed by being "disobeyed." After months of arguing in circles, they've agreed to try an adversarial collaboration and produce a joint statement, and I volunteered to draft it. Every version I write, though, is rejected by one side or the other as slanted, so I'd like you to write the draft they should be aiming for.

Here is the case that anchors their disagreement, which the statement should use as its shared reference point. At forty, after watching her mother's long decline, a woman signs a directive stating that if she ever develops advanced dementia she is to receive comfort measures only: no antibiotics for infections, no feeding tube, no hospitalization. At seventy-eight she has advanced dementia. She no longer recognizes her children and could not explain what an advance directive is, but by every outward sign she is content: she hums, eats with appetite, and squeezes the hand of whoever feeds her. She then develops pneumonia, which can be treated with a cheap course of antibiotics. Her directive says no. Her son argues that she is happy now, that treatment would let her go on being happy for years, and that the woman who signed the directive would be horrified to learn it was being used to end the life of someone as cheerful as her mother now is.

The statement should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can honestly sign: about what the case does and does not show, about what decision-making competence involves, about whether apparently contented dementia patients might have interests their behavior doesn't reveal, and about the difference between overriding a directive and genuinely being unable to apply it. Second, a list of the exact points of disagreement, each phrased in wording that the side whose view is being stated would accept as fair. Third, the cruxes: the underlying questions such that settling one of them would force at least one classmate to change her mind. I suspect one crux is whether the woman at seventy-eight is, in the morally relevant sense, the same person who signed the directive at forty, and another is whether interests in one's life going according to one's values can survive the loss of the capacity to care about those values, but you should identify the cruxes yourself rather than take my word for it. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or kind of real-world evidence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance so the collaboration could in principle be settled. Finally, if part of their quarrel turns out to be merely verbal — for instance, if they've been using "autonomy" or "her interests" in different senses without noticing — say which part, and explain why the residue is or isn't still a genuine disagreement.
