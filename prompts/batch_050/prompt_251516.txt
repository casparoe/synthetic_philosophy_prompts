So I recently left a quant job in finance to work at a place that does long-range risk assessment for funders -- think stuff like "what's the chance of a major engineered pandemic in the next 20 years" or "will this policy cause a recession." Coming from a world where everything had a price and a volatility, I did a bunch of reading to get my head straight (mostly Keynes's Treatise on Probability and some secondary stuff) and I want to check my current position actually hangs together before I embarass myself in front of my new colleagues. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Keynes was basically right that probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis -- it's the degree of belief it's *rational* to have given the evidence, not a frequency, and not just whatever credence someone happens to feel.

(2) Some propositions are non-numerical: there's simply no meaningful precise probability for things like "transformative AI by 2045" or "this novel tax policy tips us into recession." And Keynes's "weight of evidence" is a real, separate dimension -- two situations can have the same probability but differ massively in how much evidence backs them, and that matters for decisions.

(3) Still, at work we should keep assigning exact numbers (12%, 3%, whatever) to events like the ones in (2) and rank interventions by expected value, because decisions have to be commensurable and any number is better than no number.

(4) Wherever we use numerical probabilities they have to obey the probability axioms -- additivity and so on -- otherwise you're Dutch-bookable and that's decisive.

(5) When I have no relevant evidence bearing on p versus not-p, the rational degree of belief is 1/2. Indifference is just what rationality requires in the absence of information.

Which of these actually conflict, and how badly? Please don't just flag everything as problematic -- rank the tensions by severity, and for each one say what exactly would have to give and what that costs. Also tell me if some pair that looks tense actually isn't once you're careful. And if one single revision would clean up the most damage, name it.
