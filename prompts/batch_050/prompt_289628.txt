Two friends of mine (both did some phil of physics in grad school, now one's a data scientist and one's a high school teacher) got into a long argument at a BBQ about whether the quantum wavefunction is a real thing out in the world or just a way of encoding what we know. I only half followed it and I'd like a proper referee. Roughly how it went:

(1) Sam (pro-real): the wavefunction has to be a real physical thing, because it interferes with itself. In the double-slit experiment something has to actually go through both slits and recombine -- a mere bookkeeping device about our beliefs can't produce an interference pattern on a screen. Information doesn't interfere; waves do.

(2) Priya (pro-knowledge): that's way too quick. Classical probability distributions can look like a single spread-out object too, but nobody thinks a probability distribution is a physical field. And there are toy models where you treat quantum states purely as states of knowledge with a restriction on how much you can know, and you reproduce a surprising amount of quantum behavior -- interference-ish effects, no-cloning, teleportation-like stuff. If a purely epistemic state can mimic that much, interference doesn't force you into realism.

(3) Sam: but there's an actual theorem here -- PBR. It showed that if two people could assign different wavefunctions to the same system as mere states of knowledge about some underlying reality, you'd get measurement predictions that contradict quantum mechanics. So psi-epistemic views of the kind Priya wants are ruled out, not just unfashionable.

(4) Priya: PBR only goes through if you assume systems prepared independently have completely uncorrelated underlying states, and that's a real assumption, not a technicality -- retrocausal models and a few other exotic options drop it and survive. Besides, something like the QBist reading of quantum mechanics treats the state as an agent's degrees of belief and doesn't care about PBR because it denies the framework the theorem assumes. So the theorem rules out one specific way of being epistemic, not epistemicism as such.

(5) Sam: fine, then take the most straightforward ontic reading: Everett. The wavefunction is all there is, it evolves unitarily forever, no collapse, no special role for observers or knowledge. It's the interpretation that takes the formalism most literally, and taking the math literally has a good track record in physics.

(6) Priya: but notice what that costs you -- your "real wavefunction" now lives in a gigantic configuration space, not in ordinary three-dimensional space, and it describes a constantly branching multiverse. Calling that "real" is such an inflation of what "real" means that it starts to look like a verbal victory. If the price of psi being real is a universe-splitting ontology nobody can observe, maybe realism about psi wasn't the common-sense position you sold it as at step (1).

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who wins each step and why (or whether a move is a draw)? Then tell me: what are both of them missing -- e.g. is the "real vs. just knowledge" framing itself sharp enough to have a winner, or does the interesting question sit somewhere else? Neither of them will listen to me, so make it convincing.
