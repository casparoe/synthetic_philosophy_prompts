So I just got out of a two-hour argument with my flatmate about whether anything virtual is real. His line: it's all just data on a server, so nothing *in* a game can be stolen or destroyed — the only real harms are the hurt feelings on this side of the screen. I think that's wrong, but when I tried to defend it my own verdicts turned out to be kind of a mess, and I want to know what principle (if any) is actually behind them.

My verdicts:

- Someone exploits a bug to take a rare sword another player grinded 300 hours for: that's real theft.
- A griefer wrecks a Minecraft build someone spent months on: real destruction of a real thing.
- A player tortures an NPC in a single-player game: nothing real is harmed, though it's creepy and maybe says something bad about the player.
- A band plays a show inside a game and a million avatars attend: that's a real concert, a real event.
- Someone's avatar gets stalked and harassed in VR: real harm, but the harm lands on the person, not the avatar.
- The studio shuts the servers down and everyone's items vanish overnight: not theft — annoying, but nothing was taken from anyone.
- Yet: a hacker drains someone's in-game currency they never paid real money for and can't cash out: still real theft.
- A decorative digital item nobody ever looks at or cares about: honestly I don't think it's a "real object" the way the sword is.

The pair I can't reconcile myself: if the sword is real enough to be stolen, why isn't it real enough that deleting the servers destroys it? And if what makes it real is the time and care invested, the shutdown case should count as mass destruction, which I keep refusing to say.

My tentative principle: a virtual thing is real (and harms to it are real) to the degree that people have built parts of their lives around it — time, money, identity, relationships. Test that against my cases, repair it, or replace it with something better. I want: the principle stated precisely, which case motivates each clause, which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and what it says about cases I didn't mention (e.g. scarce virtual land, AI companions people grieve over).

At the end, sum it up as a table with columns: Case / My verdict / The principle's verdict / Agree? / One-line reason.
