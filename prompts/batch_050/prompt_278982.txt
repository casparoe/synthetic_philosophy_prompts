I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and we're currently on the units of selection debate. For my term paper I'm using a passage from a long-form science magazine as a foil, and before I commit to criticizing it I want to know whether it's actually a fair target -- whether I'd be attacking a real position or a distortion of one. Here's the passage:

"For much of the twentieth century, biologists explained animal behavior by asking what it does for the species: deer hold back their reproduction so the herd doesn't overgraze, and sentinels give alarm calls to protect the group. In the 1960s this picture was shown to be a mistake. George Williams demonstrated that natural selection operates on genes rather than groups, and Richard Dawkins later developed the argument into its mature form: organisms are survival machines built by genes, and genes are the true units of selection because they alone are copied intact from one generation to the next. Apparent altruism turned out to be selfishness in disguise -- a gene for alarm-calling spreads because it favors copies of itself in the caller's relatives, not because it benefits the group. Group selection has lately been revived by a few theorists under the name 'multilevel selection,' but most biologists regard this as kin selection under another label, since the mathematics comes out the same. The upshot is that explaining a trait by appeal to the good of the group is not merely usually mistaken but confused in principle: selection can only favor what benefits the replicator."

My inclination is that the piece is broadly reliable but that something goes wrong in the final sentence, though I can't articulate what. I'd like an audit I can build a paper on:

1. Go through the passage's main claims and sort them into what a working philosopher or historian of biology would accept as accurate, what they'd call a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what they'd contest -- and for anything contested, identify which side of the current debate would contest it and on what grounds.
2. Two things I'm especially unsure about: first, whether the argument that genes are the "true" unit because they alone are copied intact settles the question by itself, or whether there are distinct questions (about beneficiaries, about the level at which fitness differences manifest, about what counts as a unit of selection at all) being run together; second, whether the equivalence claim about kin selection and multilevel selection is the settled consensus the passage presents it as, or a position some theorists hold and others reject.
3. The closing claim -- that group-benefit explanations are confused in principle rather than merely empirically disfavored -- is what I want to use as the paper's target. Does it actually follow from anything true earlier in the passage? If it doesn't, what is the strongest nearby claim that does follow, and how much less rhetorically convenient is it for the magazine's purposes?
4. Finally, if you were repairing the passage with minimal edits so that both a gene's-eye-view partisan and a multilevel-selection theorist would call it fair, which sentences would you change and why?
