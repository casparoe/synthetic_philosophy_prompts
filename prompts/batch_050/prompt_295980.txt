For a review session in my philosophy of psychiatry seminar, I want a decision tree that sorts the main positions on whether delusions are beliefs, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start the tree at a shared datum: patients sincerely and persistently assert things like that their spouse has been replaced by an impostor or that they are dead, yet often fail to act accordingly (a man who insists he is dead still eats and sleeps; many patients live amicably with the alleged impostor for years). Each branch node should be a question answerable without already knowing which position it leads to — for instance, whether sincere, stable assertion settles the belief question; whether believing something requires that it be integrated with one's behavior and other attitudes; whether a state counts as a belief only if it was acquired and is maintained in something like an evidence-responsive way; and whether a two-stage etiology (an anomalous experience plus a reasoning or evaluation failure) settles the status question or merely bears on it. The leaves should include at least standard doxasticism, the two-factor doxastic account, and the imagination account (on which delusions are adopted imaginings misidentified by the patient as beliefs), plus any further position you think is genuinely occupied. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, mark it as an empty leaf and say in one sentence whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of positions your tree's questions cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap. Finally, summarize the whole instrument in a table with the following columns: Position; One-sentence characterization; Branching questions, in order, whose answers locate it; Most awkward commitment; and, for empty leaves, Diagnosis (incoherent / unattractive / unexplored).
