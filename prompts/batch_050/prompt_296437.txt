I'm a senior at a public high school, and this year I'm the student representative on our school district's Naming Review Committee. Over the next eighteen months we'll review about a dozen names and markers across the district: two school buildings, a statue on the front lawn of my school, a gymnasium, several endowed scholarships, and one street running past the elementary school. We make recommendations to the school board, which can adopt them or send them to a public vote. I get roughly one evening per case to prepare, I have no staff or budget, and whatever process I use has to be explainable in three minutes at a public meeting to people who have read nothing on the subject.

I want you to turn the ethics of commemoration into a decision procedure I can actually run on each case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation, and where it must stop and hand the question to a vote because it's a matter of values rather than analysis.

I've worked out three distinctions on my own, and the procedure must incorporate them (I've coined the terms, so please use them):

1. Encounter weight. Honors differ in how they're met. A street name is mostly background: you see it on envelopes and barely register it. A school name is recited: students chant it at games and print it on applications. A statue on the front lawn is confronted: you walk under it daily and it looks back at you. The same person, commemorated three ways, seems to impose three different burdens on the people made to encounter the honor.
2. Honor, label, or transaction. Some names were chosen to praise the person; some are just labels that accreted over time; some were purchases, where the name was the price of a donation. I suspect the case for removal differs across these, and that removing a purchased name raises a promise-breaking question that removing an honorific one doesn't.
3. Fault-to-honor distance. Sometimes the objectionable conduct is incidental to the reason for the honor; sometimes it's adjacent; sometimes the achievement being honored is itself built out of the misconduct (as with discoveries made in experiments on non-consenting subjects). I want distance treated as a dimension with consequences at each step, not as a single pass/fail gate.

Hard requirements. The procedure must: include at least one remedy short of removal (recontextualization, counter-monuments, relocation, renaming while retaining the surname for a different honoree) plus a rule for when such remedies are genuinely insufficient rather than merely cheaper; state what to do when the historical facts are honestly contested; handle living namesakes separately from dead ones; specify whose reactions have standing and how much weight they get (students currently required to attend the school versus alumni nostalgia versus donors versus the wider town); and flag every step where I'd be making a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item. It should close with a section on what the procedure cannot settle, where the honest answer is that the committee has to own the decision.

Then run it on these three cases, showing the work step by step:

Case A: A middle school named in 1961 for its founding principal, who served thirty years and is remembered as personally kind and administratively excellent. For the first half of his tenure he implemented the district's segregation policy without protest, though also without evident enthusiasm; after desegregation he ran an integrated school competently for a decade. Older alumni want the name kept; a group of current families wants it changed.

Case B: The statue on our lawn, erected in 1911 and funded by a fraternal organization whose dedication materials state openly that the man was being honored for defending the social order of his time. He was a local industrialist honored for his civic philanthropy, but the record is clear that much of the fortune he gave away came from leasing convict labor under brutal conditions. Here the misconduct seems to fund the very achievement being honored.

Case C: Our science wing, named five years ago for an alumnus whose donation paid for it. He has just been convicted of a serious financial fraud. He has written to the board asking that his name be removed because the coverage is hurting his family, while half the board wants the name kept until the donation is somehow repaid -- except the money is spent, and clawback from the school's end is legally murky.

Finally, tell me which of the three verdicts is most likely to be the one I won't want to defend at a public meeting, and why the procedure produces it anyway.
