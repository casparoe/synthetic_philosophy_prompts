I've been working through presupposition and accommodation, and I'd like to reverse the usual exercise. Rather than being handed a theory and asked what it predicts, I want to give you my verdicts on a set of cases and have you extract the theory. Assume throughout that each utterance opens a conversation with a cooperative, attentive hearer who has no prior relevant beliefs.

(1) "I should leave soon -- my sister is picking me up." Verdict: fully acceptable. The hearer simply adds that I have a sister to the common ground without protest, even though I never asserted it.

(2) "Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?" Verdict: defective, and not repairable by the hearer quietly adding anything. I take the problem to be something more specific than the question being rude.

(3) "Even John passed the exam." Verdict: acceptable. The hearer can accommodate that John was among the least likely to pass, and perhaps that others passed as well.

(4) "John passed the exam too." Verdict: infelicitous -- noticeably worse than (3), even though "someone other than John passed" seems no harder to add to the common ground than "the speaker has a sister" was in (1).

(5) "If I have a sister, my sister will be at the party." Verdict: acceptable, and crucially it does not commit me to having a sister, whereas the bare "My sister will be at the party" does.

(6) "The present king of France is not bald." Verdict: defective rather than straightforwardly true; negating the sentence does not cancel the existential commitment. (I'm aware of the opposing view on which the sentence is simply true. My verdict is the other one, and I want a principle that delivers it rather than talk me out of it.)

My tentative principle is this: a presupposition triggered by an utterance is acceptable just in case a cooperative hearer could add its content to the common ground without thereby acquiring a belief they would want to challenge. This seems to handle (1) through (3), and to explain (2) as a case where accommodation is blocked because the added content is exactly what the hearer refuses to grant. But I suspect it fails on (4) and says nothing useful about (5).

Here is what I'd like from you. First, state the most precise principle (or small set of coordinated clauses) that captures all six verdicts, and for each clause, name the case that forces it. Second, explain what makes (1) and (4) come apart -- why accommodation succeeds in one and fails in the other -- in a way that goes beyond relabeling the difference. Third, give your principle's predictions for three cases I haven't listed: (a) "Either I don't have a sister, or my sister will be at the party"; (b) "If John has stopped smoking, then Bill has stopped smoking too"; (c) "Mary knows that John's sister called." Fourth, if no reasonably simple principle saves all six verdicts, say which single verdict you would overturn, why that one, and what the resulting simpler theory looks like.

End your response with a final line in exactly one of these two forms: either "Principle:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the unified principle you settle on, or "Revise:" followed by the number of the case whose verdict you overturn.
