In his essay Of the Standard of Taste, David Hume writes: "There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliancy: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftener found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance."

Consider two parties who have argued for years about this essay: a Humean, who holds that the convergent verdicts of qualified critics fix a genuine standard of taste, so that some aesthetic disagreements have a right answer; and a relativist, who holds that divergent verdicts of taste can be faultless on both sides, with no standard beyond each judge's own responses to arbitrate between them. Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration centered on the passage above. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both accept, including an agreed reading of what the passage does and does not show -- in particular, whether the vanishing unanimity at the level of particulars is merely verbal disagreement or substantive, and what follows either way for a standard of taste; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, each formulated so that its own side would accept the wording; and (3) the cruxes, with, for each crux, a case, observation, or argument that both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Two cruxes I expect to appear: whether shared evaluative vocabulary conceals faultless divergence or instead betrays defective perception of a common standard, and whether the qualified critics whose verdicts constitute the standard can be identified independently of the very verdicts they are meant to certify. Where part of the dispute turns out to be verbal in exactly the way the passage diagnoses, say which part and why. Throughout, keep separate what is settled -- about Hume's text and about the contemporary state of the debate over aesthetic normativity -- from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
