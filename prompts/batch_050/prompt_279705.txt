I organize an AI safety reading group at my company, and for next month's session I've volunteered to give a 40-minute tutorial on the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision of AI systems. The audience is mostly ML engineers who are comfortable with RLHF at the level of having implemented it, plus two or three philosophy PhD students who know principal-agent problems but have never trained anything. My worry is that the distinction sounds trivial when stated in one sentence — reward the reasoning versus reward the result — and yet almost everyone in the room will systematically misapply it within minutes, in opposite directions: the engineers will treat it as an implementation detail, the philosophers as a moralized distinction between good and bad incentives.

So don't give me the tutorial itself; give me the plan for it. I want to know which idea has to be in place before the process/outcome distinction can even be posed properly (my current guess is that the credit-assignment problem has to come first, but maybe the notion of an evaluation that is cheaper than verification comes earlier — convince me or correct me), and what the one carrying example should be that runs through the whole session. It needs to be a case where outcome supervision demonstrably rewards the wrong thing while process supervision is at least *conceivably* available, and it must not be math proofs, because the engineers will have seen that example a hundred times and will stop listening. Then I want the misconception map: I expect at least "process supervision just means inspecting the chain of thought, so it's an interpretability technique," "process feedback can't be gamed because you're rewarding the right thing by definition," and "outcome supervision fails only because our reward functions are crude," and I suspect there are subtler ones I'm not anticipating — tell me what they are, and tell me at exactly which point in the sequence each one should be headed off, rather than corrected after the fact. Finally: give me a closing question or short exercise whose answers would let me tell whether the room has actually understood that process and outcome are endpoints of a spectrum of *what information the feedback signal uses*, rather than two competing products to choose between — and tell me what I should explicitly refuse to go into at this level (e.g., debate, recursive reward modeling, faithfulness metrics) and how to say so without sounding evasive.
