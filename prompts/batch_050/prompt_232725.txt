I'm putting together a unit on mathematical explanation for a third-year philosophy of science seminar, and I want to build it around the enhanced version of the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument: the claim that mathematics does not merely supply an indispensable representational vocabulary for science but sometimes does genuine explanatory work, and that this gives us reason to believe in mathematical objects much as we believe in electrons. My colleague in the mathematics department thinks the honeycomb case is the crown jewel of this literature; a biologist friend insists the famous cicada case is just ordinary natural selection wearing a prime-numbered costume. I would like you to settle this by ranking the standard candidate cases by how much trouble they make for the nominalist — that is, by how plausible it is, in each case, that the mathematics is really explaining the physical fact rather than merely recording or representing it.

The cases I have in mind are these. (a) Periodical cicadas emerge on 13- or 17-year life cycles, and the leading explanations appeal to the fact that primes minimize co-occurrence with the periodic cycles of predators and parasites, and with rival broods whose hybrid offspring would disrupt the cycle. (b) Honeybees build hexagonal cells, and the hexagonal tiling provably partitions a plane into equal-area cells with the least total perimeter — though some much-discussed recent biomechanical work suggests bees actually build near-circular cells that flow into hexagons as the warm wax yields under surface tension. (c) The Königsberg bridges, where the impossibility of the desired walking route is explained by the graph-theoretic structure of the city rather than by any causal fact about its geography. (d) The theorem-guaranteed fact that at any instant there are two antipodal points on the Earth's surface with exactly the same temperature and the same barometric pressure, though the theorem is non-constructive and tells us nothing whatsoever about where such a pair might be found. (e) The fact, guaranteed by the topology of the sphere, that at any moment there is at least one point on Earth with no horizontal wind at all.

Please sort these into tiers, strongest to weakest, as exhibits for the enhanced indispensability argument, with a sentence or two justifying each placement. For each case, say whether the explanation is best understood as optimizational — the mathematics contributes an optimization theorem while natural selection or physics does the actual optimizing — or structural, where the mathematics constrains what is even possible and thereby rules out the alternatives; and say whether that classification helps or hurts the case's credentials as genuinely mathematical explanation. Then tell me, for your top-ranked case, what the single best deflationary reply available to the nominalist is, and whether it survives scrutiny. Finally, give me your overall verdict on the collection: does it, taken together, support the claim that there are extra-mathematical explanations of physical phenomena, or does it instead suggest that so-called mathematical explanation in science always factors into a piece of pure mathematics plus a piece of thoroughly physical or biological explanation, with nothing left over for platonism to feed on? If there is a case I should drop from the unit as misleading, or an obvious one I have omitted, say so.
