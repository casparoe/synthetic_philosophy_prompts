For a reading group on AI safety, I drafted the following argument as a warm-up exercise and then found I could not refute my own conclusion. The topic is the "low-impact agent" program: the hope that one can reward an agent for completing a task while penalizing it for changing the world in ways the task did not require, all without ever writing down what humans actually care about. My argument says the hope is empty.

(1) An impact measure, to do its job, must penalize changes to the world for which the agent is counterfactually responsible: states of affairs that obtain given the agent's behavior but would not have obtained under some agreed baseline, such as doing nothing or following a default policy.

(2) Counterfactual responsibility is a purely causal notion. Whether a difference between the actual world and the baseline world traces back to the agent is a question about the causal structure of the environment, not about anyone's preferences.

(3) In any environment containing other agents or ongoing processes, the agent's actions initiate causal chains that ramify indefinitely, and they also preempt other causes: if the robot brings me my coffee, I do not get up to make it, and everything downstream of my making it — the people I would have passed in the hallway, the conversation that would have started — differs from baseline as well. So the set of differences for which the agent is counterfactually responsible is enormous and grows without bound over the horizon.

(4) A measure that penalizes all of those differences collapses into a penalty on acting at all. The optimal policy under such a measure approaches the baseline policy itself, which is paralysis, not safety.

(5) To avoid that collapse, the measure must penalize only the differences that matter. But specifying which differences matter — which changes count as side effects and which are neutral noise — amounts to specifying, in extension if not in intention, what humans value.

(6) Therefore any impact measure either reduces to "do nothing" or smuggles in a specification of value; there is no value-agnostic middle ground. So "avoid side effects" cannot be defined without a full specification of value.

I do not want (6) to be true, since the research program built on denying it strikes me as genuinely promising, but I cannot locate the weak joint. If I had to sacrifice something, I would sooner give up (1) than (4) — the counterfactual framing has always felt slightly off to me, whereas the paralysis worry seems obviously correct as far as it goes. Which step should I actually reject, why did it look sound, and what precisely do I pay for rejecting it? And if the flaw is not a bad premise but a false fork somewhere in (4)–(6), tell me what the third option is and whether it survives contact with the kinds of environments the argument was worried about, or merely relocates the problem one level down.
