I am a novelist, but I also teach an undergraduate seminar on self-knowledge and narrative voice, and I am currently grading midterm examinations. One question on the exam read as follows:

"Moore noted that something is amiss with utterances of the form 'It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining,' even though what is said may perfectly well be true. Explain precisely what goes wrong. Are such sentences contradictions? Treat both the omissive form ('p, but I do not believe that p') and the commissive form ('I believe that p, but not-p'), state whether your diagnosis extends to believing such a sentence as opposed to asserting it, and explain why the difficulty disappears in the third person and in the past tense."

One student answered as follows:

"Moore-paradoxical sentences are absurd to utter but not contradictory. In the omissive case, rain does not require my belief, so both conjuncts can be true at once; the defect lies not in the truth conditions but in the speech act. The commissive case is different: because 'I believe that p' already commits the speaker to p's being the case, conjoining it with 'not-p' yields an outright inconsistency, and its oddity is that of an ordinary contradiction rather than a merely unassertible truth.

The correct diagnosis of the omissive case is pragmatic. To assert p is to express one's belief that p; asserting the first conjunct therefore presents the speaker as believing p, while the second conjunct denies precisely that belief. No sincere speaker can felicitously assert the whole, since asserting it requires believing something the assertion itself disowns. This explains why the third-person and past-tense variants ('It is raining, but she does not believe it'; 'It was raining, but I did not believe it') are unproblematic: the speaker expresses only her own present belief, and there is no conflict when the disowned belief is someone else's or lies in her own past.

The same diagnosis settles the case of belief. Believing is inward assertion — saying a thing to oneself in foro interno — so whatever renders the spoken sentence unassertible renders the thought unbelievable, and there is no separate puzzle about belief once the assertoric case has been solved. Wittgenstein's observation that 'I believe that p' often functions less as a report of one's mental state than as a guarded assertion of p fits this account: if a first-person belief ascription is a kind of hedged assertion, then conjoining p with the denial of one's belief amounts to asserting and retracting in the same breath."

Please assign this answer a letter grade (A through F) and justify the grade with point-by-point comments: what the student has right, what the student has wrong, and whether the diagnosis offered actually covers everything the question asked. The comments should be specific enough that I could return them to the student unchanged.
