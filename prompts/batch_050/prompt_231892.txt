I've come to accept a functional-causal solution to the symbol grounding problem: a symbol means what it does, I hold, just in virtue of occupying the right place in a network of (i) reliable causal couplings between uses of the symbol and features of the external world, and (ii) inferential connections to other symbols in the system. On this view, a suitably embodied and causally embedded system literally has grounded symbols, and there is no further "semantic ingredient" — phenomenal or otherwise — that a functionally identical but ungrounded system would be missing. Tell me what would count against this view. I want more than the standard Chinese Room intuition: which conceivable cases would refute sufficiency (cases where the causal-inferential profile is held fixed while meaning intuitively varies or vanishes); which arguments would show that the view collapses into meaning skepticism or indeterminacy rather than solving grounding; and which empirical results — about how human or machine symbol-users actually behave — the view predicts could not occur. Note also which defeaters friends of this style of solution would themselves concede as binding, and which they would dismiss as question-begging. Finally, assess honestly whether the position risks anything at all, or whether "the right network of couplings" has been left flexible enough to absorb any counterexample.
