Construct a thought experiment in which a collective is plausibly guilty of a wrong even though every living member of it, taken individually, is innocent. The specifications: the setting must be realistic and small-scale — a village, a family firm, a religious congregation, a professional association — rather than a state committing genocide; the wrong must be serious but in the past, carried out by members who are all now dead; no living member knew of it at the time, endorsed it, helped conceal it, or profited from it; and the group's guilt must not be merely the sum of individual guilt, so the case must be designed such that no distribution of blame across individuals, living or dead, fully exhausts what the group itself is guilty of. Present the scenario in a single paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications. First, state, for each of Karl Jaspers' four categories of guilt — criminal guilt (violations of law), political guilt (co-responsibility for how one's community is governed), moral guilt (what one owes one's own conscience), and metaphysical guilt (failures of solidarity with fellow human beings) — whether it attaches to the group, to any individual, or to neither, and why. Second, identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work in sustaining collective guilt despite universal individual innocence, and say whether removing that feature collapses the case into a mere aggregate of past individual wrongs plus present obligations of remembrance. Third, give the strongest objection available to a critic who holds that guilt is by its nature an individual phenomenon and that so-called collective guilt is at best a metaphor for duties of acknowledgment and repair, and assess whether the case survives that objection. Conclude by stating what the case is for: which real disputes about inherited national guilt it would genuinely illuminate, and which it would not. Please write the answer in American English.
