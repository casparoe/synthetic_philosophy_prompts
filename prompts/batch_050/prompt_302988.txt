I do a bit of reading in decision theory on the side (I'm actually a chemist) and there's a move that shows up in basically every argument I've come across against certain kinds of preferences, and I can't work out whether it has a name or whether people just don't notice they're making it.

The setup, in case it matters: someone has preferences that go in a circle. They'd rather have an apple than a banana, a banana than a cherry, but also a cherry than an apple. The standard argument says a sharp operator can offer them: give me the banana plus a penny and I'll give you the cherry (you prefer the cherry, so sure), then the apple plus a penny for the banana, then the cherry plus a penny for the apple, and after three trades they're back with whichever fruit they started with, minus three pennies, and this can go around and around until they're cleaned out. Therefore, the argument concludes, the circular preferences were irrational.

Here's what bugs me. Every telling of this I've found quitely builds in that the offers arrive one at a time and that at each offer the person looks only at that offer. But if the operator handed them a card up front with the whole itinerary — trade one, trade two, trade three — they'd just say no thanks, I can see where this ends. And even without the card, once you've been around the loop once you can adopt a blanket policy of refusing anything this guy proposes, and then you never get pumped either. So the argument appears to need a background assumption that deliberation happens one choice at a time, that you can't bind yourself to a plan, and that a sequence of trades never gets treated as a single object you can accept or reject wholesale.

Does that assumption have a name? I've seen plenty of names for the argument itself but none for what it's leaning on. And is there an established term for the opposing stance, where the rational move is to evaluate the whole sequence as a package and commit to it in advance? Also, if the assumption is doing all the work, does the argument actually show anything about the circular preferences themselves, or does it only show something about agents who can't make and follow plans — i.e. is the irrationality in the preferences or in the way the choices get made? If none of this has an established name, feel free to coin one, but then tell me where the edges are — what happens when the later trades would be carried out by your future self with genuinely changed tastes rather than by you with a loop?
