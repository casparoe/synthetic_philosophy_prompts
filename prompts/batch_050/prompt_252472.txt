So I'm a second-year PhD student in political science, but my dissertation is drifting toward historical institutionalism and I keep bumping into the path dependence literature. My committee wants me to turn the "mechanisms" chapter into something more ambitious, basically a small research agenda I could spend the next 4-5 years on, possibly with a couple of collaborators if my advisor's grant comes through.

Here's my problem. Everyone gestures at lock-in and increasing returns, but when I read closely the field seems to have a bunch of open questions that nobody has really sorted, and I can't tell which ones are actually load-bearing. Things like: whether path dependence is a claim about the world or just about the limits of our counterfactual reasoning (how would you ever *show* that an outcome was locked in rather than just very likely, given that you only observe one history?); whether the standard distinction between self-reinforcing and merely reactive sequences survives scrutiny or collapses once you specify the mechanism precisely; the question of whether small contingent causes can really explain big durable outcomes, or whether appealing to contingency is just a way of dressing up our ignorance of the actual causes; whether "critical junctures" can be identified non-circularly, i.e. without just pointing at the moments where the outcome later changed; and whether lock-in claims survive the objection that the same outcome would have arrived by another route anyway (convergence), which seems to threaten a lot of famous cases.

What I want from you: which two or three of these (or others I'm missing) are actually most worth building a research agenda around, and in what order. Please defend the ordering, that's the part I care about. Some things I'd want the defense to engage with: which questions are upstream of which, in the sense that answering one changes how you'd even pose the others; which are tractable for someone whose toolkit is case studies and process tracing rather than formal modeling; and which ones, even if solved, wouldn't actually change how empirical researchers do their work (I suspect at least one of the ones I listed is like this -- philosophically juicy but practically inert -- but I can't decide which).

Also, for whatever you rank first: what would a genuinely good answer even look like? Like, what evidence or argument in the next few years would settle it or at least move it, as opposed to the usual pattern where everyone just restates their prior in more sophisticated vocabulary. If your honest answer is that some of these questions can't be settled with the methods historical institutionalists actually use, say that -- it would itself be a useful result for me, since it tells me wether I need to retrain or reframe.

One constraint: don't give me a reading list, I've got plenty of those. I want the agenda and the reasoning behind it.
