Write an essay about Skolem's paradox, but structured around this thought experiment: imagine a community of mathematicians, the Insiders, who happen to live inside a countable model of set theory. By Löwenheim-Skolem such a model exists if set theory is consistent, and from the outside we can see that the set the Insiders call "the real numbers" is actually countable -- there is a bijection between it and the naturals, it just isn't inside their model, so no Insider can ever find it. The Insiders dutifully prove Cantor's theorem and sincerely assert that the reals are uncountable.

The question: when the Insiders say "the reals are uncountable," are they saying something true, false, or something that lacks a determinate truth value? And here's the kicker you should wrestle with -- suppose the Insiders try to escape the relativity by saying "no, we mean uncountable relative to the REAL intended model, the standard one." Can that move do any work for them, given that every description of the so-called intended model is itself subject to the same nonstandard interpretations? Consider both sides seriously: the Skolemite view that cardinality is irredeemably model-relative so the paradox shows there is no fact of the matter wether uncountable sets really exist, versus the view that the paradox just conflates an internal with an external perspective and tells us nothing deep about mathematics itself. Weigh the strongest arguments for each, but don't sit on the fence -- reach and defend a verdict about what the Insiders' claims actually amount to, and say what follows (if anything) for our own talk of uncountability.
