You are advising the scientific board of a small foundation that can fund three postdoctoral positions in philosophy of science, all devoted to mechanistic explanation — the account, associated with Machamer, Darden, and Craver, according to which explaining a phenomenon means identifying the organized entities and activities whose operation produces it. Your task is to rank the open problems the three postdocs should work on and to defend that ranking. Weigh at least the following candidates, and feel free to substitute one you think I've missed: (a) whether "levels" of mechanisms can be made precise enough to amount to more than a relabeling of part-whole intuitions; (b) how mechanistic explanation should cope with phenomena whose mechanisms are highly degenerate or distributed, as much of neuroscience suggests; (c) whether the framework survives export beyond its home turf — would, say, a market in economics or the internal computations of a trained neural network count as a mechanism in this sense, and would anything of consequence for those fields turn on the verdict; (d) how mechanistic explanation stands to causal interventionism — rival, complement, or special case; (e) whether the literature has fixated on finished explanations at the expense of asking how mechanisms are actually discovered and validated. Before ranking, state explicitly the criteria you will use (e.g., how many other questions a problem is upstream of, tractability for a philosopher rather than a working scientist over three years, neglectedness), then apply them. For each item, explain what would move it up or down your list — which developments in philosophy or in the sciences over the next few years would change the ordering. I care less about the final list than about the trade-offs and reasoning behind it.
