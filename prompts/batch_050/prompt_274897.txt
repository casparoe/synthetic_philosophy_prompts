I serve on my university's faculty conduct board, and we've been landed with a case that has half the campus relitigating the lying-versus-misleading distinction, a topic I've followed for years on various blogs without ever having to actually rule on it. A professor wrote a graduate-school recommendation letter for a student he privately considered hopeless. The letter, in its entirety, stated that the student attended every lecture, submitted all work punctually, has exceptionally legible handwriting, and is unfailingly polite to staff. Every sentence is literally true, and the professor does not dispute his low private opinion of the student's abilities. The student was admitted, failed out within a year, and the admitting department has filed a formal complaint alleging that the letter was a knowing deception. Our conduct policy prohibits "false or misleading representations in official university communications" without defining "misleading," and the professor's defense is straightforward: he said only true things, the admissions committee drew whatever inference it drew, and a speaker cannot be sanctioned for propositions he never asserted.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before our vote. It should reach a concrete recommendation -- sanction, exoneration, or some defined middle outcome -- and defend it in a way both factions can engage with. In working through it, please take seriously the Gricean machinery, since the case is nearly a live-action staging of Grice's own stock example of flouting the maxim of quantity: distinguish what the letter said from what it conversationally implicated, and address whether a speaker is answerable for an implicature he could have cancelled with a single sentence ("though none of this speaks to his philosophical talent"). There is a complication I want treated honestly rather than waved away: Gricean implicatures only work when the hearer can calculate them, and any experienced reader of recommendation letters knows that a letter praising punctuality and handwriting is the conventional code for a weak candidate. If the implicature was transparent to competent readers, arguably the only people deceived were the incompetent ones -- which cuts in favor of the professor (no rational hearer was misled) and against him (he weaponized a code precisely because he could deny it). The strongest version of his position, as I see it, is that holding speakers liable for implicatures makes honesty hostage to hearers' inferences and would criminalize a vast amount of ordinary tactful speech; if you recommend against him, that position needs a real answer, not a dismissal.

One more constraint on the opinion's generality: whatever standard we announce will govern future cases, so the memo should state a workable test for when a literally true statement crosses into a sanctionable misleading representation -- something more principled than "we know it when we see it," and something that doesn't commit us to punishing every professor who ever wrote a lukewarm letter, withheld an unflattering anecdote, or answered a question with a true statement calculated to send the reader down the wrong path. If no such test can be stated cleanly, that itself is a finding the opinion should own up to and factor into the recommendation.
