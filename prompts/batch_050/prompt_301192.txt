So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend about advance directives, specifically whether the person you were before dementia should get to rule over the person you are now. He's fully on the "then-self is king" side -- basically the Dworkin-style line that precedent autonomy should trump whatever the demented person currently seems to want, even if she seems happy. I kept pressing him on it and he's annoyingly slippery.

Anyway, lucky timing: I'm hosting a student philosophy podcast next week and our guest is a bioethicist who's just published a book defending exactly that view -- that a clear, competently written advance directive settles things, full stop, and that "best interests of the current patient" talk is a way of pretending the real person died early when they didn't. I want questions that turn on the actual details of his position, not warm-up fluff, and I want them structured so his favorite escape hatches get used up one by one.

From our argument I already know his likely moves:
- If I press on the demented patient who seems content, he'll say contentment isn't the same as an interest in your life as a whole going the way you shaped it.
- If I bring up that the current patient may literally not be the same person psychologically, he'll say personal identity over time is a red herring because we don't question lifelong promises on those grounds.
- If I ask who counts as the right interpreter of the directive, he'll defer to "a reasonable reading of the patient's own values."

Format the response with these sections, in this order:
1. "Opening" -- one soft-ish question to establish his core claim, ~2-3 sentences.
2. "The sequence" -- five questions in escalating order, each one written out as I'd ask it, followed by the dodge I expect from him and a follow-up question that blocks that specific dodge. Each question-plus-follow-up should be roughly a short paragraph.
3. "The closer" -- one final question that puts the whole view at stake somehow, plus a sentence on why I should end there.

The questions should cover: the contented-dementia case, the personhood/identity point, and the problem of who interprets the directive -- but don't let those three be the only pressure points; find at least one more that neither I nor my friend thought of. And no "what got you into bioethics" type stuff, I only get 40 minutes with him.
