I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about the recent revival of interest in conscription across Europe -- countries that suspended it reinstating or debating it, neutral states with militia traditions holding theirs up as models, and so on. My formal training is in economics, and it's the vocabulary I actually think in: deadweight loss, in-kind versus cash taxation, human capital accumulation and depreciation, adverse selection, public goods, principal-agent problems, option value. When I read the policy and philosophy literature on military manpower, I keep having the feeling that half the arguments are economics wearing a uniform and the other half are something else entirely, and I'd like that sorted out before I file.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take the three main positions in the conscription debate and restate each one in my vocabulary, as precisely and as charitably as you can. First, the economist's standard position: that conscription is a tax collected in kind from a narrow, randomly selected segment of the young population, and that a professional army funded through general taxation dominates it on efficiency grounds. Second, the case for a militia or armed-neutrality system of the Swiss or Finnish type, where near-universal training and a large reserve are supposed to deliver deterrence at lower cost than a standing force of comparable capability. Third, the citizen-soldier argument, which claims that conscription changes the political economy of going to war itself -- that when the costs of a war fall on a random cross-section of voters' own children, democratic decision-making about war is different, and better disciplined, than when war is outsourced to volunteers drawn disproportionately from the poor. For each position, give me the cleanest translation you can manage into economic terms, then tell me explicitly what had to be stretched or redefined to fit.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report. Which claims in this debate have no honest expression in the vocabulary of taxes, incentives, and information? I'm thinking of things like equality of sacrifice, the claim that military service forms citizens rather than merely employing labor, or the idea that a state that rents its defense stands in a different relationship to its people than one that conscripts it. For each piece of residue, I want your judgment on whether it points to a genuine gap in the economic framework -- something the framework cannot say and should be able to -- or whether it reveals that the original argument was doing rhetorical work that dissolves once you try to state it in terms of costs, benefits, and constraints. I'd also like to know whether the citizen-soldier argument, which looks the most like economics on its face, actually survives translation better or worse than the openly non-economic claims, and what that asymmetry would mean for how I should present the debate to readers.
