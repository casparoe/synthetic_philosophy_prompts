I've been collecting my intuitions about when a biological trait counts as an adaptation, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. My verdicts: (1) The human chin is not an adaptation; it falls out of how the jaw's growth fields are structured. (2) The redness of vertebrate blood is not an adaptation, even though it's universal. (3) The whiteness of polar bear fur is an adaptation — though (2) and (3) are both color traits, so something has to separate them. (4) The peacock's train is an adaptation despite reducing survival, because it is sexually selected. (5) The human capacity for music is a byproduct rather than an adaptation, while the capacity for language is an adaptation — even though both are near-universal, complex, and leave no direct fossil record of selection. (6) A trait that rode to fixation by linkage with a selected allele is not an adaptation. (7) The panda's false thumb is an adaptation despite being a jerry-built wrist bone rather than a digit. (8) Feathers are adaptations for flight even if they first spread for insulation — though this is the verdict I'm least sure of. Is there a coherent criterion (a history-of-selection test, a current-function test, or some hybrid) that delivers all or most of these? Please state the principle precisely, clause by clause, indicating which of my cases motivates each clause; say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and should be dropped; and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: the human ability to read, and a trait that currently boosts fitness but reached fixation by drift.
