I've been arguing with a friend for months about trolley cases and the doctrine of double effect. I defend a fairly orthodox DDE line: it matters morally whether you intend harm as a means (footbridge) versus merely forsee it as a side effect (the bystander switch), and the loop variant shows the "mere means" idea is doing real work rather than some cruder distinction like touching vs. not touching. My friend is a deflationist — she thinks DDE is a post-hoc rationalization bolted onto intuitions that are really driven by mushier stuff (vividness, personal force, whether you can picture the victim's face), and that the whole project of reverse-engineering a moral principle from vignette responses is methodologically broken anyway.

Write the joint statement we should produce if we ran this as an actual adversarial collaboration. Structure it like this:

1. **What we both sign** — agreed claims, numbered, roughly 150–250 words. Include at least: a formulation of DDE that I'd accept as fair, what my friend concedes about the actual pattern of intuitions across the standard cases, and what we both grant is genuinely puzzling about them.
2. **Where we part ways** — a table. Each row: the disagreement stated neutrally, then my position phrased in words I'd accept, then hers phrased in words she'd accept. No slanted wording in either direction.
3. **Cruxes** — three of them, ~120 words each. For each one: why settling it would move at least one of us, and a case or finding we both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions about it written down in advance.
4. **The verbal residue** — short, ~80 words. Which part of our dispute you think dissolves once the terms get pinned down, and which part survives.

For one of the cruxes, use a case I cooked up and make both of us commit to predictions. Call it **Fool's Loop**: a runaway trolley is heading for five workers. The driver diverts it onto a side loop that rejoins the main track just ahead of the five. A large man is sitting on the loop, and the driver believes — and this belief is built into his plan — that if the trolley makes it around the loop, the man's body will stop it, killing him but saving the five. Unknown to the driver, a rockfall blocked the loop fifty meters past the man, so the man was never actually going to be hit; the trolley was always going to grind to a halt in the rubble. Did the driver do something wrong, and what exactly does the answer hinge on — the structure of his plan, or the actual causal layout of the world? Give each of our predictions, plus what the case would have to show for one of us to give ground.

Don't adjudicate who's right overall. The deliverable is a document we could both put our names to, not a verdict.
