So my team at work is doing a data ethics training, and one of the slides (written by a coworker who's usually pretty reliable) summarizes the COMPAS / recidivism fairness literature like this:

'In 2016 ProPublica accused the COMPAS recidivism algorithm of racial bias because it produced higher false positive rates for Black defendants than for white ones. Since then, computer scientists have proven that this kind of fairness is mathematically impossible to achieve: a famous impossibility theorem shows that no risk assessment tool can be fair to all groups at once, no matter how it's designed or how much data you train it on. The theorem works by showing that equalizing error rates across groups necessarily breaks calibration, so someone always has to get the short end. This basically vindicates Northpointe, the company behind COMPAS: their tool was well-calibrated, i.e. fair in the only statistically meaningful sense, and ProPublica's criticism rested on a misunderstanding of how these instruments work. The takeaway for practitioners is that if your model is well-calibrated, you've done everything fairness requires of you, and any remaining disparities in error rates are just math, not ethics.'

The slide cites Alexandra Chouldechova's paper "Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments" for all of this, but that paper itself says:

"It is important to bear in mind that fairness itself—along with the notion of disparate impact—is a social and ethical concept, not a statistical one. An instrument that is free from predictive bias may nevertheless result in disparate impact depending on how and where it is used."

That sentence feels like it's in tension with the slide's takeaway, but I can't quite put my finger on where exactly. How fair is this summary overall?
