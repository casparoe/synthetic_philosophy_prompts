I teach a seminar on decision theory and philosophy of action (about 16 students, one 75-minute session) and I want an in-class game that makes them feel the underdetermination problem in inferring preferences from behavior -- the thing that makes inverse reinforcement learning hard. Here's the specific phenomenon I want them to run into: in simple choice environments the same observed behavior is consistent with infinitely many utility functions (you can prove that certain modifications to the reward leave the optimal policy untouched), so behavior alone never pins down a unique preference ordering, and whatever you infer comes partly from your own assumptions about the agent's beliefs and rationality, not from the data. I want teams to actually try to reverse-engineer a hidden utility function from watching a classmate make a sequence of choices, commit to predictions about future rounds, and then discover that several incompatible hypotheses fit everything they've seen -- ideally that they can't even break the tie by collecting more of the same data. Give me concrete rules and materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, how to construct the hidden information so the ambiguity is guarenteed by design and not accidental, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the identifiability problem. One more thing they should feel firsthand: if the hidden agent is genuinely indifferent between two options and just picks arbitrarily, observers will confidently hallucinate a preference from the noise, so build that in too. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce (real agents don't come with fixed utility cards, for a start). Start with the core mechanic of the game in your first sentence, then work out the details -- no preamble, no restating my brief.
