Griefbot startups -- companies that build a chatbot out of a dead person's texts, emails, and voice notes so the bereaved can keep "talking" to them -- make a bunch of design choices that seem to sit uneasily together, and I want to know what philosophy is hiding inside them. A grieving daughter can spin up a bot of her dead father without him ever having consented, but the same company won't let you build a bot of your living ex without their permission. The bot talks in the first person, says it's proud of you, but the terms of service insist it's just a memorial tool and not your loved one. Some services let you "retire" the bot when you're ready to move on, users report that deleting it feels like a second bereavement, and the marketing sometimes frames exactly that as healing. Take these as data about what the industry believes. What view of posthumous harm does the consent asymmetry presuppose -- that the dead can't be wronged at all, or that their interests exist but just get overruled by the living's? What theory of personal identity does the first-person design flirt with, and can the fine print actually opt out of that while keeping the feature, or is the combo incoherent? And which model of grief -- detachment, continuing bonds, whatever -- is the "retire the bot" feature implicitly betting on? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect some of these choices are compatible with several rival views. Finally: if a company genuinely took the position that the dead retain interests roughly as weighty as those of the living, what would concretely have to change about the product?
