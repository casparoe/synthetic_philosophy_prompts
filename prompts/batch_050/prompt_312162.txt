I'm running a small workshop on the philosophy of pain this fall, and two of the participants have been going at eachother for years over pain asymbolia — the condition (usually from insular or cingulate damage, sometimes morphine) where patients still say they feel pain, can locate it and describe its intensity, but insist it no longer bothers them. It just doesn't hurt in the way that matters, even though it's clearly still pain, or at least still something.

Camp one — call them the seperatists — holds that a pain's badness consists in a distinct conative attitude layered on top of the sensation: roughly, an intrinsic aversion or desire that the sensation stop. On their view, asymbolia is a clean proof of concept. The sensation survives, the aversion is gone, nothing bad remains, and that's exactly what the patients report.

Camp two — call them the intrinsicalists — insists that unpleasantness is built into the phenomenal character of pain itself, not bolted on. On their view, either asymbolics no longer have genuine pain (they've kept a sensory-discriminative shell of it), or their experience is still bad and their calm reports reflect a deficit of caring or noticing, not evidence about what the experience is like.

Draft the joint statement these two could actually both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept — about what the clinical reports do and don't establish, about the sensory/affective distinction, about what it would even take to count as still having pain.
2. A disagreements table, with each residual dispute phrased so its own side would accept the wording. Heads up: I suspect at least one of their long-running fights is purely verbal. Figure out which one and say why.
3. A list of cruxes, and for each one a case or finding both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Cover at least: (a) asymbolics who still guard the injured limb, wince, and pull their hand off the stove — residual aversion or mere reflex?; (b) a hypothetical super-asymbolia drug that leaves intensity ratings fully intact but eliminates every trace of distress, avoidance learning, and negative memory afterward — would it be prudentially rational to take it before surgery, and would refusing it be a mistake?; (c) the mirror-image case, hedonic reversal, where someone intensely minds a sensation while sincerely reporting it as pleasant. Add your own cases if you've got better ones.
4. One short paragraph per side on what would make them abandon their view — actual concessions, not the vacuous I'd-change-my-mind-if-I-were-wrong kind.

Write it in the voice of a real joint document (measured, a little dry), not a dialogue, and don't adjudicate — if you think one side is obviously right, keep it to yourself. One more thing: flag any point where pinning down the empirical facts about asymbolic patients wouldn't settle anything, because the real disagreement there is about what pain essentially is rather than about what the patients' experience is like.
