I teach an undergrad survey in history of philosophy (about 16 students, one 75-minute session) and we're doing Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's *Respuesta a sor Filotea de la Cruz* (1691) -- her reply to the bishop who, writing under a pseudonym, had publicly praised her learning while telling her to give up secular study. Early in the letter she says she nearly answered with silence, then explains why silence alone won't work:

"de manera que aquellas cosas que no se pueden decir, es menester decir siquiera que no se pueden decir, para que se entienda que el callar no es no haber qué decir, sino no caber en las voces lo mucho que hay que decir."

(roughly: things that cannot be said still have to be said to be unsayable, so that silence is understood not as having nothing to say, but as having more to say than fits in the permitted words.)

Design an in-class activity that makes students *feel* that distinction -- and then the weirder thing about the text: she makes this point inside a dazzlingly learned letter whose official purpose is to accept that a woman like her shouldn't be writing at all, so every page of compliance is also a demonstration of exactly what's being forbidden. I don't want a discussion section; something in the rules should force them to live it, e.g. a task where part of the group is only allowed to communicate through heavily restricted channels and the rest of the class draws the natural (wrong) conclusion about what the restricted ones knew or had to say. Give me the setup, rules, materials, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto her position -- including whether her stratergy of labeling her own silence is submission, subversion, or somehow both at once. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing half the class reads the restricted group as just not participating), and be honest about one thing in her situation the activity can't reproduce -- the consequences she was facing weren't points in a game.
