I belong to a small philosophy of mind reading group, and at our last meeting one member — a committed property dualist who thinks the hard problem is real — presented an argument that he says shows his own view comes with an acceptable cost, but which I think proves something no one should accept. Here it is. (1) Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause; physics is causally closed. (2) Phenomenal consciousness — what it is like to see red or feel pain — is not itself a physical property and is not reducible to one. (3) No non-physical property can cause a physical event that already has a sufficient physical cause, since that would require either systematic overdetermination or a gap in physics. (4) So phenomenal consciousness never causes any physical event. (5) My sincerely judging that I am conscious, and saying so out loud, is a physical event — a pattern of brain states and vocal vibrations. (6) So my judgment that I am conscious is fully produced by physical causes and would occur in exactly the same form whether or not I actually was conscious; a perfect zombie duplicate of me would make the very same judgment with the very same conviction. (7) A judgment that would be made in just the same way whether or not it is true cannot count as knowledge of its truth — it is disconnected from the fact it purports to report. (8) Therefore I do not know that I am conscious, and neither does anyone else.

What unsettles me is that my friend grants (8) cheerfully — he calls it a small price for taking experience seriously — while it seems to me the one thing I know better than anything, including the premises of his argument. I suspect something slides between (4) and (7), since the judgment might be causally disconnected from consciousness itself and yet somehow not disconnected from the fact of it, but I cannot make that precise. Please tell me which single step I should reject if I want to keep the claim that I know I am conscious, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs me elsewhere — in particular, whether I can still accept the causal argument from (1) to (4), or whether resisting the conclusion about knowledge forces me to give experience a physical causal role after all. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step you reject — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
