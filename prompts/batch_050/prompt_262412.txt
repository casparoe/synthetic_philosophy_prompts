I'm a PhD student in cog sci and lately I've become convinced that the free energy principle isn't just a theory of brains -- it applies to any self-maintaining system: cells, brains, sure, but also firms, university departments, maybe whole academic disciplines. A department "models" its environment, has priors (its self-image, its hiring norms, its sense of which journals count), and acts to keep itself within viable bounds, and I think that's literally the same formal story as a bacterium, not an analogy.

Tell me what would count against this. Not just the generic "FEP is unfalsifiable" complaint, but the specifics: which observations of how institutions actually behave would genuinely trouble the view (e.g. organizations that reliably seek out surprise and disruption, or that seem to destroy their own niche), which formal weak points the extension inherits -- like where exactly the math licenses moving from "this system persists" to "this system minimizes variational free energy," and whether that step is on shakier ground for a firm than for a cell -- and whether extending the FEP this far loses whatever made the brain version substantive in the first place.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed FEP-everywhere person would concede it or shrug it off. Also: there's a cheap retreat available ("institutions just *look like* free-energy minimizers, it's a useful metaphor"), so tell me whether taking that retreat immunizes the view, and if so whether that's a defect or fine.
