I'm drafting a position statement for a hospital ethics committee on the determination of death and want a consistency check before it circulates. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) death is a univocal biological concept — a human being is dead in the same sense of the word as a dog or an oak tree; (2) the whole-brain criterion is correct for human death, and what justifies it is that a brain-dead body has lost the somatic integration characteristic of an organism as a whole, even when mechanical ventilation keeps its organs perfused; (3) patients declared dead under controlled circulatory-death protocols — typically after about five minutes of observed absent circulation, with no resuscitation attempted — are genuinely dead, so organ procurement from them under the dead donor rule is permissible; (4) the relevant standard for cessation of a function is permanence rather than strict irreversibility: the function will not return on its own and will not be restored, where whether it will be restored depends on what interventions will actually be attempted; (5) a patient in a permanent vegetative state, whose brainstem still sustains spontaneous breathing and homeostasis, is alive. Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? For each real tension, say how severe it is, state the least costly revision and what it costs, and be specific about whether the fix belongs in the concept of death, the criterion, or the clinical protocol. I'd like the tensions ranked rather than flagged as equally bad, and I haven't decided which commitment I'd give up.
