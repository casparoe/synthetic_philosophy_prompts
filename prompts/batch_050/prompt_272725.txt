In the game-theory corners of the rationalist blogs and forums I've read for years, the centipede game is almost always introduced as a starker cousin of the finitely iterated prisoner's dilemma. The standard presentation runs like this: both are games with a known, finite end; in both, reasoning backward from the final move yields a unique equilibrium in which cooperation never gets started; and so, the presentation concludes, anyone who has understood why defection unravels the iterated dilemma has thereby understood the centipede, and experimental subjects who keep passing the pot are simply failing to reason backward correctly. I've come to suspect that this framing is doing real argumentative work — for instance, in forum arguments about whether cooperation among rational agents requires an indefinite horizon, and in dismissals of human play as mere computational error — and I'd like a careful audit of how far the analogy actually holds.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly, in prose: what in the centipede plays the role of a round, of cooperating, of defecting, of the final round with no tomorrow. Then work through the disanalogies, separating the ones that matter for the standard presentation's conclusions from the ones that are merely cosmetic. The ones I have in mind as possibly load-bearing: in the iterated dilemma a defection happens inside an ongoing history and can be answered on the next round, whereas in the centipede taking ends the game outright, so there is no next move on which conditional strategies could operate; in the centipede, watching your opponent pass at an early node is itself evidence about what kind of player they are, evidence that a backward-inducting player must somehow incorporate, and it's not clear the iterated dilemma contains anything analogous; and the centipede's growing pot means that passing is a gamble whose value depends on one's model of the opponent, whereas in the dilemma the payoffs are fixed and the temptation structure is stationary. I'm sure there are others I haven't seen.

The question I most want answered: do the conclusions the analogy is typically used to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Specifically, whether passing the pot is straightforwardly a reasoning error, whether backward induction is the uniquely rational analysis of the centipede in the way it arguably is for the final round of the dilemma, and whether the comparison licenses the broader claim that finite-horizon cooperation among fully rational agents is impossible without some structural uncertainty. Where the analogy breaks, I'd also like to know what the better framing would be — whether the centipede is better understood alongside some other puzzle, or whether it is genuinely sui generis among the standard examples. I don't need citations or a literature review; reason from the structure of the games themselves.
