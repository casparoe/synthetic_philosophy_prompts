my roommate is a psych undergrad and whenever it comes up whether what you believe or want can change what you literally *see*, she pulls out the camera analogy: the eye is a camera, photons hit the retina, the signal travels up, and your beliefs can't rewrite the picture any more than a photographer's politics can change what's on the sensor. she uses this to wave away the whole literature people cite -- the thing about hills looking steeper when you're tired or wearing a heavy backpack, the studies where a ambiguous object gets seen as a gun vs a tool depending on context, all of it. her line is basically: if those effects are real they're happening in judgment or memory, after perception proper, because perception proper is a camera and cameras don't have opinions.

meanwhile a friend of mine in the phil mind program counters with the opposite analogy: perception is like autocomplete on your phone -- the brain is constantly predicting the next bit of input and filling it in from priors, so of course your beliefs and expectations end up inside what you see, there's nowhere they *couldn't* get in.

audit both analogies for me. i want the correspondence tables for each -- in the camera one, what plays the role of the lens, the sensor, the film/sd card, the darkroom; in the autocomplete one, what plays the role of the training corpus, the model weights, the context window. then where each breaks. some things i'm suspicious about: (a) the camera analogy seems to prove way less than she thinks, because the interesting question was never whether beliefs alter the retinal image -- obviously they don't -- it's whether they alter experience downstream, and the camera analogy just assumes there's a clean pipeline with a fixed point where 'perception' ends and 'judgment' begins, which is exactly what's in dispute. (b) but the autocomplete analogy seems to overcorrect -- autocomplete generates text out of nothing but priors, whereas perception is pinned to incoming stimulus at every stage, so the analogy quietly turns 'beliefs modulate perception' into 'beliefs construct perception,' which is a much stronger claim. (c) does each analogy smuggle in a picture of the architecture -- a serial pipeline with an insertion point, vs a mush with no boundary -- and then 'discover' that picture in its conclusion?

final deliverable: for each analogy, say which of its disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion it's being used to support (camera → perception is encapsulated, so the hill/gun studies must be judgment effects; autocomplete → penetration is inevitable and unremarkable), whether those conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better analogy available -- a friend suggested modern phone cameras with computational photography, where the 'photo' is already partly synthesized from statistical priors about what scenes look like, and i honestly can't tell if that's the perfect middle position or just a muddle of both. weigh in on that too.
