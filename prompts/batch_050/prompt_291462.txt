So the episode I keep coming back to: in 1973 the American Psychiatric Association's board voted to take homosexuality out of the DSM-II, where it had been listed as a disorder, and they did it partly under pressure from activists and partly after hearing presentations from sympathetic psychiatrists -- it wasn't like some new lab result came out that settled anything. They even put it to a referendum of the full membership afterward, which upheld the board. Then they kept a residual category for people distressed about their own orientation (ego-dystonic homosexuality and friends), which itself got dropped by the late 80s.

Analyze this through the naturalism vs normativism fight about the concept of disorder. By naturalism I mean roughly the Boorse-style line: disease is a matter of subnormal biological functioning, a factual/statistical question that science settles and values don't enter. By normativism I mean the view that calling something a disorder is partly a value judgment about what's bad or undesirable for a person, so no amount of biological fact settles it alone. What I want to know:

- What should the APA's decision have looked like if naturalism is right? Was a vote by psychiatrists even the right kind of procedure on that view, and if not, what would have been?
- Boorse's account famously struggles here because of reproduction -- does the episode expose that, and how bad is it for him?
- If normativism is right, does that vindicate the activists and the referendum, or does it actually make the decision look worse (values decided by majority vote of a professional guild)?
- What does the lingering ego-dystonic category show -- was the APA tacitly conceding that distress, not functioning, was doing the real work all along?
- Does Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction hybrid (a factual dysfunction component plus a harm component) actually fit this case better, or does it just inherit both problems?

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each two or three sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
