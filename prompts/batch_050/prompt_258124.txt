So I got into this stupidly long argument with my friend Marcus last night that started over a true crime podcast and ended up with him defending Abelard -- the medieval guy whose whole position, as I understand it, is that sin is located entirely in the consent of the will, not in the act or its outcome, so whether the act succeeds is morally irrelevant. I mostly agreed at first, but the more cases we threw at each other the more confused I got about what I actually think, and I want to figure out if there's a single principle behind my gut verdicts or if I'm just a mess. Here they are. Two drivers are equally careless, both texting on the same stretch of road, and one of them happens to hit a cyclist while the other gets home fine: I think they're exactly equally blameworthy. Someone fully resolves to poison his rival, puts the poison in the drink, but the rival just never takes a sip: I think he's just as bad as if she'd died. A nurse mixes up two medications despite following every protocol correctly and a patient dies: I don't think she's blameworthy at all, though Marcus kept saying Abelard would agree with me there. But then: a guy I know has, by his own account, completely consented in his heart to defrauding his business partner -- he's decided he would do it, he fantasizes about it, he's just never had the opportunity -- and while I think that's bad, I can't shake the feeling it's noticably less bad than actually attempting it, which I'm pretty sure Abelard can't say, since the consent is identical. And the other direction: a repeat drunk driver never consents to endangering anyone at all, he just doesn't think about it, but I still think he's seriously blameworthy, which also seems to break the consent-only picture, since there's no consent to harm anywhere in his psychology. Oh, and someone who unlocks the safe for robbers because there's a knife at his kid's throat does in some sense consent, but that feels almost blameless, so "consent" itself seems to be doing some hidden work.

So: is there one principle, Abelardian or patched-up Abelardian or something else entirely, that delivers all six of these verdicts? If it can't, tell me honestly which verdict you'd overturn and why, because I suspect the inward-consent one is where I'm weakest. Also tell me what your principle predicts for a case Marcus and I couldn't settle: someone shoots at what he believes is a sleeping enemy but is actually a corpse -- Abelard presumably says full murder-guilt, but does the best version of the principle agree, and does it matter for that case whether shooting corpses is something that could ever actually harm anyone?
