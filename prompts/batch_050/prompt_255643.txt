I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a long essay about the fabrications produced by large language models: invented citations, misattributed quotations, confident claims that are simply false. The industry calls these "hallucinations," and I suspect the word is doing quiet philosophical work. Write me an essay that asks what these outputs actually are, morally speaking, using Harry Frankfurt's famous distinction as the spine: the liar cares about the truth and deliberately misrepresents it, while the bullshitter is simply indifferent to whether what he says is true, caring only about its effect on the audience.

Three positions seem available, and I want the essay to weigh them seriously rather than strawman any of them. First, plain error: an LLM has no beliefs and no intentions, so calling its falsehoods lies or bullshit is a category mistake — they are malfunctions, like a miscalibrated thermometer, and "hallucination" is at worst an inapt metaphor. Second, structural bullshit: whatever the model's inner life, it was trained and tuned to produce fluent, plausible, agreeable text rather than true text, so indifference to truth is baked into the artifact even if no mind harbors it — the bullshit is in the design, not in a speaker. Third, displaced bullshit or lying: the moral action is located not in the model at all but in the people who deploy it. To sharpen this last option, consider a hypothetical I may use in the piece: a lab's internal testing shows its model fabricates a meaningful share of its citations; the lab builds a feature that flags low-confidence claims, finds in user studies that the flags depress engagement, quietly ships without them, and markets the product as a research assistant. Is the model bullshitting, or is the lab lying through it — and does that distinction survive if the people involved sincerely tell themselves that the model is "mostly reliable"?

The essay should engage the strongest objection to each view: for the error view, that we routinely hold agents responsible without Cartesian certainty about their inner states; for the structural-bullshit view, that Frankfurt's concept seems essentially tied to a speaker's attitude, and stretching it to artifacts risks draining it of content; for the displaced view, that the model's fabrications often surprise even its makers, which fits badly with lying. End with a defended verdict on what these outputs are, and say what follows practically: whether "hallucination" is a euphemism a journalist should stop using, and what vocabulary — lying, bullshit, error, or something else the essay defines — best serves readers trying to decide how much trust these systems deserve.
