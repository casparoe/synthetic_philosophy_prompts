Hume argues that promise-keeping is an "artificial" virtue: promises bind only because a social convention has arisen that attaches obligation to certain forms of words, a convention motivated by mutual self-interest. But that seems to entail that where no such convention is already up and running — between two castaways on a desert island, or between members of previously isolated societies meeting for the first time — a promise can generate no obligation at all, since there is as yet no practice for the utterance to hook into. Am I therefore right that such first-contact promises are morally inert, mere noises until a convention happens to grow up around them? And if so, doesn't that show that the conventionalist account debunks promising rather than vindicates it, since it makes our obligations hostage to historical accident?
