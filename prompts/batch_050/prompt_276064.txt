I've been thinking about the aggregation of small harms — the torture-versus-dust-specks problem and its neighbors in population ethics — and I'd like an audit of whether the following commitments of mine form a tenable package:

1. There is no harm so trivial that no number of instances of it could morally outweigh a single instance of a catastrophic harm. I reject all lexical thresholds: in principle, some astronomically large number of people each getting a dust speck in the eye could be worse than one person being tortured for decades, even if in practice the number required is beyond anything physically realizable.

2. I reject the Repugnant Conclusion. For any population of people with excellent lives, there is no imaginable larger population whose lives are barely worth living such that the latter would be better. A world of ten billion flourishing people is not made worse by comparison to a world of trillions on the edge of non-existence.

3. "Morally better than" is transitive and complete. Any two outcomes can in principle be compared, and if A is better than B and B better than C, then A is better than C.

4. Within a single life, aggregation is obviously legitimate: it would be irrational to prefer one minute of severe agony over even decades of mild recurring headaches, provided the headaches never rise above mildness. Temporal parts of a life add up.

5. I take seriously the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism: the fact that burdens and benefits fall on different individuals matters morally, and the permissibility of a trade-off cannot be settled simply by computing a sum across persons. Distribution is a further, irreducible consideration.

My motivation for (1) is that any threshold seems arbitrary — pick any point where you say "no number of specks can cross this," and I can construct a case just below it that seems implausibly close. My motivation for (2) is simply the intuition, which I share, that barely-worth-living lives don't compensate for lost excellence. My motivation for (4) is prudential rationality; my motivation for (5) is the standard complaint that utilitarianism treats persons like vessels.

Are these five jointly consistent? Please identify the tensions, ordered from most to least severe, and for each say which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned to resolve it and roughly what that revision would cost. I'd also like you to distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones — I suspect at least one pair here looks conflicting but isn't. I'm not attached to any particular resolution and haven't decided which commitment I'd surrender, so don't presume one; just map the trade-offs.
