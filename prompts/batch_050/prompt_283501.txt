So last weekend I got into this long, kinda heated argument with my friend Dan and I can't stop thinking about it. His mom died about a year ago and he told me he's been using one of those griefbot things — you upload a dead person's texts, emails, voice memos, whatever, and it chats with you in their style. I said I thought it was creepy and probably bad for him, he said it's no different than looking at old photos or rereading her texts, which he does anyway, and honestly neither of us won. The thing I couldn't quite articulate in the moment: photos don't talk back. A photo is something you have a relationship *to*, but the bot performs a relationship *with* you, and grieving seems like it's partly about slowly accepting that the relationship can't be updated anymore — that the person is now fixed, done, and you have to reorganize your life around that fact. A bot that keeps generating new responses lets you postpone that indefinitely, maybe forever.

Anyway, I'm applying for a one-semester undergraduate research fellowship and I want to turn this into the project. My half-formed thought: the ethics debate about simulating the dead is mostly framed as consent-and-dignity of the deceased versus welfare of the bereaved, and both of those miss what's actually doing the work, which is the interactivity itself and what it does to the structure of grief. The open question I want to poke at: is grief something with a kind of task or shape to it (roughly, revising your practical identity so it no longer counts on the person being there), such that a tool which blocks that revision harms the user even if it feels good and they chose it freely — or is that picture of grief itself just one contested view, with the "continuing bonds" idea (the relationship legitimately goes on, just transformed) cutting against it, and if so does a griefbot count as continuing a bond or as refusing to transform it?

Write me the proposal, about a page and a half. I need: the question stated precisely (one sentence I could put at the top), why the consent-vs-welfare framing leaves it open, and what I'd actually *do* — this is philosophy, not empirical psych, so I can't run a study on griefbot users; spell out what conceptual work looks like here, like what cases I'd compare griefbots against (photos, reread texts, mediums, actors playing the dead in documentaries, writing letters to the dead) and what the comparisons are supposed to isolate. Also: what a finished product looks like, what would count as the project failing or the answer turning out boring, and a realistic month-by-month scope for one semester part-time. Two constraints: don't rest anything on empirical claims about how good the tech is or will get — just take a working griefbot as a given — and definately no bibliography or citations, I'll fill those in myself.
