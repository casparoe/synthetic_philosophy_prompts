I am a novelist, and I keep a dream journal that I mine for material, so I have more than a hobbyist's stake in the following argument, which I constructed after reading around the debate over whether dreams are experiences had during sleep. Each premise seemed innocent; the conclusion does not. (1) For an experience to occur at a time, there must be a fact about what it was like at that time which does not consist in any later report. (2) The only evidence for the content of a dream is the dreamer's report on waking. (3) Waking reports can be confabulated without the reporter's knowledge, so they cannot fix a report-independent fact about what was experienced during sleep. (4) Therefore nothing fixes what, if anything, was experienced in the night; dreams are at most judgments formed on waking, not experiences undergone while asleep. (5) But the same reasoning applies to any past experience known only through present recollection — the pain of last year's surgery, the texture of a grief — so those too were never experiences, only current judgments. (6) That is absurd; hence one of my premises must go. Which step fails, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (3), since confabulated dream reports are a gift to fiction, and I would prefer not to be forced into the view that the felt content of a dream is constituted by whatever I happen to write down at breakfast. Please give your diagnosis as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
