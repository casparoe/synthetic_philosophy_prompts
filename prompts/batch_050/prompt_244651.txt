I volunteer as a data-quality reviewer for a citizen weather network. Hobbyists run their own stations and upload readings, and before a station's data is used by the network (and occasionally by a local farm cooperative), someone has to sign off that the instruments are trustworthy. I review a few stations each week. I have no laboratory and no budget; I can visit a station for an afternoon at most.

While doing this I have become stuck on a problem that feels conceptual rather than technical. The obvious way to check an instrument is to compare it against something else, but in practice the something else is never fully independent. Comparing two thermometers of the same make shows they agree with each other, not with the actual temperature. Testing a rain gauge by tipping a known amount of water through it checks the funnel, but the known amount came from a measuring jug nobody has ever checked. And consider a barometer that has matched the regional forecast for three years, where the forecast itself is built partly from readings by stations like this one: the instrument is, at one remove, testifying on its own behalf. Every chain of checks seems to rest on something taken on trust, and I cannot tell whether that is a scandal or simply how measurement works.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run during a site visit: the tests in order, what each test does and does not establish, where the procedure stops and refuses sign-off, and where it has to hand the decision back to a human judgment call. It should distinguish at least three situations: (1) checks that rule out a defect in this particular unit but cannot rule out a flaw shared by the whole model of instrument; (2) checks that show an instrument is consistent over time but not that it is correct; (3) checks that are genuinely circular, where the instrument's own output is used, directly or indirectly, to vouch for the instrument. It should also answer the question that bothers me most: once an instrument has been validated by some genuinely independent means, does its subsequent track record count as further evidence in its favor — that is, does self-corroboration become legitimate after independent validation, and if so, what exactly changes at that point?

Then please run the procedure on three cases: a new station whose owner shows it agrees with an established station 800 meters away; a five-year-old rain gauge, never independently checked, whose readings have always been plausible and self-consistent; and a pressure sensor the owner verifies against a phone app, where the app draws on an airport station 30 kilometers away. For at least one of these, I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like — tell me which, and why.

Finally, please state how confident you are in the procedure's central distinctions, and single out the one step or distinction you are least sure of.
