I've noticed a pattern in forecasting disputes that I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know whether the literature does. Take two analysts with sharply different starting views. Put them on recurring questions — next month's inflation print, Sunday's election result — and, because reality grades them every time, their forecasts drift toward each other over the course of a year. Now put the same two on a one-off question: whether a particular treaty will exist by 2040, whether some unprecedented technology will arrive. Both are honest, both update diligently on everything that happens, and yet a decade later their views have barely moved toward each other, because the question is never graded until it settles all at once. The folk result everyone cites — that rational agents' beliefs merge as evidence accumulates — seems to describe the first case and simply not the second, as if convergence were a property of the question's repeatability rather than of the agents' rationality.

Does this distinction have an established name? I don't mean the frequentist/subjective split, or disagreement over values; I mean the structural difference between questions that generate their own corrective feedback and questions that don't. If it doesn't have one, what would you call it, and how would you state its boundary with the ordinary convergence results? And what does it imply for the common claim that priors wash out — is persistent long-horizon disagreement among honest updaters a failure of rationality somewhere, or just a sign that those results were never about such questions in the first place?
