I’ve just had a ridiculously long argument with a mate about Scanlon’s contractualism and whether numbers count, and I think I may have talked myself into a corner. He kept saying that my position was just utilitarianism through the back door, which I definately don’t want to be true. These are the claims I found myself defending:

1. Whether an act is wrong depends on whether it would be prohibited by a principle that someone could reasonably reject. This has to be assessed person by person, according to what that individual stands to lose. There is no extra impersonal standpoint from which everybody’s burdens are simply added together.

2. Nevertheless, numbers can matter. If I can save either one person or five people, all of whom stand to lose exactly the same thing and are equally innocent, I ought to save the five. It isn’t merely permissible.

3. Equal claims must receive equal weight. So in a one-against-one rescue, where everything else really is equal, I should use a fair lottery rather than pick the person I happen to prefer.

4. Minor temporary harms do not aggregate across people against a catastrophe. No number of headaches, spread across different people, could make it right to let one person die instead.

5. In statistical policy cases, we should choose the policy with the lower expected death toll, at least when the possible deaths are genuinely equivalent. This remains true even if the policy with the lower expected total concentrates a much higher risk on a small group, while the alternative spreads a lower individual risk across millions.

6. I still reject utilitarianism, because adding welfare or expected harm across persons ignores the separateness of persons.

Can you audit the package rather than just explain contractualism back at me? Which pairs of claims are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each real tension? Please rank them and say what the least costly revision would be in each case — including exactly which numbered claim needs narrowing or abandoning. I’m particularly unsure whether my friend was right that 1 and 2 cannot coexist, but don’t stop there if 5 is the real problem.
