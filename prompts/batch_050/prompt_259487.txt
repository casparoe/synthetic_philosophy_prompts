I've spent too long reading rationalist arguments about deceptive alignment, sycophancy, and whether an LLM can "lie" if it has no beliefs in the ordinary sense. For a rationality meetup of 10–14 adults with 75 minutes, design a runnable game that makes people feel why defining deception for AI is hard when a system can produce only true sentences while reliably causing users to form false beliefs.

The activity should center on two made-up but useful categories: "belief-shaped output" (text optimized for some objective rather than asserted from belief) and "truthful misdirection" (selecting true evidence so the listener predictably builds the wrong model). Give concrete roles such as user, advisor, hidden sponsor, and judge; information asymmetry; materials; small stakes or points; and a timeline. Include at least one round where the advisor cannot say anything propositionally false and another where it can supply evidence but no recommendation. Participants should discover, before the concepts are named, that judging by sentence-level truth, the user's resulting belief, or the speaker's intention yields different verdicts.

End with a debrief mapping rounds to lying, misleading, negligent omission, sycophancy, and whether deception requires belief, intent, or merely a reliable tendency to induce false models. Also mention what will predictably go wrong the first time—prob. people calling all harmful advice a lie—and what the game can't reproduce about real AI systems, especially hidden internal states and training incentives.
