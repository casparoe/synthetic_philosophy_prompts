I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, working on self-representation, and I am drafting the literature review section of a chapter on Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory of subjectivity. I do not have my copy of his work to hand this week, so I have been leaning on a summary I found in a chapter of a well-regarded popular book on the science of consciousness, written by a science journalist rather than by Metzinger himself. Before I build on it, I would like an accuracy audit. The summary runs as follows:

"Metzinger's central claim is that neuroscience has shown the self to be an illusion: strictly speaking, no one has experiences, and consciousness itself is a kind of useful fiction the brain tells itself. What the brain actually constructs is a phenomenal self-model, an internal representation of the organism as a whole, and the feeling of being someone arises because the organism mistakes this model for reality. This is possible because the self-model is what Metzinger calls 'transparent': we see the world through it clearly, the way we see through a clean window, and with sufficient introspective training, such as meditation, we can learn to watch the model being constructed and catch the process in the act. Phenomena such as out-of-body experiences and the rubber hand illusion demonstrate how malleable the self-model is, and lucid dreaming shows that under special conditions we can indeed become aware of its representational nature. Metzinger concludes that our ordinary belief in a persisting self is not merely false but irrational, and that we should therefore abandon the idea that persons, understood as selves, have any special moral status."

My own sense is that the broad architecture is roughly right, but I suspect at least two things are off. First, the opening slide from "the self is a construct" to "consciousness is a fiction" seems to attribute to Metzinger a much stronger eliminativism than I remember him holding; I had thought he was a realist about phenomenal experience and that it is only the self, not experience, that comes out as a representational construct. Second, the gloss on transparency strikes me as nearly backwards: I had understood transparency to be precisely the feature that makes the construction process introspectively unavailable, not a kind of perceptual clarity that meditation refines. I am not confident enough in either memory to correct a published book unassisted, and I note that the summary's own definition of transparency sits awkwardly with its claim that the organism is thereby fooled.

Could you go through the summary and tell me: (a) which parts a careful defender of the self-model theory would accept without amendment; (b) which parts conflate claims Metzinger keeps apart, overstate a more modest thesis, or use his technical vocabulary in its everyday sense; (c) whether the epistemic and ethical conclusions in the final sentence actually follow from the accurate parts of the theory, or whether the author is drawing consequences Metzinger himself resists or endorses only in a heavily qualified form; and (d) where the out-of-body, rubber hand, and lucid dreaming examples genuinely support the theory and where the summary leans on them harder than they will bear.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each of your corrections, distinguishing points where Metzinger's position is textually unambiguous from points where it is subtle or arguably shifted between his major statements of the view, and single out the one point in your audit about which you are least sure.
