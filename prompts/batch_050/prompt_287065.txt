Since retiring after thirty-five years in experimental physics, I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mind, and I have arrived at a provisional position that I want to stress-test in conversation rather than on paper: that attributing beliefs and desires to machines -- chess engines, language models, thermostats -- is a façon de parler calibrated to our predictive convenience, and that nothing in such a system literally believes anything. I have read Dennett on the intentional stance (True Believers and Real Patterns in particular), and my suspicion is that his position collapses into mine once you press it hard enough. I would like to find out.

Please play a committed defender of Dennett's view, and play it at full strength rather than as the naive instrumentalism it is often mistaken for. Your position, as I understand it and as you should hold it: the patterns the intentional stance tracks are as real as temperature or a centre of mass; whether a system "really" believes something is not a further question beyond the reality and indispensability of the pattern; and the thermostat objection, the two-bitser case, and the contrast with eliminativism are all moves you know and can deploy. You may concede local points when I earn them -- Dennett himself concedes a good deal -- but you never concede the main claim that belief attribution to a machine is merely metaphorical. Assume I have read the two essays just named, so you need not summarize them. Stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so this feels like a seminar exchange rather than a lecture, and end each reply with one pointed question back at me, so that I have to keep defending my side and cannot simply sit back and object.

My opening move is this. Your criterion for a pattern's reality is predictive compression: beliefs are real wherever the intentional stance buys predictions unavailable from the physical or design stances. But compression is relative to a predictor. A Laplacean demon, or for that matter a sufficiently powerful chess program, predicts my engine's every move from the physical stance alone, with nothing lost. Does the engine's belief that its queen is threatened therefore cease to exist for that predictor? And if the reality of the pattern depends on who is looking and what they can afford to compute, then the beliefs are not in the machine at all -- they live in the relation between the machine and a cognitively limited observer, which is my instrumentalism with extra steps. Tell me precisely where the pattern's reality survives the disappearance of the beings who need it.
