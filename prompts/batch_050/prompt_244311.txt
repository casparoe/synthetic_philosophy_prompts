Write a dialogue between two appellate judges, one a committed textualist and the other a committed purposivist, who've been friends for years and are grabbing a drink after hearing oral argument on the same case. The case: their state has a statute, passed in 1987 after a scandal involving bribed health inspectors, making it a felony for any public official to "solicit or accept anything of value in exchange for being influenced in the performance of an official act." The defendant is a city council member who accepted $40,000 in campaign contributions from a developer, then voted to approve that developer's zoning variance -- there's no evidence of an explicit quid pro quo agreement, and the defense argues that campaign contributions aren't "things of value" in the relevant sense and that voting isn't something you can be "influenced in" retroactively since the contribution came first but the money was given with clear expectations. Both judges should be genuinely sharp -- no strawmen. The textualist should have to grapple with the fact that a hyper-literal reading might criminalize ordinary politics, and the purposivist should have to grapple with the fact that the legislature arguably could have written a broader statute and chose not to. Midway through, have them realize they're disagreeing about something deeper: whether "what the words mean" and "what the law is for" are actually two different questions or secretly the same question asked twice. They should respond to each other's actual arguments, concede points when they're beaten on one, and it should be fine (maybe better) if neither fully wins by closing time. Somewhere in there, have one of them bring up the scrivener's error / absurdity problem as a test case for where the other one's theory breaks. Keep it naturalistic -- they can interrupt each other, order another round, bring up an old case from their clerkship days. Around 900-1200 words.
