I write a column on science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on a national drug regulator whose seven-member licensing commission decides each application by voting on two statutory questions: first, whether the evidence shows the product is effective for its stated use, and second, whether the trials establishing this were conducted to adequate standards. Approval requires an affirmative answer to both. The commission publishes each member's vote on each question, along with the final outcome.

Its record so far: on the first application I examined, effectiveness passed 6–1, trial standards passed 5–2, five members answered yes to both, and the drug was approved. On the second, effectiveness passed 4–3, trial standards failed 2–5, only two members answered yes to both, and the drug was rejected. On the third, effectiveness passed 5–2, trial standards passed 4–3, exactly four members answered yes to both, and the drug was approved on a 4–3 vote of the commission. In a fourth case, two members abstained on the first question and one on the second; both questions passed 4–1 and 4–2 of the votes cast, and the drug was approved.

A decision due next week presents a pattern with no precedent in the record: effectiveness passes 4–3, trial standards pass 4–3, but the two majorities overlap in only two members, so just two of the seven answer yes to both questions.

Please tell me: what decision should I predict, and with what confidence? Lay out the distinct decision rules the commission could be running that are each fully consistent with its published record, state what each rule predicts for next week's case, and identify which earlier case is the closest analog and why. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, explain what principle the commission would be adopting with each choice — in particular, what it would be saying about whether its verdicts answer to its stated reasons or to its members' overall judgments, and what pathologies each commitment invites. Finally, name the single additional piece of the historical record that would most decisively settle which rule it follows.
