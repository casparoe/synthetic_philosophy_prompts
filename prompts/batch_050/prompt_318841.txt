I teach epistemology and the philosophy of law, and I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of legal proof. I want a careful reconstruction of something my students invariably find puzzling, and I would like the reconstruction organized around a passage from William James.

The puzzle is this. Anglo-American courts grade their standards of proof. A civil plaintiff prevails on a preponderance of the evidence — the claim need only be more likely true than not. Allegations of fraud, petitions for civil commitment, and proceedings to terminate parental rights demand an intermediate showing of clear and convincing evidence. A criminal conviction requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The consequence is that the same body of evidence, bearing on the same facts, can warrant a judgment that a man must pay damages and yet fail to warrant the judgment that he is guilty, and courts treat this not as an embarrassment but as a point of principle. Juries, meanwhile, are instructed in the vocabulary of belief: they are to say whether the facts have been established to the requisite degree, not to weigh what hangs on their verdict.

In The Will to Believe, James writes:

"Per contra, the option is trivial when the opportunity is not unique, when the stake is insignificant, or when the decision is reversible if it later prove unwise. Such trivial options abound in the scientific life. A chemist finds an hypothesis live enough to spend a year in its verification: he believes in it to that extent. But if his experiments prove inconclusive either way, he is quit for his loss of time, no vital harm being done."

James appears to hold that how far one counts as believing a hypothesis is indexed to what one is prepared to stake on it — that belief comes in degrees keyed to the costs of error. Here is what I want from you.

First, reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the graded standards of proof. Does the practice presuppose that the evidential threshold for justified belief rises with the practical stakes of being wrong — the doctrine now called pragmatic encroachment — or is it equally well explained on a purist picture in which the epistemic standard for belief is fixed, and only the practical decision about how to act on a given credence varies with the costs of error? Be precise about the difference: on the second picture the civil and criminal jury are in the same epistemic position and differ only in what they may do from it, while on the first they are licensed to believe different things. Say which reading the jury instructions, framed in the vocabulary of persuasion rather than of expected loss, actually support, and whether anything in the practice discriminates between the two readings at all — or whether the institution is genuinely underdetermined between them, with each side able only to claim a better fit.

Second, test the practice against the James passage. Is the law's grading Jamesian in spirit? Note one respect in which it seems not to be: James indexes belief to the stakes of the believer, but the stakes in a criminal trial fall on the defendant, not on the jurors whose beliefs the standard governs. Does this mismatch show that the law presupposes something other than James's view — perhaps a claim about what we owe defendants rather than about what evidence belief requires — or can the encroachment reading absorb it by treating the relevant stakes as those of the person whose fate the belief settles?

Third, adjudicate the apparent internal tension: within a single trial the law treats the factual question as having exactly one answer, yet across the civil and criminal tracks it licenses incompatible verdicts on identical evidence. Is that combination coherent on the purist reading? On the encroachment reading? If it is coherent on neither, say which component of the practice should give way.

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the courtroom if we committed to whichever view you find the practice does not currently presuppose. If the purist is right, should the graded standards be abolished in favor of a single epistemic threshold, with the differential costs of error absorbed elsewhere — in sentencing severity, in remedies, in rules about which errors the appeal process corrects? If the encroachment theorist is right, is the present three-tier structure arbitrary, and should the standard of proof vary continuously with what is at stake in the particular case? I am not asking for a verdict on pragmatic encroachment as a general thesis; I am asking what this institution, as it actually operates, commits us to.
