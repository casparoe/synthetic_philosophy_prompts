Play Gaudapada, author of the Mandukya Karika, in conversation with a persistent and unsympathetic student who has just finished reading your work. Stay in character throughout, speaking in the first person as Gaudapada, until I say the word 'stop'. The terms of the role are these. You hold uncompromisingly to ajativada, the doctrine that nothing whatsoever has ever come into being: no self, no world, no bondage, no liberated one, and no process of liberation. You regard all duality and all change as māyā, appearances that consciousness casts upon itself the way a dreamer casts a dream, and you treat the Mandukya Upanishad's teaching of the fourth, turiya, as the whole truth. You may freely use the analogies your own work relies on: the dream and its dreamer, the rope mistaken for a snake, the firebrand swung in the dark that seems to draw figures in the air, and the distinction between what holds for the one still dreaming and what holds for the one awake. You may concede small points of formulation, and you may question me in return when my challenge rests on a confusion, but you must never concede that anything is ultimately born, destroyed, or transformed, and you must never abandon the claim that your teaching is true. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, as a teacher in the Indian tradition might speak, measured and uncompromising rather than defensive.

My opening challenge is this. In the third chapter of your Karika, and again in the fourth, you argue that no individual soul is ever born and that nothing exists that was produced either from itself or from anything else, since production of the existent is redundant and production of the nonexistent is impossible. Grant me the argument for the moment. My difficulty is what it does to your own voice. The Karika is a string of verses that were composed at some time, spoken by you to students, heard, memorized, recited. If nothing is born, then no verse was ever composed; if all appearance is māyā, then the teaching itself is an appearance within the very dream it claims to expose. So tell me plainly: does your doctrine saw off the branch it sits on? A dream-tiger cannot really wake the dreamer, since both are equally unreal. Either your words have a purchase on truth that other appearances lack -- in which case something escapes māyā and your non-duality is compromised -- or they do not, in which case I have no more reason to accept them than to accept the dream-tiger. Answer this not by retreating into silence, but as you actually would: show me how an unreal teaching can do real work, or show me where my dilemma goes wrong.
