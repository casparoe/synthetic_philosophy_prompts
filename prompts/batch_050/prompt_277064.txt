You are a philosopher of science specialising in the dynamics of long-run predictions and the logic of theory rescue. Think carefully before settling on your answer: begin by brainstorming several candidate cases and several distinct rescue structures, and only then select the one that best meets the criteria below.

I am compiling a catalogue of long-run predictions that failed on the timescale their authors originally announced, yet whose defenders insist the underlying theory was essentially correct. For each entry I record the prediction, why it failed, and the *logical structure of the rescue* — the particular manoeuvre by which the theory is said to survive its own falsification. Here is what I have so far:

1. **Malthus (1798).** Population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, so living standards must be checked by famine and misery. Two centuries of agricultural productivity growth falsified this on its stated trajectory. The rescue is *deferral*: the mechanism is sound, the ceiling merely lies further off than expected.

2. **Ehrlich (1968).** Mass starvation was predicted for the 1970s and 1980s; the Green Revolution averted it. The rescue is again deferral, but with an added *damage-accumulation* twist: the overshoot did occur, its costs being paid in soil depletion, biodiversity loss and climate change rather than in the predicted famines. The prediction fails in its details while a ledger of costs is said to be building elsewhere.

3. **Marx on immiseration.** Wages in the advanced economies rose rather than being driven to subsistence. The rescue is *relocation*: immiseration did occur, but was exported — the proletariat of the theory turns out to live in the global South, with the wages of Western workers sustained by that displacement.

4. **Hubbert-style peak oil.** Successive dates for the peak of world oil production passed without the forecast decline, thanks to shale and other unconventional sources. The rescue is *scope revision*: the theory is reinterpreted as applying to conventional or cheap oil specifically, which arguably did peak as predicted; the original claim is preserved by redrawing the boundary of what it was about.

5. **The Limits to Growth (1972).** The models' standard-run scenario projected industrial decline beginning in the early twenty-first century. No dramatic collapse has occurred, but reassessments comparing the scenario with four decades of data claim a reasonable fit. The rescue is *loosening of fit*: the theory never committed to precise dates, only to the shape of the trajectory, and the shape is said to be on track — falsification is escaped by retreating from point predictions to pattern predictions.
