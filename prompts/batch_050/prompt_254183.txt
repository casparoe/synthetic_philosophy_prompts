Okay so I'm a novelist, mid-draft on book three, and I keep having this argument with my writing group that I suspect is secretly a philosophy argument in a trench coat. The fight is about whether a character's decision is "earned." My group's rule of thumb is basically: an action is earned if you can trace it back through stuff we've already established about the character -- their desires, habits, fears, history, even their blind spots. If the character does something purely because the plot needs it, and nothing in their established psychology gets them there, the scene "rings false" and readers feel the author's hand. Anyway, I finally read (okay, skimmed, then re-read) the Bernard Williams internal reasons stuff, where a reason for someone to act has to connect to their subjective motivational set via sound deliberation, versus external-reasons people who think there can be genuine reasons that apply to a person even if no amount of correct deliberation from their actual motives would reach them. And I went: this is literally my workshop argument with fancier words. Or... is it?

So here's what I want: restate the internal vs. external reasons debate as precisely as you can in the vocabulary of fiction craft -- character motivation, setup and payoff, plausibility vs. believability, authorial imposition, the reader's felt sense of "but would she really do that." Treat the character as the agent and the workshop reader as the person judging reasons-talk about them. Then, and this is the part I actually care about, give me the residue report. What translates cleanly (my guess: Williams' point that just being told "you have reason to X" is empty if nothing in you can reach X maps onto "the plot demanded it so she did it" being a craft failure)? What only translates with distortion (sound deliberation isn't the same as consistent characterization, right? characters can be irrational in ways that are still earned)? And what has no expression at all in craft vocabulary -- my suspicion is the normative part, the "what she should do" as opposed to "what she'd believably do," and that a novel can be perfectly earned while the character acts terribly.

Finally, take a stand on the residue: is the fact that craft talk can't quite capture the external-reasons position evidence that novelists are missing a real distinction, or evidence that philosophers are splitting a hair that dissolves once you notice fiction just merges justification and explanation in a way moral philosophy insists on keeping apart? I'm honestly not sure which answer would be better news for my draft. Also apologies if I've butchered Williams, I read it at 1am between chapters.
