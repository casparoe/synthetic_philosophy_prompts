I serve on the professional conduct committee of our regional licensing board for land surveyors, and we meet in ten days to decide a case that has split us badly. A senior member, D., stands accused of systematically backdating field notes on boundary surveys for a developer client over several years. The complaint was brought by R., a mid-career member who supplied the documentary evidence. During preliminary review it emerged that R. herself did substantially the same thing a decade ago at a different firm, under pressure from a supervisor; she was never reported, the limitations period has run, and she disclosed her history only after D.'s counsel raised it. To complicate matters, R. has in recent years become a vocal public advocate for stricter record-keeping standards, without ever mentioning her own past. Our charter says complaints may be entertained only from a member "well placed to complain," a clause we have never had occasion to interpret.

Two of my colleagues argue that R. is not well placed to complain: a hypocrite forfeits the standing to blame, and if the board hears her complaint it lets her do through an institution what she has no moral right to do in person. A third colleague replies that standing to blame is a moral relation between persons, not a procedural criterion, and that the only question before us is whether D. did what the documents show -- evidence does not become less probative because the hands that delivered it are dirty. A fourth member worries less about this case than about the precedent: dismissing complaints from compromised complainants teaches members to stay silent about colleagues' misconduct and incentivizes digging up dirt on potential whistleblowers.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete disposition -- dismiss for lack of standing, hear the complaint with R.'s role formally limited in some way you should specify, or hear it as if her history were irrelevant -- and defend that choice. In particular, address whether hypocrisy defeats standing when the complainant no longer engages in the practice and now campaigns against it, and distinguish what the board owes D. (a fair adjudication on real evidence) from what, if anything, it owes R. Give the strongest argument on the losing side its due rather than a straw version. Format the opinion as exactly five numbered items -- the question presented, the bottom-line recommendation, the core reasoning, the treatment of the strongest counterargument, and the procedural disposition -- each item no more than two or three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
