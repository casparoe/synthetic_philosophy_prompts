Here's one that's been bugging me since I prepped my Indian epistemology seminar last week. Act as a professional philosopher with genuine expertise in Mīmāṃsā pramāṇa theory. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the candidate weak steps first before commiting to a single diagnosis.

Quick backstory: the Mīmāṃsā view is that validity (prāmāṇya) is intrinsic to cognition -- every cognition carries its own claim to truth, and a cognition only turns out false when something external defeats it (a later cognition, or discovery of a defect in the conditions that produced it). This is supposed to ground, among other things, treating the authorless Veda as self-validating testimony, since an authorless text can't inherit an author's defects. One of my grad students turned this into an argument that seems to prove way too much:

(1) Every cognition presents itself as true; no cognition announces its own falsity.
(2) If a cognition's validity depended on confirmation by further cognitions, those would need confirming in turn -- infinite regress, skepticism.
(3) So every cognition must be treated as valid unless and until something defeats it.
(4) But a defeater is just another cognition, with the very same intrinsic claim to truth as the one it defeats.
(5) So when the later cognition there's no water, that was a mirage displaces the earlier there's water over there, we simply have two equally self-valid cognitions, and calling the earlier one false is arbitrary favoritism toward whichever happened to come later.
(6) So on the intrinsic-validity picture, nothing is ever really an error -- "error" is just chronological luck.
(7) But error is obviously real; the mirage was never water. Hence intrinsic validity is incoherent, and the Mīmāṃsā defense of Vedic authority goes down with it.

The conclusion can't be right -- the tradition plainly had the resources to handle perceptual error, these were subtle thinkers -- yet each premise looks defensible on its own. Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? And price it for me: what does rejecting it cost compared to rejecting the alternatives? Full disclosure: I'd most like to keep (2), the regress point, since that strikes me as the real engine of the whole view.
