I've been trying to work out how probability is supposed to function in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation, and I keep running into what looks to me like an incoherence at the heart of the view. On the Everettian picture, when a measurement is performed on a superposition — say a spin measurement on a state with unequal amplitudes for spin-up and spin-down — every outcome occurs: the universal wavefunction evolves unitarily, decoherence splits it into branches, and there are branches containing the up result and branches containing the down result. Crucially, Everettians insist that all of these branches are equally real; none is "merely possible" or "potential." But if that's the case, then the natural way to assign probabilities seems to be to count: if the branches are all equally real, and nothing makes one outcome more actual than another, a rational agent facing a measurement should regard the branches as equally likely, and the probability of an outcome should just be the proportion of branches in which it occurs. The Born rule, however, tells us to weight outcomes by the squared moduli of the amplitudes — which assigns some branches more weight than others despite their being, by hypothesis, equally real. Squaring the amplitude therefore looks either like an ad hoc device bolted on to recover the experimental statistics, or like a quiet admission that "realness" comes in degrees after all, which the interpretation officially denies. So here is my question: why can't Everettians simply define probability as the fraction of branches exhibiting a given outcome, and isn't the Born rule's departure from branch-counting better understood as a refutation of the many-worlds interpretation than as a technical problem to be solved within it?
