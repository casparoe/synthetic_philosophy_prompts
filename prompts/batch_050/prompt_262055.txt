I teach a second-year undergraduate course on philosophy of technology, roughly 24 students, and I have a single 80-minute session for the unit on epistemic dependence on AI. The reading for the week argues that the real cost of relying on AI assistants is not getting wrong answers but the slow erosion of the ability to generate, check, and explain answers oneself -- the difference between having the answer and understanding it. My students find this abstract and slightly pious; most of them use AI daily and feel fine about it. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the distinction in their own performance before I ever name it.

Design a runnable activity with the following shape. It should not involve actual AI tools in the room (phones away); the "assistant" should be simulated by game materials or a role you specify, such as an oracle deck, a designated student, or answer envelopes, so that I can control its behavior precisely. I want the simulated assistant to be deliberately imperfect in a realistic way: mostly right, occasionally confidently wrong, and I want the rules to make over-reliance tempting rather than forbidden -- participants should be free to lean on it, and some should lean on it heavily. The activity should have at least two phases with a turn: an early phase where the assistant is available and performance looks good, and a later phase where participants must work without it, or must catch it being wrong, or must explain and teach what they earlier merely submitted. The gap between the two phases is where the idea should live. Include the rules, the materials I need to prepare, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the scoring or stakes (small and non-monetary is fine) that make the temptation to offload feel real.

Then give me a debrief plan: the questions I ask, in order, that lead students to map what just happened onto the reading's claims -- performance versus learning, the difference between verifying an answer and being able to produce one, and why a system can make you more accurate while making you less competent. The debrief should connect specific moments from the activity to those concepts, not just assert the lesson. Also tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students gaming the scoring, the wrongness of the oracle being too obvious or too subtle, the debrief turning into a lecture), and how to handle each; and which feature of real AI dependence the simulation cannot reproduce and I must supply through discussion instead -- for example, the fact that real assistants adapt to you over months rather than rounds.

After the full description, close with a table summarizing the design with exactly these columns: Phase (name of the phase), What happens (one or two sentences), Concept it stages (the idea from the reading it makes vivid), Predictable failure mode (how this phase tends to break down in practice), and Debrief question (the question that surfaces the concept from that phase).
