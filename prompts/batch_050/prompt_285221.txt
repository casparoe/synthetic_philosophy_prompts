I'm a retired marine biologist, and since hanging up my wellies I've gotten into philosophy, mostly via a reading group and, my wife would add, via arguing with strangers on the internet. This week the two collided. In the reading group we're on Davidson and radical interpretation, and the 'principle of charity' came up: roughly, that to understand a speaker at all you have to take them to be mostly rational and mostly right about the world. Then, in an online spat about homeopathy, I said the other guy's position was flat-out incoherent, and a bystander told me I was violating the principle of charity and ought to 'steelman' him instead.

These strike me as different animals wearing the same name. So here's my request: lay out the distinct precise claims 'the principle of charity' could be making, as a menu. I can gesture at a few myself -- a rule of politeness, a norm of fair debate, a claim about what makes interpretation possible at all -- but for each reading I want the exact principle stated, then a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or actually false? Test each one against hard cases, say someone who sincerely asserts a contradiction, or a fishing community with systematically false beliefs about the tides. And the thing I actually need answered: which reading, if any, would justify how the slogan got used against me? Does any serious version of the principle require me to make my opponent's argument better than he made it himself, or is the internet version just the philosophy version's annoying cousin?
