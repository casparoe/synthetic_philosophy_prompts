**Draft essay: "What Death in Damascus Is Actually About"**

The Death in Damascus case is usually presented as a companion to Newcomb's problem: Death is a perfect predictor, the appointment is set wherever Death expects the merchant to be, and so fleeing to Aleppo is as hopeless as staying. The standard moral is that evidential and causal reasoning pull apart, just as they do in Newcomb.

I want to argue that this assimilation is mistaken, and that the mistake matters. In Newcomb's problem, the predictor fills the boxes before you choose, and your choice genuinely correlates with the boxes' contents without causally influencing them. In Death in Damascus, by contrast, there is no fixed prior "content of the boxes." Death does not first decree a city and then wait to see where the merchant goes; Death's appointment is *reactive* — it is set, in the story's logic, to wherever the merchant will be. The case is therefore not about prediction at all but about a guaranteed correspondence: the "predictor" does not anticipate your choice so much as anchor itself to it.

This reframing has two consequences. First, Death in Damascus cannot be used to motivate evidential decision theory in the way Newcomb is sometimes used, because there is no interesting act-state dependence to exploit — the state is defined as a function of the act. Second, the puzzle dissolves rather than deepens once we ask what would happen if the merchant could genuinely randomize: if the merchant flips a fair coin to choose his city, the story gives Death no well-defined appointment to make, which shows the scenario was never coherent under indeterminacy, whereas Newcomb's predictor can cope with randomization (it just predicts the outcome of the coin flip, and its accuracy becomes a contingent empirical matter).

I then consider a repaired version — a "fallible Death" who must fix the appointment in advance and predicts correctly with probability p — and argue that this version, unlike the original, really is structurally Newcomb-like: below some threshold of reliability the merchant should simply minimize travel costs, and above it he faces the familiar evidential tug. The threshold depends only on the stakes of being caught versus the costs of fleeing, and not on anything distinctively fatalistic. I conclude that Death in Damascus owes its grip on philosophers to an equivocation between foreknowledge and retrofitted decree, and that once the equivocation is removed, the case teaches us about storytelling rather than about decision theory.
