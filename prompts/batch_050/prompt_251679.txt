At work I got into an argument about whether AI systems like the one we ship "really" have beliefs and desires, and a colleague reached for a comparison I've since seen philosophers make too: she said beliefs are like phlogiston. Chemistry didn't discover what phlogiston really was or reduce it to something respectable; it just dropped the posit, and the theory that used it turned out to be rotten at the joints. Likewise, on her view, mature cognitive science won't find neural or computational states that beliefs turn out to be -- it'll retire the whole vocabulary, and our habit of explaining each other by what we believe and want will go the way of explaining combustion by phlogiston. I'm trying to audit this analogy rather than just accept the rhetorical punch. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly -- what plays the role of the folk theory, what plays the role of the mature science, what elimination would actually consist in -- and then tell me where it breaks? Two disanalogies I suspect but can't articulate: first, phlogiston theory made bad predictions, whereas belief-desire talk seems to predict everyday behavior pretty well; second, it's unclear to me whether ordinary belief-talk is even a "theory" in the way eighteenth-century chemistry was, and if it isn't, I don't know whether the whole comparison gets off the ground. Do those objections (or better ones) sink the analogy, or do they just weaken it -- and is there a better historical comparison for what an eliminativist thinks will happen to belief-talk?
