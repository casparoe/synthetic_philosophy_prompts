So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a feature on how AI systems (and people) should update their beliefs when the information they get comes packaged as a conditional. I want to structure the piece around a classic puzzle from the philosphy of probability -- van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem -- but with my own version of the setup. Here's my version:

A search-and-rescue drone is hunting for a lost hiker in a park divided into four zones of equal size: Northwoods-A, Northwoods-B, Southridge-A, Southridge-B. Going by the hiker's planned route, the drone's operators assign equal credence (1/4) to the hiker being in each zone. Then park dispatch radios in with a tip from a ranger who knows the terrain but can't talk long: "If she's anywhere in Southridge, she's almost certainly in Southridge-A -- say 75%." The message says nothing directly about Northwoods versus Southridge. Question: what should the drone's new credences over the four zones be?

Write an essay (this is for my own background research, so aim it at a smart reader who knows some probability but no philosophy) adjudicating between these three candidate update rules:

1. Treat the tip as ruling out Southridge-B -- as if dispatch had said "either she's not in Southridge, or she's in Southridge-A" -- and just conditionalize on that, renormalizing over the remaining possibilities.
2. Keep the overall Northwoods/Southridge split fixed at 50/50 (the tip said nothing about that comparison), and only redistribute credence within Southridge to get the 3:1 ratio.
3. Refuse to pick either and insist the right response depends on facts the puzzle leaves out -- like the probability dispatch would have sent that message given each possible location -- so no unique answer exists without filling those in.

Note that rule 1 has the apparently crazy consequence that the drone should get MORE confident the hiker is in Northwoods (from 1/2 to 2/3), purely from hearing a message that's entirely about Southridge. Rule 2 avoids that, but at the cost of treating "P(Southridge) stays fixed" as a free-floating constraint -- and it's not obvious why that marginal, rather than some other feature of the prior, deserves to be held hostage.

In the essay: work through what each rule actually delivers numerically, figure out the strongest defense available for the seemingly absurd rule 1 (or show there isn't one), consider whether the dispute is really about what conditional tips MEAN -- whether "if Southridge, then probably A" is itself a fact about the world the drone can learn, or something else entirely -- and say what your verdict implies for how we should build or audit systems that take conditional evidence as input (medical guidelines like "if the tumor is type X, the marker is usually positive" are the obvious real-world case).

Don't end on a shrug. Pick the rule you'd actually program into the drone, defend it against the best objection from the other side, and be honest about whatever your choice can't explain.
