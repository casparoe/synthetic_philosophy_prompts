So I'm a novelist, and I've written myself into a corner where I actually need to understand a philosophy thing properly instead of just name-dropping it at a dinner party scene. One of my main characters is a field linguist who ends up having to figure out a totally unknown language from scratch -- no shared words, no bilingual helper, just months of pointing at things and guessing. In researching how that could even work I stumbled into Davidson's stuff on radical interpretation and this "principle of charity," which as I understand it says you can only start understanding someone by assuming most of what they believe is true and hangs together sensibly. Fine. But here's my problem, and it's both a philosophy problem and a plot problem: my story needs the linguist to eventually conclude that this speaker is deeply, systematically wrong about something that matters -- not just mistaken about whether a bird is a sparrow, but wrong in a way that's almost a moral failing. And I can't figure out how Davidson's picture even allows that. If charity means every time someone seems crazy or horribly confused, the right move is "maybe I'm translating wrong, maybe by that word they mean something else," then it feels like massive disagreement becomes impossible by definition -- you just keep revising your translation until they come out reasonable.

Can you explain, in intuitive terms, how radical interpretation makes room for genuine large-scale error? Where does charity actually stop -- is there a principled line between "I need to reinterpret their words" and "no, I've got the words right, they really do believe this false thing"? And a related thing I keep circling: does Davidson think there's even a real difference between those two verdicts, or does he think meaning and belief are too tangled together for the question to make sense? Bonus points if you can say something about how the interpreter's own beliefs are doing work in the background, since my linguist character is realizing she can't check his claims against "the world" except through her own beliefs about the world, which feels circular to her.

Please keep it non-technical -- I bounced off the actual papers pretty hard. And if I've garbled the setup somewhere, tell me, because getting the philosophy wrong in print would be embarassing in a way my editor will not let me forget.
