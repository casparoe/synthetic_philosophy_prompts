So I’m in a PPE-ish ethics and public policy course and we’ve been doing compromise between value systems as a kind of gains-from-trade thing, and I want to road-test it on London road charging before I definately embarrass myself in seminar. Take the London congestion charge as the episode: introduced for central London in 2003, a weekday fee for driving into the zone, exemptions/discounts for some residents and vehicles, revenue largely tied to transport, then later politics shifted with the western extension coming and going and the much more contested ULEZ expansion to outer London in 2023 plus scrappage schemes. Don’t just narrate it; analyse it as a trade among value-bearers rather than individual voters: efficiency-minded commuters and firms, clean-air and asthma-focused locals, egalitarians worried about who can pay, liberty people worried about cameras and creep, outer-borough fairness people who say the centre externalised costs onto them, plus future children who never got a seat.

Use this homemade test from class and improve it if it’s rubbish: convertibility means a loss can be compensated without becoming an insult; guardrails are rights or statuses that must not be put into the bargain even if Pareto looks happy; ratchet means asking whether today’s settlement makes the next extension easier because the machinery exists or harder because trust was burned; identity leakage means the loser starts experiencing the payment as being bought off rather than respected. Map who trades what, where the apparent surplus is, what the compensations actually were, and where the model breaks: non-compensable harms, endowment effects, adaptive preferences, surveillance, voice versus exit, and the fact that a policy can be locally efficient but globally legitimating. Then run it backwards: does London confirm moral-compromise theory, expose it as technocratic, or show it needs an extra clause about standing and memory? Answer in British English please.
