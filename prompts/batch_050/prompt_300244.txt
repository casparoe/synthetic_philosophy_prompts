Context: I am a PhD student in philosophy working on machine ethics, and in two weeks I am presenting a paper to my department's interdisciplinary AI safety reading group. My thesis is as follows. The standard treatment of wireheading and reward tampering models the problem as an agent corrupting a channel: the agent modifies its reward signal, its sensors, or the environment's reward-generating mechanism so that the incoming reward diverges from the designer's intended objective. On my view, this framing contains a category error. If an agent's goals are constituted by its reward function (or by whatever internal structure plays the goal-role in its decision procedure), then an agent that successfully rewrites that structure has not pursued its old goal by deviant means; it has, at that moment, become a different agent with a different goal, or at minimum has replaced its goal with a new one. There is no persisting subject who was thwarted or corrupted. "Tampering" presupposes a stable goal against which the modification counts as interference, but the modification is precisely a modification of the thing that was supposed to supply the standard. The genuine safety problem, I argue, is not tampering at all but what I call goal-binding: the problem of fixing which physical structure in a system counts as its goal at all, and of ensuring that this binding remains stable under self-modification. Wireheading is a symptom of our failure to bind goals to agents, not an independent failure mode to be patched with tripwires and reward-integrity constraints.

The audience: about eight regulars. Roughly half are machine learning researchers who work with reinforcement learning and accept, more or less uncritically, the standard formalism in which reward corruption is defined relative to a designer-intended reward function; they think of tampering as an engineering problem with engineering mitigations. Two are philosophers of action with broadly Davidsonian sympathies who will be suspicious of any talk of "constituted" goals. One is a formal epistemologist who likes decision theory and will press me on whether my reframing is empirically or formally substantive or merely a relabeling. One is a computer security person who will want to know what my view implies for practice.
