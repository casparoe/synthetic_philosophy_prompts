A popular theology book I am reading explains Boethius's reconciliation of divine foreknowledge with free will through an analogy: God stands to time as a novelist stands to the timeline of her own novel. The novelist is not located before or after any event in the story; the whole plot is present to her at once; and her complete knowledge that on page 200 the protagonist betrays his friend does not force the betrayal, since she does not undergo it or cause it from within the story -- she simply has it in view. The book concludes that the foreknowledge dilemma dissolves: God's knowledge of what I will do constrains me no more than the author's knowledge constrains the character. A philosopher friend replies that the analogy quietly reverses the one thing Boethius insisted on, namely that God's knowledge of future acts, like our sight of present ones, does not bring them about -- because a novelist's relation to her plot is precisely one of authorship rather than observation, and because the novelist still has her own past, present, and future in which she writes, revises, and reads. Audit the analogy: lay out the mapping explicitly (author to God, plot to history, standing outside the book's timeline to eternity, and so on), say where each correspondence breaks down, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not, and assess whether anything like the book's dissolution of the dilemma survives once the breakdowns are on the table. If the novelist picture fails, propose a better analogy for Boethius's own account of eternity as the complete and simultaneous possession of unending life, and state honestly what residual difficulty even the better analogy leaves in place.
