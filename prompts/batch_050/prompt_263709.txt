Next month I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner for my aunt, who spent thirty-five years as the chief epidemic forecaster for a national public health agency. Her career has had the mirror-image structure of the Death in Damascus problem: in that story, fleeing the prediction is what fulfills it, but in hers, believing the prediction is what falsified it. The outbreaks that never happened because the public heeded her warnings are her greatest professional achievements — and her permanent reputational liability, since all anyone remembers is a death toll that failed to arrive. Roughly half the guests will be epidemiologists and statisticians who think about self-defeating forecasts for a living and will catch every sloppy inference; the other half are relatives and neighbors who privately suspect she cried wolf for three decades. Write a five-minute toast that takes the Damascus structure seriously as the spine of the speech rather than as decoration: it must make the family understand how a forecaster can be vindicated precisely by looking wrong, without telling the scientists anything false about what can and cannot be inferred from averted catastrophes — for instance, why her record of "failed" predictions is not by itself evidence her models were good, and yet why the alternative careers available to her were worse. It should end as affection, not as a lecture, and it should not pretend that every alarm she raised was the right call.
