I am trying to work out what makes pain bad, using pain asymbolia as the central puzzle. By pain asymbolia I mean a condition in which someone still detects and correctly describes a normally painful sensation—its location, intensity, burning or stabbing character—but does not find it unpleasant or feel motivated to avoid it. My initial thought was that the badness of pain consists entirely in the suffering or aversion normally attached to it. But my case judgments do not all fit that view. Maya, who has asymbolia, feels intense burning during a medical examination but says it is no more objectionable than noticing pressure on her arm; I am inclined to say the examination is not bad for her in the distinctive way it would be bad for someone who suffered through the same sensation. Leon has the same condition and therefore fails to favor either hand when one is being slowly crushed; I still think he has a strong reason to move the endangered hand, but that reason seems to come from bodily damage rather than from bad pain. Sofia welcomes intense pain during an athletic or erotic experience and judges the whole episode worthwhile, while conceding that the painful aspect remains unpleasant; unlike Maya, she is bothered by it, so I want to say something is bad for her even though the total experience is good. Theo has congenital pain insensitivity and calmly watches his foot burn; there is no bad pain, yet what happens is plainly bad for him. Finally, an infant with severe colic cannot reflect on or endorse anything, but I have no hesitation in calling her pain bad even if her aversion is merely behavioral.

I keep returning to two harder cases. First, if Maya later says that although she does not dislike the burning, she would slightly prefer that it stop because it distracts her from reading, does that trace of preference make the sensation at least a little bad, or must badness require a stronger aversive reaction? Second, suppose a contemplative practitioner reports feeling ordinary pain while remaining completely untroubled, but still chooses analgesia before surgery so that future pain will not erode her concentration. That looks structurally like Maya’s case, yet calling the anticipated pain entirely neutral seems wrong if the practitioner reasonably takes precautions against it. Please formulate the most plausible principle—or small family of principles—that captures these verdicts. Distinguish, if necessary, among the intrinsic badness of suffering, the instrumental value of pain as a warning system, the independent badness of bodily damage, and the badness of a whole experience versus one of its components. State which case motivates each part of the principle, what it implies about the two harder cases, and identify honestly any of my reported verdicts that cannot all be preserved without an ad hoc distinction.
