I am writing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in value theory, and I need one essay question, answerable in about 75 minutes, on the idea of moral compromise as a gain from trade. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the standard literature on moral dilemmas, dirty hands, or value pluralism; it should force students to work out the structure of a compromise for themselves.

Please build the question around the following scenario, which you may refine stylistically but should keep structurally intact. Two foundations must jointly fund a disaster-relief program that neither can fund alone. Foundation A is committed to maximizing welfare and regards allocating aid strictly by expected lives saved per dollar as obviously right, even though this means bypassing one remote village. Foundation B is committed to a principle of equal respect and regards deliberately bypassing that village as a serious wrong. They settle on a package: the cost-saving allocation is applied in half the districts and forbidden in the other half, which unlocks a program at a scale neither party could otherwise reach. The question should then ask students to determine whether, and under what conditions, this package is a genuine gain from trade — and, in doing so, to address: from which evaluative standpoint the alleged gain is to be assessed; whether a gain-from-trade reading requires the two value systems to be commensurable; whether a value system that contains constraints rather than only goals can register one of its own violations as compensated; and what distinguishes a genuine compromise from capitulation and from a merely strategic modus vivendi.

Then provide:

1. A model answer of roughly 600 words. It should make the following moves, though not necessarily in this order: (a) show that gains from trade do not require a common currency, by analogy with barter — each party need only prefer, by its own evaluative standard, the package it receives to its best available alternative; (b) insist that the evaluation of the package must be conducted from within each party's own system, so that specifying the relevant outside option (no program at all, versus a smaller unilateral program) is indispensable to the analysis; (c) identify the real threat to the trade reading: if B holds the bypass as a constraint on its own conduct rather than a disvalue to be minimized, then B's system may contain no rate of exchange at which its own complicity is compensated, in which case the package is a loss by B's lights and describing it as a trade silently converts constraints into goals; (d) note the agent-relative wrinkle that B can consistently prefer a world in which the bypass occurs without B's participation to one in which B authorizes it, so the terms of cooperation, not merely the distribution of outcomes, carry moral weight; (e) resolve the apparent circularity in which each party's reason to accept depends on the other's acceptance; and (f) draw the threefold distinction among compromise (endorsable ex ante by each party's own lights), capitulation, and modus vivendi (tolerated but not endorsed, hence unstable).

2. A marking scheme out of 20 points, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme should make explicit what separates an A answer from a B answer: for instance, that an A answer locates the crux in the internal structure of a value system (constraints versus goals, and the specification of the outside option), whereas a B answer merely asserts that values are or are not incommensurable and concludes that compromise is therefore possible or impossible, without ever saying from whose standpoint the gain is measured. Include also a short note on what should cap an answer at a C or below.
