I've been noticing a pattern that crops up in loads of places and I can't work out what it's really committed to, philosophically. Maths exams give method marks, so a student who sets the problem up correctly but slips on the last line scores better than a student who writes down the right answer with no working at all. Aviation regulators will go after a pilot who skipped the checklist even on a completly uneventful flight. Courts will quash a planning decision that was reached through the wrong procedure even when everyone agrees the decision itself was the right one. And yet the same societies happily run the opposite rule elsewhere: we judge surgeons partly by outcomes, we fund charities on measured impact, and nobody cares how elegant your reasoning was if your bridge falls down.

So what does that first cluster of practices presuppose about the relation between process and outcome? That a good outcome can be sheer luck and a good process is the only trustworthy sign of competence? That people can only be assessed on what was in their control? Or just that process is easier to check than outcome? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and be honest about which parts of the practice actually require it and which parts would be licensed just as well by rival views -- maybe method marks are really a teaching device rather than any epistemic stance, for instance.

And then the part I actually care about. I'm helping set up oversight for some AI assistants at work, and the two options on the table are checking their final answers (outcome supervision) versus reading through their written-out reasoning and rewarding reasoning that looks sound (process supervision). The catch is that nobody is confident the written-out reasoning is what actually produced the answer -- the system might reach its conclusion some other way entirely and then write up a plausible derivation after the fact, like the student who copies the answer from the back of the book and fabricates the working. Given whatever theory you think the human practices presuppose, does process supervision of an AI need an extra commitment the human versions don't need, something about the visible process being genuinely causally responsible for the output? And if it turned out that in these systems the written reasoning and the answer can come apart completely, what should an institution that cared about the values behind method marks actually be supervising instead?
