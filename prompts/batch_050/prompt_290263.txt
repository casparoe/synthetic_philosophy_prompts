I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I'm stuck on a follow-up piece. Several years ago I wrote a feature on the revived debate over whether cognition penetrates perception — the old New Look question of whether beliefs, desires, and expectations can alter what we literally see, as opposed to merely coloring what we think about what we see. I recently pitched a "where the debate stands now" update and found, to my surprise, that essentially nothing has moved. The same stock of studies is still doing all the work: children from poorer households reportedly overestimating the size of coins, motivated subjects resolving ambiguous figures in line with their wishes, stereotype-primed subjects misidentifying harmless objects as weapons. Proponents of penetration still cite such findings as evidence that perception is cognitively permeable; skeptics still reply, case by case, that every result can be redescribed as an effect on attention, judgment, memory, or response bias rather than on perception proper; and proponents still object that this reply is unfalsifiable, since any visual process can be relabeled after the fact. New experiments keep appearing, both sides absorb them, and no one changes position.

My editor's hypothesis is that the dispute is purely verbal — that it evaporates once the parties agree on an operational criterion for where perception ends and cognition begins — and she wants the piece to say so. I'm not convinced. It seems to me both sides could accept every experimental outcome on record and still disagree about the global architecture of the mind, in which case this is less an empirical controversy than something like the old fights over modularity, where the same data supported different overall pictures. Or perhaps there is a genuine empirical core — say, whether early visual processing is informationally encapsulated, in roughly Pylyshyn's sense — that our current methods simply cannot isolate, which would make the stall temporary rather than principled. As a diagnostic device, consider a thought experiment: suppose that in fifteen years we possess a mature, widely validated science of phenomenal decoding, able to reconstruct from early cortical activity exactly what a subject's visual field looks like to them, and everyone accepts its validity. If such decoding showed no belief- or desire-driven distortion, would the debate end? Or would proponents simply relocate the claimed penetration downstream while skeptics quietly tightened their criterion of what counts as perception?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, factual, methodological, or a dispute about what would even count as evidence? Diagnose rather than adjudicate. Engage with what each side actually says, especially the judgment-and-attention redescription move and the countercharge that it renders skepticism immune to refutation, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a specific experimental design, a formal criterion of encapsulation, a new conceptual distinction, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. And since I have to file something regardless: what is the honest headline — is this a stalled research program, a pseudoproblem kept alive by disciplinary inertia, or a healthy dispute waiting on instruments that don't yet exist?
