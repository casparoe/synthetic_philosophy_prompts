I teach a seminar on formal social choice for philosophy majors, about 14 students, and I've got one 90-minute slot on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I've lectured it before and it lands badly: they hear that social utility comes out as a weighted sum of individual utilities and immediately assume the additivity is some extra moral assumption about happiness being additive, and then we're off arguing about whether you can sum across people at all, which is exactly the confusion the theorem dissolves.

Design me an in-class game -- rules, materials, a payoff scheme with no real money -- where they feel the squeeze themselves instead of being told it. The setup I want: individuals have von Neumann-Morgenstern preferences over lotteries, the class adopts its own collective rule for ranking lotteries that hand out unequal bundles of something (tokens, chocolate, whatever), and they accept the Pareto indifference condition. Then the game should corner them: the rule's own verdicts, played out across rounds, force them to violate either independence, Pareto indifference, or the coherence of their own collective ordering, so they have to stand there and pick which axiom to give up. Most groups will grab something egalitarian-flavored, so make sure the game punishes the natural move of preferring a fair coin between two unequal outcomes over a guaranteed unequal outcome -- the Diamond-style objection -- and shows what that preference costs once you run it through compound lotteries. The weighted-sum form should feel like it closes in on them from the axioms they already signed up for, not like a definition I imposed.

Then a second, shorter phase that makes them feel the gap between the pure aggregation result and the impartial observer version (everyone choosing behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of being anyone): why the first doesn't get you utilitarianism by itself and what extra premise the second sneaks in.

Debrief: map specific moments from the game onto the theorem's premises, and only at the very end tell them whos theorem they just re-derived -- no lecture upfront, they shouldn't hear the name Harsanyi till afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: fairness norms swamp everything and the group just splits equally everytime, so their rule never actually gets stressed) and how to handle it. And be honest about what a game definately can't demonstrate -- e.g. it samples a handful of lotteries while the theorem's conclusion ranges over all of them -- and what I therefore have to just supply in lecture afterwards.
