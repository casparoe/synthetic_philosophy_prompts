Question (10 points). (a) State the independence axiom of expected utility theory. (b) Present the two choice pairs from the Allais paradox and the choices most people actually make. (c) Show that those choices violate independence. (d) Give one reason that has been offered for thinking the modal choices might be rational anyway. (e) Does prospect theory accommodate the Allais pattern? If so, by what mechanism?

Student's answer:
(a) Independence says that if you weakly prefer lottery L to lottery L', then for any third lottery M and any probability p between 0 and 1, you must weakly prefer the compound lottery pL + (1-p)M to pL' + (1-p)M. Two lotteries that coincide on some branch have to be ranked by the branches where they differ; what they have in common can't affect the ranking.

(b) Pair 1: A is $1 million for certain; B is a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. Most people choose A. Pair 2: C is a 10% chance of $1 million and a 90% chance of nothing; D is a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing. Most people choose D.

(c) The A-and-D pattern violates independence. Decompose each lottery over a common 89% chance. A and B both deliver $1M with probability .89; they differ only in the remaining .11, where A gives $1M outright while B gives $5M with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. Pair 2 is pair 1 with the common 89% component ($1M) replaced, in both options, by nothing. Independence says that altering a component common to both lotteries cannot reverse the ranking, so preferring A to B requires preferring C to D. The modal pattern is therefore incompatible with expected utility theory, whatever the utility function over money.

(d) One defense appeals to regret: passing up a sure $1M and then walking away with nothing would be uniquely painful, and if anticipated regret is a legitimate input to rational choice rather than a bias, the certainty of A can rationally outweigh B even though the same trade-off looks different in pair 2, where no certainty is on offer. On this view independence is not a requirement of rationality but a substantive constraint one can reject.

(e) Prospect theory accommodates the pattern. Because of loss aversion, people anchor on the certain $1M as a reference point, so the gamble B is coded partly as a possible loss relative to that reference point, and losses loom larger than gains. That makes A disproportionately attractive in pair 1 while leaving D attractive in pair 2.
