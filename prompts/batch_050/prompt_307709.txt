I've been reading about how different countries deal (or don't) with the worst chapters of their past, and I've got a tentative principle I want pressure-tested. My hunch: collective reckoning is genuine only when public memory works like an immune system, i.e. something that keeps the disease from coming back, and it's hollow when memory works like an alibi — rituals of remembrance that let a society feel done with the past so it can skip the harder stuff.

Trouble is, my verdicts on the actual cases don't line up cleanly with that. Germany: I'm fairly convinced it's the real deal — the Holocaust memorial in the middle of the capital, denial criminalized, the whole Vergangenheitsbewältigung thing — even though the first 15-20 postwar years were mostly silence and myth-making, and a lot of the later confrontation was pushed from outside (occupation, trials). So: imposed and late, but genuine. Japan: there have been multiple official apologies and compensation settlements, and yet with the Yasukuni shrine visits and the recurring textbook fights it feels like none of it ever stuck. Verdict: doesn't count, or barely. Turkey and the Armenian genocide: active state denial, plus pressure on other countries not to recognize it. Worst case, obviously fails, and I think active denial is worse than mere silence. The American South: taking down Confederate statues over the last decade definately feels like progress, but most of those statues went up generations after the war, during Jim Crow and the civil-rights backlash, so removing them seems like the cheapest available step — better than nothing, nowhere near reckoning.

Now the pair I can't reconcile. My Germany and Japan verdicts suggest symbolic memory work is what's doing the real work — apologies plus money don't suffice (Japan), while the culture of confrontation does (Germany). But consider: a country that committed an atrocity, never apologized, built no memorials, and just genuinely changed — treats the victims' descendants as full equals, no discrimination, nothing — versus a country drowning in memorials and commemoration ceremonies where that same group is still treated terribly. I honestly think the silent-but-changed country is the better one. But that makes all memory work merely evidential, a sign of underlying change that's worth nothing in itself, which demotes Germany's whole memory culture to decoration and undercuts my Japan verdict. And if memory work matters intrinsically, I want to know why: the dead can't benefit from it, so who is it for — descendants, the perpetrator society, third parties watching?

So: is there one principle that captures all my verdicts, or failing that, a small set of candidate principles? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and test it against my immune-system/alibi hunch — repair the hunch if it's salvageable, ditch it if not. Tell me what the best principle predicts for cases I haven't given you: Spain's deliberate pact of forgetting after Franco, Rwanda's gacaca courts, Australia's apology to the Stolen Generations. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts I'd have to drop. Max 550 words.
