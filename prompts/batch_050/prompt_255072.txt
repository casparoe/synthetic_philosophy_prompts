As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I have become suspicious of debates that remain lively even after every standard move has been repeated for decades. The paradox of blackmail seems to be one of these. It is generally permissible for me to reveal a true fact, and generally permissible for me to remain silent. Yet if I tell someone, “Pay me $10,000 or I will reveal your affair,” I have committed blackmail, even though either unconditional alternative would have been lawful and perhaps morally permissible.

The usual positions all seem to survive by changing the subject slightly. Coercion accounts say the threat narrows the victim’s options, but this runs into the fact that silence may be worth paying for: relative to disclosure, my proposal can make the victim better off. Transaction accounts say the conditional proposal is merely an offer of silence, but this appears to prove too much, since the person being offered silence is also being threatened with disclosure. Rights accounts say that I may possess information without having the right to sell silence about it, but this has trouble explaining why it is acceptable for a journalist to delay publication in exchange for an interview, or for a negotiator to withhold damaging information while bargaining. Institutional accounts distinguish legitimate social leverage from private extortion, but often seem to smuggle in the verdict they are meant to explain.

Here are four cases I would like used as pressure tests rather than replaced by a taxonomy:

1. A private investigator discovers a politician’s affair and demands money not to publish it.
2. An employee discovers that her company is violating safety regulations and says that unless the company fixes the problem, she will report it.
3. A blogger discovers an unrelated embarrassing fact about a billionaire and promises not to publish it if the billionaire donates a large sum to an effective charity.
4. A journalist agrees to delay publishing a damaging story for two weeks in exchange for exclusive access, knowing that the delay may permanently reduce the story’s impact.

My tentative diagnosis is that the debate has stalled because the participants do not agree on the baseline against which a proposal counts as worsening someone’s position: the legal status quo, the morally expected status quo, what would have happened absent the proposal, what the speaker is entitled to do unconditionally, or the institutions whose integrity the transaction might undermine. I also suspect that some people are debating the wrongness of making a threat, others the wrongness of accepting payment, and others the proper scope of criminal law, all while calling the object of disagreement “blackmail.”

Please give a committed diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Do not merely survey the accounts. Pick one principal explanation for the impasse, explain why it fits the positions and the four cases better than the alternatives, and identify any genuinely substantive residue that remains after that explanation is accepted. Finally, say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction between threats and offers, a defensible theory of moral baselines, a restriction to criminal rather than moral evaluation, a new kind of case, or something else. If you conclude that the debate cannot progress because “blackmail” bundles together several independent properties, take that position explicitly and defend it rather than presenting it as one option among many.
