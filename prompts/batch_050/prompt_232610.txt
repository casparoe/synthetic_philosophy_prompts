I sit on the defense review commission of a small, militarily non-aligned country — roughly six million people, one long border with a much larger neighbor. The commission is deciding the future of our national service model, and since my daughter is twelve, whatever we recommend will govern her cohort; I sought the seat largely for that reason. Our chair is a retired chief of defense whose judgment has become the commission's informal compass: when she endorses a model it passes, and when she dismisses one it dies. Her files are full of the Swiss militia, Finland's reserve army, and Sweden's reversal on conscription, and over the past year the secretariat has put seven hypothetical models to her. Her verdicts:

1. A fully professional force of 14,000 volunteers, well equipped, with no conscription and no reserve obligation: rejected.
2. Universal conscription — every eighteen-year-old serves eleven months, with refresher exercises afterward and a mobilization reserve of roughly a quarter-million: accepted, and called the benchmark.
3. Selective conscription — the whole cohort is tested, the top fifth serves ten months, the rest owe nothing, and trained reservists are never refreshed, their liability lapsing within a few years: rejected.
4. Universal service with a free choice between nine months of military and twelve months of civilian duty, even though on the experience of comparable countries only about a third would choose the military track: accepted.
5. A militia — four months of universal basic training, short refreshers until age forty-two, and a professional core of only 3,000: accepted.
6. Contracting experienced foreign soldiers to fill specialist posts: rejected, categorically.
7. A lottery — everyone registers, a randomly chosen quarter serves, and the rest pay a one-percent income surtax for ten years: rejected; she called the surtax an indulgence, a way of buying out of a duty.

The bill now before us is a hybrid the ministries call total resilience service: every eighteen-year-old serves ten months in a civil-protection corps — disaster relief, hospital support, emergency communications, infrastructure hardening — of which six weeks is basic military training; graduates then owe one-week civil-defense refreshers; the standing armed forces become a professional body of about 12,000, with a volunteer military reserve open to corps alumni who opt into further training.

I need to know how she will rule before I decide how to vote. Please: identify the rules that could account for all seven verdicts — I can discern at least two, one concerning the universality of personal service and one concerning the maintenance of a deep, refreshed military reserve, and perhaps a third concerning what credible armed neutrality actually requires; state what each rule predicts for the bill and where they diverge; give me your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons; name the precedent you take to be the closest analog; if you predict rejection, identify the single amendment most likely to change it; and tell me which further hypothetical model, put to her, would best discriminate among the rules you have reconstructed. Finally, say what would change your mind: which feature of her record your prediction leans on most heavily, and where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to fail.
