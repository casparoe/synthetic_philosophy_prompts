I'm a third-year philosophy major currently taking a seminar on moral responsibility (we've spent the last two weeks on Strawson's essay about freedom and resentment, the participant and objective stances, and the excusing versus exempting distinction). I'm writing my term paper on something closer to home: the way my university handles student misconduct. Cases are sorted into two tracks. There's a disciplinary track -- a hearing, findings of culpability, expectations that the student acknowledge wrongdoing, sometimes a required apology -- and a welfare track, where the same conduct (say, plagiarism or abusive emails) leads to counseling referrals, accommodations, and no assignment of blame at all. Borderline cases get argued over: a student who plagiarized during a manic episode, one who harassed a classmate while severely sleep-deprived and anxious. Nobody disputes that the sorting should happen; the fights are about where the line goes and what kind of fact decides it.

My question: what theory of moral responsibility does this two-track institution presuppose? Does running both tracks only make sense under something like Strawson's picture, where being responsible just is being a fitting target of resentment and moral demand rather than someone to be managed, or could a purely forward-looking, harm-prevention account justify the same sorting on different grounds? Relatedly, are the two tracks internally consistent -- the disciplinary track treats the student as a source of reasons while the welfare track treats them as a site of causes -- or does the university quietly presuppose different theories in different rooms? Finally, what would concretely have to change if the university adopted a hard incompatibilist view on which no one is ever basically deserving of blame?

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order: Reconstructing the practice (roughly 100 words, identifying what the two tracks actually do); The implicit theory (roughly 250 words, your best reconstruction of what the institution presupposes); Strictly presupposed versus merely compatible (roughly 200 words, separating what the practice requires from rival theories that would also license it); What would have to change (roughly 150 words, the practical consequences of the hard incompatibilist alternative).
