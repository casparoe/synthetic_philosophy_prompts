My roommate does environmental policy and she hit me with an argument about solar geoengineering that feels like it proves too much, but I can't find the crack. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) If we do stratospheric aerosol injection hard enough to mask a big chunk of warming and then stop suddenly, all the masked warming shows up within a decade or two, way faster than ecosystems and agriculture can adapt. So an abrupt stop is worse than never starting.

(2) Once it's running at that scale, nobody can guarantee it never stops suddenly. Governments fall, wars happen, budgets get slashed, treaties collapse. Stretch that over the century-plus you'd need to maintain it and an unmanaged termination is basically certain eventually.

(3) It's wrong to do something that makes a catastrophe worse than the thing you're trying to prevent near-certain, just to buy present-day relief.

(4) So deploying SAI at climate-relevant scale is wrong, full stop.

(5) And since research programs and small deployments create constituencies and path dependence toward full deployment, even research is on the hook.

The problem: nothing in this argument seems to use anything special about aerosols. Swap in "the global electricity grid," "antibiotics," or "the semiconductor supply chain" and the same logic says building those was wrong too, since we'd be wrecked if they stopped tomorrow and no one can guarantee their upkeep forever. That conclusion is absurd, so something has to give. Which step do I reject? I really want to keep (1), and I'm pretty attached to the thought that locking future people into risks they can't control is at least presumptively wrong, so if the fix is in (3) I want to know exactly what I'm paying for it.
