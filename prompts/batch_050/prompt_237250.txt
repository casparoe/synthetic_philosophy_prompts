I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on early modern European thought, and in two weeks I have to lead a forty-five-minute seminar discussion of Montaigne's essay on the cannibals (Essays, Book I) for about fifteen classmates. They will have read it exactly once, in translation, with no historical preparation, and most of them will arrive thinking it is simply an early plea for tolerance. I don't want a reading of the essay done for me; I want a plan for how to run the session so the group works through the interpretive problem instead of trading first impressions.

Please lay out the following.

First, the sequence of ideas. Tell me what has to be established before the essay's central move — Montaigne's reversal in which European cruelty during the wars of religion comes off worse than ritual cannibalism — can land as an argument rather than a provocation. I assume the group needs the context of the French wars of religion (including the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre), some sense that his information about the Tupinambá is secondhand and filtered through classical idealizations of primitive peoples, and the distinction between judging a custom strange and judging it cruel. But I'm unsure about the order: does the historical context come first, or does that front-load my own reading and rob the reversal of its force when the students discover it themselves?

Second, the misconception map. I predict three: (a) that Montaigne is a straightforward cultural relativist who thinks no cross-cultural judgment is possible; (b) that he is a naive romanticizer of the noble savage; (c) that the essay functions as ethnography rather than as a mirror held up to France. For each, tell me where in the session it should be confronted, what moment in the text to confront it with (paraphrase is fine — I don't need exact quotations), and what underlying mistake about the essay's method produces it.

Third, how to handle the framing devices: his insistence on a plain, unlearned informant, his account of speaking with Tupinambá visitors brought to Rouen, and the closing joke at the cannibals' expense. Does that ending undercut a relativist reading or confirm one? Should I let the group sit with the tension or steer toward a resolution?

Fourth, one closing question whose answer would show me whether they've grasped the essay's argumentative structure — not merely absorbed the vibe that intolerance is bad.

Throughout, please distinguish what is settled among people who work on Montaigne (the text's content, the historical setting, the essay's position within Book I) from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether Montaigne is a committed relativist, a skeptic deploying relativism as an instrument, or a moralist holding both cultures to a single natural standard. Don't present any one of these readings as the consensus, and if the session plan forces a choice among them at some point, flag that fork and advise me on how to keep it visible to the students rather than resolving it for them.
