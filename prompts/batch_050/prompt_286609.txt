I'm putting together a session for an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of punishment, and I've paired The Shawshank Redemption with readings on Norwegian corrections -- the normality principle embodied at Halden Prison, where sentences are capped, cells resemble modest dorm rooms, and staff are trained to treat daily interaction with prisoners as the actual mechanism of security -- and with material on American mass incarceration, which Shawshank's Maine state prison distantly anticipates. A colleague objected that the film is a sentimental hope-and-friendship story and can't bear the weight of a policy debate, and I suspect he's half right, so I'd like a careful reading that adjudicates this. Read the film through the punishment-versus-rehabilitation lens: Brooks Hatlen's release and suicide seem to state a thesis about institutionalization, so explain what that thesis is in terms criminologists would recognize (the pains of imprisonment, deprivation versus importation models of prison adjustment), and whether the film treats institutionalization as an effect of prison as such or only of long sentences. Then take Red's three parole hearings: the film strongly implies the board rewards a certain performance of contrition rather than the thing itself, and that Red's final, disillusioned candor is what gets him out -- what does that sequence suggest about whether institutions can even in principle measure rehabilitation, and would a Halden-style system with fixed short sentences dissolve the problem or just relocate it?

I'd also like the reading to resist easy victories. Andy builds the library, tutors inmates toward equivalency degrees, and launders the warden's money, but his flourishing happens in spite of the institution and culminates in escape, not release -- so does the film actually vindicate rehabilitation, or does it suggest that in a punitive environment the rehabilitated man is simply the one best positioned to flee, making Zihuatanejo an indictment rather than a model? Note too that Andy is innocent, which flatters the audience's sympathies; does that choice make the film useless as evidence about how we should treat the guilty, or does it still generalize? And where does the film push back against the whole reading -- Warden Norton and Hadley locate the rot in corrupt individuals rather than in the structure of incarceration, which a Norwegian criminologist would call a comforting evasion. Point me to the scenes that do the real philosophical work, say honestly where the film is muddled or merely moving, and close with what, if anything, the film contributes to the punishment-versus-rehabilitation debate that the journal literature doesn't.
