I'm leading a small undergraduate reading group in the history of ethics, and next week we turn to David Hume's posthumously published essay in which he argues that suicide is contrary to no duty — not to God, not to one's fellow citizens, and not to oneself. In class discussion, several students objected that Hume's argument, whatever its eighteenth-century merits, quietly presupposes that the person contemplating suicide is in a position to rationally weigh whether continued life serves their own interests — and that severe clinical depression, a concept unavailable to Hume, is precisely a condition that corrupts that very weighing process. Another student pushed back, claiming that Hume's reasoning is more resilient than this objection suggests, since his case rests partly on autonomy and partly on situations of irreversible bodily suffering where no recovery is on the table, and that building a blanket prohibition out of the possibility of distorted deliberation would infantilize everyone.

Please write a dialogue, roughly 800–1,100 words, between two equally competent philosophers debating this question: does Hume's case for the permissibility of suicide survive once we take seriously the phenomenon of depression as a distortion of rational deliberation? Have Philosopher A defend the reading that Hume's argument presupposes sound deliberation and therefore fails (or at least fails in its full generality) in light of depression; have Philosopher B defend the reading that the core of Hume's argument survives. Requirements: neither character should be a strawman, and each should respond to the other's actual points rather than talk past them; the dialogue should include at least two concrete moves on each side (for instance, B might press on what grounds one could paternalistically override a person's judgment without thereby licensing far broader coercion, while A might press on the asymmetry between an irreversible decision made under a potentially temporary impairment and other autonomous choices); each character should concede at least one genuine point; and the dialogue should end with the two identifying the single crux on which their disagreement ultimately turns, without announcing a winner. Write the dialogue in American English, in a natural but intellectually careful register.
