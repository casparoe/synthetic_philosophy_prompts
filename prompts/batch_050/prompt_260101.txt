I am to be the best man at a wedding next month, and I need a toast of about five minutes. The complication is the bride's family. They are observant Advaita Vedantins; her grandfather, who will be seated at the front table, taught Vedanta for thirty years and takes the doctrine with complete seriousness. At the rehearsal dinner he is certain to remind us all, as he does at every family occasion, that from the highest standpoint there is no bride and no groom, no marriage and no toasts — only Brahman, appearing to itself as a wedding. The groom's side of the room is secular Midwestern and will have no idea what any of that means; several of them will already be worried that the ceremony has asked their son to convert to something.

Write the toast so that the Advaitin teaching does real structural work rather than serving as an exotic garnish. In particular: I want to grant the grandfather his metaphysics — that the everyday world of separate selves is a misperception, the way a rope is misperceived as a snake, and that the innermost self of each of us is nothing other than Brahman — without letting the toast imply that the marriage itself is therefore unreal, a mistake, or a pleasant fiction we are humoring. I am told Advaita itself has the resources for this, in its distinction between the conventional and ultimate standpoints, and the toast should lean on that distinction rather than on any softening of the doctrine. It also must land for the groom's aunts, so the philosophy has to arrive as warmth, not as a seminar.

Structure the toast as exactly five movements, each a single short paragraph, in the order you judge best for a room that starts skeptical and must end moved. Give each movement a one-word title in bold. Provide the five movements only — no prefatory remarks, no explanation of choices, no closing note.
