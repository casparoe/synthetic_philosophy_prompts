I write literary fiction and I run a little workshop out of my flat twice a month -- eight regulars plus me, mostly people drafting novels or short stories, two hours on a Thursday evening. Lately we've been circling a problem that I think is secretly a philosophy problem: my characters keep wanting things, and I can't figure out what makes all those wantings the same KIND of thing. My protagonist loves her kid, loves bitter coffee, loves winning arguments, loves the smell of a train station at 6am, and I honestly can't tell if those four sentences share anything besides the verb. While procrastinating I fell into Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, where he claims that all of it collapses into one category: "Pains and pleasures may be called by one general word, interesting perceptions." He then offers his taxonomy of fourteen simple pleasures (sense, wealth, skill, amity, a good name, power, piety, benevolence, malevolence, memory, imagination, expectation, the ones dependent on association, and relief) as if that settles it.

Here's what I want: design me a game or structured activity for these nine people, two hours, that makes them FEEL the trouble with Bentham's confidence before anyone names it. My vague seed of an idea is that everyone arrives with four or five index cards, each describing a specific pleasure from a character they're writing (not abstract -- "the pleasure Marta gets from correcting strangers' grammar at parties," that level of detail), and then some mechanic forces the group to treat all these cards as instances of one thing: sorting them into Bentham's fourteen bins, or bartering cards against each other, or having to justify in one sentence what two wildly different cards have in common. But I'm not wedded to any of that -- improve on it. What I need is for the classification or commensuration to visibly break down somewhere (I suspect "the pleasure of finishing a jigsaw puzzle at 3am" and "the pleasure of forgiving someone" will not sit quietly in the same bin), and for participants to notice that breakdown themselves rather than have me lecture them about it.

So: full rules and materials, a rough timeline for the two hours, and what each of us facilitators-adjacent people should and shouldn't say during the activity (I want minimal front-loading). Then a debrief plan that maps the moments where the game strained onto the actual philosophical issue -- whether "pleasure" names one natural kind, a family resemblance, or just a motivational role -- and onto the specific escape hatches Bentham left himself, like that suspicious "pleasures dependent on association" catch-all. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with fiction writers specifically (we are a argumentative bunch and someone WILL try to game the bartering), and be honest about which feature of the heterogeneity problem a card game just can't reproduce and that I'd have to supply in conversation afterwards.
