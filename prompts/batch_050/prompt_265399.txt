Is there an established name for the following pattern of argument? I keep encountering it in discussions about where normative standards come from. Someone proposes that some authority or source settles what is right: God's commands, the law of the land, what evolution has fitted us to approve of, the verdicts of an idealised observer, or simply the settled preferences of the community. In each case the critic responds with what looks like the same fork: either the source endorses the thing because it is right, in which case the source is tracking an independent standard and does not itself make anything right, or the thing is right merely because the source endorses it, in which case the endorsement seems arbitrary and the source's approval would make anything right, however monstrous. I first met this fork in Plato's Euthyphro, where it is put to a specifically theological use, but I see people wielding it against naturalistic and social accounts of value with equal confidence, as though nothing essential changed when the deity dropped out of the picture.

My question is whether this generalised pattern — the fork abstracted from its theological setting — has a name of its own, or whether writers simply call it the Euthyphro dilemma by extension and leave it at that. If the latter, I would like to know whether the extension is considered legitimate: does the fork have the same force against, say, an evolutionary account of moral approval as it has against divine command theory, or does something shift when the 'source' is a causal process rather than an agent who could have commanded otherwise? I also notice that the fork is often run alongside two other moves — the complaint that one cannot infer an ought from an is, and the observation that any proposed definition of the good in natural terms can always be meaningfully questioned — and I am unsure whether these are three names for a single thought or three distinct thoughts that merely travel together. If they are distinct, where does my pattern end and the others begin? I should stress that I am not after a verdict on any particular theory of value; I want to know whether there is an established concept here that I can cite, and, if there is only the Euthyphro case plus analogy, whether anyone has examined where the analogy breaks.
