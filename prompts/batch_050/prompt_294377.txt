In the epistemology seminar I help run, we have spent two terms on conspiracy theories, and the discussion has frozen in a way I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. The literature we read splits into two camps. The generalists hold that conspiracy theories as a class warrant prima facie suspicion: they characteristically immunise themselves against disconfirmation, they multiply agents and secrets beyond plausibility, and their track record as a genre is poor, so a rational inquirer should assign them low credence before looking at the particulars. The particularists reply that this is guilt by association: real conspiracies happen constantly, the official record of any state contains proven conspiracies, and dismissing a hypothesis because of the category it has been placed in is an epistemic shortcut no one would accept elsewhere; each claim must be assessed on its evidence, full stop. What strikes me is that each side concedes the other's central point and then restates its own position unchanged. The generalists agree conspiracies occur; the particularists agree most conspiracy theories are rotten; and nothing moves.

My tentative hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about conspiracy theories at all, but about whether epistemic policy may legitimately rest on base rates for contested categories -- and that the word 'conspiracy theory' is doing the damage, because the two camps never fixed its boundaries: one counts the Tuskegee experiment and Watergate as data, the other brackets those as 'conspiracies that were exposed', so they are computing the track record of different sets. But I am not confident in this. An alternative reading is that the dispute is normative in a way neither side will own: the generalists are really arguing about what it is permissible to platform, and the particularists about what it is permissible to believe, and these have been conflated.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is there a hidden empirical question -- about the actual historical base rate -- that could, in principle, be settled? Is it a definitional dispute masquerading as an epistemological one? Or is it a clash of epistemic starting points that neither camp can argue the other out of? Please engage with what each side actually says, as I have sketched it, rather than offering a survey, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a stipulated definition, an agreed inventory of past cases, a formal treatment of evidence immunity -- or whether the honest answer is that the debate is ill-posed and should be dissolved into two better questions. And if my own hypothesis is part of the problem, say so. Please write your answer in British English.
