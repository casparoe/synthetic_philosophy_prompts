A friend of mine sits on an ethics advisory panel that reviews proposed autonomous weapons for a defence ministry, and over the years she's told me (suitably anonymised) how they've ruled on five systems:

- Approved: an automated gun on warships that spots incoming missiles and rockets and fires at them entirely by itself, on the grounds that no human could react in time.
- Rejected: a sentry gun for a border fence that would fire automatically at any person crossing a marked line, day or night.
- Approved: an attack drone where a human operator designates the target and gives the go-ahead, but the drone itself does the tracking and picks the exact moment to release the weapon.
- Rejected: a loitering munition that circles over contested ground for hours and attacks anything matching a tank's radar and visual profile, with a human supervisor who can in principal veto but mostly just watches.
- Approved: a replacement for naval mines — a torpedo that loiters in a publicly declared exclusion zone and homes in on submarine acoustic signatures, justified partly on the grounds that radio doesn't reach underwater, so nobody could be in the loop once it's deployed anyway.

Two new proposals have just landed on their desk:

(A) An upgrade to a land-based air-defence battery so it fires automatically not only at incoming missiles but at crewed enemy jets the instant they cross into defended airspace — again justified on reaction time.

(B) A "loyal wingman" drone flying alongside a crewed fighter, which returns fire automatically and instantly at whatever just shot at it, no authorisation needed.

I'm trying to figure out what rule the panel is actually running, because on the surface they've approved a machine that fires with no human decision at all while rejecting one that merely picks out vehicles. I can see at least three different rules that fit all five verdicts — one about whether a human could physically be in the loop at all, one about whether the target is defined by a clean enough signature to be near-impossible to misidentify, and one about whether the role is defensive rather than hunting — but I think they come apart on the new cases, and differently for (A) and (B).

So: give me the candidate rules that fit all five verdicts (mine or better ones), what each predicts for (A) and for (B), your best guess at how the panel will actually vote on each and why, and one further imaginary system whose ruling would best distinguish between the rules you couldn't decide between. Maximum 500 words.
