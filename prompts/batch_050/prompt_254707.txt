I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether safety work on frontier AI systems is economically self-defeating. My editors keep using the phrase "alignment tax" loosely, so I want to pin down the underlying claim precisely and get a serious probability estimate rather than another vibe. Here's the proposition I'd like your credence on: by the end of 2030, among the most capable general-purpose AI systems that are widely deployed commercially, the leading systems will be ones whose developers applied the best alignment techniques available to them at the time -- that is, the competitiveness penalty for thorough alignment work (in capability, cost, or speed to market) will be small enough that alignment effort does not systematically select against the most aligned developers. Take "small enough" to mean, roughly, that a frontier lab could adopt the strongest alignment measures its researchers endorse without losing its position near the capability frontier for more than a few months. If you think the proposition is ambiguous in a way that matters, say how you're disambiguating it before giving the number.

Please decompose your credence rather than just stating it. The sub-questions I'd expect to matter, though you should restructure them if you see a better factorization: the probability that current alignment techniques (RLHF-style training, constitutional or deliberative methods, interpretability-guided interventions) impose a large capability cost at the frontier rather than being capability-neutral or even capability-enhancing; the probability that the alignment-relevant failures we're worried about actually reduce commercial usefulness enough that market pressure selects for alignment on its own; the probability that deployment contexts where the tax would bite hardest (open-weight models, racing behavior between labs or nations) dominate the landscape; and the probability that "alignment" and "capability" turn out to be so entangled that the question dissolves. Give each term a rough number with one or two lines of justification, show how the terms combine into your final credence, and then state your overall number. I'd also like to know which term in the decomposition is carrying the most weight -- i.e., where a person at 0.2 and a person at 0.8 on this proposition most likely diverge.

Finally, an epistemic request I make of everyone I interview for this piece: be explicit about how confident you are in your own decomposition, not just in the bottom line. If some of your sub-probabilities are essentially guesses dressed as numbers, flag them as such, and single out the single point in your reasoning where you are least sure of yourself -- the place where, if you turned out to be wrong, the whole estimate would move most. I'd rather quote a shaky number with its shakiness located than a confident one with the shakiness hidden.
