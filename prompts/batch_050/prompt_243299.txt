I'm a PhD student in political theory and I've been going in circles on secession for months, so I tried writing down my gut verdicts on some hypothetical cases to figure out what I actually believe. Here they are:

1. A culturally distinct region votes 54% to 46% for independence in a clean referrendum; the central government refuses to negotiate. I think the region may secede anyway.
2. A region votes to secede largely because it sits on most of the country's oil, and independence would wreck the welfare state for everyone left behind. I don't think the majority has the right, even if the vote is fair.
3. A region whose language has been banned and whose leaders keep getting jailed votes to secede. Clearly permissible, maybe even something the rest of the country has a duty not to resist.
4. The wealthiest neighborhood of a city votes to become its own municipality so it stops funding services in poorer districts. Not permissible.
5. A region votes to secede, but a compact minority inside it (say a third of the population, concentrated in one district) votes 90% against. I feel like that district should get to stay with the old state, which seems to commit me to secession all the way down.

The pair I can't square is 1 and 2 (and honestly 1 and 4): if a fair majority in a defined territory is enough in case 1, why not in 2 or 4? And if harms to those left behind defeat the right in 2, why don't smaller harms at least weaken it in 1? Is there a principle, or a couple of principles working together, that captures most of this? Please state it precisely enough that I can tell what it says about cases I haven't listed -- say, a region seceding partly in order to criminalize abortion, or one whose departure would make the rump state non-viable as a country. And be upfront about which of my verdicts your principle just has to give up, rather than contorting itself to save all five.
