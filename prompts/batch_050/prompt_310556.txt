THE QUESTION:

In 1994, a team of economists builds a model of currency crises. In their paper, a central argument for the model is that it retrodicts the precise pattern of bank failures in the 1929 crash — data the whole team knew inside out before they started building the model. (a) Explain why, on the standard Bayesian picture of confirmation, the 1929 data apparently cannot confirm the model for anyone who already possessed that data. (b) Describe one strategy for rescuing confirmation by old evidence. One option you might consider is the "rewind" strategy: assess whether the data confirm the model relative to a background corpus from which the old evidence has been deleted. Give one serious objection to whichever strategy you describe. (c) Do you think the economists' appeal to the 1929 data carries genuine epistemic weight? Briefly defend your verdict.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

(a) On the Bayesian picture, E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H), i.e. learning E raises the agent's credence in H. But if the agent already knows E, then P(E) = 1, and a quick manipulation of the definition of conditional probability gives P(H|E) = P(H). So the old evidence cannot raise anyone's credence in the model, and confirmation is impossible. This is Glymour's problem of old evidence, and the classic historical case is the precession of Mercury's perihelion, which was known for decades before general relativity and yet seems to have strongly confirmed it. It's worth noting that the problem isn't an artefact of the ratio measure of confirmation — the difference measure, the log-likelihood measure and so on all give zero confirmation once P(E) = 1, so no choice of confirmation measure gets you out of it.

(b) One rescue strategy is the rewind strategy: rather than asking whether E confirms H given everything the agent now knows, we ask whether E would confirm H relative to a corpus with E struck out. If, relative to that diminished corpus, P(H|E) > P(H), then E genuinely supports H, and knowing this justifies the agent's current confidence. The obvious objection is that we have no idea what our credences would have been without the old evidence, and different ways of deleting it yield different answers.

However, I think the deeper point is that the rewind strategy ultimately asks us to evaluate H relative to no evidence at all — the so-called "ur-priors" an agent would have had before any observation. Since rational ur-priors are at best wildly underdetermined and at worst a fiction, the strategy relocates the problem rather than solving it.

A different and better response, due to Glymour, is that what actually confirms the theory is not the old evidence itself but the newly discovered fact that the theory entails (or assigns high probability to) the old evidence. Learning this logical fact is genuinely new evidence, so conditionalising on it can raise the theory's probability. This dissolves the problem: old evidence confirms whenever we discover the entailment after the fact.

(c) The economists' appeal to the 1929 data carries some weight, but not much. Because the model was deliberately built to accommodate that data, its success is ad hoc in a way that a successful novel prediction would not be, and accommodation of known data is generally worthless as evidence. Still, the retrodictive success shows the model is at least consistent with the data, which is not nothing. So I'd say it confirms the model only marginally.
