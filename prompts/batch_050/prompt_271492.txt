I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, so keep it accesible. I've been reading about Adam Smith's impartial spectator -- the idea that we check our own behavior by imagining how a fair, informed bystander would judge it -- and his related observation that our sympathy flows much more easily toward people close to us than toward distant strangers. I want you to analyze the Boeing 737 MAX episode through this lens: the two crashes in 2018-2019, the MCAS system that relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor, ~346 people dead, the whole fleet grounded. Take those facts as given.

Concretely: where was the sympathy of the engineers, managers, and execs actually pointing during development and certification, and does Smith's account predict exactly that failure mode? What would "running the decision past an impartial spectator" have looked like in practice inside Boeing -- is there a software analog, like a code review where the reviewer is instructed to imagine being a passenger? And then flip it around: what does this case do to Smith's theory? The victims were anonymous future passengers nobody at Boeing would ever meet or even picture. If the impartial spectator is built out of sympathy, and sympathy needs a face, is the theory equipped to handle harms to faceless strangers at industrial scale -- or does it quietly break down exactly where modern engineering ethics needs it most?
