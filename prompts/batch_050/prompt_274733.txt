I'm helping run a grad reading group on the epistemology of disagreement and we've got a bit bored of the usual set-ups. Every toy case seems rigged so that the conciliatory response comes out looking sensible: you and your equally clever friend mentally split the restaurant bill, get different totals, and obviously you should both lose confidence. I want a case that runs in exactly the opposite direction, one where it's the conciliationist, of all people, who's own view rationally requires her to stick to her guns — and where standing firm isn't stubbornness or a lapse, but what her theory itself recommends.

So: construct me a thought experiment to these specifications. The scenario has to be realistic (a workplace, a seminar room, a jury deliberation, something of that sort — no demons, telepaths or perfect predictors) and told in a single paragraph. Neither party may be irrational, exhausted, drunk or otherwise compromised, and you're not allowed to stipulate that one of them is secretly better informed or more reliable: the steadfast verdict has to fall out of conciliationism's own commitments applied to the case, not out of some smuggled-in asymmetry. As a hint you can take or leave, the obvious hunting ground is disagreement about conciliationism itself, but if you can find a genuinely different mechanism I'd love to see that instead.

After the scenario, walk me through what each side of the debate — conciliationism and steadfastness — says about it and why, and show that the conciliationist's verdict genuinely follows from applying her view rather than from quietly switching theories mid-case. Then tell me which single feature of the set-up is doing all the work, and what happens if you remove it (does the case just collapse back into a standard split-the-difference story?). Finally, sketch the conciliationist's best defence at this point: does holding the line in this one special case save her theory, or has she effectively conceded the steadfastist's main point?
