I sit on the ethics committee of a large housing cooperative whose members hold sharply divergent moral and religious commitments. One of our functions is to rule on what we call "moral swaps": private deals in which two members each agree to do, or refrain from, something the other values. I would like your help deciding our latest proposal in a way that coheres with our record.

Our past rulings:

1. Approved. A vegan member offered to donate to a colleague's preferred charity if the colleague forwent factory-farmed meat for a semester. Both confirmed satisfaction with the terms, and compliance was verifiable.

2. Approved. A member who regards alcohol service as sinful and a member who regards gambling as a moral blight each agreed to cancel an event the other found objectionable (a wine-tasting fundraiser and a poker night, respectively). Pure mutual forbearance, no money involved.

3. Approved. A member distressed by fireworks agreed to fund a silent drone display for the neighborhood if a fireworks-loving neighbor forwent his annual show.

4. Rejected. A teetotal member offered to purchase and serve the liquor for a party -- something she considers a serious wrong -- if the host banned gambling at the same event, which she considered a comparably serious wrong. We reasoned, in essence, that the deal merely substituted one wrong for another and gave her no strict improvement by her own lights.

5. Rejected. A member offered to withdraw her name from a petition against a greyhound track and to tell the council she no longer objected to it, in exchange for the track owner's promise to "give serious consideration" to shortening the season.

The new proposal: a member who regards slaughterhouse work as a grave wrong offers to work two unpaid weeks at a local slaughterhouse -- performing tasks she herself judges wrong -- in exchange for the owner's written, verifiable commitment to permanently retire six of his animals to the sanctuary she runs. She insists the exchange is overwhelmingly worthwhile by her own values: two weeks of her complicity against six permanent rescues.

Please give us, in no more than 500 words and beginning with your recommended verdict in the first sentence: (a) the ruling that best coheres with our precedents; (b) at least two general rules, each consistent with all five prior rulings, that diverge on this case, and what each predicts; (c) the prior case you take to be the closest analog and why; (d) a further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the rules you leave standing; and (e) an honest statement of which principle we would effectively be adopting whichever way we rule.
