So I help run this informal philosophy night at a pub near my place (upstairs room, every other Thursday, mostly regulars, plus two philosophy grad students from the university who show up pretty reliably and definately know their stuff). Next session I volunteered to present my hot take on a puzzle I stumbled across in a podcast and then read up on, the self-torturer thing from Warren Quinn. Quick version in my own words: a guy is hooked to a machine with a dial with like 1000 settings. At 0 he feels nothing. Each click up adds pain so tiny he honestly cannot feel the difference between one setting and the next. Each time, he's offered $10 to click the dial up one notch. Every single trade looks obviously worth it on its own, imperceptible extra pain, free money. But if he keeps saying yes he ends up in serious agony, and he would never have accepted "all this money plus all this pain" if offered it as one package upfront. My claim, which I know is the spicy part: I want to argue that every individual choice he makes can be perfectly rational, and it's the demand that your preferences have to chain together transitively across the whole sequence that's the actual problem. Basically transitivity is a bookkeeping convention we impose on people, not a genuine requirement of reason, and this puzzle is what exposes that. I got obsessed with this partly because of my own gym routine, where every single "just skip one session" decision seems fine on its own.
