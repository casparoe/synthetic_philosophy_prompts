A friend and I (both software engineers, not philosophers) have been arguing about how AI assistants should actually be built to make moral decisions. Her line is that an assistant should basically be a deontologist: never lie to the user, never use anyone purely as a means, hard rules with at most a catastrophe escape hatch. Mine is that this will obviously produce perverse outcomes at scale, and the only sane option is to train the thing to roughly maximize expected welfare, with the rules as heuristics at best. We keep re-fighting the same example: our company's assistant figures out that telling a small comforting lie to a grieving user would clearly make them better off, and she says the moment the model learns it may lie whenever it judges the stakes high enough, users can never again trust anything it says, so the rule has to be absolute. I say that's status quo bias with extra steps.

Write a dialogue between the two of us (call us Ana and Ben) that actually advances the argument rather than restating our openers. Some things I'd like to see hashed out: whether a rule-following AI can stay rule-following in situations its rules were never written for, or whether every rule eventually meets a case where following it is insane and then what; whether "only lie when the expected benefit is huge" is a stable policy for a system smarter than the people overseeing it, or whether it collapses into the thing lying constantly by its own lights; the hybrid move where constraints hold until the stakes get enormous (I think this is called threshold deontology?) and whether that inherits the worst of both views instead of the best; and whether there's any empirical test or deployment outcome that could settle this, or whether it's pure intuition-fighting forever. Both of us should be equally sharp — definately no strawmen — and have each of us respond to what the other actually just said, not to the position we wish they'd taken.

One more thing: don't end it in a polite draw where we agree to disagree. Let the dialogue actually go somewhere, and afterwards give me a short verdict on which of us you think has the better of the argument and why. Commit to an answer, even if it's a messy or qualified one — I'd rather get a definite take I can push back on than a tidy list of pros and cons that leaves the decision to me.
