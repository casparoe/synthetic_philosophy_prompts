So I write for a general-audience magazine, mostly science and ideas pieces, and the editors have roped me into doing one of those "live explainer" evenings we run at a small venue -- twenty-odd minutes, mostly Q&A-ish crowd, people who read the magazine but have zero philosophy background, plus a few who'll definately show up having half-remembered something from a podcast.

My topic: why do promises bind us? Not legally, morally. I want the session to get people from "obviously you should keep your promises" to genuinely understanding why this turns out to be a hard problem and why the main families of answers each break down in interesting ways.

Please plan the session for me rather than writing the talk. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas. What has to be in place before the question even feels like a question? Most people just never notice there's a puzzle here. Tell me how to open so that they notice it themselves, and then the order to introduce the main candidate explanations (I've got in mind things like: promises bind because keeping them benefits society, because promising creates expectations you then shouldn't disappoint, because promising is a kind of convention you're opting into, or that it's just grounded in something about respect or self-constitution -- but you tell me the right lineup and sequence, and why each must come before or after each other).

2. The one carrying example. I need a single concrete promise-scenario to run through the whole talk so each theory gets tested against the same case rather than drifting into abstraction. Tell me what properties the example must have (and what it must NOT have -- e.g. presumably no money changing hands, no courts in the picture, no huge stakes) so that the weak spots of each theory actually show up. I was vaguely thinking something about promising a dying neighbour you'll scatter her ashes, but tell me if there's a better workhorse case and why.

3. The misconception map. The objections and confusions this audience will bring, in the order they'll raise them, and at what point in the sequence each should be headed off. Things I'm guessing at: "it's just that it'd be illegal/awkward otherwise," "you keep promises because lying is wrong" (does that collapse promising into lying?), "it's all just social convention so it doesn't really bind," and someone asking about promises you're forced to make. Where do I defuse each, and which one should I actually let run for a bit because it leads somewhere?

4. The check. How do I know at the end whether they've understood the puzzle -- i.e. that they could explain why "because that's the rule" isn't an answer -- rather than just having enjoyed the talk?

5. What to refuse. What should I openly say I'm leaving out at this level (I'm assuming game-theoretic iterated-interaction stuff and evolutionary accounts of cooperation are nearby but dangerous -- confirm or correct me).

Keep it practical -- timings in rough minutes would help, and don't give me a reading list, I need this to work off the top of my head.
