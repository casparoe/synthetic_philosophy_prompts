I’m a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently been reading philosophy of maths, mostly after dinner and sometimes more enthusiastically than carefully. I started with the old question of whether 2 is literally an element of 4. On one set-theoretic construction it is, on another it isn’t, so structuralism seemed to offer a nice answer: forget the implementation and ask only about positions in the natural-number structure.

Then I tried to turn that idea into a general invariance test and now it seems to prove something absurd. Here is the argument, warts and all:

1. If mathematical objects are nothing over and above positions in a structure, then a genuinely mathematical fact about them must be invariant under every map that preserves the relevant structure.

2. Complex conjugation, which sends a + bi to a − bi, is a structure-preserving map of the complex numbers: it preserves addition, multiplication, and all the real numbers.

3. Conjugation swaps i and −i.

4. If a claim remains true under a map that swaps two objects, that claim cannot record a genuine difference between them.

5. But if i and −i really are distinct objects, some genuine mathematical fact must record that difference.

6. Therefore there is no genuinely mathematical fact that i ≠ −i.

7. So a structuralist cannot really maintain that x² + 1 = 0 has exactly two complex roots.

8. But that equation plainly does have exactly two complex roots.

9. Therefore structuralism cannot even preserve elementary algebra.

That conclusion has to be wrong, or at least I badly want it to be. But I can’t locate the misstep. Premise 4 is the one I keep staring at, though 1 may also be smuggling in more than the usual structuralist view. Please identify which premise or inference you would reject, explain why it initially looked plausible, and say what rejecting it costs.

I’d also like to understand how the diagnosis applies back to the 2 ∈ 4 case. Is the right structuralist response that there is no fact of the matter about which square root of −1 gets called i, while there is a perfectly good internal fact that there are two distinct roots? And is membership in 4 different because it belongs to a set-theoretic representation rather than to the natural-number structure itself? Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure about.
