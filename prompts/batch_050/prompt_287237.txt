I'm writing a column on the philosophy of time and I've got stuck on a passage from J.M.E. McTaggart's 1908 paper 'The Unreality of Time'. He's arguing that a mere earlier/later ordering (his B series) can't give us real change, because:

"An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is."

Here's my problem. That sentence seems to cut against what he's using it for. If events never cease to be events, that sounds like exactly what the eternalist says -- the Battle of Hastings is sitting there in 1066, tenselessly, same as ever. And yet McTaggart wheels it out against the B-theorists who basically built eternalism on his own framework.

So: act as a metaphysician who specialises in philosphy of time, and think carefully before answering. Give me your actual credence that eternalism (roughly: past, present and future events all equally exist) is the correct view of time. I want a number, and I want the decomposition: (a) the probability that McTaggart's point above is sound, i.e. that events really can't pop in and out of existence; (b) the probability that special/general relativity settles the ontology independently of the metaphysics; (c) the probability that our vivid sense of temporal passage is any evidence at all against a block universe; (d) the probability that the whole presentism/eternalism dispute is merely verbal, in which case I suppose the credence question collapses. Assign a rough number to each, say how they combine into your headline figure, and tell me which term the final answer is most senstive to and what argument would move it. Feel free to brainstorm the sub-questions first and add any I've missed.
