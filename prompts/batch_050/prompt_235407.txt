I'm a psychology student and at our department's journal club last week someone ended a long argument about predictive processing vs classic symbol-manipulation by just saying "look, whatever the details, the brain is a computer, so these debates are really about implementation details". Everyone nodded, I nodded, and then on the walk home I realised I have no idea what that sentence actually commits me to. The more I think about it the more it seems to blur at least five different claims:

1. The brain can be *modelled* computationally (which seems true of the weather, so maybe trivial?)
2. Cognition literally *is* computation, in some strong intrinsic sense
3. The brain is a *digital* computer, i.e. something in the vicinity of a Turing machine
4. Thinking is rule-governed manipulation of representations (the old "language of thought" picture)
5. Computation is observer-relative anyway, so calling the brain a computer says more about us than about the brain (someone mentioned Searle's thing about a wall "computing" any programme you like, though I've not read the original)

Could you untangle this properly? Give me the distinct precise theses "the brain is a computer" might be expressing, and for each one: is it a platitude, a substantive live hypothesis, or just false? In particular I want to know whether the triviality worry in (5) sinks the strong versions, and whether there's a reading that survives it -- maybe something about causal structure rather than mere description? Also: does the analogue/digital distinction matter here, or is that a red herring? And which of these readings does my colleague actually need for his move to work -- i.e. which one, if true, would show that debates like symbols-vs-predictive-processing are mere implementation squabbles? My suspicion is that the reading which makes the slogan plausible is weaker than the reading his argument needed, but I cant quite nail it down.
