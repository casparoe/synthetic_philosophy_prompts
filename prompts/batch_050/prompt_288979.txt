In my undergraduate course on paradoxes, students routinely treat the convergent geometric series as settling Zeno's paradoxes of motion: one-half plus one-quarter plus one-eighth sums to one, so the runner reaches the finish line and Achilles overtakes the tortoise. Please argue, against this classroom consensus, that the mathematics of infinite series resolves at most an arithmetical sub-question and leaves the genuinely paradoxical core of Zeno's argument untouched. In doing so, distinguish carefully between three distinct questions that the standard reply tends to conflate: whether an infinite sum of distances has a finite total, whether a physical body can traverse infinitely many spatial intervals in finite time, and whether an agent can coherently be said to complete an endless sequence of discrete tasks. Argue that even granting the full apparatus of limits and convergence, the runner's difficulty survives in at least one of these forms, and identify precisely where the calculus-based resolution goes silent. Conclude by stating which argument or consideration would most plausibly overturn your verdict, and where you take your own reasoning to be most vulnerable to objection. Please write in American English.
