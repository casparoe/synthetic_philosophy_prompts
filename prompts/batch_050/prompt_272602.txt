I run a monthly discussion group at my company (a mid-sized software firm) that has drifted from pure engineering topics into ethics and political philosophy. The group is about 14 people, mostly developers with no formal philosophy training, and we meet for 75 minutes after work. Next session I want to tackle the utility monster objection to total utilitarianism — the worry that if one being derives enormously more welfare per unit of resource than everyone else, maximizing aggregate welfare commits you to handing it everything, and that this reductio tells us something is wrong with aggregation as such. When I tried explaining this abstractly last month, people nodded along and said the monster was obviously unrealistic, case closed. I want them to actually feel the pull of the conclusion before they're allowed to dismiss the premise, so I'd like you to design a structured game or simulation for the session that makes the group experience the dilemma rather than hear about it.

Here's the twist I want built in, because I think it's what makes the objection interesting rather than a fantasy: in real life, the monster's superior conversion rate is not observable. So in the game, each participant should privately draw a "conversion rate" determining how much welfare they get per unit of the distributed resource, with one player drawing a dramatically higher rate — but these rates are hidden, and players report them themselves, possibly strategically. The group then has to decide, under some voting or deliberation procedure you design, how to allocate a fixed pool of resources across multiple rounds. I want the honest monster player to face the choice of whether to reveal truthfully, and the rest of the group to face the choice of whether to believe them — so that the session surfaces both the original problem (if you take the numbers seriously, equality loses) and the measurement problem (you can never get the numbers, and every aggregation rule you adopt is gamed by whoever benefits from inflating their reported intensity). Design the full activity: the materials, the conversion-rate distribution, the rules and payoff structure (no real money — maybe points convertible to something trivial like snacks at the end), a round-by-round timeline that fits the 75 minutes with at least 20 minutes reserved for debrief, and the debrief itself, which should map specific moments in the game onto the philosophical moves — siding with aggregation and biting the bullet, capping or discounting the monster's claims, or abandoning interpersonal comparison altogether — and should note what each move costs.

Two more requests. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with engineers specifically — I suspect some players will treat it purely as an optimization problem and try to "solve" the allocation mathematically rather than sit in the discomfort, and I want to know how to handle that and any other failure modes you anticipate. Second, be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the game cannot reproduce: for instance, my players will know their conversion rates were assigned by a die roll rather than being real features of their inner lives, and the stakes are snacks rather than anything that matters, so please flag where the simulation's grip on the real argument loosens and what I need to supply in the debrief by hand. Please don't lecture about utilitarianism in the design itself — the rules should do the work, and the vocabulary (utility monster, aggregation, interpersonal comparison) should only appear in the debrief materials.
