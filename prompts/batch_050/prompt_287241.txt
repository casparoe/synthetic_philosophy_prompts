I’m trying to get clearer on commitment races, and I’d like a dialogue rather than an essay. Invent two characters, Mara and Theo, discussing a made-up institution called a ratchet registry. Two firms that share a warehouse can use the registry to publicly and irreversibly give up outside options before negotiating over space: for example, one can cancel its fallback lease early, with the cancellation timestamped and visible to the other side. Burning an option makes refusing a bad offer more credible, but it also leaves the firm worse off if talks collapse. The registry allows both firms to ratchet in turn, imposes a cap on how much option value each can burn, and permits a burned option to be restored only if both firms consent.

Mara thinks the registry could prevent conflict: symmetric access, transparency and caps should replace bluffing with clear information and make mutually acceptable deals easier to find. Theo thinks it creates a commitment race: each side has reason to become inflexible first, the cap merely moves competition into unregistered commitments, and joint restoration creates another opportunity for extortion. Neither should be stupid. Have them argue through first-mover advantage, the difference between communicating a preference and physically tying one’s hands, private information about how costly a commitment really is, reputation and audience effects, whether perfect symmetry removes the race or just makes it fairer, and whether an option that can be jointly restored was ever genuinely irreversible. Each character should respond directly to what the other has just said, concede at least one point, and revise their position once without completely capitulating. It would be good if they definately end on a precise remaining disagreement rather than a vague agreement to disagree.

Write about 900 to 1,100 words in British English. Keep it readable and natural, not like a transcript of a formal debate, and do not mention real theorists or provide citations. No headings, bullet points, bold text or tables; continuous prose only, the way one would write an email or a letter, with the speakers identified as unobtrusively as possible.
