So I'm writing up my "philosophical profile" for our phil-sci reading group (we're doing confirmation theory this semester) and I want to get stress-tested before everyone else does it for me. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a plain subjective Bayesian. Rational credence = probability, learning = conditionalization. I don't think we need any extra machinery beyond that to explain what confirmation is.

(2) The old-evidence verdicts are non-negotiable for me. The precession of Mercury's perihelion was known for decades before GR, and it still massively confirmed GR in 1915. Any account of confirmation that can't deliver that verdict is just refuted, I don't care how elegant it is.

(3) That said, I think the standard fix works: when you assess whether E confirms H, you use the credences you *would* have had if you'd never learned E -- you bracket it out of your corpus and conditionalize on the rest. Problem solved, Bayesianism intact.

(4) I also think confirmation can't depend on the contingent history of inquiry. Whether some evidence supports a theory for you shouldn't turn on the order you happened to learn things in -- that's autobiography, not epistemology. Two scientists with the same total evidence should be in the same epistemic position.

(5) I think discovering that a theory *entails* some fact is itself a kind of learning -- that's what was going on when Einstein did the Mercury calculation, and it's how mathematical insight works generally. So I want my framework to be able to say that logical discovery can move your credences.

(6) Practically, when I compare hypotheses I use the likelihood ratio, P(E|H) over P(E|not-H), as my measure of how much E supports H.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Please don't just flag every pair -- rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what that costs. And if there's one single revision that cleans up the most mess, tell me which it is.
