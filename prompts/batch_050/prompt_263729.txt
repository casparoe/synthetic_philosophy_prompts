Exam question: The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg (1945–46) and the International Military Tribunal for the Far East at Tokyo (1946–48) have both been criticized as exercises in retroactive justice — punishing defendants for acts that were not crimes under international law when committed. Explain this objection in its strongest form, assess the strongest reply available to the tribunals' defenders, and state whether, in your view, the objection ultimately undermines the tribunals' legitimacy. Support your answer by reference to the tribunals' charters and the legal instruments invoked at the time.

Student answer:

The retroactivity objection rests on the principle nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege: no one may be punished for an act that was not legally prohibited at the time it was performed. The London Charter of August 1945 defined the offenses the Nuremberg tribunal would apply — crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity — and the Tokyo Charter followed its model, so critics argue that at least the first and third of these were created after the fact and applied retroactively to acts committed before the Charter existed.

The strongest reply, I think, is that aggressive war was already criminal under international law. The Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, to which Germany and Japan were parties, made the waging of aggressive war a crime for which individual state leaders could be held personally responsible; the Charter therefore merely codified existing law and supplied a forum for its enforcement. The defendants, having signed the Pact, cannot claim surprise that its breach carried penal consequences.

As for crimes against humanity, this category allowed the tribunals to reach the Nazi regime's persecution of German Jews from 1933 onward, conduct that traditional war crimes law would not have covered because it occurred within Germany's own borders against its own nationals. Even if this category was newer, it reflected principles of humanity so fundamental that punishing their violation cannot count as unjust.

My conclusion is that the retroactivity objection fails. Where law exists in substance, the absence of a prior tribunal cannot confer impunity. Justice Pal's dissent at Tokyo is often cited against this view, but his was a minority position, and the majority's reasoning that the defendants had fair notice has largely been vindicated by subsequent international law, including the Genocide Convention and the Rome Statute.
