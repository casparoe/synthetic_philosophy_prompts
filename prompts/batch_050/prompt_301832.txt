I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of historiography, and my dissertation chapter defends the legitimacy of contingency claims in history — claims of the form "had this small, avoidable event gone otherwise, this large outcome would have been different." A colleague in my reading group, who is sympathetic to cliometrics, has reconstructed an argument against me that I have been unable to answer cleanly. It seems to prove too much, since it would dissolve not just romantic "for want of a nail" narratives but, as far as I can tell, every causal judgment historians actually make. Here it is, as he states it:

(1) To say that an outcome was contingent on some event is to assert a counterfactual: had the event not occurred, the outcome would not have occurred (or would have been significantly different).
(2) A counterfactual about the past cannot be tested directly; history is run exactly once, and there are no controls.
(3) So the only evidence available for a historical counterfactual is comparative: we look at other cases where conditions were similar and see whether the outcome followed. Call this the reference-class method.
(4) Take any candidate comparison. Either the comparison case matches the original in all causally relevant respects, or it does not. If it does match, then — assuming even a modest regularity thesis, that same causes in same conditions give same effects — the outcome in the original was determined, not contingent, and the counterfactual is simply false. If it does not match, then the reference class does not govern the original case, and the counterfactual is unsupported.
(5) Therefore every contingency claim is either false or unsupported: false when we have genuine comparisons, unsupported when we do not.
(6) So contingency talk in history is a literary device — a way of expressing narrative surprise — and not a kind of claim that can be true or warranted.

He concludes that my dissertation chapter is dead on arrival unless I can break the dilemma at (4). I suspect the slippage is somewhere in the phrase "all causally relevant respects," but I cannot make the diagnosis precise, and I worry that whatever I say about contingency will also have to explain why it does not overgenerate — why not every outcome then counts as contingent on everything.

Please tell me: which step or sub-step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable? What does rejecting it cost — in particular, does my preferred diagnosis commit me to a view about determinism, about the granularity of event descriptions, or about the epistemology of counterfactuals that I might not want to hold? And finally: on your preferred diagnosis, which contingency claims survive — can the strong "Cleopatra's nose" cases be saved, or does the correct repair only vindicate modest sensitivity claims about outcomes and specific inputs, leaving the dramatic versions as the literary devices my colleague says they are?
