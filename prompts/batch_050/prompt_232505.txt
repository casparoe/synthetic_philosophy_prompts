My aunt is a retired bookkeeper, and for the last few years she has run a little game at family gatherings: she writes choices between sums of money on index cards -- some amounts guaranteed, some left to a coin flip or a draw from a hat -- and we tell her which we'd take. She answers her own cards instantly and, as far as I can tell, completely consistently. My cousin and I now have a standing bet about predicting her answers, so I've been keeping a record. Here's what she has done so far. Offered $70 for certain against a coin flip paying $150 or nothing, she took the $70. Offered $1,000 for certain against a lottery paying $1,000 with 89% probability, $5,000 with 10% probability, and nothing with 1% probability, she took the guaranteed $1,000; she said the one-in-a-hundred chance of walking away empty-handed spoiled the whole thing for her. But offered $150 for certain against a coin flip paying $300 or $90, she took the flip, remarking that even the bad outcome was close to the sure thing, so she could hardly lose.

The new card she hasn't seen yet is this: a ticket paying $1,000 with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, against a ticket paying $5,000 with 10% probability and nothing otherwise. Which one will she take? My cousin insists the rule is simply that she refuses any gamble where she could end up with nothing, and otherwise goes by the rough average; my brother says she's just conservative about large sums and the amounts matter more than the structure. I'd like your prediction, but more than that I'd like to understand the space of possibilities. What are the plausible rules or decision principles that fit all three of her past choices -- including, if there is one, a rule that has her attending to how the probabilities and outcomes are ranked rather than just averaging them -- and what does each predict about the new card? If you think one rule fits her record best, tell me why; and if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, tell me what further choice I could put on a card to tell the remaining candidate rules apart, and what each outcome of that test would show. I'm also curious whether anything about her earlier answers constrains the new one more tightly than it first appears -- whether, on some way of making her consistent, her answer to the new card is already forced.
