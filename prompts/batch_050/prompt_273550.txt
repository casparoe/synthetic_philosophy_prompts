ok so "moral trade" is the idea that when two people have genuinely different moral views (not just different tastes), they can sometimes both do better by their own lights by striking deals: e.g. I stop eating meat for a year (a cost by my lights) if you donate a chunk of money to the charity I consider best (a win by my lights), and symmetrically for you. The gains come from the same place gains from ordinary trade come from: differences between us.

here's roughly where I've landed on the ethics of this, and I want to know if it hangs together before I put it in a discussion paper:

1. Moral trades are in general good and should be encouraged. The mutual-gain logic doesn't care whether the underlying values are "preferences" or deep moral convictions.

2. Vote trading between ordinary citizens is wrong (something like: votes aren't private property to be bartered, and electoral integrity is a public good), but logrolling between legislators is perfectly fine, maybe even a virtue of functioning legislatures.

3. Within moral trades, cash payments are icky and corrupting, but in-kind concessions (I'll do X you value if you do Y I value) are fine. The money is what makes it feel like buying an indulgence.

4. It's fine to pay a company to stop doing something it had no right to do in the first place (e.g. a side payment so a polluter installs scrubbers early, or paying someone not to fly). Sometimes that's just the efficient way to get the good outcome.

5. But also: I don't think I'm obligated to respect values I believe are actually mistaken. If my would-be trading partner's moral view is just wrong (say they're morally indifferent to animal suffering), I shouldn't treat their preference for cruelty as a legitimate demand I have to accommodate.

6. Some commitments are sacred and can't be traded at any price. I could never accept a deal that required me to, say, stop advocating against torture. There has to be a floor.

A couple of things to note before you answer: in (1) I mean trades where each party genuinely believes the other's values are off but cooperates anyway, not cases where both secretly agree. And in (5) I'm not saying I'd lie to or coerce the person, just that I don't owe their mistaken view any weight.

So: which of these commitments are actually in tension, and which only look like it? For each real tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be (i.e. which revision loses me the least). And then: if I only get to revise one of the six, which single change cleans up the most? I'd rather you rank the tensions by severity than just flag everything and leave me to sort it out.
