I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a long feature on anthropic reasoning — the Doomsday argument, the simulation hypothesis, Sleeping Beauty, the whole strange family. Rather than write the usual "some philosophers say X, others say Y" piece, I want to stake out my own position in the article and defend it. Before I do that in print, I'd like to stress-test whether my commitments actually cohere, because I assembled them over several years from different corners of the literature and I've never checked them against each other all at once. Here is where I've landed:

First, I think the Doomsday argument is essentially sound: the fact that I find myself among the earliest few percent of all humans who will ever exist is genuine evidence that humanity's total run won't be astronomically long, and the usual dismissals strike me as motivated. Second, I reject the self-indication assumption and favor something in the family of the self-sampling assumption, largely because of the Presumptuous Philosopher: a cosmologist shouldn't be able to all but refute a finite-universe model from her armchair simply by noting that she exists, so any principle that delivers that verdict is disqualified. Third, I'm a halfer about Sleeping Beauty — on waking, her credence in heads should be one-half, and the thirder arguments have never moved me. Fourth, I take the simulation argument seriously: given something like substrate independence and the plausible claim that advanced civilizations would run enormous numbers of ancestor simulations, I assign substantial probability to being a simulated observer myself. Fifth, I think the fine-tuning of the physical constants is best explained by a multiverse combined with observer selection — we shouldn't be surprised to find ourselves in one of the rare habitable universes, since observers can only arise there. Sixth, I hold that learning the universe contains vastly more observers in my exact epistemic situation than I had assumed — say, discovering compelling evidence of a googolplex of near-duplicates of me scattered across the cosmos — would legitimately shift some of my credences about cosmology.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the debate. Go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs (or triples) are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, with the reasoning shown rather than asserted. For each real tension, rate its severity and identify the cheapest fix — the smallest revision that restores consistency — and be candid about what that revision costs me in the article. If any of my commitments look mutually inconsistent only at first glance but can be reconciled with care, say so explicitly rather than flagging everything. Finally, if you had to pick the single revision that does the most to clean up the whole set, which would it be? I'd rather cut one commitment than publish a position that quietly contradicts itself three paragraphs apart.
