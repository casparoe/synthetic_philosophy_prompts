Whenever my parents want something from me -- more visits, moving back closer to home, showing up to family stuff -- it comes out as some version of "after everything we did for you," like I'm carrying around an unpaid loan with compounding interest. In an ethics class years ago I ran into the opposite picture, a friendship model of filial duty: grown kids owe parents roughly what good friends owe each other, and the obligations come from the relationship as it actually stands now, not from a ledger of past sacrifices. Can you audit both analogies properly? Give me the mapping -- what's the loan principal, what's the interest, what's default, and on the other side what plays the role of friendship given that you can't exactly unfriend your mother -- then where each one breaks, what each quietly smuggles in (debts can be paid off and closed and don't require liking anyone; friends can drift apart and owe nothing to people who were merely generous to them once), and whether the conclusions people actually draw -- "you owe us this, period" versus "I only owe what's still alive between us" -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table. Also tell me what arguement or consideration would flip your verdict, or where your reasoning here is most likely to fall apart.
