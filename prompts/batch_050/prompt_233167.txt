Ok so I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and readers sometimes send me stuff. Last month a retired actuary sent me an argument that's been picking at my brain ever since. It's about what you're allowed to flat-out believe, as opposed to just sort of suspect. He says it shows something is deeply broken with the whole idea of rational belief, and I can't find the hole.

His argument, cleaned up only slightly:

1. Take a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one guaranteed winner. For any ticket you pick, it's rational to flat-out believe that ticket will lose. The chance it loses is 99.9999%, which is better odds than almost anything else I believe, including that my flight Thursday will land or that my car is still in the parking garage where I left it.
2. If it's rational to believe each of a bunch of things, it's rational to believe all of them together. Rational people get to pool thier beliefs. That's the whole point of having beliefs — you combine them and figure out what follows.
3. So it's rational to believe: ticket 1 loses, and ticket 2 loses, and ticket 3 loses... all the way down. In other words, it's rational to believe that every single ticket loses.
4. But it's also rational to believe that some ticket wins. It's a guaranteed-winner lottery; that's the setup.
5. Nobody can rationally believe every ticket loses while also believing one of them wins. That's believing a straight-up contradiction, and contradictions are the one thing rational belief can't contain.
6. So one of these premises has to go.

Then comes the part that actually keeps me up, his little closing argument:

7. Say we ditch premise 1 — you may not flat-out believe the ticket loses, you may only be very very confident. Fine, except now the rule is "no flat-out believing anything less than 99.9999% certain," and basically everything anyone believes about the actual world is less certain than that. My belief that my editor liked my draft is way shakier than a lottery ticket losing. So killing premise 1 seems to prove rational people can't flat-out believe anything empirical, which is nuts.
8. Say we ditch premise 2 instead. Then rational belief doesn't add up: you can rationally believe a million things yet be irrational for believing them all at once. Which means pooling your beliefs to draw conclusions is illegitimate, and that seems to gut reasoning itself — what else is reasoning but putting things you believe together?

So either ordinary fallible belief is irrational across the board, or combining beliefs is irrational across the board. Both conclusions seem like they prove way too much. I really want to keep both: that I obviously get to believe ordinary stuff (car in the garage, friend showing up for dinner) AND that I get to reason from my beliefs taken together.

Here's what I want from you: tell me which premise actually has to go, explain why it looks so plausible that even a careful actuary wouldn't blink at it, and then be honest about the price of dropping it. Also, the escape routes I've heard half-described at parties — "you only ever have degrees of belief, flat-out belief is a myth," "lottery cases are special because the evidence is purely statistical," "it's fine for rational beliefs to be a little inconsistent, like the author who believes every claim in her book but also believes she made a mistake somewhere" — do any of these actually slip between the horns, or does each one just pay one of the two costs in a fancier currency?
