I'm preparing teaching notes for a philosophy of language seminar on generic statements, and I've run into a puzzle I can't sort out on my own. Generics are those bare-plural (or indefinite-article) sentences like "ducks lay eggs," "mosquitoes carry malaria," or "a lion has a mane" -- they don't contain an explicit quantifier like "all" or "most," yet they seem to make general claims about a kind. What baffles me is that they behave as if they were quantified, but no familiar quantifier fits: "ducks lay eggs" is true even though only female ducks lay eggs, so it can't mean "all" or even "most"; "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes do, so it can't mean "most" either; and yet "books are paperbacks" strikes me as false or at least odd, even though a majority of books sold today are paperbacks. The truth conditions seem to slide around depending on the case.

Could you explain, at an intuitive but not dumbed-down level, what makes generic statements true and why the obvious analyses keep failing? In particular, I'd like you to (a) walk through why universal, existential, and simple majority/statistical readings each fail, with clear counterexamples; (b) explain the main theoretical moves people make in response -- for instance, appeals to what's "normal" or "characteristic" for a kind versus appeals to whether the property is dangerous, striking, or otherwise worth warning about, and the difference between a generic being about the kind's nature versus about which members of the kind are "good ways" of instantiating the property; and (c) explain why generics tolerate exceptions in a way that explicitly quantified claims do not (why is "ducks lay eggs" unscathed by male ducks, while "all ducks lay eggs" is refuted by them?).

To make this concrete, please test whatever account you give against some cases I've invented, and diagnose what it predicts and whether the prediction seems right: (i) a fictional island bird, the "skarn," of which only males of breeding age have a red crest -- is "skarns have red crests" true? (ii) a bacterium of which only 3% produce a deadly toxin, and another of which 60% produce a harmless mild smell -- do we get "skarnibacter is toxigenic" but not "skarnibacter smells faintly of lemons"? (iii) a society where, through sheer historical accident, 90% of the inhabitants happen to be left-handed even though handedness there is distributed just as elsewhere by biology -- is "the Verulians are left-handed" true in a way that "ducks lay eggs" is not? I'd like to see where your explanation bends or breaks, and what that reveals about whether generics report biological/psychological facts about kinds or facts about our conversational purposes in warning, stereotyping, and generalizing.
