A close friend and I recently spent an entire evening arguing about evolutionary explanations of human behavior, and we got stuck on a pattern I can describe but not name. I am hoping you can tell me whether it is a recognized concept.

The pattern is this. My friend defends the research program that explains present-day human traits as solutions to problems our ancestors faced. Fair enough. But over the course of the argument I noticed that no matter which way the evidence came out, the program produced a satisfying explanation. Three exchanges from the evening: First, I brought up a finding that men take more physical risks than women, and my friend explained it as ancestral competition for mates. I then brought up a finding that in some domains women take just as many risks, and without missing a beat she explained that as an ancestral strategy for protecting offspring. Second, high jealousy in one population was explained as guarding against investing in another man's child; low jealousy in another population was explained as an adaptation to communal child-rearing. Third, a reported human preference for open green landscapes was explained as a legacy of life on the savanna; when I noted that people usually prefer whatever landscape they grew up in, that too had an explanation ready, something about learning which local environments are safe.

Here is what strikes me. Each individual explanation was testable in principle -- the evidence could have come out against any one of them. So this is not quite the complaint that a theory is unfalsifiable. The complaint is that the framework as a whole seems to win either way: finding X confirms it, and finding the opposite of X also confirms it, because a new story can always be told with the same machinery. My friend replied that a true and flexible framework would also explain a wide range of outcomes, so the pattern proves nothing. I suspect she is half right, which is why the evening ended in a stalemate.

So, concretely: does this configuration have an established name, one that is more specific than unfalsifiability? How does it differ from an ordinary ad hoc rescue, where a single theory is patched after a failed prediction -- here no single prediction ever fails, because the commitments are made after the data are in? And is it the same thing as what makes a research program degenerate rather than progressive, or is it a distinct failure mode that can afflict a program that is progressive in other respects? If there is no established term, please say so plainly and propose one, with a definition and at least one clear case that falls on each side of the boundary -- an instance of the phenomenon and a near-miss that looks similar but is methodologically fine.

A bare label will not settle the argument, so please be precise about where the phenomenon ends: which of my three exchanges, if any, actually exemplify it, and which were fair moves on my friend's part. Finally, I would like an honest accounting of your confidence. Indicate how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the one claim you are least sure of.
