In the Netherlands it's normal to tell a colleague their presentation was bad, straight to their face; in Japan the same information arrives via hesitations and a "that might be a little difficult," and each side thinks the other is being rude -- the Dutch hear evasion, the Japanese hear cruelty. Meanwhile both cultures agree that lying on an expense report is wrong but that saying "lovely to see you" when it isn't is fine, sometimes even required. So what do these practices presuppose about what politeness actually is? Is politeness about protecting the other person's feelings, about treating them as a competent adult who can handle the truth, or just about following whatever convention happens to be shared? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not just "cultures differ": does Dutch directness presuppose something like a Kantian view where honesty is what respect consists in, and Japanese indirectness a view where respect means protecting the other's face -- and if so, does each culture have to regard the other as getting respect *wrong*, or could both practices fall out of the same underlying theory applied to different background assumptions about how fragile people are? Also, can any of these theories explain the thing both cultures agree on, namely why a white lie at a dinner party is fine but a white lie on a form is fraud?
