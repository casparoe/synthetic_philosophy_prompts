I’m a novelist, third book, lightly panicking, and my draft hinges on a bloke travelling back and very nearly killing his grandfather. My editor says the paradox is a solved non-issue, a physics-adjacent mate says it proves backwards travel is incoherent, and I reckon both are blagging. Write an essay that actually takes a side: does the grandfather paradox show time travel to one’s own past is metaphysically impossible, or only that any coherent version must accept brutal constraints, branches, or a lot of suspicious bananas underfoot?

Don’t name-drop or hunt for citations; argue from the concepts. Use this homemade taxonomy as the spine, but feel free to kick it: locked ledger — one timeline, so the killer’s failure is already in the record; drifting ledger — one timeline that can be edited, so grandad dies and the traveller is retroactively scrubbed; copy-shop — the act splits or selects another history, so the victim is a grandfather, just not the traveller’s own in the relevant sense. For each, separate the logical contradiction from the merely weird bit. The thing I most need untangled is whether consistency is an explanation or a cheat: if every attempt fails by coincidence, determinism seems to become a stagehand with a grudge; if it succeeds, the cause of the journey vanishes after causing it; if it branches, have we saved coherence by changing the subject from killing my grandfather to adopting a lookalike universe?

Press the follow-ups properly: nomological impossibility versus narrative improbability; whether chance can be globally forced whilst locally looking free; whether responsibility changes if the stabbed man is causally upstream but not ancestrally mine; and what the paradox does to character agency, because a protagonist who cannot fail is about as dramatic as a tax form. End with a verdict, not a shrug: which option should my novel treat as true, what cost does it pay, and what scene-level rule follows from it? It can be a hedged verdict, but make the hedge do work, not hide.
