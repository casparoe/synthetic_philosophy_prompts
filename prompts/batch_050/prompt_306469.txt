I teach philosophy of history and historiography, and I keep running into a pattern that doesn’t seem quite covered by the usual labels.

A historian starts from some present-day position, institution, or theory and then sorts the past according to how close earlier figures seem to have come to it. People who sound like precursors get treated as central, while people who were equally influential at the time but led nowhere become side characters. Earlier views are also described using the later framework, so small verbal or structural similarities get magnified and large differences of purpose get flattened. The result is that a contingent route starts looking like a developing trajectory.

This can happen even when the historian hates the present endpoint. A history of liberalism, say, might treat earlier thinkers mainly as steps toward contemporary rights talk, whether to celebrate or criticize them. Same with histories of science that make unsuccessful research programs look like mere obstacles to what we now accept. So the problem isn’t necessarily inevitability, progress, or taking the winners’ side. It’s more like the endpoint controls relevance before the historical analysis begins. In my notes I’ve been calling it endpoint drag, though I occassionally suspect I’m just rediscovering something familiar.

Is there an established name for this narrower phenomenon? How does it differ from Whig history, presentism, teleological explanation, hindsight bias, and ordinary genealogical history? If none of those fits exactly, what would you call it, and what cases would fall on either side of the boundary? I’m more interested in carving up the phenomenon carefully than in getting a bare label.
