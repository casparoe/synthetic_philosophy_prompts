I am giving a six-minute retirement toast for Professor Mara Venn, who is stepping down after thirty-seven years of teaching humour theory in our philosophy department. Half the audience has published on superiority, incongruity, relief, or benign-violation accounts of laughter; the other half consists of relatives and administrative colleagues who know those phrases mainly from having sat beside Mara at dinner. The toast must be philosophically exact enough to satisfy the first group without leaving the second behind.

Mara’s own pet notion is the procedural pratfall: laughter produced not when a person physically falls, but when an institution’s dignified procedures trip over the very purposes they were created to serve. Her standard example is the curriculum committee that spent forty-five minutes deciding whether a comma in the attendance policy was merely stylistic or created an enforceable category of absence. At the forty-sixth minute, during an especially tense budget meeting, she suggested that the question be referred to itself. The room’s laughter seemed to combine a violated expectation, the release of accumulated tension, a momentary feeling of superiority over the committee, and an institutional transgression that everyone present could recognize as harmless. Mara also kept an espresso machine that hissed, sighed, and displayed the word ready only after everyone had ceased to expect anything from it. She maintained that we laughed at the machine because it behaved like an exhausted junior colleague waiting to be recognized by the chair.

Use the following passage from Henri Bergson’s Laughter: An Essay on the Meaning of the Comic as the structural hinge of the toast: “The first point to which attention should be called is that the comic does not exist outside the pale of what is strictly HUMAN. A landscape may be beautiful, charming and sublime, or insignificant and ugly; it will never be laughable. You may laugh at an animal, but only because you have detected in it some human attitude or expression. You may laugh at a hat, but what you are making fun of, in this case, is not the piece of felt or straw, but the shape that men have given it,—the human caprice whose mould it has assumed.”

Write the toast itself, in 800–900 words excluding the quoted passage. Do not preface it with an explanation of your choices. The speech should interpret Bergson’s human-only restriction rather than merely repeat it, using the procedural pratfall and the espresso machine to test whether laughter at institutions and machines is really laughter at displaced human qualities. Let superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation emerge through the handling of the same material rather than as a classroom taxonomy: the committee story should illuminate all four, but without sounding like four labelled paragraphs. Include one serious objection to Bergson—for example, the fact that people can laugh at a dog’s clumsy pratfall without consciously attributing a human attitude to it—and say whether it overturns his thesis or only shows how easily human projection operates. Resolve the apparent conflict between an affectionate roast and superiority theory: Mara may be teased about professional habits she could recognize and laugh at herself, but not about grief, illness, family, appearance, or anything that would make the toast a disguised humiliation. End by raising a glass on a definite philosophical thesis about what the room has been laughing at all evening, rather than with the empty conclusion that every theory is partly right.
