I am teaching a seminar on Adam Smith's political economy of empire, built around Book IV, Chapter vii of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. Early in that chapter, describing the colonial practice of the Greek city-states, Smith writes:

"The mother city, though she considered the colony as a child, at all times entitled to great favour and assistance, and owing in return much gratitude and respect, yet considered it as an emancipated child over whom she pretended to claim no direct authority or jurisdiction."

My students and I have been reconstructing the pattern of verdicts Smith delivers across the cases he considers in that chapter. Our reading, in paraphrase, is as follows. He treats the Greek arrangement as unobjectionable: the colony is sent out, governs itself entirely, and remains bound to the mother city only by affection and gratitude. He treats the Roman arrangement as inferior on constitutional grounds: Roman colonists retained citizenship and privileges but remained subject to the mother city's authority, an arrangement he describes as less liberal. He condemns the Spanish and Portuguese colonial regimes for their extractive fiscal and commercial treatment of the colonies. He condemns the British monopoly of colonial trade as unjust and impolitic, while insisting that the North American colonies prospered not because of but in spite of that monopoly, owing to cheap land and liberal institutions. He condemns the territorial administration of chartered merchant companies, above all in Bengal, as a perversion in which the interest of the governed is subordinated to the interest of the governors. And he argues that, since the American colonies contribute no revenue to the mother country sufficient to cover the expense of defending them, Britain should either admit them to parliamentary representation in proportion to their contribution or else voluntarily give up its dominion over them.

Here is the case I want us to settle before class. Imagine a present-day wealthy state that retains sovereignty over a small, prosperous overseas territory. Trade between them is completely free in both directions, with no exclusive companies, no navigation restrictions, and no preferential duties of any kind. The territory elects its own assembly, which controls all domestic legislation and taxation. The sovereign state retains responsibility for the territory's defense and foreign relations and pays for both; the territory contributes nothing to the sovereign treasury and is, on net, a modest fiscal cost to it. The territory's inhabitants hold no seats in the sovereign state's legislature, and in two successive referendums they have declined an offer of representation coupled with proportional taxation, preferring the status quo.

The question: if we want our verdict on this case to be continuous with Smith's own track record, what should it be? I do not want a summary of what Smith generally thought. I want you to (a) state at least three distinct general rules, each of which is consistent with all of the verdicts listed above — for example, a rule on which the verdict tracks whether commerce is free of monopoly, a rule on which it tracks the constitutional status of the dependency (emancipated, incorporated with representation, or held under unreprepresented dominion), and a rule on which it tracks the welfare and liberty of the colonists themselves; (b) say what each rule predicts about the new case, noting where they diverge; (c) give your best prediction of the verdict Smith's record supports, identifying which earlier verdict is the closest analog and how much weight the Greek passage quoted above should carry — whether the emancipated-child model is his normative default or merely historical scene-setting for an argument that is really about trade; and (d) specify one further hypothetical case on which your preferred rule and its strongest rival would deliver opposite verdicts, so that we could tell them apart.
