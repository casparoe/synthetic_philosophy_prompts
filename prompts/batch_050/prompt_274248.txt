I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m building a magazine sidebar for a feature on insect farming, mosquito control, and whether “bug welfare” is a serious cause or a reductio ad absurdum. I want a decision-tree / sorting quiz that lets readers figure out what they actually think about the scale argument: take something like 10^18 farmed or wild insects, assign some tiny probability that the relevant ones have valenced experience, multiply, and suddenly the arithmetic screams louder than most human causes. Please don’t make it a label-matching quiz (“are you a utilitarian?”). Make 8–10 diagnostic questions that are concrete little cases: crushing a beetle on autopilot vs. breeding trillions of larvae for protein, using a gene drive that prevents malaria but may cause mass mosquito death, a pesticide that works faster but is suspected to hurt more, an ant colony that “wants” nothing in the human sense, a robot insect that passes every behavioral test but has an off switch, and a case where the expected-value calculation is enormous but the error bars are basically the whole ocean.

Each branch should be phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing where it leads, and each leaf should be a named-ish position (or “unnamed but coherent” if that’s more honest), with one sentence on the awkward thing it forces you to swallow. Please include leaves for: flat expected-value inclusion of tiny-probability insect sentience; a threshold or “below the line, don’t aggregate” view; a hedonistic-sentience-only view versus one that also counts preferences/avoidance; a normative-uncertainty compromise where the bugs get a vote but not a veto; a contract/relationship view where distant larvae barely register; a species/ecosystem view that cares about mosquitoes mostly as malaria vectors or food-web parts; and a deflationary “the math is doing all the work, so distrust the intuition pump” position. Flag any leaves your tree can’t really separate, and note at least two empty boxes in the logical space where a combination of answers seems possible but nobody would defend it — say whether that’s because it’s incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored. Tone should be friendly and a little queasy, definately not lecture-y.
