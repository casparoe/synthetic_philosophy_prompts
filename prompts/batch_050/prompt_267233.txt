I am grading midterm essays for an undergraduate course in the philosophy of art and would like help assessing one student's answer. The exam question was as follows:

"Instrumental music — a string quartet, say, with no words, title, or program — is standardly described as sad, serene, or triumphant, even though sounds, unlike people, do not literally have mental states. Set out what you take to be the most promising account of how purely instrumental music can be sad, contrast it with at least one rival account, and explain what your preferred account implies about the so-called paradox of sad music: why listeners deliberately seek out and enjoy music that is sad."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The formalist Eduard Hanslick argued that music cannot express definite emotions at all. Emotions, he claimed, require intentional objects and judgments — you cannot be sad without being sad about something — and sequences of tones supply no such objects. At most, music exhibits dynamic qualities, rushing and swelling and subsiding, which we carelessly label with emotional names. So for Hanslick, calling a quartet sad is loose talk that a rigorous aesthetics should discard.

I find this too austere, and I think the contour theory does better. On this view, defended by Peter Kivy, music is expressive in virtue of a resemblance between its auditory contours and the ways human emotion manifests in behavior: slow, low, drooping phrases resemble the speech intonation and gait of a sad person. Kivy's own example is the basset hound's face, which looks sad without the dog necessarily being sad; we perceive the sadness in the appearance. In the same way, when we listen, the resemblance between the music's contour and expressive human behavior triggers a sympathetic echo of the emotion in us, and it is in this sense that the music is sad.

A useful rival is the persona theory of Stephen Davies. Davies holds that in hearing sad music we imaginatively construct a persona, an indefinite imagined agent whose inner life the music unfolds, and we perceive the sadness as belonging to this persona rather than to the notes. This avoids attributing mental states to sounds, but at the cost of positing a fictional character that many listeners report never noticing.

The persona theory seems to me strained, and the contour theory has the further virtue of dissolving the paradox of sad music. Since, on that account, sad music neither contains sadness nor produces it, listening involves nothing negative, so there is simply no puzzle left about why we enjoy it: we enjoy beautiful, moving contours, and the sadness is only perceived, never felt. The paradox is therefore fully dissolved rather than solved."

Please grade this answer as I would, on a standard A–F scale, and give me the specific comments that justify the grade. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify what the answer gets right, flag any errors of attribution, conflation, or reasoning that a quick read might miss, and say how much weight each should carry in the final grade. Where the answer touches on matters that are genuinely contested among philosophers of music — for instance, whether sad music actually makes listeners sad, or whether the paradox of enjoyment is resolvable at all — please say so explicitly and do not treat a disputed position as the scholarly consensus; distinguish what is a matter of record from what remains an open question. Finally, please write your assessment as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
