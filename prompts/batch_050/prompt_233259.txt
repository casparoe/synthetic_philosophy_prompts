Next month I'm presenting at a two-day workshop on interactive epistemology and experimental game theory. The audience is about two-thirds experimental economists who have run centipede and related extensive-form games in the lab and think of the backward induction prediction mainly as something that fails empirically, and one-third epistemic game theorists who work with belief hierarchies, common knowledge of rationality, and extensive-form rationalizability. There are no pure philosophers of action in the room, and nobody who works on decision theory as such.

My talk defends the following thesis: the standard backward induction reasoning in the centipede game is not paradoxical in the way usually advertised. The familiar worry is that if the game ever reaches a late node, backward induction has already been refuted, so neither player has a rational basis for continuing to reason backward inductively -- and therefore the prediction unravels from within. I argue that this appearance of self-refutation rests on conflating two things: what players believe at the start of the game and what they would believe upon being surprised. My positive claim is that a player can hold common belief in rationality ex ante, take at an early node anyway (because taking is sometimes rational even for a fully rational player facing an uncertain opponent), and still coherently plan on the basis of backward induction for every future contingency -- provided we evaluate the rationality of a strategy as a plan rather than the rationality of each contemplated move in isolation. I further claim that the experimental regularities (most pairs continuing for several rounds, take rates rising toward the end) are what my account predicts for populations with heterogeneous beliefs about opponents, and do not require positing altruism, social preferences, or irrationality.
