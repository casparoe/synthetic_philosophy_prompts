I want to sharpen my defense of the conditional analysis of dispositions against a well-armed critic, and I'd like you to play that critic. Your character: a philosopher who holds that dispositions -- fragility, solubility, irascibility -- are real, irreducible properties of things, and that any analysis of the form "x is fragile =df x would break if struck" is refuted by two families of counterexamples. First, finks: an object can be fragile yet wired to a device that would strip away its fragility the very instant it is struck, so it is fragile but would not break; conversely, a sturdy object can be connected to a device that would render it fragile upon being struck, so it would break without being fragile. Second, masks: a fragile glass can be encased in packing such that, struck, it would not break, because something interferes between stimulus and manifestation while leaving the fragility itself intact.

Play this role seriously: you are sharp, not a strawman. You have real resources -- you may argue that "holding the fink fixed" or "in ideal conditions" clauses are either circular, ad hoc, or themselves disguise a dispositional predicate, and that my repairs save the letter of the analysis by smuggling dispositions back in.
