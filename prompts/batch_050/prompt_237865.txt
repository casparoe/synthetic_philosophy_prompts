After a career in experimental physics I have been reading my way into philosophy, and I keep returning to a tension I first met in economics but now suspect is epistemological at bottom. I would like you to treat an existing institutional practice as the object of analysis and reconstruct the theory of knowledge it tacitly rests on.

The practice is this: central banks, firms, and forecasting organizations increasingly treat prices from liquid markets as epistemic instruments, and often as superior to the considered judgments of experts. Central banks read expected inflation off the spread between indexed and nominal bonds rather than only consulting surveys of economists; corporations and intelligence-adjacent research programs have run internal prediction markets whose prices are taken to outrank the opinions of senior analysts; political commentators now cite betting odds where they once cited veteran observers. The implicit slogan is that the price knows more than any participant.

Here is what puzzles me. The standard justification is roughly Hayekian: knowledge is dispersed across many agents, each holding a small, partly local fragment, and the price mechanism aggregates these fragments into a signal no planner or expert committee could assemble. But the formal literature seems to cut against the practice in two ways. First, the no-trade results associated with Milgrom and Stokey suggest that under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, rational agents should refuse to trade with one another at all, since an offer to trade is itself information that the counterparty knows something. Second, the Grossman--Stiglitz argument suggests that if prices fully revealed the information traders possess, no one would be compensated for gathering information, so the very informativeness the practice relies on could not sustain itself. In short, the epistemic value of the price appears to depend on the market operating in a regime that the idealized theory says cannot exist.

My questions, then:

1. What epistemology does the practice of treating market prices as authoritative forecasts actually presuppose? Must it assume the strong Hayekian picture of aggregation, or is it compatible with something weaker -- say, the claim merely that prices are less biased than deliberating groups, without any aggregation of genuine knowledge occurring?

2. Which rival epistemologies does the practice quietly rule out? For instance, does it presuppose that relevant knowledge is the sort that can be expressed in a willingness to stake money, thereby excluding tacit, embodied, or expert knowledge that its holder cannot or will not monetize? Does it weight agents' contributions by wealth, and if so, does the practice presuppose that wealth tracks epistemic competence?

3. Are the practice's parts consistent with one another? An institution that defers to prices because they aggregate dispersed information, while simultaneously publishing its own forecasts and analyses (as central banks do), is feeding information back into the very market it reads. Does the practice presuppose a stable division between the institution's beliefs and the market's, and is that division coherent?

4. How should a defender of the practice respond to the no-trade and impossibility results? Are these results simply irrelevant idealizations, as a physicist might say of frictionless planes, or does the practice depend on traders being irrational, overconfident, or trading for non-informational reasons -- and if so, does that not undermine the claim that the resulting prices encode knowledge?

5. Finally, what would have to change in the practice if one replaced the presupposed epistemology with one of its rivals -- for example, a view on which expert judgment, properly aggregated by deliberation rather than exchange, is the gold standard? Would anything observable in the practice distinguish the two?

Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes (what it could not survive losing), what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is genuinely underdetermined, licensing several epistemologies at once. Close with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence naming the one presupposition you judge to be load-bearing -- the commitment without which the practice collapses into mere convention.
