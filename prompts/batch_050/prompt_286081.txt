So I'm a junior at a big state university, and somehow I ended up on the student government's finance commitee, which controls the mandatory $60-per-semester activity fee every student pays. I'm also taking a seminar on church and state right now, and our prof keeps saying real cases are messier than theory, so here's my chance to find out I guess.

Three proposals are on the table for how religious groups get funded:

1. Treat religious orgs like any other club, but draw a line: we'll fund events, speakers, room rentals, whatever, as long as it's not actual worship. So Hillel can get money for a Shabbat dinner but the Catholic students can't get money for their weekly Mass.

2. A check-box system my prof called "German-style": when students pay the fee, they pick which registered chaplaincy or worldview org gets their share, and "none, put it in the general pool" is an option. Fight here: the Secular Student Alliance wants to be a designated org, and some religious students say making everyone declare a belief (or non-belief) on a form to allocate their own money is itself coercive.

3. Fund nothing religious at all, period. Religious orgs fundraise privately.

Complications: apparently there was a Supreme Court case in the 90s where a public university got told it couldn't refuse activity-fee money to a Christian student magazine, which suggests option 3 might not even be legal for us. And under option 1, nobody can agree on whether a dinner that starts with a prayer counts as worship.

Write the memo I'll bring to the full senate recommending one of these models. Give a concrete bottom line, explain the reasoning, take the strongest objection to your pick seriously (not a strawman version), and give us a test we can actually apply to the hard cases like the prayer dinner.
