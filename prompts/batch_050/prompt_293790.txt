Consider a two-member housing cooperative whose charter contains a personal-sphere clause: each resident has final, non-waivable say over matters that fall within their own protected domain. Malik observes dawn devotions and may chant them aloud or silently in his room; Tove practices saxophone in the evenings with her window either open or fitted with a mute. Three social states are on the table:

- x: both exercise restraint (Malik silent, Tove muted)
- y: Tove plays with the window open, Malik prays silently
- z: Malik chants aloud, Tove practices muted

Under the charter, Malik is decisive between x and z (the states are identical except for his mode of prayer), and Tove is decisive between x and y (identical except for her mode of practice). Their preference rankings are as follows. Malik, a former session musician for whom Tove's playing is the great pleasure of the building, ranks y above z above x: he would trade his own chanting for her open window, but given her silence he would rather chant than sit in the quiet. Tove, who finds the dawn chanting genuinely distressing and who is mildly ascetic about her own playing, ranks x above y above z: restraint first, but if anything is to sound she would rather it be her saxophone than his voice.

Unanimity then requires y to be socially preferred to z; Malik's charter right requires z to be socially preferred to x; Tove's charter right requires x to be socially preferred to y. The cooperative's ordering cycles — a small-scale replica of Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, with a unanimity rule, two personal-sphere rights, and meddlesome preferences doing the work.

My question is not whether the formal theorem is true — it is — but whether this case is a genuine instance of it. Please give me your credence in the following proposition: the conflict above is a real and practically significant instance of the liberal paradox, in the sense that the charter clauses do confer Sen-style decisiveness, the preference pattern is the kind that actually arises in such institutions, the Pareto comparison is legitimate, and no standard repair dissolves the cycle without abandoning the substance of either the rights or the unanimity requirement.

Decompose the estimate into at least the following terms, with a probability and one or two lines of justification for each:

1. The probability that the charter's clauses genuinely satisfy Sen's minimal liberalism condition — that the pairs {x, z} and {x, y} differ only in the relevant resident's private affair. Note the pressure point: the paradox arises precisely because the prayer and the playing are audible to the other party, so whether the states can be individuated such that each pair concerns one person's sphere alone is contestable.
2. The probability that preference profiles with this structure — rankings driven in part by what the other party does within their protected sphere — arise with non-trivial frequency in real small-scale institutions, as opposed to being a constructed curiosity that a well-drafted charter could simply legislate out of existence.
3. The probability that the weak Pareto condition is legitimately applied to the y-versus-z comparison, given the familiar objection that preferences over other people's private conduct should be laundered out of the social test, or that agents who genuinely understood themselves to hold rights would not form such preferences in the relevant way.
4. The probability that none of the standard repair strategies succeeds here: restricting the admissible preference profiles; making rights alienable or conditional so that holders trade away decisiveness in exactly the troublesome cases; recasting liberalism as a constraint on permissible strategies rather than decisiveness over social states; or relaxing the requirement of a single acyclic social ordering.

Then tell me how you combine the terms — they are presumably not independent, so indicate where the dependencies lie — state the final credence, and identify the single term to which the number is most sensitive: what argument or evidence would move it most, and in which direction?
