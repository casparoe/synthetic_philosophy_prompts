I'm a doctoral student working on early modern philosophy of religion, and I've been asked to contribute to the satire number of our department's graduate journal. I'd like a satirical piece on Hume's argument against justified belief in miracle reports (Section X of the first Enquiry), in the following form: the published guidance and specimen correspondence of a fictional regulatory agency -- call it the Office for the Certification of Miracles -- whose staff have converted Hume's epistemic criteria into literal licensing procedure. About 800 words, structured as a short official notice explaining the application process, followed by three or four letters of determination sent to applicants, all but one of them unsuccessful.

The piece should be written in flawless, deadpan bureaucratic legalese, with no winking at the reader, and the humor should come from faithfully operationalizing Hume's actual argument rather than from mocking a caricature. A reader who knows the essay well should recognize each procedural rule as a real component of it; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny as a portrait of an impossible agency. Please build in the following beats:

1. The asymmetry of the evidential contest. Because a law of nature rests on the whole of uniform human experience, the application form should require the petitioner to submit evidence sufficient to outweigh the entire observational record of the species, with a fee schedule and expected processing times to match.

2. The testimonial scoring rubric. Witnesses are graded and counted: their number, good sense, education, integrity, and standing in the world, with deductions for any detectable delight in the marvelous, since the love of wonder is on file as a known psychological contaminant.

3. One letter rejects an otherwise promising application largely on the grounds that the event was witnessed in a region with an insufficient density of persons of quality. The satire should stay in character here, but the reader should quietly register that Hume's standard writes the social geography of his own milieu into epistemology -- that testimony is discounted precisely where his contemporaries' prejudices said it should be.

4. The mutual cancellation register. The Office maintains an archive in which the well-attested wonders of rival religions are filed against one another as counter-testimony, so that an application may be denied simply because a competitor sect's miracle, equally documented, is already on the books.

5. The single successful application, which should do the philosophical work of the piece. Applying the maxim that testimony can establish a miracle only when its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event reported, the Office approves one petition -- a prolonged darkness attested by all observers in all nations, on the model of the hypothetical Hume himself concedes late in the essay. Include an internal marginal note from a senior examiner querying whether the Office's founding charter (the a priori argument of the essay's first part) in fact permits any approvals whatsoever, and a junior clerk's reply noting that the charter's author seems to have conceded the point himself.

The irony should remain legible throughout: a reader should be able to tell, without ever being told, that the target is the unstable combination of the essay's two parts -- the apparently exceptionless argument of Part I and the concessive empiricism of Part II -- together with the question of whose experience gets to count as uniform.
