My state recently passed a ballot initiative by a margin of about 52 to 48, and the legislature is now considering amendments to its implementation that are arguably in tension with its spirit. At a public hearing I attended, an opponent of the amendments ended his remarks by declaring that "the will of the people" had already been expressed and that revisiting the measure meant overriding it. The phrase clearly did a lot of work in that room, and I have since noticed it doing similar work in arguments about Brexit and about recall elections. But I genuinely cannot tell what precise claim it is making. Does "the will of the people" refer to the bare fact that a majority of those who voted marked one box rather than another -- a purely descriptive claim about an aggregation procedure? Does it make a normative claim that the majority's choice, once registered, is thereby legitimate or authoritative? Does it smuggle in an epistemic claim that the electorate has somehow discerned the correct answer? Or is it a claim about finality -- that a referendum result is a different *kind* of political act than ordinary legislation, one that subsequent majorities cannot properly revise even if preferences have changed?

Please lay out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing -- I suspect there are at least four or five, including perhaps a Rousseau-style "general will" reading that is stronger than mere majority preference -- and for each one say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and whether that reading would actually support the specific use it was put to at the hearing, namely blocking a legislature from modifying a narrowly passed initiative. Feel free to note where the ambiguity is doing real rhetorical work, for instance if one reading is what makes the slogan sound obviously right while a different reading is what the conclusion actually needs. Where there is genuine disagreement among political theorists and democratic theorists about which of these claims holds, mark that as contested rather than presenting any single reading as the settled view. Keep the whole response under 800 words, and do not pad it with a survey of referendum history -- the deliverable I need is the disambiguation with verdicts, which I intend to bring to a seminar discussion.
