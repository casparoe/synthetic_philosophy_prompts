I cover climate and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long explainer about solar geoengineering -- mostly stratospheric aerosol injection. My editor is in love with a line that shows up in basicly every op-ed against the stuff: even researching it is a moral hazard, like handing a chain smoker a cheap lung-cancer insurance policy. Once people believe there's a plan B, the argument goes, they'll never quit emissions, so the responsible move is to not develop the option at all. Before I let that line carry a whole section of the piece, I want to know if it's actually load-bearing or just vibes.

So audit the analogy for me. Start with the correspondence table: in the original insurance-economics sense, moral hazard is a specific mechanism -- the insured party behaves differently because someone else is holding the bill. So who exactly is the policyholder here, who's the insurer, what's the premium, what's the insured loss, and what's the risky behavior supposedly being subsidized? Then walk me through where the mapping breaks. A few things bug me already: SRM doesn't really pay out on the loss (the CO2 is still up there, the oceans keep acidifying); the people who'd "buy the insurance" are not the same people whose emissions behavior needs to change; and an insurance policy that leaves you worse off than no policy if the insurer goes under is a pretty strange policy (hello, termination shock). Which of these breaks actually matter for the op-ed conclusion and which are just pedantic? Also tell me what the analogy smuggles in without arguing for it -- e.g. that mitigation is fundamentally a willpower problem rather than a coordination problem, or that having the option can only ever sap effort and never sharpen it. And then the part I really need: even taking the analogy as seriously as it can bear, does it support a moratorium on research, or only goveranance rules on deployment? Insurance companies do do actuarial math, after all. If there's a better analogy for what SRM actually is -- chemotherapy, a credit card, a spare parachute -- give me that too and say what it buys us.
