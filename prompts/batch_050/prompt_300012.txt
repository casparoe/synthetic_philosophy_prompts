I am a doctoral student working in formal epistemology and philosophy of science, and I have spent the past year on the problem of old evidence -- the difficulty, first pressed by Glymour, that a Bayesian conditionalization rule seems unable to explain why already-known data can confirm a new theory. When Einstein's general relativity accounted for the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, everyone took this as strong confirmation of the theory, even though the precession had been well established for decades; but if the evidence E is already in the agent's corpus, then P(E) = 1, and conditionalizing on E leaves P(H) unchanged.

The literature offers two broad families of responses, and the exchange between them seems to me to have gone quiet without being settled. The counterfactual or historical line (associated with Garber, Jeffrey, and Niiniluoto) holds that what did the confirming was not learning E but learning that the theory entails or explains E: we should evaluate confirmation against the agent's corpus before the entailment was discovered, not before E was learned. Critics reply that this merely relocates the problem, since for a logically omniscient Bayesian the entailment is also "old" -- P(H entails E) = 1 all along -- and that idealizing away from omniscience breaks the confirmation relation in other places. The second family concedes the formal point and either restricts the synchronic theory (Howson, for instance, treats the problem as showing that conditionalization needs the agent's actual learning history) or argues, as Christensen does, that old evidence is not a special puzzle at all but a symptom of the general problem of modeling logical learning, which Bayesianism owes us anyway.

My working hypothesis -- offered as a hypothesis, and I would like it tested rather than confirmed -- is that the debate stalled because the two families are answering different questions under one label: one side is asking what made the perihelion data confirm general relativity (a question about objective evidential support), while the other is asking how a bounded agent can justifiably raise her credence in a theory she already knows entails the data (a question about rational belief revision). On this diagnosis, the impasse is not really about old evidence but about whether confirmation is a relation between propositions or a relation between an agent and propositions, and no further work on the old-evidence case specifically will resolve that.

Is this diagnosis right? I am not asking for a survey of positions or a verdict on who wins. I want an analysis of the structure of the disagreement: which premises each side actually shares, whether the persistent difference is verbal, normative, or about which idealizations a confirmation theory may help itself to, and whether the logical-omniscience framing absorbs the original puzzle or merely renames it. And given your diagnosis: what would count as progress here -- a formal result, a distinction that dissolves the case, an account of logical learning that both families could accept -- or is there a principled reason to expect none?
