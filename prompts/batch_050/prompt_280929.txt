So I've landed on a position about Mill and empire and I want it stress-tested, not cheered on. The position: Mill's support for British rule in India isn't a betrayal of his liberalism but a straightforward application of it — government, for him, is a tool judged by results, and self-government is one of those tools, appropriate only where a people has the habits to work it. The "colonial Mill" and the "liberal Mill" are the same Mill.

The hook for me is this bit from chapter I of Considerations on Representative Government (1861), where he's describing the view of government he basically shares: "They look upon a constitution in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plow, or a threshing machine." If constitutions really are threshing machines for Mill — judged by output, fitted to the material they have to work on — then withholding representative institutions from India looks less like hypocrisy and more like consistency: you don't use the machine where it can't work. My question is what would count AGAINST this reconciling reading.

Be concrete about the defeaters. Would it damage the reading if passages elsewhere in Mill treat political participation as intrinsically valuable rather than merely instrumental — does his own case for representative government in the same book rest on what it does to people's character in a way that makes indefinitely postponing it for whole peoples costlier than my reading admits? What about the fact that the "unfitness" criterion is applied by the ruling power to the ruled — is that a practical problem or does it quietly wreck the threshing-machine picture, since someone has to decide when the material is ready? Does it matter that Mill spent his career at the East India Company and arguably had incentives to find this view — or is that just ad hominem and irrelevant to whether the reading is right? And does the reading risk becoming unfalsifiable: any liberal principle can be deferred forever by declaring the subjects not yet ready, so if nothing could show Mill was being inconsistent, maybe my "coherent Mill" is just a Mill with an escape hatch.

Tell me which of these a sympathetic defender of the reconciling reading would actually concede as trouble versus brush off, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely annoying, and say what you'd personally find most persuasive either way. If the honest verdict is that the passage supports my reading but the reading is more exposed than it looks, I'd rather hear that.
