I work in game theory, and I want to test how rigorous the "dark forest" hypothesis actually is. For those who haven't encountered it: it's an answer to the Fermi paradox, popularized by Liu Cixin's fiction, holding that the universe is full of civilizations that stay silent because revealing your location invites preemptive destruction. The underlying picture rests on two axioms -- survival is every civilization's primary goal, and civilizations grow while the total matter in the universe stays constant -- plus two further mechanisms: "chains of suspicion" (civilizations cannot communicate quickly enough, or verify well enough, ever to establish mutual good intentions, and each side knows the other is reasoning the same way about it) and "technological explosion" (a civilization that looks harmless now may leap past you in capability on timescales short compared to communication delays, so a present non-threat is no evidence of a future non-threat). The conclusion is that shooting on detection is rational, and hiding is rational.

Restate this hypothesis in the vocabulary I actually use -- players, strategies, payoffs, information sets, one-shot versus repeated interaction, cheap talk versus costly signaling, dominance arguments, equilibrium refinements, trembles -- as faithfully as you can. Build the game explicitly: tell me what the players know and don't know, what moves are available, and under what parameter values the dark forest outcome (everyone hides; anyone revealed gets struck) is an equilibrium, and of what kind. Then give me the residue report. Which elements of the hypothesis translate cleanly into equilibrium reasoning? Which translate only by distorting something -- I'm suspicious, for instance, that technological explosion is just a non-stationary payoff structure dressed up, and that chains of suspicion is just higher-order uncertainty we already have tools for? And which parts have no expression in the vocabulary at all? In particular: does the claim that intentions can never be verified even in principle exceed what incomplete information can model, and does treating survival as lexicographically prior to all other goods do real work that an ordinary utility function can't?

Finally, the verdict I actually care about: does the residue point to something game theory is missing -- something it structurally cannot represent -- or does it show that the dark forest hypothesis is less a theorem than an evocative parameter choice? If the latter, tell me which parameters, and what empirical facts about actual civilizations would have to hold for those choices to be forced rather than merely permitted.
