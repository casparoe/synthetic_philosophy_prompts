I am a novelist at work on a book about a young man raised in near-total isolation by a reclusive guardian, who, upon entering ordinary society at twenty, must assemble his entire moral outlook secondhand -- from a mentor's letters, from conversations with strangers, from being told what is honorable and what is shameful, never from having faced a genuine moral choice himself. For the book's arc to land, I have committed myself, as a working assumption, to a position I believe philosophers call optimism about moral testimony: roughly, that a person can come to know moral truths by being told them, in much the same way one comes to know historical or geographical facts, and that such secondhand knowledge can be fully sufficient for acting rightly and even for acting admirably.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in moral epistemology and the epistemology of testimony. Think carefully and take your time. Before answering, begin with a short brainstorming section listing every candidate defeater you can think of, however weak; then proceed to the main analysis.

What I need is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it. Please address, at minimum, the following. Which possible cases would genuinely refute the view -- for instance, does the gap between knowing that cruelty is wrong and grasping why it is wrong threaten the claim itself, or only a stronger version of it? Which objections from the pessimist tradition (that testimony yields knowledge without understanding, that moral deference undermines autonomy, that actions done on borrowed judgment lack full moral worth) are conceptual, which are normative, and which turn on empirical facts about how moral learning actually works? What would the position's own defenders concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only its critics press? Is there a result or argument that would show the view collapses into a triviality or into its rival? And has the view been insulated by qualification -- retreating, say, to the claim that testimony transmits knowledge but never understanding, or that it suffices for permissibility but not for praise -- and if so, is that retreat a sign of intellectual honesty or of a position that risks nothing?

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether it would force me to abandon the position or only to complicate my protagonist. Since my interest is ultimately narrative, I would also value, for each serious defeater, one concrete fictional situation in which the objection would become visible on the page rather than remaining an abstraction. Finally, tell me honestly what single consideration you would personally find most persuasive against the view, and why.

Please write your entire answer in American English.
