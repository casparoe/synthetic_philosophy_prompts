I am a PhD student in philosophy of physics, working on the foundations of quantum mechanics. I have provisionally committed myself to the following position, which I would now like stress-tested rather than defended.

My position: the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic program succeeds, at least in outline, in solving the probability problem for the Everett interpretation. More specifically, I accept (i) that a rational agent who knows she inhabits an Everettian multiverse can and should assign credences to branches according to the Born-rule weights, derivable from the structure of the wavefunction plus constraints on rational preference (something like the axioms in the original proofs, e.g. diachronic consistency, compositionality, or their successors); and (ii) that this is enough — that once the decision-theoretic result is in place, talk of "uncertainty" can either be reconstructed (self-locating uncertainty across branches) or shown to be dispensable, so there is no residual incoherence in applying probabilistic confirmation theory to Everettian quantum mechanics. In short: Everett recovers the Born rule where collapse theories merely postulate it, and the supposed problem of probability in many-worlds is dissolved rather than merely managed.

Please act as an expert in the philosophy of physics — someone who knows both the Everettian literature (Deutsch, Wallace, Saunders, Greaves, and their critics such as Kent, Albert, and Price) and the broader literature on self-locating uncertainty and confirmation. Before writing your answer, brainstorm internally: enumerate everything you know that has been or could be said against this position, then filter and organize rather than dumping the brainstorm.

What I want is a map of my position's exposure, in roughly this structure:

1. Which objections would count as genuinely decisive if they succeeded — e.g., a demonstration that the decision-theoretic axioms smuggle in probabilistic assumptions, that the concept of self-locating uncertainty is ill-defined when all outcomes occur, or that the program collapses into circularity — and which are merely awkward (e.g., discomfort with betting behavior as the foundation of physical probability)?
2. For each objection, indicate whether committed Everettians themselves would concede it as a threat, or whether they would dismiss it as resting on a misunderstanding (of decoherence, of what "branch" means, of what the derivation claims). I want to know where the fault lines run inside the camp, not just between camps.
3. What, if anything, would empirical or formal developments do to the view? For instance, would a failure of some structural assumption of the proofs, or a rival interpretation achieving the same empirical content with less metaphysics, count against my position — or is it insulated against such developments in a way that should worry me?
4. Finally, an honest verdict: has the position been qualified into near-unfalsifiability (every objection answered by refining the notion of rationality or of branch weight), and if so, is that a defect for a thesis of this kind, or is it the normal shape of a successful foundational program?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, and flag the single objection you think I am least prepared to answer. Think carefully and take your time; precision about what the derivation does and does not establish matters more to me than breadth.
