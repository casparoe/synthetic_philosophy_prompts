I serve on my university's central instrumentation certification board, which decides whether shared research facilities may label their measurements as board-certified for use in grant applications and published work. Our current case concerns a metabolomics core facility seeking certification for a newly acquired, top-of-the-line mass spectrometer. The sticking point is the facility's validation package, and our committee has split down the middle over it, with a decision due at next month's meeting.

The package rests on three legs. First, internal consistency: thousands of repeated runs of identical samples agree closely, and the manufacturer argues this demonstrates the instrument's accuracy, not merely its precision. Second, cross-validation: the new machine's readings match those of the facility's older spectrometer — though I note, and the dissenting members emphasize, that the older instrument's own certificate was issued on the basis of agreement with instruments of the same design lineage, so that chain of agreement never bottoms out in anything that isn't, at some remove, another spectrometer. Third, and most controversially, self-validation: the machine's onboard diagnostics flag runs performed under degraded conditions, and the facility proposes to establish the reliability of that flagging system by pointing out that, once flagged runs are excluded, the remaining readings cohere beautifully with the diagnostics' verdicts. The facility's written submission leans into the point rather than away from it: demanding a fully independent check, they argue, is demanding something no instrument has ever had, since every reference standard was itself calibrated by some prior instrument, and the history of measurement is a web of mutual support rather than a ladder down to bedrock. Applied consistently, they conclude, our scruples would decertify every instrument on campus, including the ones traceable to national standards.

Roughly half the board finds this persuasive: if everyone bootstraps, singling out this facility is arbitrary, and coherence across a large body of mutually supporting measurements is simply what instrument reliability consists in. The other half thinks the third leg is a swindle — that a systematically biased machine would pass the facility's own test provided it was consistently biased, and that an instrument cannot serve as the chief witness to the trustworthiness of its own error reports. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting: it should recommend certify, reject, or certify with conditions (specifying concretely what additional checks would be required, given that perfect independence from prior instrumentation is unattainable), and — whichever way it comes out — it should identify precisely where the facility's "everyone bootstraps" argument succeeds or breaks down, rather than dismissing it or accepting it wholesale. The losing side's strongest consideration should be stated well enough that the facility could tell we understood them.
