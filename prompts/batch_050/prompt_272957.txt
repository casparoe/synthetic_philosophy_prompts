I'm taking an undergraduate course in the philosophy of mathematics, and for our unit on the foundations crisis I've been reading a popular history of mathematics that a classmate swears by. Its chapter on set theory summarizes Russell's paradox like this: 'While working on the foundations of mathematics around 1901, Bertrand Russell discovered that the very notion of a set harbors a contradiction. Take the collection of all sets that are not members of themselves: if this collection is a member of itself, then by its own definition it isn't, and if it isn't, then it is. Since the contradiction arises from self-membership, the remedy eventually adopted in modern set theory is an axiom forbidding any set from being a member of itself, which makes the offending collection impossible to form. The episode shook the field for a few years — Frege, whose life's work had assumed otherwise, was devastated — but once the right axiom was in place, mathematics proceeded exactly as before, and today the paradox is mostly remembered as a clever puzzle in the same family as the barber who shaves all and only those men who do not shave themselves.' My classmate thinks this chapter is basically sound, but the resolution strikes me as suspiciously tidy, and I'd like to be able to say precisely where, if anywhere, the summary goes wrong before our discussion section meets.

Could you audit it for me? Which parts would a logician or set theorist accept as accurate, which are harmless simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, is the book right about what generates the contradiction, and about how modern set theory in fact avoids it? And does the closing reassurance — that mathematics could simply carry on unchanged once the appropriate axiom was adopted, leaving the paradox as little more than a brain-teaser like the barber puzzle — actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it lean on the errors? I admit I'm inclined to defer to the author, since he is a professional mathematician and I am not, but the claim that a contradiction in the foundations of the subject was settled by essentially legislating the problem away seems like the sort of thing a popularizer might say because it makes a cleaner story.
