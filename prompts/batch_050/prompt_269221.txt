I teach an undergraduate ethics seminar, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We're about to start the unit on duties of aid. Before anyone reads or hears about Singer, I want an in-class activity that makes them first endorse, and then feel the strain of, a principle like: if you can prevent something very bad at small cost to yourself, you ought to. Design a staged game or simulation with rounds, simple materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money, structured so that in the first round or two every student finds it obvious that they must help, but as the rounds continue they discover that helping keeps being demanded, that the costs accumulate, and that non-helpers can free-ride on the helpers -- so that the class fractures between those who keep paying, those who stop, and those who propose changing the rules of the game itself. Include: exact rules and setup, a timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the strong and moderate versions of Singer's principle and the fair-share objection, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and an honest note on which features of the real-world case (e.g., distance, identifiability of victims, whether suffering is truly preventable) the activity cannot reproduce.
