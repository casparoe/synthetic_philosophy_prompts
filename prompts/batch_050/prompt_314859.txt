I write novels for a living, mostly midlist literary stuff, and last month I was at this little writers retreat in Vermont with two friends of mine who also write fiction, Marta and Dev. Over drinks they got into a genuinely heated argument about the state of commercial fiction and about a passage from Scott Alexander's essay "Meditations on Moloch" (Slate Star Codex, 2014), which Marta had been pushing on everyone all week. The bit she kept quoting was his thought experiment about the dictatorless dystopia where everyone has to shock themselves eight hours a day:

"So you shock yourself for eight hours a day, because you know if you don't everyone else will kill you, because if they don't, everyone else will kill them, and so on. Every single citizen hates the system, but for lack of a good coordination mechanism it endures. From a god's-eye-view, we can optimize the system to "everyone agrees to stop doing this at once", but no one within the system is able to effect the transition without great risk to themselves."

Here's how the argument went, as best I remember it.

(1) Marta said the passage is literally our industry. Nobody -- not writers, not editors, maybe not even readers -- actually wants every thriller to run on the same four tropes with the same twist on page 280, but everyone complies because the rec algorithms and the acquisition meetings punish deviation. We all zap ourselves eight hours a day and call it a career. The proof that it's a trap and not just taste is that everyone she knows privately complains about it, alot, while publicly complying.

(2) Dev said the passage proves the opposite of what she thinks. The shock country works because there's an enforcement mechanism -- defect and they kill you. In publishing nobody kills you for writing the odd slow book; you just sell fewer copies. And if readers secretly hated the sludge, a defecting writer would clean up, so the system would have exits everywhere. The whole difference between "everyone hates it but can't coordinate" and "people actually want this stuff" is exactly what revealed preference measures, and the market runs that experiment every single week. So what Marta calls Moloch is just the discovery that readers' tastes aren't hers.

(3) Marta said the punishment doesn't have to be death to count. Alexander's own standard is "great risk," and falling off the algorithm means falling off your income, which for a working writer is plenty risky. And revealed preference is broken when the menu itself is set upstream -- readers buy what's surfaced to them, and what's surfaced is whatever performed last quarter, so the experiment Dev trusts never actually gets run. The market measures preferences over a menu Moloch already cooked.

(4) Dev said listen to yourself -- if big sales prove the public wants the sludge and small sales for literary books also prove the public would want them if properly shown, then Moloch explains everything and therefore nothing, its unfalsifiable. Plus weird books do break out every year, which is exactly what shouldn't happen in the shock country. A trap with regular successful escapes isn't the thing in that passage.

Then they both looked at me to referee and I kept quiet because I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it -- and I've got a stake in this, since I'm deciding right now whether to write the strange book I want or the marketable one.

Please adjudicate this properly, move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly right to clearly mistaken and justify the rating. Some things I'd like the answer to get into: whose reading of the passage is more faithful (does "great risk to themselves" cover lost income, and does the enforcement detail matter as much as Dev thinks?), whether Marta's upstream-menu point saves revealed preference from Dev's objection or just relocates the problem, and whether Dev's falsifiability charge lands given that Alexander offers the trap as a diagnosis, not a prediction. And finally: what are both of them missing -- is there something in the trap/taste distinction itself, or in the "god's-eye-view" clause, that would dissolve part of the disagreement? Who's more right overall?
