I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I'm presenting a chapter from my book-in-progress at a philosophy department colloquium—an audience with several Kierkegaard specialists, a couple of Kantians, and graduate students who will have read Fear and Trembling recently and closely. My thesis: the rhetoric of visionary technology founders, who ask the rest of us to tolerate present harms and broken norms in the name of a world-historical mission, amounts to what I call a counterfeit teleological suspension of the ethical. On my reading, Abraham's suspension is intelligible only against a direct divine command, a telos that is genuinely absolute, and a willingness to sacrifice what is dearest to him rather than merely impose costs on others; the founder-suspension borrows the outer structure—the singular individual claiming to stand above the universal, justified by a higher purpose, unintelligible to ordinary moral discourse—while lacking everything that made Abraham's case even arguable. I argue the comparison explains why this rhetoric registers as blasphemous rather than merely reckless.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come and from whom, phrased as these scholars would actually phrase them; mark which have real force; tell me what to concede up front and what to hold; and identify the one question I'm hoping nobody asks—I suspect it has to do with the pseudonym and whether the book even endorses Abraham.
