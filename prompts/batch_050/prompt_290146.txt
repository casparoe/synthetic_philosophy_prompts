So my friend and I got into this stupidly long argument at the pub last night and it's still bugging me. It started because I said "mosquitoes carry malaria" and she goes "no they don't, like 99% of mosquitoes have never carried malaria in their lives, you mean SOME mosquitoes carry malaria." And obviously she's technically right about the numbers but the sentence still seems... true? Like if a school textbook said it nobody would blink.

So we spent two hours trying to come up with a rule for when sentences like this are true and every rule failed immediately. To save you some time, here's the wreckage:

- "It's true if most of them do it" — fails because "ducks lay eggs" is clearly true even though male ducks (half of all ducks!) lay zero eggs, and it's really only the female ducks of the right age. Meanwhile "ducks are female" is false even though about half of ducks ARE female. So the truth of these sentences seems weirdly disconnected from the actual percentages.
- "It's true if the thing is characteristic of the kind" — this was my attempt, but then she hit me with "ticks carry Lyme disease," which is true even though only a small minority of ticks actually carry it. Carrying Lyme is arguably not "characteristic" of ticks at all, it's rare. But the sentence is true and important.
- "Ok so rare-but-dangerous counts" — her attempt to rescue it, but then compare "mosquitoes carry malaria" (true) with something like "sharks have 14 letters in their name" or idk, "mosquitoes were born on a Tuesday" — those don't work even if I could arrange the percentages to match. And worse: "books are paperbacks" sounds false even though plausibly most books sold are paperbacks, while "ticks carry Lyme disease" sounds true with single-digit percentages. Danger can't be the whole story either.

So please explain: what's going on with generics? Why do these sentences resist every simple quantificational analysis — are "all," "most," "some," and "the typical" all just wrong glosses of what the bare plural means, or is the meaning of the sentence fine and the problem is in what makes it true? Walk me through the main candidate explanations (I'm aware there are like, normalcy-based ones and ones where the generalization is about the kind rather than the individuals, but I don't really get how either handles the ducks-vs-ticks asymmetry) and tell me which one, if any, actually predicts that "ducks lay eggs" and "ticks carry Lyme disease" are true while "ducks are female" and "books are paperbacks" are false. And at the end, settle the bet: is she right that "mosquitoes carry malaria" is strictly speaking false and we just talk loosely, or is there a coherent sense in which it's true and her "SOME mosquitoes" correction is actually the worse sentence?
