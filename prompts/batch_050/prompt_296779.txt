I am a PhD student in philosophy, working on practical rationality, and I convene a weekly tutorial for twelve second-year undergraduates on a course about rational choice. We are approaching the week on Parfit's hitchhiker case and, more broadly, the idea that it can be rational to be an irrational kind of agent -- that a disposition to act against one's interests at the moment of action can itself be in one's interests to have. In past years I have presented the case in the usual way (stranded in the desert, the driver who will only rescue you if he is confident you will pay him on arrival, the perfect detector of sincerity), and I find students nod along without ever feeling the pinch: they either say they would simply pay, without noticing that paying is against their interests once they are safely in town, or they say they would not pay, without noticing that this disposition is precisely what leaves them in the desert.

Design a classroom activity for this tutorial that makes the pinch unavoidable before the case is ever mentioned. I have ninety minutes. The activity must be genuinely runnable: concrete rules, payoffs, and a structure I can operate in an ordinary seminar room. Some desiderata:

1. Real stakes, however modest (I am willing to bring chocolate or to offer points redeemable for something trivial). Students should stand to actually gain or lose something by the choices they make.

2. The scenario should be played rather than described -- a game in which some students occupy the rescuer's role and others the stranded one's -- and its rules should force participants to confront, in their own decisions, the difference between (a) what it is rational to do once rescued and (b) what disposition it is rational to be known to have beforehand. Ideally the group should first play a version in which paying on arrival is straightforwardly rational (e.g. because rounds repeat and conduct is remembered), and then a version in which that support is removed, so that they experience the ground giving way rather than being told it will.

3. The rescuer's quasi-perfect insight into the passenger's sincerity needs to be simulated by some workable mechanism within the game (interrogation, advance declarations, visible tells, or whatever you devise). Please specify this mechanism in full operational detail, since it is the crux.

4. No lecture beforehand, and Parfit's name must not come up until the debrief.

5. A debrief plan that maps particular moments of the activity onto the components of the hitchhiker case and onto the wider phenomenon of rational irrationality (threats, promises, deterrence), that tells me what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run with real students and how to respond when it does, and that is candid about which feature of the original case the game cannot reproduce and I will simply have to supply in discussion.

I would rather have one well-specified activity with these properties than a menu of sketches.
