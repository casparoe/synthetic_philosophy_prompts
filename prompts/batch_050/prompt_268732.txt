I'm in a normative ethics seminar and we've just spent two weeks on the question of whether the numbers count in rescue choices. Rather than argue abstractly, I want to pin down my own intuitions and see whether any principle underlies them. Here are my verdicts, which I'm confident about individually but less sure about jointly:

(1) A ferry can reach either of two islets before the tide turns; five strangers are stranded on one, one stranger on the other, all in equal danger. I must steer for the five. Flipping a coin would be wrong, not merely odd.

(2) Same setup, but the lone person is my sister. I may save her. I don't think I'm required to, but I'm permitted.

(3) I control a single dose of a scarce drug. One patient needs the whole dose to survive the night; five patients each need a fifth of it to survive the night. Give it to the five.

(4) Same drug, but the five each need a fifth of it only to cure severe recurring migraines, while the one needs the full dose to live. Save the one; the migraines don't add up to a life.

(5) Diverting a runaway trolley from a track where it will kill five onto a siding where it will kill one is permissible, even required. Killing one healthy patient to redistribute his organs to five dying patients is murder. I want to keep both halves of this.

(6) A policymaker can fund a program that will save one identifiable group member's life, or a program that will relieve one billion people of a single mild headache each. I lean toward saving the life -- but I notice this sits badly with (1) and (3), where I let sheer numbers settle things.

What principle, or small family of principles, captures as many of these verdicts as possible? Please state it precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case, tell me what it implies about cases I haven't considered -- for instance, whether a weighted lottery giving the lone person in (1) a one-in-six chance would be acceptable -- and be honest about which of my verdicts a clean theory would have to overturn. If the cost of saving (6) is an implausibly sharp discontinuity in how harms aggregate, I'd rather know that than be offered a baroque principle that accommodates everything.
