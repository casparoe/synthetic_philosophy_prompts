I am at work on a novel set in Paris in 1791, and two of my characters, both deputies of the Constituent Assembly, are locked in an argument I need to get philosophically right. One speaks of the constitution as a machine to be designed and built by rational men; the other insists it must grow out of the nation's habits or not at all. In the opening chapter of Considerations on Representative Government, John Stuart Mill describes precisely these two camps. Of the first he writes that they look upon a constitution "in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plow, or a threshing machine." Of the second: "Governments can not be constructed by premeditated design. They 'are not made, but grow.'" Mill treats the two as rival exaggerations, each grasping part of the truth.

Both analogies are still doing heavy work in interpretations of the French Revolution, and that is what I need your help with. The machine picture underwrites readings on which the Terror was the output of ideology and deliberate choice: men deciding, catastrophically, what to build. The organic picture underwrites structural readings on which events unfolded from conditions no one chose, and could hardly have gone otherwise. Please audit both analogies as applied to the French case. Give me the correspondence table for each: in the machine picture, what plays the role of the engineer, the blueprint, the raw material, the defective part the guillotine removes; in the organic picture, what plays the role of soil, seed, season, and gardener. Then show me where each mapping breaks, and which features each analogy quietly imports that the revolutionary situation lacked. For instance: does the machine picture smuggle in a standpoint outside society from which to rebuild it, and a responsibility of the builder that no historical actor could actually bear? Does the organic picture smuggle in exculpation, rendering the Terror as blameless as a hailstorm? And does either analogy accommodate the way the revolutionaries' own self-understanding fed back into events? Finally, tell me whether Mill's synthesis survives the audit, and if you can propose a better governing analogy for what happened in France between 1789 and 1794, I will steal it for my novel.
