I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been part of a monthly philosophy-and-literature reading group at my company for about two years, and next month it's my turn to lead. We just finished Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, and my presentation defends a thesis I've been developing on my own: that the teleological suspension of the ethical is not primarily a story about divine command or religious exceptionalism, but a thought experiment about what happens when an agent's deepest obligation cannot be expressed in the universal, publicly justifiable language that ethics requires -- and that Abraham's silence, not his knife, is the real center of the book. I argue that Kierkegaard is diagnosing a structural limit of ethical life rather than licensing violations of it, and that the knight of faith is terrifying precisely because his action is unjustifiable to everyone, including, in some sense, to himself.

Here's the audience I need to prepare for. The group has about ten regulars: two members with philosophy PhDs who now work in industry, one of whom is a committed Kantian who thinks universalizability is non-negotiable and has already told me over lunch that he finds the book morally repugnant; a former seminary student who tends to read Kierkegaard devotionally and resists what she calls psychologizing the text; several engineers like me who default to treating any claim about incommunicable obligations as either irrationalism or an excuse for fanaticism; and one person who keeps bringing everything back to Levinas and the face of the other. I expect the Kantian to push hardest, but I suspect the devotional reader may actually cause me more trouble, since my reading strips out the theological payoff she cares about.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order and the form they'll actually come from this specific audience, phrased the way each person would phrase them rather than in generic textbook terms. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without notes. Flag where each reply is weakest so I'm not caught off guard when someone presses on it. Also tell me which of my claims is most vulnerable to the textual evidence itself -- I've only read the book once, in translation, and I'm sure there are passages that cut against my reading that I should either address head-on or quietly stop depending on. Finally, include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and the best available answer to it.
