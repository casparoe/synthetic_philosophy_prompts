I'm a nurse turned part-time philosophy student, and a pattern from my working life keeps resurfacing in my ethics seminar without, as far as I can tell, getting a name of its own. Here's the shape of it. The only thing standing between a person and some harm is an ongoing contribution that a second person has been voluntarily making -- not a one-off gift, but a continuous input of effort, resources, or equipment that the recipient has come to depend on. Then the contributor stops, and specifically stops *because* they want the harm to happen, or at least are indifferent to it. A few cases: a man who owns a stretch of coastal path has for years maintained a rope barrier along the eroded cliff edge at his own expense; after a falling-out with the walking club, he takes it down in full knowledge that the tourist season is starting. A hospital keeps a patient alive on a machine the hospital owns and staffs; treatment is withdrawn, at the family's request or because funding runs out. A wealthier neighbor has for years topped up a struggling family's food budget every month and then, feeling slighted, simply stops, knowing winter is coming. In every case, nothing is pushed, broken, or taken away that the agent didn't themselves supply; all that happens is that something that was flowing stops flowing.

What strikes me is that our standard vocabulary seems to straddle these cases uncomfortably. My seminar treats killing and letting die as the two poles, with the boundary usually drawn at whether the agent set the harmful sequence in motion or merely declined to interrupt a sequence already underway. But in my cases the agent arguably did set something in motion long ago -- the protected, supported state of the victim is itself the agent's creation -- and then withdraws it mid-course, so the death or harm happens against a baseline the agent built. People in class keep reaching for different poles depending on framing: taking down the rope sounds like letting nature take its course, until you add that he did it hoping someone would fall; unplugging the machine sounds like killing, until you add that the machine was never the patient's to begin with. I've heard there is literature on "withdrawing aid" that touches some of this, but my impression is it mostly asks whether withdrawal is permissible, not what withdrawal *is* -- whether it's a third thing alongside doing and allowing, or one of them in disguise, or something whose moral character depends entirely on the agent's reasons and the recipient's expectations.

So my question is: does this configuration -- deliberate discontinuation of one's own ongoing, relied-upon contribution, where the discontinued thing is the sole barrier between victim and harm -- have an established name, and is there work that treats it as a category in its own right rather than as a hard case of killing or of letting die? If it doesn't have a name, I'd be glad of one, but more than that I want the carving: what exactly distinguishes it from (a) refusing to start helping, (b) taking away something that belongs to the victim, and (c) ceasing aid that nobody had reason to expect would continue -- and does the agent's motive belong in the definition of the act-type or only in the evaluation of the act?
