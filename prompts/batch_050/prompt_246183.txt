In my graduate seminar on epistemic injustice we spent two sessions on testimonial injustice and the idea of a credibility deficit, and the discussion went well enough until the final half hour, when one student asked whether a credibility judgment can also err in the other direction — whether a speaker can be accorded more credibility than her evidence warrants, and if so what the harms of that are. Before I could say anything, another student responded with "Believe women," delivered as a full stop, and the room treated the question as having been answered. I let it go at the time, but I've been uneasy since, because I genuinely cannot say what proposition was being asserted, and I suspect the student couldn't either. The slogan plainly does real work in public discourse, and I don't want to be the professor who dismisses it as empty; but my seminar needs it to be a claim, or several, before we can ask whether it's true.

What I'd like is a menu of the distinct, precise theses the slogan could be expressing, laid out so that I can distribute it at the next meeting. I can gesture at candidates to show the range I have in mind: a near-platitudinous reading on which women's testimony should not be discounted simply because it is women's, which nobody in my seminar would deny; a corrective reading on which hearers should up-credit women to compensate for a documented, systematic deficit, which is substantive and where the interesting questions about mechanism and overshoot live; an institutional reading on which "believe" governs the default stance of police, employers, and universities rather than of private individuals deliberating about a particular case; a speech-act reading on which the imperative isn't a doxastic demand at all but an injunction to take reports seriously, investigate, and not dismiss out of hand; and a stronger reading on which particular allegations carry a presumption of truth that it would take positive counterevidence to defeat. There may be others I'm missing, including an empirical claim about the rarity of false reports, which sometimes seems to be doing the work but is a different kind of claim entirely. For each reading, I want a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and probably false — and, crucially, an answer to the question my seminar actually needs: which of these readings, if any, would license shutting down the overshoot question the way my student did, and is that reading among the defensible ones, or does the conversational move require a stronger thesis than anyone would explicitly endorse?

One more thing. I'd like you to mark how confident you are in each verdict and to flag where I've set the problem up badly rather than answer it as posed. If, for instance, it's a mistake to treat a movement slogan as a proposition awaiting disambiguation at all — if its function is irreducibly practical or coalitional, so that asking "which thesis does it assert?" distorts it — I'd rather be told that plainly and told what the better question is. And if the overshoot question I described is itself confused, or can be posed in a way that doesn't invite the exchange I had, say so. I'm not looking for a verdict on the slogan's politics; I'm looking for enough precision that my students can disagree about something definite.
