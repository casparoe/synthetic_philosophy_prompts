I teach a third-year module on the philosophy of animal minds, and at the end of the unit on anthropomorphism I like to open the final seminar with a short poem before we get into the week's readings. I would like you to write it for me: a sonnet, in British English, about Morgan's canon.

A few stipulations about content, because accuracy matters more to me than ornament. The octave should present the canon the way it was intended: as a methodological safeguard stating that an animal's behaviour should not be explained by appeal to a higher psychological faculty if it can adequately be explained by a lower one. Feel free to let the Clever Hans affair stand in as the cautionary case -- a horse apparently doing arithmetic, revealed to be reading his questioners' unconscious cues -- since it is exactly the sort of embarrassment the canon exists to prevent. The volta, though, should pivot to the complication I want my students to sit with: that the canon is double-edged. Deployed uncritically, an austere preference for "lower" explanations can itself become a bias, reflexively denying cognition to animals on grounds of parsimony alone, and there is no neutral fact of the matter about which faculty counts as simpler. The closing couplet should leave open whether the canon is a scalpel or a blindfold -- I do not want the poem to settle the question, because the seminar is meant to.

The sonnet should observe the form properly (fourteen lines, a recognisable turn at line nine, a concluding couplet), and a philosopher of animal cognition should be able to recognise the actual logical structure of the debate in it rather than a vague gesture at "animal minds." No footnotes, no references, and please do not quote any historical text verbatim.
