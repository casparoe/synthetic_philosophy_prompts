I serve on the five-member allocation committee of a regional disaster-preparedness fund. Twice a year we distribute roughly four million dollars among proposals -- levee reinforcements, medical stockpiles, emergency communications systems, shelter retrofits. Our recurring problem is that the most consequential proposals involve risks for which no defensible probabilities exist: unmodeled flood regimes, pathogens whose transmissibility is genuinely unknown, supply chains that have never been stress-tested. My colleagues currently handle this in two bad ways: some invent spurious probabilities and run an expected-value calculation on them, while others throw up their hands and vote on instinct. I want a written decision procedure, usable in a ninety-minute meeting by members with no decision-theoretic training, for proposals where the uncertainty is real.

Please operationalize the standard apparatus for choice under ignorance as a procedure we could actually run. Specifically:

First, a gatekeeping step. State, as a question with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer, how the committee should decide whether a proposal involves risk (probabilities we can defend), uncertainty (probabilities exist somewhere but we cannot responsibly assign them), or genuine ignorance. This step must say what to do when staff supply numbers we do not trust: when we may proceed with them anyway, and when their existence should not count as possessing probabilities at all.

Second, for proposals judged to lie in genuine ignorance, a rule for selecting among three decision rules: maximin, minimax regret, and expected utility computed under a uniform prior over states of the world. Render the conditions under which maximin is the rational choice -- of the sort Rawls associated with the original position -- as concrete operational tests the committee can apply to a proposal, and do the same for the Harsanyi-style insistence that a rational agent facing ignorance should assign equal probabilities and maximize expected value. For minimax regret, state explicitly what the rule requires (a fixed menu of options and an outcome matrix) and address its known failure mode: the ranking of options can reverse when a new option is added or an existing one withdrawn, even though nothing about the original options has changed. Tell us how the committee should construct and freeze its option menu so that this instability is at least visible rather than hidden.

Third, an explicit treatment of reversibility. Some of our decisions can be revisited in two years; others cannot. State how the possibility of later revision should move us off maximin, and what the procedure should do when reversibility itself is uncertain.

Fourth, mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a values judgment -- for instance, how the committee weighs a worst-case outcome against the gains it forgoes by insuring against it -- and be candid about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.

Fifth, apply the procedure in full to three cases: (a) a choice between two levee designs, one cheaper with an unknown failure threshold under unmodeled sea-level scenarios, one expensive with a well-understood one; (b) a choice between stockpiling a well-tested antiviral and a cheaper novel compound whose failure mode is unknown; and (c) a case of your construction in which including or excluding a fourth, plausible proposal flips the minimax-regret ranking of the first three. At least one worked application must deliver an answer the committee will dislike -- for instance, the procedure selecting the dull, expensive option -- and where that happens, say plainly that this is the procedure working as intended rather than a defect.

Conclude with a short section on what the procedure cannot settle. In particular, it should not pretend to resolve the underlying dispute between the Rawlsian willingness to set probabilities aside and the Harsanyian insistence that doing so is irrational; identify instead which steps of the procedure silently take a side on that dispute, so the committee at least knows where the theory is doing work we have not voted on.
