Construct a thought experiment designed to test whether the procreation asymmetry — the claim that there is strong moral reason not to bring a miserable person into existence, but no comparably strong reason to bring a happy person into existence — is really an asymmetry about procreation, or whether it reflects a deeper asymmetry about existence itself. Your scenario should therefore not present a choice between creating and not creating a person; instead, it should force the mirror question into the open: if failing to cause a happy person to exist is morally neutral, is failing to keep an equally happy person in existence neutral as well?

Constraints: the scenario must be realistic — set it in a fertility clinic, a hospital, or a public-health funding decision, with no science-fictional devices, no demons, and no stipulations of perfect foresight; the scenario itself must fit in a single paragraph; the welfare level of every person in every outcome must be fully specified, so that no verdict can rest on uncertainty about how lives would go; and the verdict the asymmetry delivers must not be obtainable by a simple appeal to the wrongness of killing — the loss of life in your case must result from something like declining to fund, declining to intervene, or letting nature take its course, not from an act ordinarily classed as killing.

After presenting the scenario: (1) state the verdict delivered by the procreation asymmetry, and explain why it follows rather than merely being asserted; (2) state the verdicts delivered by total utilitarianism and by a person-affecting view, and explain why each follows; (3) identify the single feature of the case doing the work — the feature whose removal would collapse the disagreement between these views — and say what each view concludes once that feature is removed; and (4) steelman the objection a defender of the asymmetry would raise against your construction (for instance, that the asymmetry was only ever a claim about coming into existence, so that your case simply falls outside its scope), and judge whether the objection succeeds or instead concedes that the asymmetry is narrower than its name suggests.
