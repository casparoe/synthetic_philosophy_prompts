So I chair my university's animal care and use committee and we got a proposal last week that's splitting the room, and honestly I think it's the speciesism debate wearing a lab coat. Here's the setup: a postdoc in the bio department wants to run aversive-learning experiments on crickets -- basically pairing mild electric shocks with certain smells and then checking whether the crickets avoid the smell later, and also whether they'll put up with some shock to get at food vs. flee. Her pitch is that this actually *tests* whether insects show the behavioral signatures of pain rather than just nociception (flexing away from a stimulus versus the more complicated trade-off behavior), and that this matters for how labs everywhere should treat insect subjects.

The fight on the committee: half the members say insects aren't covered by our welfare framework at all, full stop -- we approve mouse studies with way worse procedures than this, and nobody loses sleep over flies in a genetics lab, so treating crickets as morally considerable is just sentimental inconsistency. The other half says that's exactly the speciesist move -- picking whichever line happens to be convenient for us -- and that where we're uncertain about sentience we should apply something like a precautionary principle, especially since the whole *point* of the experiment is to gather evidence about the very capacity whose absence is supposed to license it. There's also a third grumble from a philosopher-adjacent member (me) that the design is in a weird way self-undermining: if the crickets come back showing no pain-like behavior, skeptics will say the test was never sensitive enough to detect it anyway, and if they do show it, we've harmed them to find out -- so it's not obvious the experiment can actually settle the question it's supposed to settle.

Write me the committee's decision memo. Approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- but commit to one and give the reasoning. Give the strongest version of the losing side a fair shake (if you approve, really reckon with the "we don't do this for crickets and consistency doesn't demand we start" argument; if you reject, reckon with the "this research could *reduce* massive future insect suffering in labs and you're blocking the evidence-gathering" argument). And whatever you recommend, spell out conditions or next steps the researcher could concretely respond to, not just abstract hand-waving about welfare. Aim for something a real committee could vote on.
