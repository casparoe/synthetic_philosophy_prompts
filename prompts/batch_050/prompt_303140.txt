so my dads 70th birthday is in two weeks and I've been voluntold to write something to read at the dinner, and I want to do something slightly weird with it. in my applied ethics seminar we just did the unit on filial obligations and read Jane English's piece on what grown children owe their parents -- her line is roughly that the "debt" framing is all wrong, because things done out of love aren't loans, and while a genuine favor might create a debt of gratitude, most of what parents do was never a favor in that sense, so the better model for the relationship is friendship: duties that are real but that exist only while the friendship does. my group spent the whole session fighting about whether thats cold or actually the warmest possible view, and I landed on: warmest possible view, but only if you really feel the sting in it.

so: write me a sonnet i can put in the card / read aloud, where the form does the arguments work. i want the octave written in ledger language -- itemized costs, invoices, interest accruing, balance due, the whole bookkeeping of sacrifice -- building up the naive picture that a childhood is something you pay off. the volta should be the moment the speaker realizes that actually settling the account would end the relationship rather than honor it, since a paid-off debt just closes the file. then the sestet should shift registers into the idiom of friendship -- showing up, calling, giving things that were never invoiced -- and the final couplet should hold the friendship models edge: nothing here was owed, which is the only thing that makes any of it a gift. someone whos read English should be able to recognize the moves (the favor/debt distinction, love not generating debt, friendship as the surviving model) without the poem ever name-checking her or smelling like a philosophy seminar, and someone who hasnt should just think its a good birthday poem with a slightly unsentimental turn.

hard constraints: a proper sonnet, 14 lines max, and please keep the ledger metaphors concrete (actual receipts, a checkbook, compound interest) rather than abstract talk about "debt".
