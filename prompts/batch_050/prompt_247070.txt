I’ve ended up in one too many late-night threads about acausal trade, simulation threats, and Goodhart, so for my own decision-theoretic “constitution” I tried to write down the line I actually endorse rather than just vibing from case to case. Roughly: (1) In ordinary causally isolated cases, an agent should refuse acausal blackmail: if some distant mind can gain by predicting that I won’t comply and then arranging something awful, paying anyway turns every sufficiently predictable agent into a mugging magnet. (2) Genuine acausal trade is still possible and sometimes worth doing; two agents who cannot communicate can each choose policies partly because of what they infer about the other’s reasoning, provided the arrangement is one both could endorse from behind a veil of ignorance before knowing which side they occupied. (3) I’m sympathetic to policy-level rather than purely causal decision theory: one-box, pay the counterfactual mugger when the predictor’s accuracy and the coin were settled beforehand, and generally choose the policy you would want accurate predictors to expect. (4) Simulation or substrate doesn’t affect moral patienthood: if simulated people suffer, that suffering really counts; but being algorithmically similar enough to predict me doesn’t make a simulation me, and doesn’t give its creator ownership over my decisions. (5) When an agent cannot directly optimize some hard-to-measure base good, it should adopt a legible surrogate goal and make that proxy sticky under pressure, rather than allowing case-by-case rationalization to hollow it out. (6) At the same time, the surrogate never becomes terminal: if sufficiently strong evidence shows that it has separated from the base good, the agent has to revise it; refusing to revise is just institutionalized Goodhart. (7) The difference between a threat and a trade should be a structural fact about the incentives an agreement creates, not a matter of vibes, framing, or who happened to make the first move.

Can you audit whether these commitments hang together? I’m especially interested in non-obvious tensions: places where the motivation for one principle secretly assumes something another principle denies, not just places where two bullets use different vocabulary. Please go through the important pairs, distinguish real contradictions from merely apparent ones, and rank the tensions by severity. For each real tension, identify the smallest revision that would resolve it and what that revision costs. If some pair looks inconsistent but actually isn’t, explain what distinction dissolves it. I don’t want a generic lecture on UDT or simulation ethics; treat this as an audit of this particular package, and if one commitment has to go, say which one rather than spreading the damage evenly.
