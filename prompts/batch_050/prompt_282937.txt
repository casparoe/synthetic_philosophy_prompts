I'm writing a short column for a student philosophy magazine on what classical Daoism would make of modern death-tech, and I keep getting stuck on Zhuangzi specifically. What I'd like from you is a reasoned reconstruction of what he'd most plausibly think about two things that obviously didn't exist in the Warring States period. First, cryonic suspension: someone has their body vitrified at death in the hope that future medicine revives and cures them, and its defenders insist this isn't a refusal of death at all, just a hedge, a kind of ambulance ride to a hospital that doesn't exist yet. Given the way Zhuangzi treats death as one more transformation among the ten thousand changes -- the story where he drums on a pot and sings after his wife dies, the bit where he sleeps on a roadside skull and dreams it prefers death to life -- would he see cryonics as a deep category error, i.e. clinging to one particular form instead of accepting the transformation into whatever comes next? Or could a good Zhuangzian just shrug and say that revival is simply another change of form, no different in principle from the body feeding the grass, and that resisting it and demanding it are equally daft? Second, griefbots: chatbots trained on a dead person's messages so the bereaved can keep talking to them. Would he treat the bot as continuous with the person in some way (one more shape in the long chain of transformations, like the butterfly dream running in both directions), or as a husk that mocks the mourner? And would keeping up the chats be unhealthy grasping at a fixed identity, or perfectly harmless play, since he doesn't seem to think identities were ever that fixed in the first place?

Please argue from his actual attitudes -- death as transformation, equanimity towards the whole business, scepticism about hard lines between life and death and self and other -- rather than generic hand-waving about Daoism and harmony and flow. Where the extrapolation genuinely breaks down, say so: for instance his equanimity seems to presuppose that death is simply unavoidable, and cryonics at least purports to make it optional, which might change the whole calculus, or might not. And don't put words in his mouth with invented quotations; paraphrase the anecdotes instead. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence -- roughly what he'd think about each of the two technologies -- then give the reasoning, with no preamble and definately no restating my question back at me.
