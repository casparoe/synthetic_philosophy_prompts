So I manage a team of about 15 people at a mid-sized logistics company, and a few times a year I have to respond to misconduct -- missed deadlines that someone hid, a rude email to a client, an expense report that doesn't add up. Nothing criminal, but stuff I can't ignore either. HR signs off on anything serious, but the first conversation is always mine, usually within a day or two of finding out, and I usually get maybe 30 minutes to prep.

Here's what I keep circling around. I've read a bit about the guilt vs shame distinction -- the idea that guilt is about judging what you did ("I did a bad thing") while shame is about judging what you are ("I am bad"), and that guilt tends to push people toward fixing things while shame pushes them toward hiding, lashing out, or quitting. If that's even roughly right, it seems like it should change HOW I run these conversations: what I say, what I deliberately don't say, what I ask the person to do afterward. So I want you to turn that distinction into a procedure I can actually follow.

Specifically: a sequence of questions I work through before the meeting, in order, with what counts as a good enough answer to each -- things like whether the incident was a one-off act or part of a pattern (because criticizing a pattern seems closer to criticizing the person, and maybe that's sometimes appropriate?), whether the harm was public and the team is expecting some kind of acknowledgment, whether the person has already shown they feel bad or is in denial, and whether they come from a background where face-saving works differently than I'm used to. For each question, tell me what it should change about my response: private vs with witnesses, language that targets the act vs language that drifts toward the person, what kind of amends to ask for, what follow-up to schedule so I can tell if I've triggered withdrawal instead of repair.

Also I want you to take seriously the second half of the distinction: can an organization actually CHOOSE to be a guilt culture rather than a shame culture? If emotions are things that happen to people, maybe "choosing a culture" is confused. But if it isn't, tell me which levers I actually control as one mid-level manager (my meeting scripts, how I talk about my own mistakes, what happens to people after they apologize) and which are above my pay grade.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
1. A good analyst sent a client a report with a serious error, got a terse complaint, and has now gone quiet -- missing standups, slow on everything. I suspect shame spiral but I'm not sure.
2. A repeat offender, third time bending the same rule, and honestly the pattern kind of IS who he is -- the case where my procedure probably tells me something I won't like, because act-focused language seems designed to fail here.
3. A popular senior person made a cutting joke about a junior colleague in front of everyone, and the junior person's friends are waiting to see whether I make her apologize publicly -- where I suspect the procedure will say public acknowledgment is owed even though it feels like forcing a shaming ritual.

Flag every point where the procedure has to stop and hand things to my judgment, and be honest about what this whole framework can't tell me -- I don't want a flowchart that pretends reading another human being is mechanical.
