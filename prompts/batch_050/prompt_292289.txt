I'm in a small undergrad seminar on moral revolutions, and our prof has this running gimmick: whenever a historical norm collapse comes up, she makes us classify what actually drove it -- moral arguments winning on the merits, material interests shifting underneath, or honor (what Appiah talks about with dueling and footbinding, the practice becoming disgraceful or just ridiculous). She's pretty committed to the Williams-ish line that anti-slavery arguments were available and basically sound for centuries before abolition actually happened, so mere arguments don't get credit for much in her book.

Her verdicts so far: British Atlantic abolition -- interests, full stop: the moral case was conceded even by plenty of slaveholders well before 1807, and the practice died when the economics and geopolitics turned. English dueling -- honor: anti-dueling laws and sermons were ancient, and it died out by mid-century once gentlemen started laughing at duelists instead of admiring them. Chinese footbinding -- honor: reformers' arguments circulated for decades with little effect, then the marriage-market pledge societies flipped what counted as a respectable bride and the whole thing collapsed in about a generation. And public executions in England -- honor again: the deterrence debate was never decisively won by anyone, but the spectacle ended in the late 1860s once respectable opinion decided that attending a hanging, let alone hosting one, was itself degrading.

Last week my roommate proposed the next case and she's letting us argue it Tuesday: the revolution in American attitudes toward drunk driving, roughly 1965 to 2000. The tangle, as I see it: the statistical case against drunk driving was solid by the 60s, so arguments were in place early; the penalties actually started biting in the early-to-mid 80s (MADD, the push to 21, tougher enforcement) and alcohol-related deaths started dropping around then; but the stigma shift -- the comic-drunk trope dying out, the designated driver becoming a badge of decency -- seems to have consolidated later, and plenty of people kept driving drunk for years even where penalties were already stiff. Predict her verdict, tell me which of her past cases is the closest analog, and say what rule she's implicitly running: credit whichever mechanism arrived last before the collapse, or credit the one whose absence had been the bottleneck while the other two sat there accomplishing nothing, or some third option I haven't thought of. Her four verdicts strike me as consistent with more than one of those rules, so also name the case, historical or hypothetical, that would actually settle which one she's using -- and if you think the drunk driving record genuinely underdetermines her verdict, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each choice.
