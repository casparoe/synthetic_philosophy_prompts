I teach an undergrad political theory course and this term we're doing a unit on the boundary problem — the question of who gets to count as part of the demos in the first place, before any voting even happens. My students keep treating "rule by the people" as if the people were just given by nature, so I want to build a decision-tree handout that forces them to land on a position by answering concrete cases, not by picking whichever label sounds nicest. Something they can walk through with a pen in the first ten minutes of the seminar.

So: build me the tree. Seven to nine diagnostic questions, each one a concrete case where the standard positions genuinely come apart — I'm thinking things like: a woman born in Berlin to Turkish parents who's lived there her whole life but never naturalized; a German citizen who's lived in Canada for twenty years and still votes absentee; a Polish trucker stopped at the German border; Bangladeshi farmers losing their land partly because of European climate policy; a fourteen-year-old; a tourist on a two-week visit. Phrase each question so someone can answer it without knowing any terminology. The leaves should include at least: the all-affected-interests princple (Goodin's version), the all-subjected-to-coercion principle — including the twist where being coerced at the border itself buys you a say in immigration policy (the Abizadeh move) — a residency-or-stakeholder view, and a birthright/nationality view. I'd also like one leaf for the response that there is no democratic solution to the boundary question at all, since any rule for constituting the demos would itself have had to be chosen by some demos (the regress you get from Whelan).

Two more things. First, flag the cases that can't actually separate two leaves — my guess is the lifelong Berlin resident gets enfranchised by affected-interests, coercion, AND residency views alike, so the tree needs sharper cases to pry those three apart, and if some pair of leaves can't be separated by any realistic case, say so rather than papering over it. Second, for any combination of answers that seems to be unoccupied (say, someone who enfranchises expat citizens but not long-term residents, or vice versa), add a one-line diagnosis of whether that spot is empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored. And somewhere the tree has to confront the recursion head-on: whatever principle you use to draw the boundary had to be picked by somebody, so tell me where that objection bites each leaf.
