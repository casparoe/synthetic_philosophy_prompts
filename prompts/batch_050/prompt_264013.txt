Roughly a quarter of the evolutionary biology proposals that cross my desk each funding cycle invoke selection at some level other than the individual organism: tumor growth framed as selection among cells, termite eusociality explained by colony-level selection, long-term trends in mammal body size attributed to species selection, cooperation in microbes chalked up to group selection. I screen these proposals for a private foundation -- about a hundred a year, ninety minutes each -- and my decision determines which applications go to full review, so errors are expensive in both directions. I have a PhD in biology, but it's fifteen years old, and I've been burned twice: once I dismissed a proposal for saying "group selection" when the objection turned out to be purely terminological, and once I waved through a proposal whose group-level framing concealed an individual-level mechanism that made the experimental design unworkable as written. My panel reviewers are competent biologists but none are philosophers of biology, so the screening tool has to be usable by me alone, before review, without specialist backup.

What I want is a screening procedure I can run on any proposal claiming that selection operates at a particular level: an ordered sequence of tests, each with a statement of what counts as passing and what counts as failing, built from what the units-of-selection debate has actually settled rather than what it still contests. Draw on Lewontin's minimal conditions for any selection process, the replicator/vehicle (or replicator/interactor) distinction, the difference between a trait spreading because its bearers outcompete others within groups and its spreading because groups possessing it outreproduce rival groups, the bookkeeping question of whether between-group differences carry causal weight once within-group selection is fully accounted for, and the kin-selection/group-selection equivalence problem. The procedure must force an early determination of what question the proposal is even asking -- a claim about the causal locus of selection versus a claim about which bookkeeping scheme is most convenient -- because I suspect half the disputes on my desk dissolve at that point, but I want the procedure to make that call, not me. For each test, specify what evidence in a proposal counts as an answer to it; specify where the procedure terminates in a definite verdict (the framing is adequate, or it is not) versus where it hands me an irreducible judgment call, and flag honestly which steps are judgment wearing the costume of procedure. Above all, do not give me a flowchart that resolves every hard case with "both levels of description have their merits": where two framings are genuinely equivalent redescriptions, the procedure should say so and state which one is more useful for evaluating the proposal's design, and where a framing is simply mistaken it should name the error. I would rather run a procedure that commits and is occasionally wrong than one that never commits.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals, inventing plausible details as needed: (1) a field study of termite eusociality proposing colony-level selection experiments; (2) a cancer biology proposal modeling tumor progression as multilevel selection; (3) a paleontology proposal attributing the body-size trend in fossil horses to species selection. At least one run should end in a verdict I will find unwelcome -- I am personally skeptical of group-selection talk, so if the procedure convicts an individual-level framing of being a redescription in disguise, or acquits a group-level framing I would have rejected, say so plainly rather than softening it -- and close with a candid note on what the procedure cannot do and where it will systematically misfire.
