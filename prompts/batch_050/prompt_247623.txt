I'm writing the final exam for a cross-listed course (philosophy of science meets ML theory, strong 3rd/4th year undergrads). We've done two units: Hume's problem of induction (the original circularity argument, plus a quick detour through Goodman) and the no-free-lunch theorems for optimization and supervised learning -- Wolpert's results, stated informally: if you average performance uniformly over all possible target/cost functions, every algorithm does equally well, so there is no universally best learner or search strategy.

Here's my problem. In office hours last week, half the students walked away saying "so the NFL theorems basically prove Hume was right, induction is rationally unjustified, end of story," and the other half said "no, NFL just shows you need domain-specific assumptions, which nobody ever doubted." I think both of these are too quick but for different reasons, and I want the exam to find out who actually gets why.

Write me ONE essay question that forces them to adjudicate this dispute instead of reciting the theorem statement or retelling the Hume story. Constraints: 50 minutes, 40 marks, closed book, and it should be answerable without producing any proofs (they've seen the formal setup but I don't want symbol-pushing -- I want them reasoning about what the uniform averaging over all possible worlds is actually doing philosophically, and how it relates to the uniformity-of-nature assumption Hume says we can't justify). Ideally the question punishes anyone who just writes "induction is hard" in fancier words.

Then give me:

1. A model answer of roughly 600-700 words that commits to a definite verdict on the office-hours dispute -- take a side on which of the two camps is closer to right and argue it, rather than surveying the options and leaving it to the reader. I'd like it to get into whether the NFL averaging move is doing the same work as Hume's point, more work, or less.

2. A marking scheme out of 40 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make, and lists the 3-4 standard misreadings (e.g. "NFL proves learning is impossible") that should cap an answer at a B or below no matter how polished the prose is.

Be opinionated in the model answer, btw -- I need something I can defend in front of students who'll push back, not a both-sides summary.
