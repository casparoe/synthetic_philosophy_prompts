I am a PhD candidate in aesthetics, and this semester I have been teaching assistant for an undergraduate seminar on the paradox of fiction: the trilemma among (1) our apparently genuine emotional responses to characters in stories, (2) our settled knowledge that those characters do not exist, and (3) the intuitive principle that emotions like pity and fear are only intelligible, only the emotions they are, when one believes their objects exist and stand in some danger or distress. For the final meeting, I would like to give my students a poem that compresses the whole arc of the course, and I am hoping you will write it.

Please compose a sestina — six sextains followed by a three-line envoi, with the six end-words permuted in the traditional pattern rather than in an ad hoc order. For the end-words, use two per proposition of the trilemma: grief and pity for the reality of the response, fiction and invented for the knowledge of nonexistence, belief and existence for the principle that ties emotion to belief. The first stanza should simply stage the experience: someone weeping over a novel late at night, and then feeling faintly ridiculous about it. The middle three stanzas should each give voice to one of the standard exits from the paradox, in this order: first the view that what we feel is not real emotion at all but a kind of make-believe or quasi-emotion; second the view that genuinely entertaining the thought of the character's suffering is sufficient, so the belief requirement was always too strong; third the diagnosis that our responses are simply incoherent and the paradox is a fact about our irrationality rather than a puzzle with a solution. Each of these stanzas should be voiced sympathetically, as the view's proponent would want it voiced. The sixth stanza should register why each exit, in turn, fails to let the reader off the hook — the weeping continues, the knowledge persists, the principle still has its grip. The envoi, which by the form's own rules must gather all six end-words together, should let that formal necessity do the philosophical work: no resolution gets to discard any of the three propositions.

Two further requirements. First, accuracy: someone who has taught this literature should be able to identify which stanza voices which position without being told, and should find nothing in the voicings to wince at — the quasi-emotion stanza must not merely say "the feelings are fake," and the thought-theory stanza must not pretend the view claims the characters are real. Second, the poem should still work for my students as a poem: a reader who has never heard of the paradox should find a moving piece about mourning someone who never was, and only afterward notice that the argument was happening in the form itself.
