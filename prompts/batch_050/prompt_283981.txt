So I'm a software guy, no philosphy training, and my roommate (med student) and I got into it last night over this argument she half-endorses and I can't find the bug in, but the conclusion is clearly nuts. Lay it out:

(1) The only reason brain death counts as *death* — real death, the kind where you can take the organs even though the heart's still beating on a ventilator — is that the person has irreversibly lost whatever it is that makes them a living someone rather than a living something. Call it the capacity for consciousness, agency, experiencing stuff, whatever.

(2) If that capacity is irreversibly gone, the person is dead. Full stop. Whether some tissue is still metabolizing is irrelevant — a corpse on a heart-lung machine is still a corpse.

(3) Patients in a diagnosed permanent vegetative state have irreversibly lost exactly that capacity. That's what the diagnosis means — the higher brain stuff is gone for good, it's not coming back.

(4) So permanent vegetative state patients are dead, in the same sense brain-dead patients are dead.

(5) Dead bodies can be buried, and their organs can be harvested with prior consent.

(6) So we could bury a PVS patient, or harvest their organs, with exactly the same ethical clearance we have for brain-dead donors.

Now, (6) seems monstrous to me, and my roommate agrees, but she says she can't find a principled place to stop that doesn't also wreck organ donation from brain-dead donors, which she very much wants to keep. I'd also like to keep brain death being real death if possible — the alternative where it's just a legal fiction seems worse.

So: which premise do I give up? For the one you pick, explain why it looked obviously true, and then tell me what it costs to reject each of the *other* candidates instead — like, if I instead deny (1) or (3), what am I now committed to? Feel free to poke at whether "irreversible" and "capacity" are doing sneaky double duty somewhere, that's my main suspicion but I can't pin it down.
