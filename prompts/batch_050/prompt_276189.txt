In aesthetics and philosophy of mind, the puzzle of imaginative resistance has been on the table for decades without anything like a consensus solution. Roughly: competent readers slide smoothly along with all manner of wild fictional stipulations — time travel, magic, worlds where people fly — but balk when a story flatly stipulates that, in the fiction, cruelty is virtuous, or that murdering a child for amusement is morally right. We seem unable, or unwilling, to take these up in the same spirit in which we take up a flying man. The literature divides, roughly, into camps: those who hold that we genuinely cannot imagine morally deviant worlds, because something about the structure of imagination blocks it; those who hold that we can but simply decline to, so that the phenomenon is refusal rather than incapacity; and those who hold that the phenomenon is real but has been misclassified — that it is really about disbelief, about genre conventions, about our reluctance to grant an author authority over the moral facts of her own story, or about something else again.

What strikes me is that after all these years the camps cite the same handful of cases and trade the same objections, and nobody moves. So I am not asking for another solution to the puzzle; I am asking for a diagnosis of the impasse. Is this a substantive disagreement about the architecture of the mind — about whether imagining is belief-like enough to inherit morality's constraints — or a largely verbal disagreement in which the parties mean different things by imagining: supposing for the sake of argument, conceiving, taking the fiction's word for it, engaging with emotional uptake? Or is it a dispute about what counts as data — perhaps the parties have genuinely different reactions to the same stories and each generalizes from its own response? Work from what each camp actually argues, and given your diagnosis of the kind of disagreement this is, tell me what would count as progress: a psychological experiment on readers' responses, a sharper taxonomy of imaginative attitudes, better-designed test cases, or an argument that the puzzle should be dissolved rather than solved. And if part of your diagnosis is that no resolution should be expected — because the phenomenon is heterogeneous, or because the intuitions at the bottom are bedrock — argue for that conclusion as well.
