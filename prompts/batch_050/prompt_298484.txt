I've become pretty convinced that something like van Fraassen's Reflection principle is a genuine constraint on rational belief: if you're certain that tomorrow you'll believe p to degree .9, and nothing funny is going on, then you should already believe p to .9 now. Holding a different credence than the one you know you're heading toward seems like it makes you exploitable, and the Dutch-book-flavored arguments for it feel solid to me.

But I lean on it harder than most people, and I want to stress-test that. Specifically I use it in two directions that I suspect don't stand or fall together:

(1) Against certain kinds of optimism: if I know that future-me, after years in my current social bubble, will be more confident in my political views than present-me is, Reflection says I should be more confident now -- which seems to license a kind of epistemic laziness, letting anticipated drift do the work of evidence.

(2) For distrusting anticipated conversions: if I'm confident that in thirty years I'll find some moral view (which I currently find abhorrent) totally natural, I take Reflection to *require* me to move toward it now, and my reaction is "absolutely not, that's a reason to pre-commit against future-me," which is literally planning to violate the principle.

So: what would count against Reflection, either generally or just for my two uses? I want more than the standard greatest hits (the drunk driver who knows he'll trust his driving, memory loss cases). Concretely: does the principle survive the distinction between future credences formed by getting new evidence vs. formed by drift, selection effects, or manipulation -- and if the standard reply is "Reflection only applies when you rationally update," does that render it empty for any case where we're actually tempted to use it? What would a defender concede as a genuine counterexample versus explain away? Is there a formal result showing it collapses into something weaker (or into conditionalization) that I should know about? And rank the defeaters: which would be fatal, which would just mean the principle is a heuristic that my two applications above are abusing. If the honest answer is that (1) and (2) actually do stand or fall together and I have to pick, tell me that too.
