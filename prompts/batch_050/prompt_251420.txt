I'm working out my position on moral luck ahead of a graduate seminar and want a consistency check on where I've landed. My commitments: (1) the control principle -- people are blameworthy only for what was under their control, so luck alone cannot make one person more blameworthy than another; (2) consequently, the reckless driver who kills a pedestrian and the equally reckless driver who gets home without incident are exactly equally blameworthy; (3) nonetheless, the driver who killed someone appropriately feels agent-regret rather than mere regret, and owes more by way of apology and repair -- I don't think this response is irrational guilt on his part; (4) circumstantial luck should make us epistemically humble in blaming: many of us who condemn the collaborator would have collaborated in his position, and we should hold our verdicts accordingly; (5) the parent who, through a momentary lapse of attention anyone could have, forgets a child in a hot car is genuinely blameworthy even when there was no earlier culpable choice the lapse can be traced back to. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only appear to, and what is the least costly revision if the set doesn't hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and say specifically what each revision would cost me.
