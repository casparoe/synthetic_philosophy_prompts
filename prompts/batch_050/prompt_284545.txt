The topic is Marx on machinery, and here is my half-formed idea. There's a well-known apparent tension between the Grundrisse and Capital. In the famous passage on automatic machinery in the Grundrisse, Marx suggests that once production comes to rest on fully developed machinery and the "general intellect," the direct labor time expended in production ceases to be the measure of wealth, and production based on exchange value thereby collapses. In the machinery chapter of Capital, by contrast, machines merely transfer their own value to the product and create none, and capital's drive to mechanize appears as one tendency among others within an intact value regime. The literature, as I understand it, splits into roughly three readings: the Grundrisse passage is a speculative excess the mature Marx abandoned; it describes a tendency fully consistent with Capital; or it has been realized in contemporary cognitive or immaterial capitalism, so that value is now effectively immeasurable.

My suspicion is that all three readings share an assumption that ought to be examined rather than inherited: they treat Marx's claim about measure as an empirical prediction about whether economies can in fact still quantify wealth in labor time. My alternative is that the claim is criterial rather than predictive. Value requires labor time to be imposed as a social measure, and automation does not abolish labor time so much as render its continued imposition as the measure progressively arbitrary — so that capital must enforce it politically, through the length and intensity of the working day, through unemployment as a discipline, and so on, at precisely the rate at which it technologically undermines it. The research question is whether a precise sense of arbitrariness can be specified: one that does genuine critical work, is distinct from the tendential fall of the rate of profit, and does not depend on any empirical collapse thesis.

Please turn this into a proper proposal.
