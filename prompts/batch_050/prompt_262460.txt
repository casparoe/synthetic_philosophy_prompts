I teach a course on non-consequentialist ethics, and each year I close the aggregation unit with Taurek's argument in "Should the Numbers Count?" that when I must choose between saving one stranger and saving five others, morality does not require me to save the five merely because they are more numerous, since no single person in the larger group stands to lose more than the one, and a fair coin toss respects each person's claim equally. This year I want to anchor the lecture in a real episode rather than the usual lifeboat fictions, and I have settled on the ICU triage debates of the early COVID-19 pandemic. Here are the salient facts as I intend to present them, so take them as given for the purposes of your analysis: in March 2020, Italian intensive care societies issued guidance suggesting that when demand for ICU beds and ventilators exceeded supply, clinicians should prioritize patients with the best prognosis and greatest expected life-years, sometimes invoking age-based cutoffs; in the United States, most states' crisis standards of care directed hospitals to allocate scarce ventilators by sequential organ failure scores and short-term survival probability, with lotteries or first-come-first-served as tie-breakers; and disability advocates filed complaints arguing that some protocols discriminated by deprioritizing patients with pre-existing conditions. Please analyze this episode through the lens of Taurek's position.

I would like the analysis to run in both directions, and to be disciplined about the mapping: who, on Taurek's view, are the claimants in an overwhelmed ICU, what is each of them owed, and does a triage protocol that maximizes lives saved (or life-years saved) amount to precisely the aggregation Taurek rejects, or is it doing something else, such as comparing prospects of benefit rather than counting heads? Consider whether Taurek's coin toss has any real analogue in the episode — the lottery tie-breakers come to mind, as does the widespread public discomfort with using lotteries between unequal-sized groups — and ask what he would make of prognosis-based allocation, which seems neither to count numbers nor to treat each claimant symmetrically. Then reverse the direction: does the episode confirm Taurek, embarrass him, or expose a case his framework never anticipated? I am especially interested in whether the near-universal instinct among clinicians and bioethicists that saving more lives is better than saving fewer, other things equal, constitutes evidence against the view or merely restates what it denies, and whether the disability discrimination controversies reveal anything about what Taurek's equal-chance proposal would require in practice.

Where the theory is simply silent — on scarcity created by institutional failure rather than nature, on the role of clinicians versus policymakers, on uncertainty about survival probabilities — say so rather than forcing it to speak. Please write your response as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
