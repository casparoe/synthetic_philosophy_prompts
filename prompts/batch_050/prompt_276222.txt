ok so I'm in this value theory class and last week we did discounting and pure time preference — the idea that it's irrational to care less about some benefit just because it comes later in your life. My friend thinks she has a knockdown argument that pure time preference is irrational, but I've been staring at it and I think it proves way too much, because the same argument seems to also "prove" something that's obviously false. We wrote it up like this:

(1) If two experiences would be exactly alike in every intrinsic respect, it can't be rational to prefer one over the other solely because of when it occurs. A mere date is not a reason.
(2) Pure time preference is preferring a good thing earlier and a bad thing later purely because of the timing, holding everything else fixed.
(3) So pure time preference is irrational.
(4) But whether an experience is past or future relative to you is also just a fact about when it occurs. So by (1), it can't be rational to prefer that your pains be in the past and your pleasures in the future, holding the experiences themselves fixed.
(5) Future bias is obviously rational though. There's this case from lecture: you wake up in a hospital bed with amnesia and the nurse tells you that either you already had a three-hour painful operation yesterday, or you have a thirty-minute one coming up later today. Everyone hopes it's the yesterday one — even though yesterday's version was six times longer. If (4) is right, that preference is irrational, which seems crazy.
(6) So (1) has to be false, and the whole standard case against discounting the future falls apart.

I feel like something is getting slipped in between (1) and (4) but I can't name it. Like, "Tuesday vs. Thursday" seems like a mere date in a way that "already happened vs. hasn't happened yet" maybe isn't? But I can't say what the relevant difference is without it sounding ad hoc. And I also worry the fix cuts both ways: if past/future isn't mere timing, then isn't "now vs. next year" also not mere timing, since next year is future and will someday be past? So maybe saving future bias infects the anti-discounting argument anyway.

For what it's worth, I'd rather keep future bias and give up the anti-time-preference conclusion than the other way around, even though I basically believe discounting is irrational. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? And if you think (1) survives in some restricted form, can that restricted version still do the work against pure time preference, or does it definately collapse into something question-begging?
