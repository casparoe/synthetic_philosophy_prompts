My sister and I have run a small charitable giving pool together for years — each December we each put in the same amount and split the total between our two favorite causes. She directs her half to a political advocacy organization; I direct mine to a public health charity. This year she made me a proposal that I can't stop turning over. She pointed out that I think her organization is not merely ineffective but actively harmful — I believe its policy goals, if achieved, would make people worse off — while she thinks my charity's work is fine but trivial compared to hers. Her suggestion: she'll redirect half of her usual contribution to my health charity if I redirect half of mine to her advocacy group. She frames this as obviously good for both of us: by her lights, more money flows to the cause that matters enormously; by mine, less money flows to the cause I consider harmful, and more to the one I consider genuinely good. On paper, each of us seems to end up better off according to our own values.

And yet something about it makes me deeply uneasy, and I want help articulating what that unease is and whether it's justified. Part of it is that I'd be writing a check, with my own name on it, to an organization I regard as a force for bad outcomes — the fact that the total amount they receive shrinks doesn't change that I'm the one funding part of it. Is that a real moral objection or just an aversion to having my hands dirty? Part of it is that I can't fully rule out that her values are right and mine are wrong — and if I took that possibility seriously, would that strengthen or weaken the case for the deal? I also wonder whether the arrangement only works because we happen to contribute equal amounts: if she were far wealthier than me, would the same logic license a trade in which my modest leverage ends up amplifying her agenda far beyond anything I could counterbalance?

Please advise me on whether to accept, but don't just give me generic thoughts about compromise. I want to understand the underlying structure: under what conditions deals of this form are genuinely good, which features of our situation are doing the moral work, and what differences — if my objections are weightier than hers, if the causes are incommensurable, if I simply distrust her to hold up her end in future years — would flip the answer.
