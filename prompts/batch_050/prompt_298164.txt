I chair the science review panel of a regional education authority in England, and we must decide whether to approve three shortlisted biology textbooks for use in the final two years of secondary school. All three lean heavily on code and information language: DNA is variously described as a code, a set of instructions, and, in one book, a program that runs during development. Our standing style guidance says that instructional language must be literal where possible and must not attribute purposes or intentions to natural processes, and the panel has split over whether these books violate it. A molecular biologist on the panel insists that "genetic code" is a technical term rather than a metaphor: the mapping from codons to amino acids is a genuine code precisely because the assignments are arbitrary and could have evolved otherwise, and striking the phrase would both misrepresent the science and leave students unprepared for national examinations, which use it freely. Another member, trained in philosophy, replies that arbitrariness makes a cipher, not a meaning: nothing reads DNA the way a reader reads a sentence, so talk of genes "containing instructions" for traits quietly licenses the deterministic "gene for X" reasoning we spend half the course trying to undo. Complicating matters, one publisher has preemptively replaced "instructions" with "recipe," which both members dislike for opposite reasons, and all three publishers warn that substantive revisions at this stage would delay adoption by a full year, with one threatening to withdraw its book entirely. Draft the panel's decision memo: approve the books, approve them subject to specified revisions, or reject them, stating the recommendation in the opening paragraph. The memo should distinguish, and treat separately, three claims the textbooks run together: that the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is a code in the cipher sense, that genes carry information about traits in a stronger semantic sense, and that the genome amounts to a blueprint or program for the whole organism. It should give criteria we can apply to future submissions, address the examination-alignment problem honestly rather than wishing it away, and engage the strongest argument of whichever side loses. Please write the memo in British English.
