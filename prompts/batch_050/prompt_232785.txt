At a philosophy society meeting last week, a graduate student gave me a brisk summary of the debate over the identity of indiscernibles, and I want to know how much of it to trust. Her version ran as follows. Leibniz's Law states that if two things share all their properties, they are in fact one and the same thing. Max Black refuted this principle with a famous thought experiment: imagine a universe containing nothing except two perfectly identical iron spheres, alike in every intrinsic and relational respect. Since we can describe such a universe without contradiction, and since the spheres are obviously two rather than one, the principle is simply false. She added that this also undermines Leibniz's theological motivation for the principle — his claim that God would have no sufficient reason to create two perfectly alike things — and she closed by saying that modern metaphysicians therefore treat the principle as a historical curiosity rather than a live position.

Something in this felt too quick to me, but I cannot locate the problem. Is her summary fair? In particular: does she state the principle itself correctly, or has she run together two claims that defenders of the view keep apart? Is the two-sphere scenario as uncontroversially coherent as she assumes, and does granting its coherence settle the matter, or are there moves — about which properties count, about what the description smuggles in, about how the principle should be formulated — that a defender would make? Is 'obviously two' an observation or the very point at issue? And finally, does her sociological closing claim about the principle's current status follow from the argument she gave, or could the argument succeed while that claim fails (or vice versa)? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible but contested step presented as settled, and what a careful defender of the principle would flatly reject.
