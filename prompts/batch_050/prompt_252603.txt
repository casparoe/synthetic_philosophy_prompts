So this started in the comments of one of those eternal Allais threads and then spilled over into my local meetup, where a friend has been pushing a line on me that I can't find the hole in even though the conclusion is obviously insane. Some context: I've gotten pretty sympathetic to risk-weighted expected utility — the Buchak-style idea that rational agents can weight outcomes by how they rank within a gamble, with worse outcomes getting extra weight, rather than just taking plain expectations. It seemed like the cleanest way to make Allais-style risk aversion respectable instead of treating it as a bias to be debiased away. My friend thinks the whole framework self-destructs, and his argument goes like this:

(1) It can be rational to prefer a guaranteed $450 to a 50/50 shot at $1,000 or nothing. Ordinary risk aversion isn't always a cognitive error.
(2) The principled way to permit (1) is risk-weighting: in deliberation, worse-ranked outcomes get more weight than their raw probability would assign them, and better-ranked ones get less.
(3) A principle of rationality can't apply selectively. Whatever makes extra weight on the worst case rational in one decision makes it rational in every decision you face.
(4) Daily life is just a giant stack of small favorable gambles, each with a catastrophic worst case somewhere in the support — driving to work, eating food you didn't prepare, investing instead of stuffing cash in a mattress, getting on a plane, going to the new restaraunt.
(5) So a rational agent puts extra weight on the worst case in each of these and declines nearly all of them: never drives, never flies, never invests, never tries anything new.
(6) But nobody is rationally required to live like that. A decision theory that demands total paralysis has gone wrong somewhere.
(7) Therefore (2) is false: there is no principled way to weight worse outcomes more. Either plain expected utility is the whole story and Allais preferences are flat-out irrational, or rationality is unprincipled vibes all the way down.

He says this shows risk-weighting proves too much and collapses back to expected utility. My gut says something slipped between (3) and (5), because (2) only ever promised more weight on bad outcomes, not decisive weight — but when I push on that he says any nonzero distortion, applied across the thousands of little gambles that make up a life, adds up to paralysis anyway, and I don't have a crisp answer to that. So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1), (2), and (6) — I want Allais to be rational and I want to be allowed to drive — so tell me what I have to give up to keep them, and whether the EU-purist bullet (Allais was just wrong) is actually cheaper than it looks.
