I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in twentieth-century critical theory. Below is a question from the final exam, followed by a student's answer. The question refers to the following passage from the chapter "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" in Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944/47):

"The step from the telephone to the radio has clearly distinguished the roles. The former still allowed the subscriber to play the role of subject, and was liberal. The latter is democratic: it turns all participants into listeners and authoritatively subjects them to broadcast programs which are all exactly the same. No machinery of rejoinder has been devised, and private broadcasters are denied any freedom. They are confined to the apocryphal field of the 'amateur,' and also have to accept organisation from above."

Exam question: (a) Explain what Adorno and Horkheimer take the contrast between the telephone and the radio to show about the position of the individual under the culture industry. In particular, explain why they call the telephone "liberal" and the radio "democratic," and what the absence of a "machinery of rejoinder" contributes to their broader argument. (b) Some critics argue that interactive media restore the position of the subject that one-way broadcasting abolished. Using Adorno and Horkheimer's own concepts, give the strongest version of their likely reply, and briefly state whether you find it convincing.

Student's answer:

Adorno and Horkheimer's contrast between the telephone and the radio illustrates their thesis that mass communication eliminates the position of the subject. The telephone is "liberal" because it preserves two-way exchange: the subscriber can still answer back, and so appears as a subject within the conversation. The radio is "democratic" in an ironic sense: it equalizes everyone, but only by reducing everyone to a listener. Participation is universal, yet participation now means mere reception. The missing "machinery of rejoinder" is the crux: the listener cannot respond, and because the programs are all exactly the same, switching stations changes nothing. This supports their claim that the culture industry standardizes everything it touches, since broadcast content delivers a single uniform political message, and apparent variety conceals enforced sameness. The deeper point of the contrast is that the captains of the media deliberately designed one-way broadcasting in order to keep the masses docile and incapable of critical reply. As for interactive media, they seem to restore the rejoinder the radio abolished: users post, comment, and choose rather than merely listen. But Adorno and Horkheimer's likely reply is that such interactivity extends pseudo-individuality rather than overcoming it: the choices offered are choices among pre-structured options inside a standardized system, so the user's apparent activity is administered in advance, and clicking and commenting become further forms of consumption rather than the recovery of subjectivity. I find this reply largely convincing, since most online interaction consists of selecting among options designed by others.
