Write a dialogue between two old university friends, Priya and Tom, who've fallen out over something odd: not a moral disagreement, exactly, but a disagreement about how you're allowed to arrive at your morals. The background is that Priya has recently stopped eating meat and started donating ten percent of her income, and when Tom asked her what changed her mind, she admitted -- cheerfully, unbothered -- that she hasn't actually worked through any of the arguments herself. She just trusts her friend Nadia, who did a philosophy PhD and who Priya describes as the most thoughtful and clear-eyed person she knows. If Nadia says factory farming is a moral catastrophe and that most of us give far too little, that's good enough for Priya, the same way her dentist's word is good enough on flossing. Tom is genuinely scandalised by this, not because he disagrees with the conclusions (he's agnostic on both) but because he thinks there's something fishy, maybe even corrupting, about taking your moral beliefs on trust rather than seeing for yourself why they're true.

Have them actually argue it out over an evening, with each responding to what the other just said rather than delivering little speeches. Tom should press the idea that morality is different from dentistry: that if you can't give the reasons yourself, you don't really believe the thing in the right way, you're just conforming, and that this matters even if the beliefs happen to be correct. Priya should push back hard with the strongest version of her side -- that we defer constantly and unreflectively on matters of fact, that understanding the arguments isn't obviously required for a belief to be genuinely yours, and that Tom's demand that everyone re-derive morality from scratch is both impossible and a bit precious coming from someone whose own views are a mishmash of things he absorbed from his parents and the internet. Let them tangle with some real cases: is there a difference between deferring about whether eating meat is wrong and deferring about whether you should lie to a friend who's asked you a painful question? Does it matter that Priya acts on her borrowed beliefs (real sacrifices, real money) rather than just holding them? What happens to moral progress if nobody is allowed to take anyone's word for anything?

Neither of them should be a pushover or a caricature -- Priya isn't lazy and Tom isn't a snob, they're both making the best case they can. It should feel like a real conversation between two people who like each other and are getting slightly more heated than they expected. Somewhere in the middle, have them stumble onto the question of whether the disagreement is even about truth at all, or about what kind of person you're being when you act on a conviction you couldn't defend in an argument. You don't have to make them reach agreement, but by the end they should at least have located exactly where their disagreement lives.
