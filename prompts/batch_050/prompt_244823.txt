Last week at dinner my sister asked me why I love my girlfriend, and I said something like "because she's funny, and kind, and she actually gets me," and she immediately came back with: so if you met someone funnier and kinder who got you even better, you'd have reason to trade up? I had nothing. It's been bugging me since, and a friend pointed me to the appraisal vs. bestowal debate in the philosophy of love (Frankfurt, Velleman, Kolodny, that whole cluster), but everything I've skimmed just lists positions without telling me which question is the one that actually forks you into one camp or another.

So build me the decision tree I wish I'd had. Start from someone who takes it for granted that they have *some* reason to love their partner but has never thought about where that reason comes from, and end at named positions. I want roughly six to eight branch nodes, and every node has to be a concrete case you can answer without knowing the literature — no "do you endorse bestowal?" nonsense. Work these cases in somewhere, since they're the ones that seem diagnostic to me: the trading-up case (someone strictly better on every dimension shows up); the qualitative twin case (millions of strangers share your partner's entire profile of good qualities — does your reason reach them too?); the amnesia case (your shared history is wiped from both of you but the qualities remain — does the reason survive?); and the change case (she gradually becomes selfish and cold — does the reason fade, vanish, or hold?). Make sure the leaves cover at least: pure quality/appraisal views, love-creates-its-own-reasons bestowal views, relationship-history views, at least one quality-plus-history hybrid, and a "love is arational, stop asking for reasons" exit. Attach the standard names to leaves where they genuinely belong, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually pull apart, and if there are combinations of answers nobody seems to occupy, say whether that's because the spot is incoherent, just unattractive, or weirdly unexplored.

Then, at the end, run me down the tree: tell me which leaf my dinner-table answer ("she's funny and kind and gets me") commits me to whether I like it or not, and which assumption my sister's comeback was smuggling in. Be honest if my answer was just philosophically naive.
