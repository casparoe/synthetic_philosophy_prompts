I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. My team recently shipped an internal tool that aggregates ranked ballots to make collective decisions — currently used for things like prioritizing the product roadmap and choosing among candidate architectures. While testing it on real data (about 40 voters, 5–8 options per decision), we hit what I eventually learned is a classic majority cycle: option A beat B head-to-head, B beat C, and C beat A, on ballots that all looked individually reasonable. Reading up on this led me to Arrow's impossibility theorem, which as I understand it says that no ranked-ballot aggregation rule over three or more options can simultaneously satisfy a short list of plausible conditions — roughly, that it works for any possible set of ballots, respects unanimous preferences, makes the relative ranking of two options depend only on how voters rank those two against each other, and doesn't amount to a dictatorship.

My question: give me your credence that this result should function as a binding design constraint for small-to-medium-scale collective decision systems (engineering teams, committees, product prioritization — explicitly not national elections), rather than a formal curiosity that essentially never bites in practice. By "binding" I mean: occurs often enough, with consequences bad enough, that a competent designer must consciously choose which desideratum to sacrifice rather than picking a rule on performance and usability grounds. I want a single committed number, not a survey of positions. My own credence is about 0.25 — I suspect our cycle was a fluke of correlated preferences in a small team — and I'd like to know whether I'm wrong.

Please derive your number by decomposition, with a rough probability and one or two lines of justification for each term:

1. The probability that realistic preference profiles — ballots from actual humans on actual decisions, not adversarially constructed examples — contain majority cycles or near-cycles often enough to matter, say in more than 5% of decisions involving five or more options.
2. The probability that the standard escape routes fail on realistic domains: that real preferences do not reliably exhibit the structural regularities (such as being single-peaked along some natural dimension) under which well-behaved aggregation is possible.
3. The probability that the fairness conditions are jointly non-negotiable in practice: that whichever condition a shipped system relaxes, the relaxation produces outcomes users will correctly perceive as arbitrary or unfair, rather than as acceptable quirks.
4. The probability that impossibility considerations add real constraint beyond what strategic-manipulation concerns already impose on any non-trivial rule — i.e., that cycling-type failures are a distinct design problem rather than a footnote to gaming.
5. Any further term you think the credence genuinely depends on that I've missed.

State how the terms combine (independence assumptions, weakest-link logic, whatever you actually use), then give the final number. Tell me which single sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and what empirical evidence or argument would move that term most. If you think my decomposition double-counts or misses the real crux, restructure it before answering — but still end with a committed number, and briefly justify why it isn't lower (if you conclude this rarely matters) or higher (if you conclude it's a genuine constraint).
