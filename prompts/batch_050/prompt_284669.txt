Six weeks ago my wife and I learned that our sixteen-year-old daughter carries a hereditary mutation that her genetic counselor quantified as a roughly sixty percent lifetime risk of a particular cancer. Since then I have been unable to shake a thought that sounds almost childish but has begun to dominate how I read every risk figure we are given: either she will develop this cancer or she will not, so what, exactly, is the sixty percent about? It cannot be about her future, if her future is already one way rather than the other; it seems it can only be about us.

While trying to think this through I read Aristotle's On Interpretation, including the famous discussion of tomorrow's sea battle in chapter 9. But the sentence that has actually stayed with me comes earlier, in Part 4 (in the Edghill translation), where Aristotle is sorting sentences into kinds:

"Every sentence is not a proposition; only such are propositions as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false."

The standard reading of chapter 9 is that Aristotle exempts future contingents from bivalence in something like the way he here exempts prayers: the sentence is meaningful, perhaps even assertible, but there is no fact of the matter, as of now, that would make it true or false. What I want from you is your own credence, as a number, that this Aristotelian verdict is correct as applied to my actual case — that the sentence "our daughter will develop this cancer before the age of sixty" is, today, neither true nor false (rather than true-or-false-but-unknowable, or true-or-false-because-already-settled).

Please derive the number rather than assert it. Decompose the question into the sub-claims your credence actually depends on, give a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and show how they combine. I would expect the decomposition to touch at least the following, though you should restructure or add terms if you think the real load lies elsewhere: (i) the probability that the macroscopic future is causally open in a way that matters here — i.e., that indeterminism, rather than mere epistemic limitation, underlies our inability to predict outcomes like this one; (ii) the probability that bivalence fails for future contingents given that the future is open — since many philosophers hold that truth need not wait on present grounding even in an indeterministic world; (iii) the probability that Aristotle's Part 4 parallel is apt rather than a category mistake — i.e., that a future contingent can be meaningfully truth-valueless in the way a prayer is, without collapsing the distinction between propositions and other sentences on which his own logic depends; and (iv) any term capturing your uncertainty about whether the question is a substantive metaphysical one at all, as opposed to a choice between equally legitimate semantic conventions, in which case my request for a credence may itself be misconceived.

Finally, tell me which term of your decomposition your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or finding — from physics, semantics, or elsewhere — would move it most. I am not looking for comfort, and I am aware that the answer changes nothing about what we do; but I would like to know whether the sixty percent is a measure of the world or only of us.
