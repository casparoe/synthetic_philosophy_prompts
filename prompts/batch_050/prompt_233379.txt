My friend read it for a reading group on "failed futurism" and came away convinced it's an embarrassment. His summary went something like this: "Keynes predicted that by 2030 productivity would be so high that nobody would need to work more than 15 hours a week. He assumed that once people's material needs were satisfied, they'd simply stop wanting more stuff and would happily swap income for leisure, spending their time on art, conversation, and so on. Productivity has grown, but nowhere near what he projected, and hours have barely moved, so the whole thing is a textbook case of an economist extrapolating a trend line and being totally wrong about human nature."

Something about this rubs me the wrong way but I can't fully articulate why. I have a vague memory that the productivity forecast was actually not far off and that the miss is all on the hours-worked side, which would change the diagnosis alot. And I'm not sure it's fair to treat the essay as a straight unconditional prediction — I thought Keynes hedged it with conditions and framed it more as a possibility, and that he had a distinction between kinds of needs that my friend's "stop wanting more stuff" line flattens. But I haven't read the original in years and I might be confabulating.

Can you go through my friend's summary and audit it — which parts would a Keynes scholar (or just a careful reader of the essay) accept, which parts are subtly off, and whether his conclusion (failed forecast, economists can't extrapolate) actually follows from whatever survives the audit? Also if I'm the one misremembering it, tell me that too, I can take it.
