I am co-teaching a graduate reading group in philosophy of biology next term, and we keep stalling in the same place: every paper on biological individuality -- on holobionts, eusocial colonies, siphonophores, clonal aspens, lichens, the human microbiome -- presupposes a different standard for what counts as an organism, and the discussion degenerates into trading intuitions about favorite cases. I got here via Julian Huxley's 1912 monograph The Individual in the Animal Kingdom, whose central move, as I read it, is to insist that individuality in animals is not one property but several, and that each comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing; a siphonophore colony and a dog are not rival answers to the same question so much as different profiles along partly independent dimensions. I don't want an interpretation of Huxley himself so much as an updated version of his move: before we read any contemporary theory, I want the specification written first.

So write, in American English, the desiderata for an adequate theory of biological individuality -- the requirements such a theory must meet to count as adequate for the purpose of a working philosophy of biology that has to serve both evolutionary theorists and physiologists. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: which problem cases it has to classify in some principled way (I would expect at minimum: the Portuguese man o' war and other colonial siphonophores; a eusocial ant or termite colony; Pando and other clonal plant stands; the cellular slime mold life cycle, where scattered amoebae aggregate into a fruiting slug; lichens and other obligate symbioses; a mammal plus its gut microbiome; chimeric organisms such as fusion marmosets; and, as control cases, a tumor and a transplanted organ); which distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse (for instance, physiological or metabolic integration versus evolutionary individuality as a unit of selection; being an organism versus being a Darwinian individual; continuous spatial boundedness versus functional unity; and the difference between an individual that fails a criterion and a criterion that fails an individual); and which established results in biology it must not contradict. For each item, say why it is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one camp, and mark whether you take it to be negotiable or non-negotiable.

Then do three further things. First, identify any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- for example, whether a reproductive-autonomy requirement can coexist with a requirement that handles obligate symbiosis, or whether a criterion strict enough to exclude the microbiome case is bound to exclude things everyone calls organisms -- and if so, treat that conflict itself as a finding and say which horn a theorist should grasp. Second, say which single requirement eliminates the most popular candidates, and which popular demand (I suspect something like the germ-soma bottleneck requirement) is actually doing less work than its proponents claim. Third, briefly and secondarily, score three or four of the main contender families against your list -- metabolic/physiological accounts, evolutionary accounts in the lineage of Godfrey-Smith's Darwinian-populations framework, immunological accounts of the sort associated with Thomas Pradeu, and Huxley-style pluralism -- but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, since the criteria are the deliverable and the scoring only leans on them. A table for the final scoring is fine.
