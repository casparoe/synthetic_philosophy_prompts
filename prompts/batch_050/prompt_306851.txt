Last weekend a friend and I argued past midnight about whether it can ever be rational to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. She invoked Hofstadter's old notion of superrationality; I defended it; and by the end we had traded enough cases that I can no longer tell whether my own judgments form a coherent position or just a mood. I would like you to do reflective equilibrium on my verdicts: extract the principle, if there is one, and tell me honestly what it costs.

Here are my verdicts, as near as I can state them.

1. Against an exact replica of myself — same dispositions, same reasoning, same inputs — I cooperate, and I don't think this is sentimental. Whatever conclusion I reach, it reaches, so the real choice is between mutual cooperation and mutual defection.
2. Against a human stranger, one-shot, anonymous, no future interaction: I defect, and I would call cooperation here a mistake, not a virtue.
3. Against an agent whose reasoning is known to match mine about 80% of the time on problems like this one: I cooperate, but uneasily, and I want to know how the 80% figure was established before I'd stake anything on it.
4. In Newcomb's problem with a predictor of demonstrated high reliability, I take one box, and I regard two-boxing as leaving money on the table.
5. I vote in national elections and consider it rational, while granting that my ballot's chance of being pivotal is negligible — because people in my situation, reasoning as I do, tend to turn out or stay home together.
6. When my friend says I shouldn't cut across the lawn because of what would happen if everyone did it, I find the argument hollow. If I refrain, it's because I don't want to ruin the grass, not because of any generalization about everyone.

Two of these sit poorly together, I realize: case 2 and case 5 seem to turn on the same kind of correlation I exploit in one and dismiss in the other, and case 6 rejects outright the form of reasoning case 5 relies on. So: what decision-theoretic principle, stated precisely rather than by label, captures as many of my verdicts as possible? For each clause of your principle, name the case that forces it. Then test it on cases I haven't considered: a replica identical to me except for one arbitrarily small difference; a collective dilemma with ten thousand agents running my exact reasoning; an agent that will mirror whatever it first observes me do; and a replica of my friend rather than of me. Finally, tell me which of my six verdicts the simplest workable principle must abandon, whether any non-arbitrary line can be drawn between case 3 and case 2, and whether my friend's parting shot — that my pattern is just cooperate when it feels warm, defect when it doesn't — is fair.
