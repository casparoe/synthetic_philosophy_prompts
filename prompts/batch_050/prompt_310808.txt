Exam question: State what Gödel's two incompleteness theorems establish, and explain what they do not establish, with particular attention to the philosophical conclusions that are commonly but mistakenly drawn from them outside mathematics.

Student's answer: Gödel's theorems concern formal systems capable of expressing elementary arithmetic. The first theorem says that any such system, if consistent, is incomplete: Gödel constructs a sentence G which in effect says of itself that it is not provable in the system. If the system is consistent, G is true but unprovable in it. So there are arithmetical truths that no formal system can establish: G is absolutely unprovable, and even adding G as a new axiom only yields a stronger system with its own true-but-unprovable sentence. The second theorem says that no consistent system can prove its own consistency, which dealt a decisive blow to Hilbert's program of securing mathematics by finitary consistency proofs, at least in that program's original form.

Outside mathematics the theorems are routinely overextended. They do not show that there are truths science can never discover in some general sense, still less anything about God or the limits of human knowledge as such; they concern provability within formal systems, not knowability in general. Gödel did, however, prove as a direct corollary of his theorems that the human mind is not a machine: we can see that G is true, whereas the machine — the formal system — cannot prove it, so no machine can capture human mathematical reasoning. This is one of the few rigorously established consequences of the theorems outside logic itself.
