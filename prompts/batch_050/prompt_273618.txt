I help run a debating society where, over the past year, I've watched well-informed and sincere members repeatedly end factual discussions by agreeing to disagree -- which has made me want to confront them with Aumann's agreement theorem: the claim that rational agents who share prior beliefs and honestly exchange their posteriors cannot persistently disagree. I have one 75-minute workshop with about 16 participants, no math beyond basic probability. Design an in-class activity in which the participants themselves feel the pressure toward convergence before the theorem is ever named: for instance, rounds of publicly announced estimates of some quantity, where each person holds a different piece of private evidence and the information structure is shared. Give me the full rules, what is private and what is public in the setup, a scoring scheme that rewards accuracy without real money, and the debrief mapping stages of the game onto the theorem's components -- common priors, common knowledge of rationality, iterative announcement of posteriors. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (anchoring on early announcements, participants failing to treat others' estimates as evidence, or premature convergence on a wrong answer) and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which assumption of the theorem the activity cannot actually reproduce in a room of real people, and how I should flag that gap in the debrief.
