Before I commit to a position in the debate over naturalizing intentionality, I want the adequacy conditions for a theory of mental content spelled out on their own, independent of any particular theory's prospects. Take the purpose to be fixed in advance: a theory of content good enough to underwrite the explanatory work content ascriptions actually do in cognitive science and ordinary psychology — prediction, explanation of behavior, and the assessment of states as accurate or inaccurate.

To anchor the request, here are two cases I would like the desiderata to bear on:

1. A snail lineage possesses a neural circuit that triggers withdrawal whenever the surrounding water exhibits a particular acidity-temperature profile. Ancestrally, only predator secretions produced that profile; after a climate shift, algae blooms produce it as well, and the snails now withdraw constantly. Did the circuit's content change when the environment changed, was it disjunctive all along, or was it never about predators in the first place?

2. Engineers evolve a robot controller through simulated selection over thousands of generations, with no designer input about what the robot should detect. The winning circuit steers the robot toward charging stations by firing at a shape-and-glow profile that, in the training environment, only charging stations exhibited. Do this circuit's states have content at all, and if so, is it charging station or something at the grain of the proximal profile?

Now give me the requirements list. For each requirement: state it precisely; give the case or consideration that motivates it (the two above, the classic frog-and-fly style problems, Swampman-style duplicates, or cases you construct yourself); and indicate which distinctions the requirement forbids a theory from collapsing, such as carrying information versus having content, a mechanism's function versus its actual operation, or failing to fire versus misfiring. For each requirement, also say whether it is genuinely non-negotiable given the stated purpose or a prejudice of one research camp — for instance, whether explaining the possibility of misrepresentation is truly mandatory in a way that vindicating intuitions about Swampman, or matching pre-theoretical verdicts about content grain, or remaining consistent with a thoroughgoing naturalism free of irreducible intentionality, arguably is not. Flag any pair of requirements that stand in tension, and say whether the full list looks jointly satisfiable; if it does not, that is itself a finding I want stated plainly. Then identify the single requirement that excludes the most live contenders. Only at the end, and briefly, score the main families of theories — covariational or informational accounts, teleological accounts, and functional or conceptual-role accounts — against the list, since my interest is in the standard rather than the standings.

Throughout, keep track of epistemic status: where something approximates a settled result among people who work on these questions, such as the inability of crude causal covariation by itself to explain error, mark it as settled; where a demand is contested between camps, present it as contested, and do not write as though one camp's wish list were the received view.
