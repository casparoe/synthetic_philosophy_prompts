I run a small podcast where we interview historians and political economists, and in a couple weeks we've got a guest whose new book defends what you could call the balance-sheet view of the British Raj: roughly, that whatever its crimes, British rule left India with institutions, infrastructure, and a unified state that more than paid for themselves, and that the nationalist ledger (Tharoor-style) systematically ignores the counterfactual. Think Ferguson rather than Tharoor, but our guy is an academic, not a polemicist, and he's careful.

Draft 8-10 interview questions. I dont want the softball version of this debate where he just gets to say "railways" and "the rule of law" and I nod. So:

- At least one question has to get at the counterfactual problem head-on: beneficial compared to *what*? A fragmented subcontinent of successor states, a Japan-style indigenous modernizer, continued Mughal decline? Press him on which counterfactual his balance sheet is actually measured against and whether that choice does all the work.
- At least one on the railways specifically: the guaranteed returns to British investors, the financing structure, whether a freight-and-troops network built on those terms counts as a gift or a very profitable loan.
- At least one on deindustrialization: India's falling share of world manufacturing output is about as settled as anything in this debate, but whether that was British policy or just what the industrial revolution would've done to any textile economy is not. Make him pick.
- At least one on the famines, including 1943, and whether "the Raj prevented worse famines" or "policy failures caused them" is even resolvable, plus the point people steal from Sen about democracies and famine.
- And one that challenges the whole balance-sheet framing: costs and benefits landing on different people, incommensurable harms, whose ledger are we even adding up.

For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so I'm not left flat-footed when he deflects into "that's the nationalist narrative."

One more thing: for each question, add a short note for me (not for air) separating what's basically settled among economic historians from what's genuinely contested -- I don't want to concede a premise on air that specialists actually fight over, or conversely challenge him on something that's solid ground.
