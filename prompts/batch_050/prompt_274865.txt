I am a novelist working on a political book in which the protagonist keeps insisting that history is not a lottery but a flood: those who build channels before the waters rise seem gifted, while those who merely possess good intentions are carried away. In trying to make this more than a slogan, I have provisionally accepted a reading of Machiavelli on which virtù is neither ordinary moral goodness nor mere ruthlessness, but a developed capacity to vary one’s mode of proceeding so that it fits the temper of the times, while fortuna names the standing mismatch between human plans and an order of events that rewards fit, speed and force rather than desert. Call this, for my own purposes, the hydraulic-fitness view: fortune is never wholly masterable, because circumstances change faster than character, but it is tractable at the edges by agents flexible enough to be lion and fox as required and disciplined enough to use cruelty, if at all, only as an instrument that can be stabilised afterwards. I am not asking for a bibliography or for settled textual scholarship; I want to know, philosophically, what would count against this reading if I built a novel around it.

Please map its exposure rather than simply attacking it. Which intuitions about agency, responsibility, necessity and political judgement would have to be rejected for the hydraulic-fitness view to survive; which familiar cases from The Prince and the Discourses would strain it; and which would embarrass rival readings more? I would like the possible defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: for example, evidence that Machiavelli sometimes treats virtù as intrinsically admirable even when it fails; passages or patterns suggesting that fortuna is caprice rather than a name for changing constraints; a collapse into the banal claim that success requires adaptability; or a tension between praising founders who impose form on matter and blaming later rulers who inherit forms they did not choose. Say what a committed defender would concede as genuinely damaging, what they would dismiss as moralising hindsight, and whether the view has been insulated by qualifications until it predicts almost nothing that the prudential, republican or amoral-power readings do not also predict. Since I am dramatising these ideas rather than writing a monograph, please end by stating how confident you are in the overall map and identifying the single point on which you are least sure.
