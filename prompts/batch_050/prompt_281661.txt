Ok so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of museum restitution cases, and I want to figure out what principle, if any, is actually generating them. The Benin Bronzes: the British army sacked Benin City in 1897 and carted them off, so obviously they go back, that's the easy one. Human remains taken from Indigenous graves by 19th century collectors: also give them back, no question, and honestly this one feels even stronger than the bronzes even though the remains have real scientific value. The Parthenon Marbles: I think they should go back to Athens, but I know Elgin supposedly had some kind of Ottoman permission, so it's murkier than the bronzes and yet my gut says return just as hard. A ceremonial mask bought in the 1930s from a community member who apparently had the standing to sell it, at a fair price, but the community now says it's sacred and wants it back: no, keep it, though I'd be fine with a long-term loan. The Rosetta Stone: taken from the French as war spoils under a treaty, and I find myself shrugging -- keep it, even though Egypt didn't consent either and I can't fully explain why this is different from Benin. A painting a Jewish family sold under duress in 1938 Germany: goes back, obviously, even to heirs who never saw it. Cuneiform tablets looted from Iraq after 2003 and bought in good faith by a museum: return, and the museum eats the loss. And finally, a dinosaur skeleton excavated with full permits from public land in Argentina and legally exported a century ago: keep, no issue.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these, or close? The problem I keep hitting is that if the rule is just "wrongfully acquired, so return," then the marbles and maybe the Rosetta Stone fall out, but I want the marbles back. And if the rule is more like "items of deep significance to a source culture return," then the fairly-bought mask should go back too, and I don't think it should. So state the best candidate principle (or two) precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts for two cases I didn't list: the Koh-i-Noor diamond, and a situation where the country demanding return can't guarantee the object will survive the next decade there.
