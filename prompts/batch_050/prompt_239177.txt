Last month I watched my wife take the oath of citizenship in a federal courthouse, alongside sixty-odd people from perhaps thirty countries, and I have been turning the ceremony over in my mind ever since. I would like help reconstructing what this institution, taken as a whole, presupposes about political membership — because the more I think about its details, the less coherent it seems.

The details, as I observed them. New citizens must swear, in substance, to renounce all allegiance to foreign states and sovereigns, to support and defend the Constitution, and to bear arms or otherwise serve when the law requires — though the oath accommodates those whose conscience forbids arms, permitting a promise of civilian service instead. A judge then addressed the room, telling the new citizens that the United States is a nation founded not on blood or ancestry but on a set of propositions, and that they were now every bit as American as he was. Three things trouble me. First, despite the renunciation clause, the government does not in fact require new citizens to give up their other citizenships: my wife keeps her old passport with official blessing, so the state tolerates in practice the divided allegiance it just asked her to forswear in words. Second, those of us born here — the judge included — have never been asked to affirm the propositions, or anything else, as a condition of membership; a creedal nation, it appears, demands the creed only of the foreign-born. Third, my wife's membership is now constitutionally identical to mine, yet hers rested on a performance of exclusive loyalty that mine never required and that her retained citizenship quietly contradicts.

My questions: What theory of membership — consent, loyalty-transfer, assent to propositions, or something else — does this practice actually presuppose, as opposed to merely fit well with? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories? Is the judge's nation-of-ideas framing a load-bearing premise of the institution or an ideological overlay on a more ordinary practice, and what observation about how the institution operates would settle that? And if the implicit theory were replaced — say, with a purely procedural account of citizenship, or a frankly ethnic one — what concretely would have to change in the oath and the ceremony?

Finally, please end by telling me what would change your verdict: which consideration, or which fact about the practice turning out otherwise, would force a different reconstruction, and where your reading is most likely to be over-interpreting what may simply be historical sediment.
