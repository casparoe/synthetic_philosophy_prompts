So I'm a philosophy PhD student (epistemology / philosophy of science) and I've been circling around Peirce for a while -- the fixation of belief stuff, the pragmatic maxim, the whole fallibilist picture of inquiry. I have roughly a year and a half of dissertation time to commit to *something* in this area, and I need to pick a focus that isn't just another exegesis of what Peirce "really meant."

Here's where I'd love your help. The Peircean framework strikes me as having a handful of genuinely unresolved internal tensions, and I'm trying to figure out which one is most worth making the center of a dissertation. The ones I keep coming back to:

1. Whether the pragmatic maxim -- cashing out meaning in terms of conceivable practical effects -- is actually compatible with Peirce's own scientific realism, or whether it quietly commits him to something closer to verificationism that he'd have hated being associated with
2. Whether the "fixation of belief" story (doubt as the irritant that drives inquiry, belief as the resting state) can survive contact with what we now know about motivated reasoning -- i.e., whether Peirce's four methods of fixing belief map onto anything real, or are just a nice taxonomy
3. Whether fallibilism + convergence-on-truth-in-the-long-run is coherent, or whether the long-run convergence claim is doing illegitimate work to save the picture from collapsing into "truth is just whatever survives inquiry"
4. How Peircean inquiry is supposed to handle cases where the "practical effects" of a belief are themselves contested or value-laden (seems relevant to how science actually works in politically charged domains)

Which of these -- or some tension I'm not seeing -- should be the load-bearing one, and why? I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning: which question is upstream of the others in the sense that getting it right changes how you'd approach the rest, which is tractable for someone doing armchair-ish conceptual work rather than archival Peirce scholarship, and which ones you'd deprioritize because even a good answer wouldn't move much. Also, honestly, which of these is just *done to death* in the literature and I should avoid on neglectedness grounds alone? If it helps to know the use case: I eventually want this to feed into something about how scientific communities should handle persistent disagreement, so bonus points if you can tell me which thread has the best claim to mattering outside Peirce scholarship itself.
