I direct a regional health system’s Exceptional Access Fund, which decides about forty funding appeals each month. Every case has already been judged safe, medically plausible, and outside ordinary insurance coverage. The fund is fixed, so approving one set of treatments means rejecting another. A panelist has thirty minutes per case; the decision is final unless the chief medical officer formally dissents.

Our recurring difficulty is contractualist. One patient may need an expensive intervention to avoid death or catastrophic disability, while the same money would relieve serious but lesser burdens for dozens of others. Panel members oscillate between saying that numbers never count and simply maximizing total expected benefit. Neither is acceptable. I want an auditable procedure grounded in Scanlonian contractualism, not a summary of the theory.

Translate the idea of reasonable rejection into an ordered decision procedure that physicians and administrators without philosophical training can use. The procedure should:

1. assess each person’s complaint individually, including severity, urgency, probability of benefit, and what that person could reasonably object to if passed over;
2. distinguish aggregating different benefits within one life from pooling benefits across different lives;
3. state when many lesser complaints may together outweigh one greater complaint, if ever, and avoid making that threshold depend on ad hoc intuition;
4. define when competing claims are relevantly similar enough that the number of claimants may break the tie;
5. explain how uncertainty should enter without letting a tiny probability of an enormous benefit automatically dominate;
6. specify whether prior waiting time, age, caretaking responsibilities, or conduct contributing to illness may count as generic reasons rather than impermissible favoritism; and
7. identify exactly where professional judgment remains unavoidable rather than concealing it inside a score.

Present the result as a flowchart in words, followed by a one-page decision record the panel could complete. Include explicit stop rules and the questions to ask in order.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

- Case A: one patient, age 22, has a 70 percent chance of cure from an expensive intervention; the same funds would provide certain, substantial relief from disabling chronic pain to 120 patients.
- Case B: one patient, age 80, has a 15 percent chance of gaining three months of life; the same funds would give twelve patients a 60 percent chance of regaining independent mobility.
- Case C: forty patients have materially identical claims, but only twenty-five can be funded this cycle; some have waited longer, some have dependents, and some became ill partly through voluntary conduct.

Work through each case step by step. At least one application should reach a conclusion the panel is likely to find uncomfortable, and you should say plainly that it does. Conclude with the procedure’s limits: the cases it cannot settle, the point where your contractualist construction is most contestable, and the argument or consideration that would most plausibly change your verdict. Give a determinate procedure where possible rather than surveying alternatives. Do not provide citations or a reading list. The full response must be at most 1,500 words.
