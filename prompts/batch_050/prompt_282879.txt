I'm finalizing a short position statement for a graduate seminar on the limits of free expression, and before I defend it I'd like a rigorous audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Please take on the role of a political philosopher with deep expertise in Mill's On Liberty and in contemporary free-speech theory. Work carefully: first restate each of my commitments in its strongest defensible form, then test them against one another pairwise, and only then deliver your verdicts. I want discrimination, not a laundry list — flagging every pair as problematic is as useless to me as flagging none.

Here is where I've landed:

1. The harm principle, as I understand Mill, is the sole legitimate basis for coercing competent adults: the state may restrict conduct only to prevent harm to others, never for a person's own good, and never merely because the conduct offends or disgusts.

2. Psychological offense — shock, distress, wounded feelings at what others say or depict — is not harm in the principle's sense. Treating it as harm would collapse the principle entirely, since virtually any unpopular utterance wounds someone.

3. The state is justified in banning sustained racist harassment in public spaces even in cases where it neither incites violence nor is plausibly expected to lead to any, on grounds of the severe distress and the sense of civic exclusion it inflicts on those it targets.

4. Mill is right that paternalism toward competent adults is never justified: no one may be coerced for their own good, full stop.

5. Prescription-only drug laws and mandatory seatbelt laws are legitimate exercises of state power.

6. Mill's own carve-out — that a competent adult may not be permitted to sell themselves into permanent, irrevocable slavery — is coherent within the harm principle, and I endorse it.

What I want from you: identify which commitments genuinely conflict and which only appear to conflict before dissolving on inspection. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely what has to give — which commitment must be revised or abandoned — along with the cheapest available revision and what that revision costs me in argumentative territory I can no longer occupy. Then tell me which single change would restore the most coherence to the set overall. Please do not assume I already know where the problem lies, and don't soften the verdict to spare the set.
