Two of my colleagues, Dana and Eli, both Bayesian in outlook, got into an extended argument last week over a case I described to them, and I would like it settled. The case: a witness glimpsed a car leaving the scene of an incident at dusk and, pressed to quantify, says she is 60% confident the car was blue. A suspect owns a blue car. The question on the table was how the probability that the suspect was involved (call it H) should be revised in light of the glimpse.

Here is how the argument went.

(1) Dana moved first. Since the witness did not become certain of anything -- she did not learn that the car was blue, only shifted to 0.6 -- standard conditionalization has no proposition to conditionalize on. The correct tool is Jeffrey conditionalization: set the new probability of H to 0.6 times the old conditional probability of H given the car was blue, plus 0.4 times the old conditional probability of H given it was not blue, holding those conditionals fixed at their prior values.

(2) Eli replied that the premise of move (1) is false. There is always a proposition the inquirer is certain of -- here, that the witness reported 60% confidence that the car was blue (or, if you prefer, that she has the confidence she has). Conditionalize on that fact in the ordinary way. Jeffrey's rule, he said, is a kludge that survives only because people insist on treating the contents of experiences, rather than facts about experiences, as evidence.

(3) Dana's response had two parts. First, conditionalizing on the report requires likelihoods -- the probability that the witness would report 60% confidence given the car was blue, and given it was not -- and nothing in the case supplies them; the 0.6 is itself the distilled output of her perceptual system and already encodes whatever is known about her reliability at dusk, so demanding propositional evidence does not eliminate the uncertainty but merely relocates it. Second, and worse for Eli: suppose the identical 60%-confidence report had come from a blindfolded witness who had simply guessed. The proposition conditionalized on would be the same, yet plainly a different update would be warranted. So the evidential force of the glimpse cannot be captured by any proposition of the sort Eli proposes; what matters is the glimpse's causal pedigree, which is exactly what a direct shift in the probability of the color proposition is meant to register.

(4) Eli conceded that the evidence proposition must be enriched to include facts about the witness's reliability, but insisted this is an argument for specifying evidence properly, not for adopting a new rule. He then attacked the computation in move (1) on its own terms. Jeffrey's rule is valid only under a rigidity condition: the uncertain evidence must bear on H solely through the color proposition, so that the conditional probabilities of H given blue and given not-blue survive the update unchanged. In this case, he claimed, rigidity fails -- the fact that the witness could glimpse the car's color at all tells us the streetlight on that road was on, which independently bears on H (it affects how plausible it is that the suspect took that route, or that a driver would risk it). Dana's number is therefore wrong even if her framework is right.

(5) Dana answered that rigidity failure is not an objection to Jeffrey's rule but to a coarse application of it: the remedy is to Jeffrey-update over a finer partition that includes the streetlight, and no rule, Eli's included, escapes the need to choose the right partition -- the analogous failure for Eli would be conditionalizing on an impoverished evidence proposition, which is what his move (2) did.

Please adjudicate the dispute. Go move by move: for each of the five, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the verdict in a few sentences. Then answer the general questions the exchange raises: Is ordinary conditionalization genuinely available in cases of perceptual uncertainty, or does it merely push the problem into the choice and likelihood-assignment of an evidence proposition? Is Dana's final number for H defensible given move (4), and if not, whose fault is that -- the rule's or the user's? And finally: is there something both parties are missing -- some assumption they share that a third party might reject?
