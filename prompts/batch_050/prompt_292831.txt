I spent my career as a seismologist and only got round to moral philosophy after retiring, so bear with me if I mangle the terminology. I've been working through Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, and I got stuck hard at the very end, in the concluding chapter where he admits defeat on what he calls the dualism of practical reason. As I understand him (and please correct me if I've misread it), he thinks reason hands us two equally self-evident principles: roughly, that each person ought rationally to pursue their own good on the whole, and that from an impartial point of view no one's good counts for more than anyone else's, so we ought to promote the general happiness. And he says he can't prove these two will always agree unless you bring in God as a postulate guaranteeing that duty and interest coincide, which he clearly finds unsatisfying.

Here's what I want explained, at gut level rather than with a pile of references:

First, why did Sidgwick think the egoist principle is a genuine substantive axiom on a par with the impartial one, rather than either (a) a trivial truth dressed up -- of course it's rational to want your own life to go well, who ever denied it -- or (b) something the impartial principle simply beats when they clash? I can see why a chemist wants two reagents to both be real, but I can't yet feel why he couldn't just rank them and dissolve the problem.

Second, the bit that really nags me: is the dualism actually a collision between two commands of one faculty called reason, or is Sidgwick sliding between two different notions -- reasons for me, indexed to a person, and reasons from nowhere in particular -- and then acting surprised they don't line up? If the latter, does the whole problem partly evaporate once you're careful about whose reason a reason is? Or does that careful bookkeeping just rename the conflict instead of resolving it?

Third, does the theological fix even work on its own terms? If God arranges things so that doing your impartial duty always maximizes your own good, hasn't the egoist principle just become a lucky consequence of the impartial one rather than an independent axiom -- in which case the dualism was never really harmonized, only outflanked? Sidgwick seemed to think this was a serious failure of his whole system, and I'd like to understand whether a modern secular reader should agree it was a failure, or whether the honest verdict is that he proved something important: that you can't have both axioms without remainder.

Please keep it conceptual and explain it like you'd explain it to a smart colleague from another field over coffee. I've had enough of footnotes for one lifetime.
