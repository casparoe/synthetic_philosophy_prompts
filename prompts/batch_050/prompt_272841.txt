A colleague of mine in a philosophy reading group has developed a settled pattern of judgments about when it is permissible to impose a risk of harm on other people without their consent. Here are her verdicts so far:

1. Driving attentively at the speed limit on an ordinary street, thereby imposing a small but real risk of seriously injuring pedestrians: permissible.
2. Firing a rifle into the air in celebration in a populated area: impermissible.
3. Going about one's normal day while carrying a mild contagious cold, knowing that one might infect strangers: permissible.
4. Keeping an unfenced backyard swimming pool in a neighborhood with young children: impermissible.
5. Demolishing a building with explosives adjacent to occupied homes, using expert contractors, industry-standard precautions, and giving residents advance notice: permissible.

She has not articulated any general principle; she simply finds each verdict obvious.

A new case has now come up in discussion: a delivery company proposes to operate autonomous drones over a residential neighborhood. The per-flight probability of a crash causing serious injury is extremely low, the company employs state-of-the-art maintenance and routing so that no further feasible precautions remain, residents were notified by mail but have no opt-out, and the benefits flow mostly to customers while the crash risk falls on everyone below, including residents who never order anything.

Please do the following: First, predict her verdict on the drone case and justify the prediction. Second, identify at least three distinct general principles, each consistent with all five earlier verdicts, that she might be implicitly relying on -- I expect candidates will include a threshold on the magnitude of risk, a requirement that the risk be part of a generally beneficial practice whose benefits are shared by those exposed, and something in the family of a cost-justified-precautions or negligence standard, but feel free to refine or replace these. Third, show how the drone case discriminates among your candidate principles: which principles deliver which verdict, and does the divergence turn on the non-reciprocity of the benefit, the lowness of the risk, or the exhaustion of precautions? Finally, describe one or two further hypothetical cases, different from the drone case, whose verdicts would best reveal which principle she is actually running, and explain what each of her possible answers would rule in or out. I am less interested in the morally correct answer to the drone case than in the structure of her view and the most efficient way to discover it.
