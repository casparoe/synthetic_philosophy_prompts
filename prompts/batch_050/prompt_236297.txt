As a novelist, I recently workshopped a chapter in which a dying woman is visited by a demon who offers her Nietzsche's proposition: her life, repeated exactly, forever. She accepts. Two of my early readers -- my editor and a critic friend -- fell into a disagreement about the scene that I could not settle, and I would like a referee.

My editor held that the scene only works if eternal recurrence is treated as a psychological test rather than a cosmology: the woman's "yes" is a verdict on the life she already had, and whether the cycles actually occur is beside the point. On his reading, my decision to leave the demon's existence ambiguous was the chapter's strength.

The critic replied that this guts the test. If recurrence is merely hypothetical, the woman's affirmation costs her nothing -- it collapses into the ordinary advice to live without regrets, and the demon's weight comes precisely from the offer being real. The ambiguity, he said, was evasion dressed as subtlety.

My editor countered that literal recurrence makes affirmation meaningless in the opposite direction: if the cycles are truly identical down to the last detail, then the woman herself recurs identically, never knowing she has accepted before -- so no recurrence is ever a fresh choice, and willing the repetition is like willing the sun to rise. A test that changes nothing tests nothing.

The critic's last move was that this confuses two things. What recurs, he said, is a life of exactly this *type*, and the question of whether the woman in the next cycle is *numerically the same person* is what gives the scene its horror: if she is, the acceptance is a self-imposed sentence; if she is merely a perfect duplicate, acceptance is closer to wishing one's life upon a stranger -- which, he suggested, might actually be the *harder* thing to affirm honestly.

Which of them is right at each step? In particular: does the recurrence thought experiment lose its force if hypothetical, as the critic first claimed, or if literal, as the editor claims? And does the critic's type-versus-identity distinction resolve the standoff or merely relocate it? Please adjudicate point by point, and say what both are missing, if anything.
