I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I’m facilitating a 60-minute session for 16 colleagues at an engineering reading group. None of them has studied political philosophy.

Design a classroom activity that makes participants feel why “equality of what?” is a substantive question rather than a semantic one. Use a fictional engineering scenario: a team is preparing to launch a service, and the facilitator can distribute a limited pool of launch resources such as compute credits, reviewer time, documentation support, and access to test environments. Participants should be assigned different “conversion profiles” determining how those resources translate into things they can actually do, as well as private satisfaction schedules describing which accomplishments matter to them.

The activity should expose three distinct standards without naming them until the debrief:

1. equalizing bundles of resources;
2. equalizing participants’ effective opportunities or capabilities;
3. equalizing participants’ welfare or satisfaction.

I want the tensions among these standards to arise through play, not through an introductory lecture. For example, an equal division of resources might produce sharply unequal options; compensating people according to conversion profiles might leave someone with unusually expensive or modest preferences dissatisfied; and maximizing reported satisfaction might require giving a person fewer real options than others because they happen to be easily pleased. Include at least one complication involving adaptive preferences or strategically reported preferences, and one involving a resource that can be shared but not transferred, so that the answer is not simply “give everyone the same number of tokens.”

Please provide:

- setup instructions and all required materials;
- the structure and timing of each round;
- concrete rules for allocating, trading, or pooling resources;
- several sample conversion profiles and satisfaction schedules, designed so that the three standards recommend visibly different allocations;
- instructions for whether participants may communicate, revise their preferences, or inspect one another’s profiles;
- a scoring or outcome mechanism that does not involve real money and does not presuppose that one of the three standards is correct;
- a facilitator’s debrief mapping specific moments in the game onto equality of resources, equality of capabilities, and equality of welfare;
- at least three predictable ways the activity could fail when run with engineers, along with fixes;
- an honest account of which philosophical features the game cannot capture—for example, the difference between actual satisfaction and informed preference, or the difference between a genuine capability and a merely theoretical option.

The activity should end with a forced team decision about the fictional launch, followed by a vote under each of the three standards. Ideally, most groups should change their ranking at least once after seeing the full profiles. Avoid presenting any standard as obviously correct, and do not mention Sen, Dworkin, or the phrase “equality of what?” until the debrief.
