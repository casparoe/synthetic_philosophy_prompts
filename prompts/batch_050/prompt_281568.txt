So Epicurus's line is basically: while you're alive, death isn't there; once death is there, you're not; so death is never anything to you, and can't be bad for you. I've been collecting the standard replies and I want one more that isn't on my list. Here's what I have:

1. Deprivationism: death is bad because it deprives you of the good life you would otherwise have had. You're worse off in the world where you die than in the one where you carry on, even if you never feel the difference.
2. The "proves too much" reply: if unfelt deprivations don't count as harms, then being secretly deceived by your friends, or having your reputation trashed after you stop paying attention, or losing something you never find out you lost, wouldn't be bad for you either -- and they obviously are.
3. The desire-frustration reply: your projects and desires get cut off, and frustrated desires are bad for you whether or not the frustration registers in experience.
4. The dying-vs-being-dead reply: Epicurus's argument at most shows that the state of being dead is no harm; the process of dying can still be awful, and that's what people actually fear.
5. The posthumous-harm reply: a dead person can still be wronged -- broken promises, defamation, betrayal of their life's work -- so there's nothing incoherent about harms without an experiencing subject, and death itself could work the same way.
6. Nagel-style asymmetry against the Lucretius symmetry argument (which says prenatal nonexistence and posthumous nonexistence are equally nothing): we can coherently dread future nonexistence while being indifferent to past nonexistence, because what's lost in the first case is open future goods.
7. The objective-list reply: "bad for you" isn't exhausted by felt experience -- achievements, knowledge, loving relationships can make your life go worse without your noticing, and death's badness works like that.

Notice the list already covers the experienced-harm objection from several angles, the timing/subject problem, the symmetry maneuver, and the conflation worry. What I want is a reply that is genuinely different from all seven -- not a rewording, not a special case, not two of them stapled together. It can appeal to something weird (personal identity, the value of a life's narrative shape, the perspective from which we evaluate lives, whatever), but it has to actually engage Epicurus's argument rather than just changing the subject to why death is scary.
