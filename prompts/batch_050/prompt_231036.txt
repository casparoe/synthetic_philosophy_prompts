Last weekend a friend and I argued until two in the morning over a claim I defended and she flatly rejected: that there are reasons no one has ever had. Not undiscovered reasons awaiting some future thinker, but reasons that operate without ever being entertained — reasons that explain why a behavior persists and why it works, even though no mind has ever grasped them. My stock examples were the usual ones: camouflage patterns refined by selection, traditions that solve a problem no practitioner could state, conventions that coordinate millions of strangers without a designer. She held that all of this is a façon de parler — that a reason must be someone's reason, and that speaking of a rationale floating free of any reasoner mistakes our retrospective gloss for something in the world. We parted unconvinced.

I teach a first-year undergraduate seminar called "Minds and Reasons" (18 students, one 90-minute meeting), and I want to stage this dispute for them — or rather, make them live it. Design an activity in which the students' own behavior comes to embody a rationale none of them possesses: something where a strategy or convention arises among them through copying, elimination, or transmission rather than deliberation, where what makes it a good strategy is discoverable only afterwards, and where at least some participants, mid-game, confidently articulate the wrong reason for what they are doing while doing the right thing. The students should not hear the phrase "free-floating rationale" or anything like it beforehand; the experience should put them in a position where my friend's verdict — someone must have had this reason — becomes palpably hard to deliver.

Please include:

1. The setup and rules in enough detail that I could run it cold: materials, the payoff or selection structure, what participants can and cannot see, and a timeline for the ninety minutes.
2. At least two rounds or phases with an interlude, so the gap between doing and understanding has time to open and participants can voice (wrong) theories before being confronted with what actually happened.
3. A debrief plan mapping specific, predictable moments of the activity onto the components of the idea: where the reason was located (in the payoff structure? the environment? the selection process?), who failed to have it, and in what sense it was nonetheless operative. Include the two or three objections students will predictably raise on my friend's behalf — for instance, that I chose the rules, so the reason had a reasoner after all, namely me — and how to make those objections productive rather than deflating.
4. What will go wrong when this is run and how to handle each failure: strategies collapsing too fast, a participant who reverse-engineers the structure in round one and ruins the mystery, copying that converges on noise.
5. An honest statement of what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect the deepest problem is that in real selection there is no game designer, whereas in my classroom there is one; say whether, and how far, the activity can gesture at that asymmetry, and what I must simply tell the students rather than show them.

I want to be able to report back to my friend that eighteen undergraduates spent an afternoon doing something for a reason none of them had, and that when the reason was finally stated aloud, it described their behavior better than any of their own theories did.
