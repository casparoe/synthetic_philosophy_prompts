I cover economics and science policy for a general-interest magazine, and for the past three years I have also taught a graduate seminar at a journalism school called Reading Models, aimed at reporters who must evaluate quantitative claims — climate projections, macroeconomic forecasts, epidemiological curves — without being able to re-derive them. One week of the course is devoted to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics and the so-called F-twist: the suggestion that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its assessment, that theories should be judged by predictive success alone, and indeed that genuinely significant theories will typically traffic in assumptions everyone concedes are false. My students reliably leave that week having memorized a slogan — assumptions do not matter, predictions do — together with the standard counter-slogan that false premises must infect any conclusion built on them. What they cannot reliably do is say when each response is the right one, which is precisely the judgment they will need when a central bank or a modeling consortium hands them results resting on patently unrealistic idealizations.

Please write one essay question for the course's final exam, answerable in 75 minutes, that cannot be answered by reciting either slogan. The question should embed a concrete modeling scenario — you may invent it, but keep it plausible, something like a macroeconomic forecasting model with perfectly optimizing agents or a climate ensemble with parametrized sub-grid processes — in which a defender of the model offers the F-twist in response to criticism, and it should require students to adjudicate that defense rather than merely describe it. A well-constructed question should force students to distinguish at least three different things an "unrealistic assumption" can be (roughly: a harmless omission of negligible factors, a deliberate distortion meant to isolate a single causal mechanism, and a substantive empirical claim about the world that happens to be false); to explain what predictive success within a calibration period does and does not license, particularly when the model is used to evaluate policy interventions outside that period; and to confront the point that a failed prediction indicts a whole package of assumptions plus auxiliaries rather than any single one, which cuts against naive versions of both Friedman and his critics. Along with the question, give me a model answer of about 600 words, written to the standard of the best student I have had rather than as a survey of the literature, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the way students typically fumble that point. Close with a few sentences explaining what separates an answer that merely passes from one that earns the top band, so that I can defend the grading when students appeal.
