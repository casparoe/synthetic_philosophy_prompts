Hume's case against believing miracle reports has been fought over for going on three centuries and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch. Part 1 gives the a priori weighing of testimony against uniform experience, Part 2 piles on the a posteriori stuff about credulous witnesses, wonder-loving audiences, rival miracle claims in different religions cancelling each other out, and still both sides think they won. My hunch is that the dispute isn't really about testimony at all -- it's about what priors you're allowed to bring to the evidence. I.e. whether "nature has always run the same way" is itself a piece of evidence or a presupposition that rigs the game before any testimony arrives, so that theists and Humeans are just talking past each other with different starting points. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement buried here (about how testimony transmits warrant, about what counts as a law of nature, about whether Bayesian reconstructions of the argument settle anything or just translate the stalemate into math)? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what, plus a take on what could actually produce progress -- a better epistemology of testimony, a formal result, or is it hopeless. Also tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and which part of it you're least sure about.
