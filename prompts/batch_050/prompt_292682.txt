Ok so this is a bit in the weeds but I've been chewing on it for weeks. I read a lot of the mechanistic interpretability stuff (the circuits-style work, grokking, the usual) and I keep running into the same undefended move: people say a model 'uses the same mechanism' here and 'a different mechanism' there, or that a phase change in training is the model 'switching mechanisms', and nobody ever says what makes two mechanisms the same one versus two different ones that happen to produce the same outputs.

Context for why I care: eventually I want to build or at least evaluate anomaly detectors for training runs -- things that flag when the mechanism a model is using for some behavior has changed, even when the behavior on the training distribution hasn't. To even spec that you need a criterion of mechanism individuation, and I'd rather not read five more papers before knowing what to demand of one.

So: write me the desiderata list for a theory or criterion of 'same mechanism', adequate FOR THIS PURPOSE (anomaly detection over a system's internals, not metaphysics for its own sake). Specifically:

- The cases it has to have an answer for, with the case behind each requirement. At minimum it should handle: two networks with identical behavior on every input you'll ever test but different internal organization; one network before and after a tiny weight perturbation that changes nothing observable; a system whose components get swapped out one at a time until nothing original remains; two completely different designs computing the same function (a bimetallic-strip thermostat and a digital one); and the grokking case, where generalization behavior shifts suddenly at some training step long after the loss has flattened.
- The distinctions it can't collapse. I'm pretty sure 'same function' and 'same mechanism' have to come apart, and 'same mechanism type, different parameter settings' vs 'genuinely different mechanism' has to be a real distinction, but tell me if I'm wrong and why.
- Which requirements are non-negotiable given the stated purpose, and which are just camp preferences (pure behavior people vs causal-organization people vs whoever).
- And the part I most want: are any of the natural desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? I have a bad feeling that 'individuate by internal causal structure' and 'mechanism identity must be decidable from input/output behavior alone' can't both hold, since distinct mechanisms can be behaviorally identical -- but maybe there's a clever fix I'm missing, like individuating relative to a level of abstraction. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and say which one you'd give up.

Don't be wishy-washy about it: pick a side on the hard calls and defend the choice, including on that last question. If you think the whole framing is confused, that's a position too, but you have to actually argue it, not just gesture at 'it's vague'.

At the very end, give me the single requirement you'd refuse to trade away if forced, in the form \answer{...}
