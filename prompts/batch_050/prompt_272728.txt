Something I've been mulling over: when a pile-on on social media gets someone fired, deplatformed, or chased off the internet, the people participating almost always insist it's "not punishment, just accountability" or "consequences, not cancellation." But the practice looks pretty punishment-shaped on the surface -- the target loses their job or reputation, there's often a demand for a public apology, the goal is clearly to make them worse off -- while lacking basically everything actual punishment systems build in. No proportionality: the severity tracks how viral the post went, not how bad the thing was. No double jeopardy: the same old tweet can get you sanctioned again five years later. No way to have "served your time" and be done with it. And no one is in charge, so there's no one whose job it is to decide the sanction has gone far enough.

So here's my question: what theory of punishment (or of social sanction more broadly) does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory -- what would a deterrence theorist, a retributivist, and someone who thinks punishment is about social self-defense each say the practice is *really* doing, and which of those fits the details best (the virality-tracking severity, the no-double-jeopardy thing, the apology rituals)? Which features of the practice are load-bearing versus incidental? And what about the "it's not punishment, just consequences" move -- what does someone making that move have to believe about what punishment is for the distinction to hold up, and does it hold up? Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which parts of it could be licensed by several different theories, and what would concretely have to change about pile-ons if the participants took seriously, say, proportionality or a statute of limitations.
