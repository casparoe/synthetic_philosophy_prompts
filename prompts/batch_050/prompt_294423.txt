I'm a novelist, midway through a draft, and I've just spent an unhappy week at a writers' retreat where a colleague told me, cheerfully, that he'd lifted the central premise of a novel I'd been circulating in manuscript -- the conceit of the whole thing, the part I'm proudest of -- for his own book, and that there was nothing I could do or even legitimately complain about. He backed this up by reading me a passage from a well-known craft-and-law handbook for writers, which I later copied down. The handbook's summary runs roughly as follows: copyright protects only the particular expression of an idea -- your actual sentences -- and never the idea itself, so plots, premises, characters in the abstract, and settings are free for anyone to take, and this is not a loophole but the deliberate design of the law, on the theory that ideas are non-rivalrous goods which no one can meaningfully own; the tradition behind this, the handbook says, is Locke's account of property, under which you acquire ownership of something by mixing your labor with it, and since a premise is simply discovered rather than made -- it was, so to speak, already there in the commons of possible stories -- no amount of labor in elaborating it can make it yours; from which the handbook concludes that a writer whose idea is taken has suffered no injury at all, only the ordinary sting of competition, and that feeling otherwise reflects a confusion about what creative work consists in.

I want to know whether this is fair -- as a summary of the law, and separately as a piece of philosophy. I gather that the first part, about expression versus ideas, gestures at something real in copyright doctrine, but I suspect the handbook is smoothing over complications: I've heard murmurs about protection for sufficiently delineated characters, about infringement that doesn't involve copying anyone's sentences verbatim, and about a distinction between taking a bare premise and taking a plot's detailed sequence of events, and I don't know whether those are exceptions that swallow the handbook's rule or footnotes to it. The Locke part I'm even less sure of. My recollection from a college philosophy course is that Locke attaches conditions to labor-mixing -- something about leaving enough and as good for others, which you'd think ideas satisfy trivially, since my using a premise doesn't deplete it -- and I can't tell whether the handbook is applying Locke correctly, inverting him, or simply borrowing his name for a conclusion he wouldn't recognize. And the final move, from the law doesn't protect this to you have no legitimate complaint, strikes me as the sort of slide from is to ought that philosophers warn about, but perhaps there's a stronger version of the argument I'm not seeing, one that says something genuine about what originality in fiction really is.

So: please take the handbook's summary apart claim by claim. For each, tell me what an intellectual property scholar would accept as accurate, what they'd mark as an overstatement or a conflation, and what -- if anything -- is simply wrong. Then do the same from the philosophical side: does the Lockean framework, charitably read, support the handbook's conclusion, complicate it, or actually cut against it, and what would a defender of moral (as opposed to legal) rights in one's ideas -- a novelist who thinks premise-theft is a real wrong even where it's perfectly lawful -- say in response? Finally, tell me whether the handbook's closing consolation follows from whichever parts of the summary survive scrutiny, or whether my colleague has been hiding behind a popularization.
