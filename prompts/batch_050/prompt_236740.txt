I'm a TA for a intro labor studies / political theory course at a big public university, about 28 undergrads, mostly not philosophy majors, and I've got a 75-minute slot with them. We were in section last week and a student basically said: if the union wins a raise for everyone on the shop floor whether or not you personally join, then joining the strike is just... donating your paycheck to other people? And honestly the room kind of agreed with him, and I found it hard to give a satisfying answer on the spot, because on some level the logic is right and thats the whole problem. I want an activity where the students slam into that wall themselves instead of me explaining it.

So: design a game, runnable in the 75 minutes, that makes them feel the collective action problem at the heart of organizing -- individually it keeps being rational to cross the picket line or skip the dues, and collectively that logic eats the thing that was making everyone better off. Ideally it should have multiple rounds with actual payoffs (points, candy, whatever), and I'd love for the rules to do some extra work beyond the bare prisoner's-dilemma version: e.g. some kind of threshold or majority rule so the union only "exists" if enough people sign cards, asymmetric stakes so a few students have more to lose from striking than others (a student playing a single parent vs one with savings, that kind of thing), and if possible a phase where they're allowed to talk and make pledges to each other, so they can discover for themselves what talk is and isn't worth without enforcement. Don't let me lecture beforehand, and they shouldn't hear the phrase "free rider" or "collective action problem" until the debrief.

Then give me the full package: exact setup, roles, the payoff numbers or at least how to calibrate them, a timeline broken into chunks, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the real stuff -- open shops and right-to-work laws, why strike funds and picket-line norms exist, why solidarity is a practice and not just a feeling. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it (e.g. the one guy who defects every round and finds it hilarious, or rounds collapsing too fast), and be honest about what a game with candy stakes fundamentally can't reproduce about an actual strike -- like real fear of getting fired, or relationships that have to last decades -- and how I should handle that gap in the debrief without ruining the mood.
