France's laïcité, which comes from the 1905 law, means religion is pushed out of public institutions entirely so that everyone appears in public as equal citizens first. Canada's multiculturalism is basically the opposite: instead of asking minorities to blend in, the state officially recognizes and even supports their cultural identities, on the theory that people integrate better when they don't have to give up who they are.

The two models reflect different ideas of equality. The French model treats equality as sameness in the public sphere: the rules apply identically to everyone regardless of religion. The Canadian model treats equality as something that sometimes requires accommodation, because the same rule can burden different groups differently.

In my view the Canadian model handles religious diversity in schools better. Canada shows that you can have social cohesion without forcing assimilation, and since multiculturalism worked in Canada, there's no reason it couldn't work in France too, since both are liberal democracies dealing with immigration.

In conclusion, laïcité and multiculturalism both aim at integration and equality but define equality differently, and the Canadian approach is more consistent with the actual goal of equal citizenship."
