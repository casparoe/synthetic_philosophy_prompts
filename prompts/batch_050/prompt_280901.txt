I'm designing the policy unit for my grad seminar on well-being and want to use this as a take-home, so help me pressure-test it first.

Setup: the (fictional, small, reasonably well-off) country of Velmar has decided its official government target will no longer be GDP per capita but "national happiness," and a commission has shortlisted five candidate measures. Assume all five are cheap to administer and methodologically competent. They are:

1. The ladder question: people rate their life on a 0–10 scale, worst to best possible life for them.
2. Experienced affect: from yesterday-diary reconstructions, positive emotions minus negative emotions across the day.
3. A flourishing composite: meaning, relationships, engagement, health, accomplishment, self-rated.
4. The swap test: people are asked how much they'd have to be paid to permanently swap lives (their whole situation, not their body) with a randomly drawn fellow citizen; national score is how little compensation people demand.
5. The eulogy probe: once a year, people write two paragraphs describing the life they hope to have lived by age 80, then rate how closely their current trajectory matches it.

Now the actual task, two parts.

Part one: rank the five measures into tiers on two separate dimensions -- (a) fidelity: how well the number tracks what happiness actually is, and (b) target-safety: how badly things go if a clever ministry spends ten years optimizing the number without caring about the thing itself (Goodhart-type worries). Then give one combined ranking and say, per measure, which of the two dimensions did the decisive work in placing it.

Part two, and this is where I think the interesting philosophy lives: suppose Velmar picks measure 1 and a ministry realizes it can raise scores by lowering people's imagined alternatives -- saturating the media with how miserable life is abroad, defunding libraries' travel sections, whatever. Call this benchmark suppression. Nobody's experienced life changes at all; only the reference point moves. Does national happiness go up? Your answer presumably commits you to something about whether life evaluation is partly a relational/comparative state, so say what you think happiness is such that this counts or doesn't, and whether your ranking in part one survives your own answer here.

Numbers and tiers, not just vibes -- I need to be able to grade agaisnt something.
