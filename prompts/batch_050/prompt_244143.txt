Last year I abandoned a business I'd sunk two years into, and the most galling part was that nothing surprised me except my own confidence: I'd seen the survival statistics for ventures of that kind, and I'd set them aside because my case, I kept explaining, was different. It wasn't. I'm now helping to run a fortnightly seminar for master's students in behavioural science (about sixteen participants, ninety minutes), and for our session on forecasting and judgement I want them to have the experience I failed to have cheaply — feeling the pull of one's own detailed, vivid, private story, and then watching a dreary base rate beat it. I don't want a lecture on the inside view and the outside view, and I don't want them to hear those terms, or the phrase epistemic modesty, until after the activity is over.

Design me the session. It should be a proper game, not a worksheet: participants should make a series of real estimates or predictions under a scoring scheme that rewards accuracy without needing money (points, a leaderboard, something mildly embarrassing for the lowest scorer is fine). Crucially, I want the structure to force three moments rather than just one. First, a round where they must commit to estimates on questions deliberately designed to invite inside-view reasoning (questions about themselves, their own group, their own plans) before any reference-class information is available. Second, the reveal, where the relevant base rates come out and the scoring shows how the outside view would have done relative to what they actually wrote down. Third — and this is the part I most care about, because the takeaway shouldn't be that deferring to statistics is always free — at least one round where someone's genuinely private information beats the reference class, so that modesty has a visible cost as well as a visible payoff, and the interesting question becomes how to tell the cases apart. Give me the rules and a minute-by-minute timeline, the scoring scheme, and a handful of candidate questions with the base rates I could use (questions where I can state the relevant frequencies from general knowledge, not ones requiring me to look anything up). Then the debrief: how to map each moment onto the inside view/outside view distinction and onto the difference between modesty as deference and modesty as knowing when one's own information is load-bearing, plus the reference-class problem — that choosing the class is itself judgement, which the debrief should not let them forget.

Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect at least one student will retrofit a base rate to justify an answer they reached by gut, and another will insist their case really is different, and I want a plan for handling both without lecturing. Finish with an honest note on which feature of real epistemic modesty a ninety-minute game cannot reproduce and which I would have to supply by other means. Please keep the whole reply under 800 words.
