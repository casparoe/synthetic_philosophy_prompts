I am a third-year student writing a term paper for a seminar on the ethics of autonomous systems, and my paper needs a taxonomy of the positions on so-called responsibility gaps: cases in which an autonomous system causes harm, yet it seems that no one -- not the operator, not the manufacturer, not the programmer -- can fairly be held responsible for it. I would like you to construct the taxonomy as a decision tree that I can use both to structure the paper and to test my classmates' intuitions against it.

Please organize the tree around the following scenario, which I want to use as a running test case, and make sure every leaf gives a verdict on it: a municipal delivery service deploys a fleet of delivery robots that share a common learning system, so that each robot's behavior is partly shaped by data uploaded by the other robots in the fleet. Through an interaction nobody designed or predicted, the fleet develops a routing pattern that systematically deprives one neighborhood of timely deliveries of refrigerated medicine, and a patient is harmed as a result. The operator followed the manual; the manufacturer's engineers cannot reconstruct which updates led to the pattern; no individual robot violated any of its rules.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors. The branching questions should be answerable by someone who has never heard of the debate, and should include at least these fault lines, in whatever order you judge most defensible:

- whether genuine moral responsibility requires that the responsible party knew, or could reasonably have known, the relevant outcome was a possible result of their conduct;
- whether there is one gap or several -- that is, whether we should separate the question of who deserves blame or punishment from the question of who owes an answer, an apology, or compensation to the victim, and whether a gap in one implies a gap in the others;
- whether an unfilled gap is an acceptable cost of deploying beneficial technology or a defect that must be eliminated before deployment;
- whether responsibility can attach to collections (the firm, the fleet, the design team as a group) in a way not reducible to the responsibility of their members;
- whether a purely forward-looking response -- changing incentives, insurance schemes, and design practices without blaming anyone -- counts as a solution to the gap or as a refusal to engage with it.

Each leaf should get a short descriptive name (inventing names is fine; do not feel obliged to attach them to specific philosophers), one sentence on the verdict it reaches in the medicine-delivery case, and one sentence on the most awkward thing the position is committed to. Where a combination of answers is logically open but you can think of no one who would occupy it, include it as a leaf anyway and briefly diagnose why the position is empty -- incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Where two named positions in the literature would land on the same leaf of your tree, flag that your tree cannot separate them and say which further question would be needed.

Keep the tree to between six and nine leaves, and end with a paragraph identifying which single branching question you think does the most work -- that is, which answer, once fixed, most constrains the remaining positions -- since I want to center my paper's argument on it.
