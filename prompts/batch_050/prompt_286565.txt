So a couple weeks ago my roommate skipped a flight to visit his mum because he woke up with a bad feeling about it, and then spent the next day checking whether the plane had crashed. It hadn't. We got into this long argument about whether he was being irrational even though nothing happened, and it sent me down a rabbit hole that ended with me writing a short essay for our philosophy course's end-of-term zine. I'm not a philosophy student, I'm in econ, so I'm a bit out of my depth and want comments before I submit it. Here it is:

---

The Appointment Book

Everyone knows the old story. A man meets Death in the market in Damascus, and Death looks suprised to see him. Convinced his time has come, he flees to Aleppo that same day. That night Death knocks on his door in Aleppo and explains that she was only surprised to see him in Damascus because she knew their appointment was in Aleppo. The standard moral, as I understand it: when the world keeps a book about you, you can't dodge your own entry. The man took Death's expression as evidence and acted on it, but the appointment was never going to change, because it was an appointment with him, wherever he ended up. His fear was the mechanism of its own fulfillment.

But I think the story smuggles in it's pessimism. Death's book only works because Death is a perfect recorder -- the entry is written from the end, so to speak. Real prediction isn't like that. Actuarial tables, risk models, the friend who says "I knew you'd say that": these are all built from people like you, up until now. Which means the book leaks forward. If you genuinely flee, against type, the entry written about you was written about the kind of person who doesn't flee, and it can just be wrong.

Here's my variant. Death keeps the appointment book in an office staffed by clerks who forecast from last year's records. The man's cousin is a clerk, and he leaks it: your name is down for Damascus because nobody like you has ever left Damascus. Now does fleeing help? I think obviously yes. And I don't think the variant changes the original story -- I think it exposes what the original story was quietly assuming.

The conclusion I'm tempted by: fatalism is only ever locally true. The book is real, but it's written in pencil, and acting is erasing.

---

What I want from you, in order of how much it matters: (1) have I got the standard moral right, or have I flattened it into a strawman? (2) Does the cousin variant actually expose a hidden assumption, or have I just swapped in an easier story and declared victory? (3) The pencil line at the end -- does it survive the obvious comeback that risk models update, so the "fleeing" just becomes the new data? (4) Anything a decision theorist would flag as a straight-up error. Be blunt, I'd rather fix it than feel good about it. Give me your comments as a list, most important first, max 450 words total, and end with the single thing I should fix before anything else.
