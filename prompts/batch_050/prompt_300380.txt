I spent 40 years as a field ecologist, mostly soils and watersheds, and now in retirement I’m reading moral philosophy with more enthusiasm than technique, so please don’t send me to the library or give me a bibliography. I want you to work from the cases below and tell me what principle I’m actually using, if any.

My gut verdicts on population size, stated concretely: (1) A valley supports 1,000 people at very good lives; adding 200 more people whose lives are good but a bit less good, without making the first 1,000 worse off, seems clearly better. (2) But replacing that same valley with 100,000 lives barely worth living, huge total welfare but thin gruel individually, seems worse, even monstrous. (3) If a coming dry century means choosing between keeping 900 current people at good lives or letting numbers rise to 1,500 at only mediocre lives on the same resource base, I say protect the 900. (4) A nudge that leads already-secure people to have one more child who would very probably have a good life, at no real cost to others, seems fine, maybe good. (5) Preventing the conception of a child whose life would be predictably awful seems right even though it lowers population and no one exists to complain. (6) In nonidentity cases where our choice changes which future people exist, I still think we should pick the cleaner technology even if the alternative future has different people with lives still worth living; identity shouldn’t launder the outcome. (7) Yet if humanity ended painlessly after the current generation, no fear, no grief beyond us, I still call that a catastrophe, which maybe conflicts with (6). (8) Moving people from low-productivity, high-risk places to safer richer places where the same people flourish more seems good even before any change in total headcount. (9) I feel no duty to maximize the number of possible future genomes, dialects, or cuisines as museum pieces; diversity matters mainly when lives go better or capacities widen. (10) I definately reject the idea that a population is better just because the average is higher if that is achieved by quietly letting the worst-off disappear.

Is there one principle, or two clean candidates, that capture most of this? I suspect something like person-affecting priority for existing lives plus a critical-level cutoff plus a separate badness of extinction, but that may be a patchwork. State your best principle precisely enough that I could code it, name which of my verdicts it cannot save, and say which single verdict you would overturn first if forced. Then give its verdicts on three cases I haven’t listed: a colony ship that can send either 100 near-certain excellent lives or 10,000 lives with a 60 percent chance each of being barely worth living; a policy that raises average welfare by discouraging births only among the poorest; and a painless sterilization of the last generation after every living person has consented. Lead with the principle in one sentence, and keep the whole reply under 450 words.
