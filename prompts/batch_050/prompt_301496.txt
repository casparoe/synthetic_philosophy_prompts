I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I am drafting a piece about self-control and incremental temptation. A philosopher friend has offered me an argument which he claims shows that one of the standard axioms of rational choice is a superstition. I suspect he has smuggled something in, but I cannot locate where. Here is the argument, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Imagine a device fitted to your arm with a dial of a thousand settings. Each notch upward delivers a slightly stronger electric stimulus and a payment of £10,000. The increments are calibrated so finely that no subject can feel any difference between adjacent settings: setting 437 feels exactly like setting 438.

(1) If two states are indistinguishable in respect of how much they hurt, then £10,000 is ample compensation for moving from one to the other; so each single notch, taken on its own, is worth advancing.
(2) What is worth taking considered on its own may rationally be taken. A rational agent need not refuse a genuine gain merely because gains of that kind, if repeated often enough, would add up to something bad — otherwise nobody could rationally accept a single biscuit, a single glass of wine, or a single cheque, since any of these, iterated, ends somewhere regrettable.
(3) So the rational policy is to keep advancing the dial for as long as the payments continue.
(4) But at setting 1000 the agent is in constant agony and would gladly surrender everything he has received to be back at setting 0. His preferences therefore run in a circle: each notch preferred to the one below, yet setting 0 preferred to setting 1000.
(5) Every preference in that circle was certified rational by step (1). Hence cyclical preferences are not irrational in themselves; transitivity is not a genuine requirement of rationality, and the familiar argument that intransitive agents can be "money-pumped" must rest on a mistake.

I very much want to keep transitivity — the second half of my piece rather depends on it — and the conclusion strikes me as proving far too much. Yet each premise seems to have something going for it, and I cannot isolate the culprit. Which step should I reject, why does that step manage to look innocent, and what exactly would rejecting it cost me? If more than one step is suspect, I would be glad to know which rejection is cheapest. Please answer in British English.
