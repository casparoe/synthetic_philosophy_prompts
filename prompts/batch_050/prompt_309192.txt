I'm preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar on psychopathy and moral responsibility, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Full blameworthiness requires moral knowledge: someone who genuinely cannot know that what they are doing is wrong may be dangerous and may need to be restrained, but is not a fit target of blame in the way a knowing wrongdoer is.

(2) Psychopaths do know that what they do is wrong. They state moral rules accurately, deploy them fluently to predict and manipulate others, lie about their intentions, and take steps to avoid detection — all of which seems to require exactly the knowledge in question.

(3) I accept judgment internalism about moral judgment: sincerely judging an action to be wrong, as opposed to merely classifying it the way one's community does, necessarily involves some motivational aversion to performing it.

(4) Psychopaths characteristically lack any such aversion; their talk of right and wrong is better understood as fluency in a social convention, so they do not genuinely judge their actions wrong.

(5) I think Prichard's 1835 category of moral insanity in his Treatise on Insanity — a corruption of the affective and moral dispositions with the reasoning and intellectual faculties left intact — is essentially the right way to describe the psychopath, and shows that the moral capacity is a distinct faculty that can fail while reason is preserved.

(6) Psychopaths who commit serious crimes are fully blameworthy and punishment of them is ordinarily justified on retributive, not merely preventive, grounds.

Which of these commitments are actually in tension, which pairs only appear to be, and in each genuine case what is the cheapest revision and what does it cost? Please order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and be specific about what (2) and (4) would each have to concede for both to survive.
