I co-host a small philosophy-ish podcast and next month we're interviewing a philosopher who defends the friendship model of what grown kids owe thier parents -- roughly the line Jane English made famous: raising a child is either a duty or something done out of love, so it doesn't generate a debt of gratitude, and whatever adult children owe their parents is just whatever flows from the ongoing friendship-like relationship between them. Which means that if there is no such relationship, nothing much is owed.

Draft 8 to 10 interview questions for me. I don't want the soft stuff (she's done the "so what IS the friendship model" bit on three other shows, I've listened to all of them). Instead I want questions that press on the parts of the view that make normal people squirm. Specifically I'd like at least one question each on:

- the dutiful-but-cold parent: the dad who paid for everything, never missed a recital, and never once hugged you. On her view the adult kid owes this guy basically nothing. Does she bite that bullet?
- the exit problem: friendships can be ended, and nobody thinks you owe your ex-friend weekend visits. If filial obligations survive estrangement the way most people think they do, isn't she quietly relying on something that isn't friendship?
- dementia and one-sided decline: a parent who can no longer recognize the child, let alone be anyone's friend. What does the model even say here, since the relationship it grounds duties in is gone?
- the gratitude she keeps dismissing: why doesn't a huge unasked-for sacrifice generate obligations even if it was given lovingly? Push her on the favors-vs-debts move specifically.
- whether the view is parochial -- it sounds very Western individualist, and filial-piety traditions would find it monstrous.

For each question, write the follow-up I'd ask after her most likely first answer (she tends to retreat to "friendship duties are just conditional on the relationship continuing," so at least two follow-ups should be built to make that move expensive). Also give me one closing question asking what finding or case would make her give the view up. Order them so the tension builds rather than front-loading the hardest one.
