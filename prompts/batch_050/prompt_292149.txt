So I'm a second year taking a philosophy of cognitive science course, and I made the mistake of volunteering to run one session of our undergrad cog-sci society. Usually like 12-14 people show up and I get about 75 minutes. I want to do something on concept learning from tiny samples, using Bongard-style puzzles — you know, six boxes on the left that all share some property, six on the right that don't, find the rule.

Here's the thing I want the activity to make people feel, not just nod along to. Our prof keeps hammering that no finite set of examples ever pins down one rule — infinitely many rules fit any data — so "learning the concept" is really about the learner's built-in biases, not the evidence. (We did the whole Goodman grue thing in class.) So my rough idea: I play a "rule designer" who picks secret rules, the group plays concept-learners over several rounds of Bongard-type puzzles, but secretly some of my rules are grue-ified — like "circles on the left... but only for the first four rounds, after that it's actually stripes" — so no amount of early success can tell them whether they found THE rule or just a rule that works so far. And maybe one round where three different rules fit equally well and I score them on which one I personally meant, so they feel how much of concept learning is basically mind-reading the designer.

What I need from you: the full activity. Actual puzzle sets or at least a really concrete recipe for making ~8 rounds of them fast (drawing Bongard problems is harder than it looks), the rules of the game, when to spring the grue reveal, and a points scheme with no real money (chocolate? a leaderboard? idk). Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical points — underdetermination, simplicity biases, why "just look at the data" isn't a theory of learning. Someone will definately claim the grue rounds were cheating, and honestly I think that's the most interesting objection possible, so tell me how to run that argument instead of fumbling it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (someone guessing a real rule instantly, people getting genuinly annoyed at me, rounds dragging) and what to do about each. And be honest about what the activity gets wrong — in real life there's no designer whose mind you're reading, so what does the game misrepresent about actual concept learning?

Oh and nobody should hear the word grue or Goodman until the debrief, I want it to hit them after.
