I help run a political theory workshop, and for the past year its two longest-standing participants have been deadlocked over the boundary problem. One defends the all-affected-interests principle — anyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision is entitled to some say in it. The other defends the all-coerced or all-subjected principle — entitlement to a say tracks subjection to the coercive authority that issues the decision, not mere affectedness. At my urging they have agreed to stop trading replies and write a single joint statement instead, and I have volunteered to produce the first draft. Write that draft for me.

Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims. It should include the concessions I believe each side has already made in discussion: both accept that no decision rule can democratically select its own electorate without circularity, so their principles can at most constrain, not constitute, the demos; the all-affected side concedes that enfranchising everyone affected by every decision is unworkable — a town's zoning vote affects the whole planet in some thin sense — and owes an account of how affectedness is to be weighted or scoped; the all-coerced side concedes that cross-border harms like drifting pollution injure people without subjecting them to law, and that the question of who counts as "subjected" itself depends on jurisdictional boundaries, which is precisely what is in dispute. Each agreed claim should rule something out: if a formulation would be true on every possible view of the boundary problem, cut it. Second, for each remaining disagreement, a short block containing the disagreement stated in neutral terms both parties would sign, each side's position phrased so that the side itself would accept the wording, the crux, and the case both agree bears on the crux with each side's prediction recorded in advance. They have pre-committed to three test cases: a secession referendum (does the rest of the country get a vote, or only the seceding region?), an upstream state's dam whose reservoir floods farmland across the border (who is owed a say, and an equal one?), and an entrenched constitutional clause binding future generations (do the not-yet-born count as affected, as subjected, or as neither — and what does each side do with its answer?).

Close with a short section flagging any disagreement the two concluded was verbal rather than genuine. I suspect at least part of their dispute dissolves once coercion is treated as an especially grave species of affectedness, or once the two principles are assigned different jobs — one constitutive, one corrective — but I want the draft to state exactly which part of the disagreement survives such moves and why, rather than gesturing at a synthesis.
