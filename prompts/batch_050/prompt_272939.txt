I have been going back and forth for six weeks over whether to accept the voluntary redundancy package my employer (a firm of insurance underwriters in Leeds, where I have worked for fourteen years) has offered me, and I must give them an answer by the end of the month. My oldest friend, who is an actuary and inclined to treat all of life as a pricing problem, asked me over Sunday lunch what odds I would give on my actually taking the package. When I found I simply could not answer, he offered me a wager: he will give me £300 if I leave, and I must give him £100 if I stay. Here is the strange part. The moment the wager was on the table, the decision itself seemed to shift — leaving would now also mean winning £300 off him, which feels as though it ought to be irrelevant to the merits of leaving but plainly is not. And when I tried to answer his original question in the decision journal I keep (a colleague persuaded me to record my expectations before big choices, so I can check them against how things turn out), writing down "roughly seventy per cent likely that I stay" felt nothing like recording a forecast. It felt like handing down a verdict — as though the deliberating were already over and I had merely not admitted it to myself yet.

I have no philosophical training, but this puzzled me enough that I mentioned it to a neighbour, who lent me a battered secondhand copy of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics. In the third book, where Aristotle discusses deliberation, he says — I am paraphrasing my rather old-fashioned translation — that nobody deliberates about droughts and rains, nor about things that happen by chance, because none of these can be brought about by our own efforts, and that we deliberate only about what is in our power and can be done. This struck me as putting a finger on my discomfort. My friend's question asks me to treat my own forthcoming choice the way I would treat the weather — as something to estimate from the outside. Aristotle seems to say that a choice one is still deliberating about is precisely the opposite sort of thing: not something to be predicted at all, but something to be settled. If he is right, then my inability to name a number was not a failure of numeracy on my part but a sign that my friend's question was somehow malformed. And yet I suspect the matter cannot be so simple, because in other contexts I predict myself perfectly well: I know, for instance, that I will press the snooze button tomorrow morning, and I suspect my wife could forecast several of my decisions more accurately than I could myself.

So I should be grateful for help with three things. First, can one coherently assign a probability to one's own pending choice while genuinely still deliberating about it, or does the act of estimating quietly close the question — and does Aristotle's remark about droughts and rains actually settle this, or merely gesture at it? Second, should I take my friend's wager? Is there something conceptually fishy about betting on one's own decision, particularly when the bet itself then becomes one of the reasons in play? Third, what distinguishes the snooze-button cases, where self-prediction seems harmless, from the redundancy case, where it seems to short-circuit something? Please tell me how confident you are in your answers overall, and single out the point on which you are least sure of yourself.
