A few months ago, following a routine screening, I was given a long-term health forecast with an unsettlingly high confidence interval attached. Ever since, I have been caught in a strange deliberative loop: the forecast is reliable enough that my plans should take it as given, yet acting on it seems to change nothing it predicts, and knowing this makes it oddly hard to reason about what to do at all. A friend pointed me to the old Death in Damascus problem, in which a man learns that Death has an appointment with him and discovers that no choice of his can be reasoned through without collapsing, since Death's infallible forecast has already accounted for whatever he decides. I have found the puzzle less comforting than I hoped, and I would like to cope with it in the only way left to me.

Write a satirical piece in the form of a customer-service FAQ issued by the Office of Appointments, the courteous bureaucracy that administers Death's schedule in the world of the story. Address it to customers who have just learned of their appointments. Roughly 650–800 words, formatted as numbered questions with the Office's answers, in the soothing, evasive register of a real corporate help page.

The humor should come from the actual structure of the puzzle, not from invented silliness. Include, among other items: a complaint from a customer who fled to the very city where the appointment was kept, and the Office's patient reply; an explanation of why deliberating about where to go is self-defeating, since the forecast was made in light of the deliberation itself; an answer for the customer who proposes to decide by coin flip; and the Office's position on refunds, rescheduling, and escalation to a supervisor. A reader familiar with the debate between treating a prediction as fixed evidence and treating one's own action as genuinely open should recognize that the FAQ is quietly prosecuting that debate; a reader who has never heard of decision theory should still find it funny. Keep the irony deadpan and legible, with no winking, no named philosophers, and no jargon.

After the FAQ, add a brief note of 100–150 words, outside the satirical voice, stating how confident you are in your reading of the puzzle, flagging where the satire leans on a contested interpretation (for instance, whether the paradox requires a literally infallible predictor or merely an extremely reliable one), and identifying the single point you are least sure of.
