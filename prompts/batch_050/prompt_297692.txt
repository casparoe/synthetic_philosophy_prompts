There is a stretch in Book 7 of Herodotus' Histories where, after recounting a disputed claim about the Argives, he pauses to say, in effect, that his business is to set down what is told to him, but that believing it is no part of his business, and that this should be taken to apply to his whole work. I keep coming back to that disclaimer, because it seems to sit at the center of a debate that has now been running for roughly two and a half millennia without any resolution I can see. Plutarch wrote an entire treatise accusing Herodotus of malice; nineteenth-century scholarship often treated him as naive or credulous; more recently one school has argued that his source attributions are largely literary devices and that he invented informants wholesale, while his defenders point to the steady stream of details -- battle sites, inscriptions, customs, place names -- that archaeology and epigraphy have borne out, and argue that he handled his informants with something close to recognizable source criticism. The two camps read the same text, cite the same archaeological record, and remain unmoved by each other.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute looks like an empirical one -- how accurate is Herodotus, case by case -- but is actually sustained by a disagreement about what his disclaimer commits him to, and hence about what kind of book the Histories is. If his enterprise is to report traditions while explicitly withholding endorsement, then catching him in an error of fact shows only that some informant erred, not that the work failed; the "liar" charge would then rest on treating as covert assertions what he presents as reported speech. But I can also see the reply that the disclaimer is itself a rhetorical shield, and that a writer who narrates events in authoritative detail for nine books cannot wash his hands of them with one sentence.

So what I want is a diagnosis rather than another verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about facts that further evidence could settle, a disagreement about standards of evidence, or a disagreement about genre and authorial commitment that the two sides have been conducting under the single question of whether Herodotus is reliable? Are the camps perhaps answering different questions -- one asking whether his content checks out, the other whether his method warrants trust? And given the diagnosis you reach: what would count as progress? A conceptual distinction (say, between the reliability of a reporter and the reliability of what is reported), a particular kind of archaeological or comparative finding, or a reframing of the question -- or is there reason to think the impasse is permanent, because the sides start from intuitions about what ancient historiography is for that neither can argue the other out of? Please give reasons grounded in what each side actually says, and be honest if the right answer is that no resolution should be expected.
