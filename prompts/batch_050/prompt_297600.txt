I am preparing a fifty-minute undergraduate tutorial on Moritz Schlick’s account of the meaning of life. The students have encountered the essay but tend to reduce it to a slogan about pleasure, youth, or doing what one loves. For the purposes of the tutorial, take the central thesis to be that life has meaning insofar as it contains activities valued for their own sake—Schlick’s expanded notion of play—and that youth names a mode of engagement rather than chronological age. Do not write the tutorial; plan how I should teach it.

Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced, explaining the dependency at each step: the difference between asking what a life means and asking what makes it worth living; the regress generated when every activity is justified only by a later result; the distinction between an activity’s external goal, its internal point, and the pleasure it happens to produce; the move from purposeless amusement to play as activity whose point is contained in the activity itself; and the claim that youth is the capacity for this kind of engagement.

Choose one concrete example to carry the entire tutorial. It should allow the same person to perform substantially the same activity in several ways: solely for payment, solely for a future result, as a duty, and as something absorbing whose point lies partly in the doing. Explain why your example can separate these dimensions better than a more obvious case involving art, sport, or leisure, and show exactly which features of it should be introduced at which stage.

Provide a misconception map. For each predictable error—Schlick thinks meaningful activity has no external purpose; he identifies meaning with subjective enjoyment; he makes paid work or morally serious projects meaningless by definition; he uses youth to mean biological age; and he implies that any personally chosen pastime is automatically meaningful—state when in the sequence it is likely to arise, how to forestall it without derailing the lesson, and what formulation on the board would prevent it from returning.

Finish with one closing question that would reveal whether students have grasped the distinction between an activity that is enjoyed, an activity that is useful, and an activity that has its point within itself. The question should require them to classify a new case and defend the classification, not merely repeat definitions. Finally, say what I should deliberately leave out at this level—such as detailed historical context, competing interpretations of the essay, or comparisons with other theories of meaning—and give the one-sentence deferral I should offer if students ask.
