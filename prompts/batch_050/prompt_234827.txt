For my final project in a high school philosophy elective, I am writing a paper that reads Roberto Benigni's 1997 film Life Is Beautiful through Augustine's treatise On Lying (De Mendacio), and I would like help developing the reading.

First, the framework as I understand it. Augustine defines a lie as uttering something one believes to be false with the intention that the hearer believe it, which is why he says jokes are not lies: the joker's tone signals that no deception is meant. He insists that every lie is a sin, because lying turns speech against the speaker's own mind and injures the soul, which matters more than the body. But he does not treat all lies as equal. He ranks them in eight grades, from most serious to least: lies about religious teaching; lies that harm someone and benefit no one; lies that harm one person in order to benefit another; lies told for the sheer pleasure of deceiving; lies told merely to be agreeable in conversation; lies that harm no one and protect someone's worldly interests; lies that harm no one and shield someone from harm; and, least serious of all, lies that harm no one and shield someone from bodily violation. Famously, Augustine refuses even the hardest case: he argues that one may not lie to save an innocent person's life, because no bodily good can justify corrupting the soul.

The film seems almost designed to test that framework. Guido, imprisoned with his young son in a concentration camp, convinces the boy that the camp is an elaborate competition in which staying quiet, hiding, and enduring hardships earn points, and that the grand prize is a real tank. The lie works: the boy is spared the knowledge of what is happening, survives, and experiences even his father's final walk toward execution as one more comic performance in the game. The film plainly treats Guido's deception not as a sin but as the deepest expression of his love, perhaps the one truthful thing in the camp.

Here is what I would like the reading to do:

- Locate Guido's lie within Augustine's taxonomy. It seems to fall among the least serious grades, since it harms no one and shields someone, but I suspect the fit is not clean: is protecting a child's inner world from horror the kind of good Augustine's ladder even measures, or does the ladder only count bodily and material goods?
- Take Augustine's hard line seriously rather than dismissing it. Augustine would have to say that Guido sins, however admirably motivated. What is the strongest version of that verdict? Does his reasoning, that the liar damages his own soul, the part of himself ordered toward truth, capture anything the film itself shows about what the performance costs Guido?
- Ask whether the film complicates Augustine's definition. Guido's game is not a bare false statement; it is an elaborate shared fiction, sustained through play, which the boy half-collaborates in maintaining. Does it still count as deception in Augustine's sense? Is there a meaningful difference between lying to someone and building a false world with them?
- Note where the film resists the reading. Does the ending, in which a real tank actually appears, cheat by letting the fiction be retrospectively vindicated in a way Augustine would call mere luck? And is the film's implied claim, that some truths are too heavy for a child and that withholding them can be a form of care, something Augustine's framework cannot even state?

Please keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of the taxonomy, and do not soften Augustine to make him agree with the movie. End by saying what, if anything, the film contributes to the philosophical debate about lying that Augustine's argument alone does not, and where, honestly, the film simply refuses the question Augustine thinks is decisive.
