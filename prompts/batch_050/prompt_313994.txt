In Book I, Chapter VIII ("Of the Wages of Labour") of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith writes:

"In all such disputes the masters can hold out much longer. A landlord, a farmer, a master manufacturer, a merchant, though they did not employ a single workman, could generally live a year or two upon the stocks which they have already acquired. Many workmen could not subsist a week, few could subsist a month, and scarce any a year without employment. In the long run the workman may be as necessary to his master as his master is to him; but the necessity is not so immediate."

Treat this passage as articulating what we might call the endurance asymmetry thesis: in a bargaining conflict between employers and workers, each side's power depends less on the merits of its demands than on how long it can afford to wait, and the two sides differ systematically in that waiting capacity. Please describe the most important implications of this thesis for how a just legal order should structure the right to organize and to strike. In particular, address the following: (1) If Smith is right that bargaining outcomes track endurance rather than desert, does this give the state a reason to reshape the endurance of the parties rather than merely to referee their conflict—and if so, does justice favor leveling workers' endurance up (for instance, through legal protection of strike funds, eligibility of strikers for public income support, or prohibitions on permanently replacing striking workers) or leveling employers' endurance down (for instance, through restrictions on lockouts or on stockpiling inventory in anticipation of a strike)? (2) Smith observes that the long-run mutual dependence of master and workman does nothing to equalize their short-run positions. What follows from this temporal structure for the legal treatment of tactics, such as secondary picketing and sympathy strikes, whose whole point is to make the employer's necessity more immediate by widening the conflict? (3) Where, if anywhere, does the endurance asymmetry thesis break down in a modern economy—consider wealthy workers, dual-earner households, firms with thin margins and perishable output, and strikes in essential services—and what should happen to the legal privileges justified by the thesis in those cases? Finally, assess whether endurance asymmetry, even where it persists, is actually an injustice calling for correction, or merely a morally neutral fact like differential talent that labor law exploits for its own purposes.
