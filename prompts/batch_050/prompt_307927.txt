Two friends of mine had a proper row in my kitchen last weekend, and as a retired chemist who's only lately started reading philosophy I'm finding I can't settle it, so I'm handing it over. Background: Geoff is selling his cottage up in the valley. The buyer asked him straight out whether the place had ever flooded. Geoff answered, truthfully, that he'd never had a drop of water in the house. What he didn't say is that he bought the cottage in 2009, two years after it was waist-deep in the 2007 floods, which he knew all about. Sale went through. My other friend Anne found out and called him a liar to his face. It escalated. Roughly how it went:

(1) Geoff: a lie is a false statement. Every word I said was true. The buyer drew his own conclusion from my answer; conclusions are the buyer's department, not mine.

(2) Anne: the wrongness of a lie can't live in the falsity of the sentence, because the harm Geoff did is identical to the harm if he'd said it never flooded. Same intention, same false belief planted, same money extracted. If the words were just the delivery mechanism, the morality can't hinge on whether the mechanism was an assertion or an inference.

(3) Geoff: it hinges on who does the work. When I assert something, I'm vouching for it — the hearer gets the belief on my authority, so if it's false the corruption is entirely mine. When you infer something from my true statement, you've done part of the epistemic labour yourself, and that splits the responsibility. Lying is forging someone's signature; misleading is leaving the office door unlocked and letting them walk in. Both might be wrong, but they're not the same wrong.

(4) Anne: by that logic every con artist is half-exonerated, since cons only work because the victim does some of the inferring. And she tried a variant: suppose Geoff had got his wife to open the door and say the same true sentence. Is she less guilty than if she'd said "it never flooded"? Suppose he'd said it in a letter rather than in person? Anne said she couldn't see how any of those dials change anything, and a principle that makes them matter is a principle that rewards careful phrasing rather than honest dealing.

(5) Geoff then wheeled out his big idea, which he calls the "division of epistemic labour": in cooperative talk — asking a friend for directions, a patient asking a doctor — the hearer reasonably expects the whole picture, so misleading is just as bad as lying. But in adversarial talk — haggling, poker, job interviews, being interrogated — the hearer is on notice that you're not their research assistant, and then misleading is fine, and arguably lying is too. And a house sale, he says, is adversarial: caveat emptor and all that.

(6) Anne: a sale isn't poker. The buyer asked a direct factual question in plain English, which switches the exchange back into cooperative mode; you can't unilaterally declare the conversation adversarial the moment an awkward question arrives, or "adversarial" just means "convenient for me". She also asked him where his principle puts a nurse answering a patient who's scared of an operation — cooperative, adversarial, or does it flip question by question?

(7) Geoff's last stand: if planting a false belief is the whole crime, then tactful silence is lying too. He brought up the eulogy Anne gave at a funeral last spring, which she privately thought was the dullest speech ever delivered — she told the family it was "very moving", which was technically true because it moved her to check her watch twice. By her own rule she's a liar as well, and a less careful one than him.

I have my own leanings but I'll keep them out of it. Can you go through move by move and say who's right at each step — not just the overall winner? In particular: does the vouching/inferring distinction in (3) actually do moral work, does Geoff's cooperative/adversarial split survive Anne's objection in (6), and is the eulogy case in (7) a genuine reductio or is something different going on with tact? Also, if there's something both of them are missing entirely, I'd like to know — I've turned it over for days and keep flip-flopping, which is an unfamiliar sensation for someone who spent forty years settling disputes with a graph.
