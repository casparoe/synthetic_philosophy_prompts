My study group has been working through the divine foreknowledge and human freedom debate, and our discussion leader circulated a one-page handout on Molinism that I'd like vetted before we rely on it. It reads as follows:

"Middle knowledge is God's knowledge, logically prior to any creative decree, of what every possible free creature would freely do in any set of circumstances in which God might place them. Standing between God's natural knowledge of all possibilities and his free knowledge of what will actually happen, it lets God select which possible world to actualize in full knowledge of how every free choice within it will go. Providence is thereby rendered meticulous -- God guarantees the course of history -- while freedom is preserved, because God does not cause the choices; he merely knows, in advance of willing anything, what the creatures would do.

Critics raise the so-called grounding objection: if the agents and their circumstances do not yet exist, what makes these counterfactuals true? Molinists answer that the counterfactuals are grounded in the creatures themselves, since a free agent simply is the kind of being with a settled disposition to act one way rather than another in given circumstances, and God knows each agent's disposition.

The view also explains how prophecy about free actions can be infallible, and some Molinists invoke 'transworld depravity' to explain why God could not have actualized a world in which everyone freely accepts salvation: in every feasible world, some creatures would freely refuse grace."

Something about the grounding paragraph strikes me as too quick -- if the counterfactuals are fixed by the creature's settled disposition, I would have thought that was precisely the sort of thing the objection means to pressure, and I dimly recall that transworld depravity belongs to a different argument about the problem of evil rather than to questions about salvation. But the handout's author is more read in this literature than I am, and the overall architecture (three logical moments in God's knowledge, world-selection in light of counterfactuals) matches what I've seen elsewhere.

Could you go through the handout and tell me which parts a knowledgeable Molinist would endorse as stated, which are fair simplifications, and which are misrepresentations -- whether of Molinism itself or of the neighboring notions it borrows? In particular: does the handout's answer to the grounding objection reflect how Molinists actually respond, and does the response, as stated, secure libertarian freedom or quietly trade it away? And if the problematic passages are corrected, does the handout's larger claim -- that middle knowledge reconciles exhaustive providence with genuinely free choice -- stand on firmer or shakier ground than the handout suggests?
