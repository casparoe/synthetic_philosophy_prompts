I'm a second-year philosophy student taking a module on metaphysics, and we've just spent two weeks on facts, propositions, and predication. Our lecturer mentioned in passing that there is a famous regress argument in this area, due to a British idealist, which some people think destroys the very notion of a fact and others think is a sophism. After the seminar, a friend of mine tried to reconstruct the argument for me over coffee, and I've written it up as cleanly as I can. The trouble is that every step looks plausible to me when I stare at it individually, yet the conclusion strikes me as insane, and I'd like to know exactly where I'm being had.

Here is the argument. (1) A proposition or fact, such as Desdemona's loving Cassio, is not a mere list of its constituents: the list Desdemona, loving, Cassio contains precisely the same items, but a list is not the sort of thing that is true or false, and nothing about the list settles that loving runs from Desdemona to Cassio rather than the other way around. (2) So there must be something in virtue of which the constituents are unified into a single truth-evaluable whole, rather than remaining a list. (3) Whatever does this unifying must itself be an item that stands in some relation to the things it unifies, since there is no other way for it to connect them and do any explanatory work. (4) But then the unifying item, together with the original constituents, once again forms only a list: Desdemona, loving, Cassio, unifier. By the reasoning of (2), something further is needed to unify these, and then something further still, without end. (5) A regress of this kind is vicious: at every stage, the work of unification is simply handed off to the next stage, so it is never actually completed at any stage, which means no unified whole ever gets delivered. (6) Therefore nothing can unify the constituents of a would-be proposition or fact. (7) Therefore there are no propositions or facts: nothing is ever the case, and nothing is true or false.

I refuse to accept (7), partly because (7) itself appears to be the sort of thing that would have to be true if anything were. If I had to protect one premise, it would be (1), since the contrast between a sentence's meaning and a mere list seems to me the one solid thing in the area; I'm also attached to the idea in (2) that the difference calls for explanation rather than silence. Please tell me which single step I should reject, and commit to your answer rather than surveying the options for me. Explain why that step looked innocent to me in the first place, and then tell me honestly what rejecting it costs: which intuitive claims I must give up, whether it commits me to treating some things in reality as fundamentally different in kind from others (and whether that is a respectable position or a stipulation), and whether rejecting your chosen step actually dissolves the argument or merely blocks this version while leaving a nearby one standing.
