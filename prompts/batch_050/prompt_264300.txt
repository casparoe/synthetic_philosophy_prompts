So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general audience magazine, and I'm doing a long feature on whether today's chatbots are "really" thinking. As part of my prep I went back to Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I last read in college, and a friend of mine who's a grad student in CS wrote up a little explainer I was thinking of adapting into a sidebar. Here's what he gave me:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows that any system of rules powerful enough to do arithmetic contains true statements that the system can never prove. So in a sense, no machine built on fixed rules can ever know all the truths that we humans can know, because we can step outside any system and see the truth of its Gödel sentence from the outside. This is why Hofstadter wrote GEB: his argument is that consciousness comes from exactly this trick, the mind being a self-referential 'strange loop' that talks about itself the way the Gödel sentence talks about arithmetic. Since a machine can't escape its own rules the way a self-aware mind can, Hofstadter's conclusion is that genuine machine intelligence is impossible -- the loop is something only biological brains can pull off."

Now, something smells off to me here. My memory of GEB (which admittedly is hazy, it's been years) is that Hofstadter was actually pretty bullish on machine intelligence, and that the strange loop stuff was supposed to explain how selves arise in any sufficiently rich system, not prove brains are special. Also the "we can always see the truth the machine can't" move rings a bell as something other people -- Penrose? -- have argued, and I thought it was considered pretty contentious.

Can you give me an accuracy audit I can actually use? Specifically: go through it claim by claim and tell me (1) what's basically right, (2) what's the kind of simplification that's defensible in a sidebar, (3) what's a genuine error or misattribution I'd get letters about. And then the big one: if I fix the errors, does the closing line about machine intelligence being impossible survive at all, or does the corrected version actually point the other way? I'd also love a sentence or two on how the strange loop idea and the Gödel sentence analogy are actually related in the book, since I suspect my friend has the direction of that argument backwards. Don't worry about giving me page numbers or a bibliography, I can chase citations myself -- I just need to know what to trust before I hand this to my editor.
