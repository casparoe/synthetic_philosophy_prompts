I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about analogy -- basically, the claim some cognitive scientists make that analogy isn't just a rhetorical device but is the engine of all thinking, right down to recognizing that a chair is a chair. My editor wants me to actually take a position on this rather than just quoting people, so I've been stress-testing my own gut reactions. Here's where I land on cases:

1. Using water waves to explain sound waves to a lay reader: genuine analogy, and a good one.
2. The old picture of the atom as a tiny solar system: genuine analogy, useful in its time, and its failures were as instructive as its successes.
3. Saying the heart is a pump: analogy, and maybe the single best one in all of physiology writing.
4. Calling the internet an "information superhighway": technically an analogy but a bad one, because the disanalogies did more harm than the analogy did good.
5. A kid who says shoes are like gloves for your feet: genuinely analogical, and honestly kind of impressive.
6. A pun, like calling a dull dentist "a little boring": not an analogy at all, just a sound coincidence. Nothing carries over.
7. My boss is a real pig: not an analogy, just an insult wearing analogy's clothes.
8. Noticing that standing in an airport security line is "the same thing" as the queue for a rollercoaster: that IS an analogy, and a pretty deep one, even though nothing is being explained.
9. A scientist recognizing that an unsolved problem in her field has the same shape as a solved one from a different field: the deepest analogy of all, this is basically how discovery works.
10. Just categorizing something ordinary -- seeing a weird piece of furniture and going "that's a chair": NOT an analogy. This is the one the analogy-is-everything people need, and I'm not buying it.

Now, I can feel the tension in my own list. 6, 7, and 8 are the problem. If the security line counts because a whole relational structure maps over (waiting, crowd control, the psychology of impatience), then why doesn't the pig, which maps a relation too (how the boss treats people)? And if the difference is about how much structure transfers, the pun seems to transfer none and yet the kid's gloves thing transfers barely any either -- it's mostly surface. So I have three cases my instincts treat differently and I can't say why.

Is there one principled account that delivers all ten verdicts -- say, some combination of "real analogies map systems of relations, not isolated features" plus something about the mapping doing explanatory or inferential work -- or do I have to give one up? If I have to give one up, tell me which one you'd sacrifice and why. And then tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't put on the list: a translator choosing a word in the target language because it "feels like" the original, and an AI system that produces a great analogy by pattern-matching without (arguably) understanding anything. That second one matters because my feature ends on AI, obviously.
