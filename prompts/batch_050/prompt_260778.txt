I have no academic background in philosophy, but over the past year I have fallen down a rabbit hole of early Chinese thought, reading translations of Mencius and Xunzi on my own and arguing about them with a friend who teaches kindergarten. By day I coordinate the adult-education program at our public library, and I have rashly agreed to give a short talk at a weekend workshop run by a local education cooperative. The audience will be about thirty people: mostly elementary-school teachers trained in progressive, child-centered pedagogy, plus a handful of parents and one or two administrators. Their instincts run strongly in the direction that children are naturally curious, prosocial, and good, and that the educator's job is to remove obstacles and provide a rich environment rather than to shape or correct. In other words, this is about the most Mencian room imaginable, and I intend to stand up in front of it and defend Xunzi.

My thesis is that Xunzi is closer to the truth about human nature than Mencius, and that classrooms should be designed accordingly: that children's spontaneous impulses point toward self-interest, envy, and immediate gratification, that goodness is an artifact built through deliberate practice, external structure, and ritualized habit -- Xunzi's image of warped wood that becomes straight only through steaming, pressing, and sustained force -- and that Mencius's rival picture, in which virtue is present from the start as tender sprouts needing only nourishment, illustrated by the involuntary alarm anyone supposedly feels at seeing a child about to fall into a well, flatters teachers into under-structuring their classrooms and then blaming individual children when disorder results. I plan to concede that both thinkers agree ordinary people can become exemplary, so my real quarrel is not with Mencius scholarship but with the modern romanticism that has absorbed his conclusions without his arguments. My own wrinkle, which I am least sure of, is that the two masters disagree less about the destination than about whether becoming good is a process of discovery or of construction, and that construction is the better model for education.

Please prepare me for the question period: twenty minutes of talk followed by thirty of questions. I want the objections this particular audience will actually raise, not the ones a panel of sinologists would raise -- phrased in the vocabulary these teachers genuinely use, things like intrinsic motivation, behaviorism, deficit framing, self-esteem, and growth mindset, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how much damage it would do if I handle it badly. For each, tell me whether it has real force (I am especially worried about developmental evidence that very young children show spontaneous helping behavior, since the child-at-the-well scenario is roughly testable and may partly vindicate Mencius), give me a two-minute reply, and mark where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks.
