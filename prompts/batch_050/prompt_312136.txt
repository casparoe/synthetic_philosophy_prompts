I work on the backend for a wildlife tracking app (think a bootleg iNaturalist) and I've run into something that's honestly melting my brain a little, and I want to know what philosophy is hiding inside our data model.

Here's the setup. We sync against a big public taxonomic database. In our schema, a species is basically a row in a table: stable ID, latin name, done. Every user observation points at a species ID. But every year the upstream database publishes updates where species get "split" (one species becomes two, usually because someone's DNA study says two populations are more different than we thought) or "lumped" (two species get merged into one). When that happens, thousands of our existing observations now point at a taxon that either doesn't exist anymore or means something different than it did when the user logged it. There's no clean migration path, we just kind of shrug and remap.

Then there's the photo-ID model. It's trained to output a species label, and we score it as wrong when it says species A and the community says species B. Except the split/lump situation means sometimes taxonomists themselves don't agree on whether A and B are even different species. The model isn't making a biology error, it's caught in a bookkeeping dispute. Meanwhile a couple of our contributors keep pushing us to just define species by a genetic-distance cutoff ("over 2% different in this one gene = different species") because it would make the database self-consistent and automatable.

And the kicker: conservation status is attached per-species. Split one "common, least concern" species into two and you can manufacture two endangered species overnight, without a single animal's situation changing.

So: what theory of what a species IS does this whole stack presuppose? Like, does the data model only make sense if species are real things out there in nature that we discover and occasionally get wrong (in which case splits are bug fixes)? Or would it work just as well if species are basically filing conventions humans invented for convenience (in which case splits are just schema migrations and nobody's wrong)? Is our setup even internally consistent, given that the photo model treats species labels as having one right answer while the taxonomic backbone treats them as perpetually revisable? And what would actually have to change in the design if we committed to one view or the other -- I'm especially curious what the "species are just a convention" version of the app would look like, because my gut says it might work *better*, wich feels like it should be a red flag.

Please seperate out what the practice strictly assumes from what it just happens to be compatible with -- I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined and several theories of species would license the exact same schema.
