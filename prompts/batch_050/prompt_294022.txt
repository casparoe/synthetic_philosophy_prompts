I've been working through money-pump arguments on my own, and I keep finding that my judgments about cases don't sit under any principle I can state. Below are five cases with my verdicts, then three further cases I haven't made up my mind about. What I want from you: a candidate principle (or at most two) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically; a clause-by-clause account of which case motivates which part; verdicts on the three undecided cases; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, since I suspect at least one has to go. Please write in American English.

Case 1. Priya prefers a window seat to an aisle seat, an aisle to a middle seat, and a middle to a window, and would pay $1 to trade up at each step. A broker cycles her through all three, collects $3, and returns her to the window. Verdict: her preferences are irrational, and the pump is what reveals it.

Case 2. Marcus trades his convertible plus $500 for a sedan after learning his insurance premium has tripled. Externally this is one step of a pump: money out, a swap, a reversal relative to last month. Verdict: fully rational. So the bare pattern of paying to swap cannot itself be the defect.

Case 3. Dana knows that tonight she will want a third glass of wine and tomorrow morning she will regret it. This afternoon she pays a clinic $40 for a drug that makes alcohol nauseating for a day. Money flows out; nothing comes back but the removal of an option. Verdict: rational, maybe the paradigm of diachronic rationality.

Case 4. Elias plans to invest steadily for twenty years and ignore market panics. Midway, a panic hits and at that moment selling feels clearly better to him. He sticks to the plan anyway. Verdict: rational.

Case 5. Farah has Priya's cyclic seat preferences but, offered the first $1 swap, refuses, because she sees the whole tree. Note that at each individual node the swap is worth $1 to her by her own lights; only backward induction saves her. Verdict: also rational.

The trouble: in a suitably constructed tree, Elias's resolute choice and Farah's sophisticated reasoning would recommend opposite moves at the same node, and I called both rational. And Dana pays against her own future preferences while Priya pays in accordance with her stable ones, and I split them. Cases I haven't decided: (a) a reverse pump, where the broker pays Priya fifty cents per swap to cycle her endlessly; (b) an agent who ranks neither of two options above the other and is exploited through a sequence of forced trades with small fees; (c) a committee with cyclic majority preferences that an agenda-setter steers while extracting concessions at each vote. Finally, tell me whether your principle treats pumps as proofs of irrationality or merely as marks of imprudence, and what follows from that for the status of transitivity as a requirement.
