So I've been chewing on the unilateralist's curse — roughly, the idea that when any member of a group can single-handedly launch some initiative, it'll tend to get launched even when it shouldn't, because the person with the most optimistic estimate of its value is the one who acts. I want to figure out what I actually think about when going it alone is ok, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle (if any) captures the pattern.

1. A ten-person lab works on a modified pathogen. Any member can independently email the full genome to an outside collaborator who's been asking for it. The member most confident it's harmless hits send. My verdict: reckless, clearly wrong, should've gone through the group.

2. Eight employees each suspect the CFO is cooking the books, and any of them could report it to the regulator on their own. The most confident one does, and turns out to be right. My verdict: totally fine. Good, even.

3. Four friends all suspect that their friend Maya's boyfriend is cheating on her. Any of them could just tell her. The most confident one does, over brunch. My verdict: bad move — they should have talked amongst themselves first.

4. One engineer at an AI lab leaks internal evals showing the model deceives users in ways the public doesn't know about, against the consensus of the safety team, who wanted more time. My verdict: I actually think this one's justified.

5. In 1938 a private citizen has the chance to assassinate Hitler and takes the shot. My verdict: obviously fine?? Even though it's the most unilateral act imaginable and nobody affected consented.

6. A hospital allows either parent to consent to an experimental treatment for their kid. The more optimistic parent signs the form while the other is still mulling it over. My verdict: fine, that's just how consent works there.

A few things that bug me: 2 and 3 look structurally identical (one of several, most optimistic person acts, irreversible disclosure of info) but I judge them opposite. 1 and 4 both involve defying the group's epistemic consensus about a risky disclosure, and I judge those opposite too. And 5 seems to wreck any principle based on consent or authority. Also I'm not convinced the curse itself — the winner's-curse point that the most optimistic estimate is probably inflated — is what's actually doing the work in all of these; in some of them the issue feels more like who has the right to decide.

My tentative principle was: unilateral action is fine iff it's reversible. But whistleblowing and leaking are irreversible and I still think they're fine, so that's broken. Repair it or replace it.

What I want from you: state a principle (or two candidates if one won't do it) precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Be honest about which of my verdicts you can't save, and if you have to overturn one, say which and why. Then apply your principle to two cases I didn't list: (a) a security researcher who finds a zero-day in widely used software and can unilaterally publish working exploit code; (b) one of twelve jurors who can trigger a mistrial by revealing she did her own research on the case.
