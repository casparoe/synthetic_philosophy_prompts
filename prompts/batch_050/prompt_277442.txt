I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is an elderly analytic philosopher — in his youth an uncompromising verificationist, loosely in the mold of A. J. Ayer — who suffers a cardiac arrest during an operation, is clinically dead for several minutes, and is revived with a report of an experience he cannot bring himself to dismiss. (Ayer himself, after his own near-death episode late in life, conceded that it had slightly weakened his confidence that death ends everything, while insisting it gave him no evidence for survival; my novel takes that concession and runs with it.) A young philosopher visits the old man in convalescence and presses him on the question the novel's plot needs answered honestly: what would a theory of personal survival of bodily death have to look like before someone of his intellectual conscience could take it seriously? I want that scene to rest on real philosophy, not on my intuitions, so I am asking you to draft the specification the young philosopher would lay out.

Give me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of life after death must meet to count as a serious candidate, with the case or consideration behind each. Among the ones I already suspect belong on the list, and would like you to develop, sharpen, or overturn: the theory must say what survives and in what sense it is the same person who died, rather than a replica, a successor, or merely someone carrying the deceased's memories; it must state what evidence could in principle count for or against it, answering the verificationist's charge that survival-talk floats free of any possible observation; it must confront the problem that testimony from mediums, apparitions, and apparent memories of earlier lives seems equally well explained by undiscovered faculties of the living, so that nothing in the evidence discriminates survival from exotic alternatives; it must have a position on duplication cases — if two bodies could be reconstituted, or a perfect psychological copy made, which if either would be the survivor, and what the question even means; it must choose between disembodied survival and some form of bodily resurrection and own the costs of the choice; and it must either square with the apparent dependence of mind on brain or say precisely where it breaks with the science.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is a genuine requirement on any theory or merely the prejudice of one camp (orthodox theologians, empirical parapsychologists, reductive materialists), and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I particularly want to know whether empirical confirmability in principle and genuine numerical identity of the survivor with the deceased can hold together, given that any behavioural or memory-based evidence seems compatible with a mere simulacrum. Deliver the list as a table with exactly these columns: Requirement; Motivating case or consideration; Essential or camp-specific (if the latter, name the camp); In tension with (list the conflicting rows, if any). After the table, add two short prose paragraphs: one identifying the single requirement that excludes the most existing contenders, and one stating which subset of the desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable and what a theorist gives up by dropping each member of it. No bibliography or reading list — the young philosopher works from the armchair, and so should you.
