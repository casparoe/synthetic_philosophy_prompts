So I work as a backend engineer at a mid-size consumer electronics company, and somehow I've ended up on our "responsible sourcing committee" (I volunteered thinking it would be like two meetings a year, lol). We just got our audit results back for a factory in Vietnam that assembles one of our products, and it's a mess: wages are above the local legal minimum and above what comparable farms/shops nearby pay, but overtime regularly blows past legal caps, a couple of fire exits were blocked, and dorm conditions are rough. Half the committee wants to cut the contract and find a cleaner supplier. The complication is that a group of workers there, through a local labor NGO, has basically begged us not to leave -- pulling the contract would likely mean layoffs, and they say this job, bad as it is, is better than their alternatives. On top of that, our own procurement team quietly admits that our habit of last-minute order changes and aggressive price squeezes is a big part of why the factory leans on forced overtime in the first place.

Here's where I'm stuck philosophically (I have zero philosophy background, so bear with me): if the workers genuinely prefer us staying, and staying makes them better off than any realistic alternative, is our relationship with them still exploitative in a bad way, or is it mutually beneficial and therefore fine-ish? And does it change things that we're partly causing the bad conditions through our own purchasing behavior, rather than just "finding" them there? My gut says leaving lets us feel clean while making the workers worse off, which seems backwards, but my coworker argues that staying means we're actively profiting from coercion-ish conditions and just using the workers' consent as a fig leaf.

Please draft a memo I can actually bring to the committee: a clear recommendation (stay-and-fix, exit, or some third option) with the reasoning laid out, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the side you come out against -- don't strawman it. Also tell me what would change your mind: what fact or argument would flip the recommendation, and where your reasoning is most likely to fall apart. End the memo with a last line that starts with "Verdict:" followed by the recommendation in a few words.
