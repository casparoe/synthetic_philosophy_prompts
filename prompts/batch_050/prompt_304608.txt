I am preparing an application for a nine-month graduate research grant in epistemology and need a research proposal of roughly 1,500 words. The project concerns peer disagreement, and I have a half-formed idea I would like you to develop into a proper proposal rather than simply restate.

The motivating thought is this. The standard debate between conciliationist and steadfast views asks what you should believe upon discovering that an epistemic peer judges differently from you. But the two parties in real disagreements may themselves hold different views about that question. Suppose I am a conciliationist and my peer is steadfast. I lower my confidence towards hers; she does not move at all. Repeat the encounter across many cases and the conciliationist appears to haemorrhage confidence in precisely those of her beliefs that stubborn people reject, while the steadfast person is never penalised. It looks as though conciliationism systematically rewards stubbornness and punishes the very disposition it recommends. I want to know whether this asymmetry is a genuine structural defect of conciliationist views or an illusion produced by how the scenario is set up, and what follows for how conciliationism should be formulated. One possibility I want explored is whether conciliationists should say that one defers not to a peer's opinions but to a peer's method of responding to disagreement, which might change what conciliation demands when the peer's method is steadfastness itself.

Please write the proposal itself, addressed to a general academic selection committee rather than to specialists, and structured to include the following:

1. A precise statement of the central question, framed so that it admits of a determinate answer, together with two or three sub-questions that fall out of it.
2. An explanation of why the existing conciliationism-versus-steadfastness debate, as it is standardly conducted, leaves this question to one side, and why the answer is not obvious from either camp's standard commitments.
3. The method: what I would actually do over the nine months, broken into phases. This should be conceptual and formal work that a single researcher can do without empirical resources, and should include some attempt to model the iterated-disagreement scenario precisely, since intuitions about it are likely to mislead without a model.
4. What the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide between them, and what a deliverable would look like in each case.
5. What would count as the project failing, and an honest account of the main way it could fail, for example if the asymmetry turns out to dissolve under any reasonable formalisation.
6. A realistic assessment of scope: what has to be excluded (e.g. the bearing of this on disagreement about religion or politics) for nine months to suffice.

Do not include a bibliography or a literature review with references; you may name positions and arguments in the usual way and I will supply citations myself. Please write the proposal in British English.
