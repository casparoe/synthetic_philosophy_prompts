I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for more than a decade, and the threads on free will, blame, and criminal punishment are among the ones that most reliably generate heat without light. My diagnosis is that participants use the word desert for at least five distinct commitments and then argue past each other for hundreds of comments. Having recently worked through the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on just deserts and punishment, I want to build a proper sorting instrument for my reading group: a decision tree that walks a reader from an initial commitment about punishment to a named position, so that our next discussion can begin from where people actually land rather than from the slogans they endorse.

Requirements for the tree:

- It begins from a single starting commitment, roughly: the state's practice of deliberately imposing suffering on people judged to have done wrong stands in need of justification, and I want to know which justification, if any, succeeds.
- Six to eight branch nodes. Each node is a question built around a concrete case or claim that an intelligent non-philosopher can answer without knowing any position labels. No node text may contain terms like basic desert, compatibilism, or consequentialism; those belong only in the key.
- The following cases must appear somewhere in the tree, one per node at most: a convicted wrongdoer who could be given a pill producing the felt suffering of a ten-year sentence in a single afternoon, after which he retains no memory of it and functions normally; a man whose violent impulses turn out to be caused by a treatable brain tumor, the removal of which ends the impulses; framing and punishing a defendant known to be innocent, where doing so would reliably prevent a deadly riot and would never be discovered; a carrier of a dangerous infectious disease who has done nothing wrong but must be confined to protect others; and a community that could eliminate three quarters of its violent crime over a generation by changing childhood conditions, at real cost to other goods.
- The leaves must include at least the following, though you may add others if the tree generates them: the view that wrongdoers simply deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrongdoing, full stop; the view associated with Dennett, on which creatures who can understand and respond to reasons are properly held responsible, blame and proportionate sanction are justified by what they do for such creatures, and the demand for some deeper ultimate desert is a mistake; the view associated with Caruso, on which no one is deserving of blame or punishment in the deep sense because everything we do results from factors beyond our control, and coercive measures against dangerous people are justified only on the model of quarantine, with the least restrictive means and no suffering for its own sake; a purely forward-looking deterrence view that imposes no constraint against punishing the innocent; a communicative view on which punishment addresses the wrongdoer as a fellow citizen who owes an answer for what he did; and a restorative or abolitionist view that wants to replace punishment with repair.
- Build the tree from the logical space first: the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. For every combination of answers that no listed position occupies, mark the empty cell and give a one- or two-sentence diagnosis: is it empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?
- For each pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, say so explicitly and name the question that would separate them.

Format of the response, in this order:

1. Diagnostic questions. The six to eight node questions, each followed by one sentence on why the positions come apart at that point. About 60 words per question.
2. The tree. A nested outline or diagram from the starting commitment to the leaves, as compact as you can make it while remaining legible.
3. Position key. Each leaf mapped to its named position, one or two sentences each.
4. Empty cells. Roughly 150 words.
5. Limits of the instrument. Roughly 150 words: which leaves the tree cannot separate, what would separate them, and one kind of reader it will misclassify.

The intended reader is empirically minded, allergic to jargon, and suspicious of any question that smuggles its answer into its wording. Write the node questions accordingly.
