In my school's philosophy club, we've been discussing the idea that good explanations of complex things should be cranes — built up gradually from simple, non-miraculous processes — rather than skyhooks that drop in fully formed from nowhere. Last week two members, Jordan and Maya, got into a disagreement about whether one particular explanation actually counted as a crane, and neither would concede. I'd like you to settle it, but move by move rather than just declaring a winner.

The background: Jordan had explained why exaggerated health scares spread so much faster on social media than corrections do. His explanation was that posts compete for attention, that alarming posts get shared more, and so over many rounds of sharing, alarming content outcompetes calm content — no one has to intend the outcome, it just emerges from selection. He called this a textbook crane.

(1) Maya objected that this was greedy reductionism: he'd skipped from "alarming posts get shared more" straight to the whole ecosystem of misinformation, ignoring the psychology of why people share alarming things, the design of the platforms' recommendation systems, and the role of accounts that deliberately manufacture scares. A real crane, she said, has to be built floor by floor, and he'd just gestured at the ground and the roof.

(2) Jordan replied that every explanation skips levels — her biology teacher explains photosynthesis without mentioning quantum mechanics, and nobody calls that greedy. Skipping levels can't itself be the sin, or no explanation would ever pass. The question has to be whether the level you work at does the explanatory work, and he claimed selection-on-shares genuinely does explain why scares outrun corrections.

(3) Maya answered that she'd never said skipping levels was the problem. The problem, she said, is assuming the crane reaches before checking. His selection story explains why alarming content spreads once people are inclined to share it, but it silently takes for granted the inclination itself — where did the audience's appetite for scares come from? If that appetite is doing the real work, then his crane is only scaffolding around a hidden assumption, and a crane built around an unexplained miracle is a skyhook wearing a hard hat.

(4) Jordan's final move was that her standard makes the whole crane/skyhook distinction useless. Every crane rests on something it doesn't explain — natural selection presupposes a physics it never justifies — so if any unexplained foundation turns a crane into a disguised skyhook, then there are no cranes at all, and the word skyhook stops meaning anything. She'd built a criticism that can't fail, which he said is exactly the kind of too-easy move the concept of greedy reductionism was meant to warn against.

Please go through moves (1) through (4) one at a time: for each, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and explain why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether there's anything — about what makes an explanation genuinely bottom-up rather than just bottom-flavored — that both of them seem to be missing.
