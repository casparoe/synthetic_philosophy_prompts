A friend from my philosophy reading group came up with this argument and I find the conclusion absurd but can't pin down the hole. (1) Suppose some action -- sending an email, publishing a draft, raising an alarm -- can be taken by any one member of a big group, and will be taken by whichever member's private estimate of its value is most optimistic. (2) Estimates are noisy: some err high, some err low, and the highest of many noisy estimates typically overshoots the true value, same as the winner's curse in auctions. (3) So in setups like this the action gets taken even when its true value is negative, and the bigger the group the more often that happens (this is apparently called the unilateralist's curse). (4) If you knew your estimate was the most optimistic one in the group, you'd have to discount it as biased upward, enough that it stops justifying the action. (5) You can never tell whose estimate is highest, but in a large group its very unlikely to be yours, so you should discount yours just the same, and the conclusion stands: don't act. (6) So whenever an action could just as well be taken by lots of other people, you shouldn't take it yourself. (7) But nearly anything worth doing -- warning a colleague about a danger, reporting a security bug, speaking up in a meeting -- is something lots of others could also do. (8) So you should almost never warn, report, or speak up, which is crazy. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (2) and (7) than accept the conclusion, but walk me through what each candidate fix costs, including just biting the bullet at (8).
