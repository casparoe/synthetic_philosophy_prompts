I am a biostatistician. My working vocabulary is hierarchical models, partial pooling, shrinkage, exchangeability, conditional independence, and out-of-sample validation. I have recently been reading the philosophical literature on the reference class problem and on the "problem of the single case" for frequency interpretations of probability, and I want to know whether this is a problem I already solve in my day job or a problem I cannot even see from where I stand.

Work with this concrete setup. An insurer must price a mortality risk for one specific applicant: a 44-year-old woman who smokes, runs long distances, is recently divorced, lives in a high-altitude city, has early cardiac death on her mother's side of the family but not her father's, and a clean recent screening. For every frequency you might use, there is a class — all smokers, all runners, all smoking runners, all divorced 44-year-old smoking runners at altitude with maternal cardiac history — and the implied frequencies disagree. Finer classes give less stable numbers; coarser classes ignore features that seem obviously relevant; and for any finite case you can always add one more feature and change the number again. The classic proposal in the frequentist literature is, roughly: take the narrowest class for which you have dependable statistics. The philosophical worry is that this is a rule for coping with sparse data, not an answer to the question of which frequency is her probability.

Task one: translate. Restate the reference class problem, the narrowest-class proposal, and the single-case worry in my vocabulary, as precisely as you can. Which parts map cleanly onto choosing an exchangeability partition or a level of a hierarchical model? Is the narrowest-class rule just the bias-variance tradeoff with a different name? Does "her probability" translate as a posterior predictive mean, a random effect, an estimand, or nothing at all?

Task two: the residue report. Which elements of the philosophical debate did you have to distort to fit, and which have no expression in my framework at all? I am especially interested in whether the idea that there is a fact of the matter about this one woman's probability — as opposed to a question of which model predicts best out-of-sample — survives translation, and whether the distinction between classes that are causally homogeneous and classes that merely predict well does.

Task three: my working suspicion is that the reference class problem is a model-selection problem and that statistics dissolved it with shrinkage and cross-validation, leaving the philosophers to argue about a pseudo-question. Test that suspicion honestly rather than confirming it. If there is a residue no statistical method addresses — about single-case chance, or about what it is rational for an agent who must act once to believe — say whether it indicts my framework or the philosophers' question. If it helps, consider whether your answer changes when the single case is not a premium but a court asked to find liability on the strength of a frequency alone.
