I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately joined a reading group of fellow scientists working through an introduction to phenomenology, and next month I lead our session on Husserl's epoché and the phenomenological reduction. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

The group is sharp but scientifically trained, and I expect them to hear 'bracketing the existence of the world' as either a skeptic's party trick or a confession of solipsism. Please lay out: in what order the pieces must be introduced — I assume the 'natural attitude' has to be secured before suspending it can mean anything, but tell me whether I am right and why; which single example should carry the session (an act of perception, a remembered event, a mathematical object?), and what that example must have and must lack to do the work; the misconceptions I should anticipate from this audience — that the epoché is Descartes' doubt under another name, that it denies the external world rather than merely declining to use it, that reduction means introspection, that talk of 'constitution' smuggles in idealism — and at what point in the sequence each should be headed off; whether in one evening I should distinguish the epoché from the transcendental reduction or treat them as a single move; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level (noesis and noema?) while openly saying so. Finally, give me one closing question by which I can tell whether they have grasped that the reduction is a change of attitude toward the same world, not a retreat from it.
