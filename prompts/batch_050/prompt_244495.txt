I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm finishing a long piece on how researchers think about creativity. My editor, after reading a draft, sent me a paragraph he wants to work into the introduction as a way of orienting readers. It concerns Douglas Hofstadter's account of creativity as variations on a theme -- the "knob-twiddling" picture he developed in his essays and in the research program behind the Copycat model of analogy-making. His paragraph reads as follows:

"Hofstadter's claim is that creativity is nothing but variations on a theme: every creative act is a matter of tweaking the parameters -- he calls them knobs -- of a concept that already exists, so that invention reduces to a kind of mental knob-twiddling. And since a knob can be twiddled at random, creative thought is in principle mechanical, which is what his Copycat program was built to prove: a computer making random mutations to letter strings can be genuinely creative. It follows, on his view, that there is no such thing as a radical conceptual breakthrough -- what looks like one, Einstein being the stock example, is just many small knob-twiddles strung together -- and so genius is an illusion we project in hindsight."

My instinct is that this is roughly the right neighborhood but that something in it is off, and I'd rather not inherit my editor's framing uncritically, since the paragraph will set up everything that follows. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me: which parts a careful reader of Hofstadter would accept as a fair, if compressed, statement of the view; which parts overstate or quietly distort it -- for instance, whether the role of randomness is described correctly, whether Copycat was intended as the demonstration he says it was, and whether the deflationary moral about genius is something Hofstadter actually draws or something being pinned on him. Where the view distinguishes two things the paragraph runs together, name the distinction. Then tell me whether my editor's intended editorial payoff -- that the science of creativity has, on this account, "explained creativity away" -- survives once the summary is corrected, or whether the corrected view supports a different and more interesting framing for the piece.

If any part of this request rests on a confused premise -- say, if I've misidentified what the knob-twiddling picture is a theory of, or if the accuracy question can't be separated from an interpretive dispute I'm not seeing -- I'd rather you say so plainly than grade the paragraph as written.
