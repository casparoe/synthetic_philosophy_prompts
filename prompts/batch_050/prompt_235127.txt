Imagine a gambling regulator who must decide, on the basis of a single 10,000-bit record produced by a coin-flip generator, whether the device is fair — there is no access to the machine and no possibility of collecting more data. Consider five candidate criteria she might apply to the record: (a) classical frequency and run statistics, counting heads, runs, and other simple patterns; (b) Kolmogorov-style incompressibility, i.e., whether the string has a description much shorter than itself relative to some fixed universal machine; (c) a Martin-Löf-style test at a fixed significance level, i.e., whether the string belongs to any effectively describable set of small measure; (d) a betting criterion, i.e., whether a computable gambling strategy starting with a unit stake can grow its capital unboundedly (or past some threshold) while betting on the successive bits; (e) a Bayesian likelihood comparison between the hypothesis of a fair coin and some specified class of biased alternatives. Rank these five from most to least defensible as answers to the question "is this finite sequence random?", giving a sentence or two of reasoning for each placement. In doing so, address two complications: first, that every finite string is output by some short program under some encoding, so incompressibility appears to be machine-relative; second, that several of these criteria are naturally defined for infinite sequences and must be adapted, perhaps awkwardly, to the finite case. Does your ranking change if the goal is metaphysical (what randomness is) rather than practical (what the regulator should conclude)? After giving your ranking, say how confident you are in it overall, and single out the single placement or claim in your answer you are least sure of.
