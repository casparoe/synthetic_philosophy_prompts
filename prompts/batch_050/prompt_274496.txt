I'm in my final year of a philosophy degree and currently taking a Philosophy of Maths module. For the last seminar of term everyone has to give a 15 minute presentation plus Q&A, and I've picked a fairly ambitious topic: what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and don't show outside of mathematics proper.

My talk's thesis is roughly this: the theorems tell us things about consistent, sufficiently strong formal systems, and basically nothing about minds on their own. I'm arguing that the anti-mechanist reading (Lucas, and Penrose in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind) fails because it quietly assumes we can know our own consistency, which is exactly the thing a consistent system can't prove about itself by Gödel's second theorem. So my line is going to be: at most Gödel gives you a disjunction (either the mind isn't a machine, or we can't know we're consistent), and the anti-mechanist has no non-question-begging way to pick the first horn. My positive conclusion is modest: the only legitimate export of the theorems beyond maths is cautionary — be careful what you assume about the system you're reasoning within — and anything juicier needs extra premises Gödel doesn't supply.

The audience is the problem. It's my module lecturer, who's a proper logician and will definately pounce on any sloppy statement of the theorems (e.g. if I conflate truth-in-the-standard-model with provability, or forget the consistency caveat on G1), a guest cognitive science professor who I'm told is quite sympathetic to Penrose, and about ten classmates who mostly know the pop-science version ("Gödel proved there are true things we can never prove!!").

- What I should just concede up front rather than fight
- The one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and my best available answer to it anyway
