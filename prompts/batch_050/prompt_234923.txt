I'm prepping a seminar on King Lear for a course on the ethics of family relationships, and I want to read the play against Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation. English's line, roughly, is that parental sacrifices create occasions for gratitude, not debts to be repaid, and that what grown children owe their parents is just what friends owe each other -- ongoing while the friendship survives, lapsed when it doesn't. Lear, though, runs the opening scene as a kind of debt auction, love declared in exchange for shares of the kingdom, and his fixation later on ingratitude treats his daughters' behavior as defaulting on what they owe. Two things I'd like worked out. First, on English's model, do Goneril and Regan actually do anything wrong *as daughters*? By the time Lear is calling down curses, he's torched any plausible friendship with them, so the model seems to leave them with only the generic duties anyone owes anyone -- but the play plainly doesn't see it that way. Does that mean English's model is too permissive, or does the play itself smuggle in the debt model English is attacking? Second, Cordelia: her refusal to flatter looks friendship-model (no performative repayment on demand), but her return to rescue a father who banished and disinherited her looks like something stronger and more unconditional than friendship, closer to exactly what English denies we owe. Read the play both ways and tell me where it resists English, where it quietly vindicates her, and what each side should say about the storm scenes and the Gloucester/Edgar subplot. Don't just pick a winner -- I want to know what the play dramatizes that English's paper leaves out.
