After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of mind, and my local reading group has asked me to present my position for scrutiny. Before I do, I would like the set of commitments checked for coherence. Here it is:

(a) Functionalism is correct: what makes a state mental is its causal-functional organization, not the material in which that organization happens to be realized.

(b) The proper way to test such views is by thought experiment, and our intuitions about cases -- such as gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement of a brain with silicon -- are genuine evidence.

(c) A sufficiently detailed book, of the sort Hofstadter imagines recording the complete state-transition structure of Einstein's brain, so that by mechanically following its rules one could reproduce everything Einstein would say and do, would be conscious if actually operated.

(d) The same book sitting unread on a shelf is not conscious: consciousness is a process, and a mere static description of a process is not itself the process.

(e) The intuition that no mere rule-following mechanism could be conscious, as pressed in Searle's Chinese Room argument, carries no evidential weight, because we simply cannot intuit systems of that complexity.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank any tensions by severity, indicate for each what would have to give, and identify the single least costly revision that would leave the overall position intact.
