I am a professor of philosophy applying for a one-semester research leave, and I need a two-page proposal for the project described below. Please write the proposal itself, not advice about how to write one.

The standard contrast between Peirce's and James's theories of truth is well worn: Peirce ties truth to the opinion that inquiry would converge on at its ideal limit; James ties it to what proves expedient, satisfactory, and verifying within actual experience. Textbook treatments leave open whether these are rival answers to one question or answers to two different questions, and casual claims that the two accounts converge "in practice" are common but never made precise. My guiding conjecture is that the two accounts yield the same verdict for every proposition whose truth-value makes a difference to some possible course of experience, and can diverge only over propositions that are experience-transcendent in a sense the project itself must define. If that is right, then the divergence class — assuming it is non-empty — marks exactly the boundary where the two theories stop being competitors.

The proposal should state the question precisely: characterize the class of propositions on which suitably reconstructed Peircean and Jamesian accounts deliver different truth-verdicts, and determine whether that class is empty. It should explain why existing treatments — exegesis of either figure, and general verdicts about which theory is superior — leave this question untouched. For method, it should propose fixing explicit, non-caricatured reconstructions of each account (specifying the bearer of truth, the operative notion of ideal inquiry, and the operative notion of expediency) and then testing them against a battery of cases: truths about events no observer could in principle have recorded, future contingents, counterfactual truths about what inquiry itself would have concluded under evidential conditions that will never obtain, and moral claims. It should justify this approach over the obvious alternatives (pure exegesis, or attempting to crown one theory correct outright), say what a finished result looks like — a taxonomy of the divergence class plus a principled verdict on whether divergence, if found, refutes one account or dissolves the rivalry — and give an honest assessment of what could make the project too large for a single semester. It should also state what would count as failure: which finding would most directly show the guiding conjecture is mistaken, and where the argument is most likely to break down. Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will supply those myself. Write in American English. End with a final line of the form "Summary: ..." stating the project's central question and conjecture in one sentence.
