Okay so last week my friend and I got into one of those arguments that starts over dinner and is somehow still going at 2am, about Quinn's self-torturer case (the one where there's a dial with a huge number of settings, each click up adds an imperceptible amount of pain and a chunk of money, and by the end you're in agony even though every single step seemed worth taking). We did not resolve it, obviously, but it turned into something useful: I'm giving a short talk about it next month at a joint philosophy-economics reading group, and I want to be properly prepared rather than winging it.

First, act as a professional philosopher who also knows decision theory well -- the kind of person who is comfortable in both a philosophy seminar and an econ brown-bag. Think carefully and work through this step by step rather than pattern-matching to the first textbook response. Before writing anything final, brainstorm a long list of possible objections (at least a dozen, including bad ones), then pick the ones that will actually get asked and order them.

My thesis for the talk: the self-torturer case shows that rationality can't be purely local. Roughly my argument:

1. Each individual turn of the dial is rational -- the pain difference is imperceptible, the money is real.
2. But anyone who keeps making locally rational turns ends up in a state they would never have chosen from the start.
3. So a rational agent sometimes has to be *resolute*: commit to stopping at some point and stick to it even when the next click looks free.
4. This is not just "having different preferences" (that changes the case rather than solving it), and it's not blind rule-worship either, because the plan is itself rationally adopted.
5. My friend's objection, which I'll have to answer: any stopping point is arbitrary, since there's no perceptible difference between adjacent settings, so resoluteness just relocates the arbitrariness instead of removing it. And worse -- if the agent can't even tell the clicks apart, how is the plan enforceable, even by the agent against themselves?

The audience: about half economists of the revealed-preference, transitivity-as-definitional-of-rationality persuasion; two or three moral philosophers who will mostly come at it from a "just take the money and stop, consistency is a fetish" angle; and one behavioral economist who I suspect will try to reframe everything in terms of time-inconsistency and precommitment devices. Nobody in the room works on vagueness, which I suspect is relevant but I can't count on them to spot it.
