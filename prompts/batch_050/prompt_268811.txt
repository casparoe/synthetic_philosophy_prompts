I am writing a paper on Molyneux's problem and want to fix the standard of adequacy in advance, so that the paper evaluates proposed answers against a specification rather than against my sympathies. For definiteness: the problem asks whether a person blind from birth, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch alone, would on gaining sight be able to tell which is which by vision alone, before being permitted to touch them again.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate resolution has to deliver, with the consideration that makes each item a genuine requirement rather than the preference of a particular camp. Address at least the following, and add anything I have missed:

1. Coverage of variants. Must a theory give the same verdict for every feature — shape, color, faces, motion, numerosity — or is a mixed answer (yes for some, no for others) coherent? If mixed answers are permitted, state what principled line an answer must draw and what would count as gerrymandering.
2. The transfer-versus-learning distinction. An answer must distinguish immediate cross-modal recognition from rapid recalibration after sight restoration; explain why a resolution that collapses these two misreads the original question, since even a strict empiricist predicts fast learning.
3. Accommodation of the evidence. For each relevant kind of finding — studies of patients whose sight was restored after early congenital blindness (which typically report failure to transfer touch-learned shape discriminations to vision at first, followed by fast learning), cross-modal matching experiments in young infants, and neuroimaging results suggesting shared shape representations across modalities — state what it establishes, what it cannot establish, and what it would have shown had it come out the other way.
4. Distinctions that must be respected: possessing a shape concept versus being able to perceptually discriminate instances; "the same shape" as fixed by an external geometric criterion versus as it figures in the subject's experience; and the question as asked about a particular experimental setup versus as a question about the mind's architecture.
5. A meta-requirement. Must an adequate answer explain why competent thinkers have found the question obviously answerable in both directions, or is accounting for the disagreement's persistence optional?
6. Which desiderata are non-negotiable and which belong to particular camps (rationalists, empiricists, enactivists), and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if so, identify it, since that would itself be the interesting result.

Then, briefly, score the main existing answer-types against the list — a straightforward yes grounded in amodal spatial representations, a straightforward no grounded in modality-specific concepts, and the deflationary verdict that the question is ill-posed as stated — and identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most candidates. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating considerations, not on the scorecard.
