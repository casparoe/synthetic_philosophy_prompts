In Volume II, Book Three, Chapter XIX of Democracy in America (Henry Reeve translation), the chapter titled "Why So Many Ambitious Men and So Little Lofty Ambition Are to Be Found in the United States," Alexis de Tocqueville offers a diagnosis of ambition under conditions of equality that differs sharply from the two explanations people usually reach for. The familiar candidates are the American Dream story (equality unleashes ambition by making every summit reachable) and the tall-poppy or Jantelagen story (egalitarian cultures cut down conspicuous success, so people hide or suppress great ambition). Tocqueville instead writes:

"What chiefly diverts the men of democracies from lofty ambition is not the scantiness of their fortunes, but the vehemence of the exertions they daily make to improve them. They strain their faculties to the utmost to achieve paltry results, and this cannot fail speedily to limit their range of view and to circumscribe their powers. They might be much poorer and still be greater."

On his account, equality does not suppress ambition through envy or social sanction, nor does it liberate it: it makes ambition "ardent and continual" while keeping its aim low, because open competition on equal terms forces everyone into exhausting daily striving, and because, as he argues in the same chapter, the equal openness of all paths slows everyone's advance toward great positions.
