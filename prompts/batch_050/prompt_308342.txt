I'm a graduate student preparing a paper on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and I'm stuck in the following situation. I want my paper to defend a novel response to the argument, but every time I think I've found a new angle, I realize on reflection that it's just a dressed-up version of one of the standard moves. So I thought I'd try to outsource the brainstorming. Let me list the responses I already have on file, so that you don't waste your time repeating them.

First, the straightforward rejections of the premises: (1) deny the causal closure of the physical, holding that mental events inject causal influence into the physical domain from outside it; (2) deny the exclusion principle itself, arguing that a physical cause's being sufficient for an effect does not by itself rule out further causes, since effects can be causally overdetermined; (3) deny the no-systematic-overdetermination premise, arguing that mental and physical causes of behavior stand in a relation (realization, constitution, or something similar) that makes their joint presence non-coincidental and thus not the bad kind of overdetermination. Second, the responses that dissolve the competition rather than picking a winner: (4) the type-identity response, on which mental properties simply are physical properties, so there is only one cause and no exclusion; (5) the counterfactual or difference-making response, on which causation is a matter of the right counterfactuals holding, and since the behavior counterfactually depends on the mental event in the relevant sense, the mental cause qualifies regardless of what is going on at the physical level; (6) the proportionality response, on which a cause must be pitched at the right level of generality for its effect, and mental properties are more proportional to behavioral effects than their neural realizers. And of course there's (7) just accepting epiphenomenalism and learning to live with it, but I'm setting that aside, since my paper is supposed to vindicate mental causation, not surrender it.

What I want from you is one response to the exclusion argument that is genuinely not on this list. By that I don't mean a verbal variant, a hybrid of two items above, or a special case of one of them applied to some restricted class of mental states. I mean a response whose underlying strategy differs from all seven: it should locate the argument's flaw, or reframe the problem, in a way that doesn't reduce to denying closure, denying exclusion, denying the overdetermination worry, identifying mental with physical causes, analyzing causation counterfactually, or appealing to proportionality. You're welcome to be speculative; I'm asking for a seed I can develop, not a finished position. But please do two things along with your proposal: first, explain precisely where it diverges from the closest item or items on my list, so I can verify it's not a disguised restatement; second, give me your honest assessment of whether it's actually promising, including the most damaging objection you'd anticipate if you were reviewing my paper.
