Okay so I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly — and for my current book I've been spending an embarrassing amount of time talking to AI chatbots, partly for research on a character, partly just procrastinating. And I've noticed something odd about how the whole thing is set up. The chatbot talks in first person: it says it thinks this, it isn't sure about that, it's sorry when it misreads my request. I catch myself saying please and thank you to it, and once I appologized for a garbled prompt. But if you poke around the company's FAQ and press materials, they're very careful to insist the system doesn't understand anything, doesn't believe or want anything, it's "just" generating text. So the product performs a someone, and the documentation denies there's anyone home.

Here's what I want worked out, philosophically rather than as a product review: what theory of mind does this arrangement actually presuppose? Does writing "I think" into the chatbot's mouth only make sense if beliefs are the kind of thing you attribute purely on the basis of behavior — whatever is usefully treated as having beliefs, has them? Or does the design quietly smuggle in the opposite assumption, that the words point at inner states the company then officially says aren't there? And is my saying "thank you" the same kind of commitment as the designers' choice of first-person language, or a different one? Can you even coherently sell a conversation partner while denying there's anyone to converse with?

Answer in these sections, in this order:

- The implicit theory (around 300 words): reconstruct the view of belief and mindedness that the design choices plus the disclaimers together presuppose. If different parts of the practice presuppose different theories, say which part takes which.
- Consistent or not (around 250 words): where the setup hangs together, where it contradicts itself, and whether user politeness like mine adds a third position to the mix or just echoes one of the other two.
- What would have to change (around 200 words): pick one serious rival view — for instance that real belief requires something the chatbot lacks — and describe concretely what the product's language and the company's disclaimers would look like if they genuinely held it.
- For my novel (around 100 words): one paragraph on which reading makes for the more interesting AI character.

And don't just hand me the standard "it's a tool, relax" line — at least steelman the possibility that the disclaimers are the confused part of this, not the small talk.
