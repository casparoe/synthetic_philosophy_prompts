I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long feature on the strange standoff in the theory of metaphor. On one side are the Davidsonians, who hold that a metaphor has only its literal meaning and that whatever else it does -- nudging us to notice things, inviting comparisons, jolting us out of habitual thought -- is a matter of use and effect, not of any encoded "metaphorical meaning" a hearer decodes. On the other side are proponents of conceptual-metaphor theory, who hold that metaphors reflect systematic mappings between conceptual domains (argument understood in terms of combat, time in terms of motion through space) and that these mappings are part of our cognitive equipment, not mere stylistic ornament. Each camp tells me the other is confused: the Davidsonians say their opponents mistake psychological processing facts for semantic facts, and the conceptual-metaphor theorists say their opponents have no account of why metaphorical language is so patterned and productive rather than random.

Since neither side will yield, I've decided to commission an adversarial collaboration instead, and I want you to draft the joint statement such a collaboration should produce. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims both camps can sign without reservation -- for instance, that metaphors can be cognitively powerful without that power settling any semantic question, and that some metaphors are so conventionalized that speakers no longer notice them. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each stated in one or two sentences that are neutral enough that each party would endorse the wording of its own position -- be careful here to separate genuine disagreements from ones that are merely terminological, and say explicitly which is which. Third, a section on cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to revise its view. I suspect the cruxes include whether the systematicity of cross-domain mappings requires positing metaphorical meaning, whether "dead" or conventionalized metaphors are evidence for or against the Davidsonian picture, and whether the alleged unparaphrasability of rich metaphors shows anything about meaning rather than about the limits of prose summary -- but refine or replace these if you think I've misdiagnosed the fault lines.

For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or body of linguistic evidence that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side would predict. For example: how should each camp expect processing times for novel versus conventional metaphors to compare with literal paraphrases, and what would each side make of a conventional metaphor that speakers process exactly like literal language? What would each predict about how speakers of a language that maps time onto space differently from English reason about time? The tone throughout should be that of two serious opponents who have agreed to stop talking past each other, not a compromise document that papers over the dispute -- the value for my readers is in seeing the disagreement made exact.
