My friend Mara, a physicist who sits in on our department's philosophy of probability reading group, has developed a reputation for having firm intuitions about anthropic reasoning cases. We've started keeping a record of her verdicts, and I want to figure out the rule she's actually running before next week's session. Her track record so far: on the presumptuous philosopher case, she says the armchair cosmologists are making an egregious error and that learning of one's own existence should do essentially nothing to boost confidence in a universe containing vastly more observers; on Sleeping Beauty, she says the awakened Beauty should assign credence one-third to heads; on the Doomsday argument, she says the reasoning from one's birth rank has genuine force and shouldn't be dismissed, even if it isn't decisive; and on the Adam-and-Eve incubator case, she says Adam, upon learning he exists, should assign roughly one-half to the coin having landed heads rather than being swayed by the fact that tails would have produced more people.

Next week we're discussing a case I've constructed, and I'd like to predict her answer before she gives it. The case: you wake in a hospital with total amnesia about your identity, knowing only what the intake documents say. The hospital runs one of two protocols, chosen by a fair coin. Under Protocol A, exactly one patient is admitted and stays ten days, waking exactly once each morning. Under Protocol B, ten distinct patients are admitted, each stays a single day, and each wakes exactly once. So the two protocols generate the same total number of patient-awakenings (ten), but different numbers of patients. You are awake now, it's your first morning as far as you can tell, and you learn nothing else indexical. What credence should you assign to Protocol B?

Please predict Mara's credence and, more importantly, tell me which principle she is implicitly running -- self-sampling with a reference class of awakenings, self-sampling with a reference class of persons, self-indication despite her verdict on the presumptuous philosopher, or some hybrid I haven't thought of. Since I suspect more than one rule fits her four verdicts, lay out the candidate rules that fit them equally well, state what each predicts about my hospital case, give your best guess with reasons, and tell me which further case, if I put it to her, would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving rules.
