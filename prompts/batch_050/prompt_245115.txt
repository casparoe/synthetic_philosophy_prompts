I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and I'm putting together a one-page prospectus for a nine-month dissertation project that I need to present to my committee next month. The project sits at the intersection of animal consciousness research and formal epistemology, and I want the prospectus written up properly, as a plan for work I haven't done yet rather than an essay defending a conclusion. Here's the idea, which is still half-baked: scientists assessing the distribution of consciousness across animals typically proceed by listing indicators — behavioral flexibility, motivational trade-offs, certain forms of neural integration, responses to analgesics — and then checking which taxa possess how many of them. There's a familiar pattern in the results: convergence on mammals and birds, spreading disagreement as one moves to cephalopods, crustaceans, fish, and insects. The standard response to this disagreement is to argue about which indicator is the right one, or to add more indicators and count. My hunch is that this misses a prior question. Convergence among tests only raises confidence if the tests are at least somewhat independent of one another — if they fail independently. But the standard indicators may not be independent at all: they may all quietly encode the same background assumptions about what consciousness is for (say, flexible integration of information in the service of action selection), in which case their agreement on mammals is nearly worthless as cumulative evidence, and their disagreement on insects is telling us something about the indicators' shared blind spot rather than about insects.

So the project would be: map the dependency structure among the leading indicators of consciousness — that is, for each pair of indicators, ask whether the theoretical reasons for trusting them draw on the same commitments about the function or mechanism of experience — and then work out what the map implies about how much confidence the current convergence pattern actually warrants, and which empirical findings would be genuinely diagnostic as opposed to merely adding another correlated voice. Write the prospectus: state the question precisely, explain why the existing literature's way of handling indicator disagreement leaves this question untouched, lay out the method (I assume it's some mix of conceptual analysis of what justifies each indicator and formal or semi-formal reasoning about correlated evidence, but you should argue for the method and against the obvious alternatives, including just waiting for neuroscience to settle things empirically), and say what a successful result would look like — concretely, what I'd hand to a working animal welfare scientist at the end — and equally what would count as the project failing. Be realistic about what fits in nine months; if the full map is too ambitious, carve off the piece that stands alone. Also include a paragraph anticipating the committee member who asks why this isn't just philosophy of science with an animal-themed example, i.e., why the consciousness case specifically needs this treatment rather than serving as decoration on a general point about correlated evidence.

Two constraints. First, don't give me a bibliography or cite specific papers and authors — I'll supply the references myself; you may describe positions and research programs generically (e.g., indicator-based approaches, higher-order theories, precision-weighting views) and I'll match them to the literature. Second, keep it to roughly one page of proposal prose; I'd rather have a tight prospectus for a modest project than a bloated one for an impossible project.
