I teach an undergraduate epistemology course, and next week we reach the unit on epistemic circularity. The students (about 16, second- and third-year, no formal logic background beyond one intro course) consistently read the bootstrapping problem as a technical curiosity: they grant in discussion that a source of belief cannot certify itself by its own lights, and then, ten minutes later, happily reassure one another that their sense perception is reliable because it has "checked out" countless times in the past. They have memorized the verdict without feeling the trap.

Please design a 75-minute in-class activity that makes them feel it. The rough shape I have in mind is this, though you should improve on it: each small group receives some kind of instrument or oracle whose pronouncements they can consult -- for instance, a device that answers questions about hidden states of affairs in the room -- and their task is to establish, with evidence, whether the instrument is reliable. The catch, which they should not be told in advance, is that the only tools available for checking it are the instrument itself and other instruments in the same epistemic predicament. I want at least some groups to construct, in full earnest, a track-record argument for their instrument's reliability using only its own outputs, and to present that argument to the class with real confidence before anyone points out what they have done. Ideally the activity should also include a moment where groups attempt to validate instruments against one another, so that the difference between circular self-certification and genuinely independent corroboration becomes visible rather than merely asserted.

Concretely, I need from you: the full setup and rules, including what materials to prepare and how to keep the mechanism hidden without being deceptive in a way students will resent; a minute-by-minute timeline; the roles or team structures, if any; the exact point at which the trap should be sprung and how (by me, or better, by the students discovering it); a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical material -- bootstrapping a track record for a basic source, "easy knowledge" of a source's reliability, the contrast with genuine cross-checking -- and that connects the instrument case back to sense perception, which is where the stakes actually live; and a prediction of the two or three ways the activity will predictably go wrong (for example, students guessing the gimmick early, or concluding nihilistically that no source can ever be validated, which is not the lesson) with instructions for handling each. End with a candid note on which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect the deep problem, that we have no standpoint outside perception at all, resists simulation precisely because the students can step outside their instrument's perspective in a way we cannot step outside our own -- and tell me what I must supply in lecture to close that gap.
