I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogs for well over a decade, and I co-organize a rationality meetup that draws about fifteen people — mostly software engineers and grad students, comfortable with probability, allergic to being lectured at. We have ninety minutes, a table, index cards, and no budget.

I want to run a session on generics: bare-plural sentences like ducks lay eggs, mosquitoes carry malaria, sharks kill swimmers. The phenomenon I want people to viscerally grasp is that these sentences do not answer to frequency the way quantified sentences do. Most ducks never lay an egg (males, juveniles), yet ducks lay eggs is unimpeachable. A vanishingly small fraction of mosquitoes carry malaria, yet mosquitoes carry malaria is true, while books are paperbacks sounds false even where paperbacks dominate sales. Acceptance seems to track whether a property is characteristic, distinctive, or dangerous rather than how common it is. The upshot that matters for this crowd: generics are nearly counterexample-proof, so they do enormous quiet work in persuasion. People are selfish, the media lies, voters are irrational — you can concede exception after exception and the generic survives intact, which makes it a solvent on careful argument. I'd also like the session to connect, in the debrief and not before, to the noncentral fallacy and to motte-and-bailey dynamics, since this group already has those handles.

Design me a runnable activity that makes participants discover the frequency-independence of generics themselves, before anyone names it. Concretely, I'm imagining something with real choices and small stakes — perhaps a game where players write or select generic claims for some purpose (a field guide, a safety poster, a country briefing) and are then given actual base-rate information, so that their own keep/discard decisions reveal what their acceptance was really tracking — but please improve on or replace this skeleton if you can. I need:

1. Full rules and materials: what goes on the cards, how turns work, what the stakes or scoring are (no real money, no phones), and a timeline broken into phases with rough minute counts.
2. The moment of discovery: specify how the activity forces participants to notice, in their own behavior, that they accepted low-frequency-but-striking generics and rejected high-frequency-but-boring ones — and later, that a generic they themselves endorsed could not be dislodged by exceptions.
3. A debrief script that maps specific moments of the game onto the theoretical points (frequency-sensitivity of most versus the generic's indifference to it; the role of strikingness and danger; counterexample-resistance), and only then introduces the term generic and the connection to the noncentral fallacy.
4. An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, participants catching on early and refusing to assert any generic at all, which would break the game — and how to handle each failure mode.
5. A frank note on which feature of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce. My own candidate: in the wild, nobody hands you the base-rate card, so the game's central scaffold is exactly what's absent when generics do their work in ordinary discourse. Tell me whether you think that's the irreducible gap or whether there's a deeper one.

No lecture up front; the word generic shouldn't be uttered until the debrief.
