I teach a second-year undergraduate course in normative ethics, and I am writing this year's midterm. Students will have one essay question on Singer's drowning child and duties of rescue, answerable in 45 minutes and marked out of 20. In past years the question I set was too easy to fake: students recited the pond case, stated the principle that one ought to prevent bad things when one can do so at modest cost, mentioned charity, and concluded that we are all moral monsters, without ever touching the machinery of the argument. This year I want a question that cannot be answered that way, and I would like you to write it for me, along with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Here is the crux I want the question to force students onto. I plan to have the question itself present an invented variant case, so that memorised summaries are useless: a coastal town where rip currents drown a predictable number of swimmers every year, where any resident could, for the cost of a modest weekly expense, fund lifeguard coverage that would prevent one of those deaths, but where the coverage rotates unpredictably, so no particular death can ever be traced to any particular resident's failure to contribute. The question should then ask candidates to determine whether Singer's argument condemns the non-contributing resident exactly as much as it condemns the walker who passes the pond, which requires them to identify what work, if any, the identifiability of the victim is doing in the original case, to say whether Singer's argument actually runs through an analogy between pond and poverty or through a general principle for which the pond is merely an intuition pump, and to confront the fact that the lifeguard case has many potential rescuers and a recurring rather than one-off cost, so that the demandingness objection and the problem of dividing obligations among many hands both become live. A strong question will make it impossible to earn top marks by listing standard disanalogies between pond and famine relief without assessing whether those disanalogies are morally relevant to the principle.

Please deliver three things. First, the exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the full statement of the lifeguard case and the instructions to candidates. Second, a model answer of roughly 500 to 600 words, written at the level of the best student I have ever taught rather than the level of a published philosopher. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 built around five specific moves a first-class answer must make, stating how many marks each move is worth and adding, for each, a one-sentence note on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble that point, plus a closing statement of what distinguishes a first-class script from a good upper-second. Please write everything as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
