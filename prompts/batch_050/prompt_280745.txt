Two people in my philosophy reading group got into a genuine fight last week and I'd like a referee. For context: we were reading the Maitreyi dialogue from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (Max Müller's old translation), where Yajnavalkya tells his wife:

"As a lump of salt, when thrown into water, becomes dissolved into water, and could not be taken out again, but wherever we taste (the water) it is salt,—thus verily, O Maitreyî, does this great Being, endless, unlimited, consisting of nothing but knowledge, rise from out these elements, and vanish again in them. When he has departed, there is no more knowledge (name), I say, O Maitreyî." ... Then Maitreyî said: "Here thou hast bewildered me, Sir, when thou sayest that having departed, there is no more knowledge." But Yâgñavalkya replied: "O Maitreyî, I say nothing that is bewildering... For when there is as it were duality, then one sees the other, one smells the other, one hears the other... but when the Self only is all this, how should he smell another, how should he see another, how should he hear another...? How should he know Him by whom he knows all this? How, O beloved, should he know (himself), the Knower?"

Anyway, Noor and Ben started arguing about whether this passage sinks Advaita or not. Roughly:

(1) Noor said the passage convicts Advaita of incoherence. If liberation is a state where "there is no more knowledge," then it can't be known, described, valued, or aimed at -- you can't rationally pursue something you can't in principle know anything about. And worse, the passage undercuts itself: Yajnavalkya is *teaching* Maitreyi, which presupposes duality (teacher, student, thing taught) -- the very thing the passage says is an illusion. A teaching that can only be delivered from within the illusion it claims to dispel is self-defeating.

(2) Ben said this completely misses how Advaita works. There are two levels of truth, conventional and ultimate. At the conventional level teaching, pursuit, and moral distinctions are perfectly real; at the ultimate level only Brahman is real. "No more knowledge" means no more *object*-knowledge -- knowledge that requires a knower standing over against a known -- not a blackout of awareness. The salt doesn't cease to exist when it dissolves; it ceases to be a graspable lump. Same with the knower.

(3) Noor said the two-levels move is unfalsifiable bookkeeping. Any time the doctrine says something incoherent, the defender demotes the incoherence to the "conventional" level and declares victory. A view that absorbs every objection this way isn't deep, it's rigged. And the dream analogy Advaitins love -- the dreamt tiger that wakes you up -- doesn't help: a dreamt tiger doesn't really wake you, it just seems to from inside the dream, so the analogy "proves" the teaching doesn't actually liberate anyone.

(4) Ben said the unfalsifiability charge is a category mistake, because Advaita isn't an empirical hypothesis competing with physics, it's a soteriology -- a practice aimed at a transformation of the one practicing. Asking it to be falsifiable is like complaining that learning to swim doesn't generate testable predictions about water. And on her specific point: the tradition explicitly says the Self is self-luminous, so liberation isn't ignorance, it's the one case of knowledge that doesn't need a subject-object split.

(5) Noor's final move: that last claim is exactly what the passage forbids. Yajnavalkya ends by asking how the Knower could be known -- and Ben's answer is that in liberation the Self *is* known, just in a special non-dual way. So either liberation involves knowledge of the Self (contra the passage's "how should he know the Knower?") or it doesn't (contra "the Self is self-luminous knowledge"). You can't fix the contradiction by italicizing the word knowledge.

So -- act as a professional philosopher with real expertise in classical Indian thought, and adjudicate this properly. Before giving verdicts, spend a few sentences reconstructing the strongest version of each side's overall case. Then go move by move through (1) to (5): for each one, say who's right on that specific point and why (not just a global winner). I'm especially interested in whether Noor's point (5) actually lands or whether there's a defensible reading of "self-luminous" that escapes it, and whether her unfalsifiability charge in (3) is fair given that the same charge could be leveled at, say, Kant's distinction between phenomena and noumena. Finally, tell me what *both* of them are missing. Don't just split the difference to be diplomatic -- if one side is clearly more right, say so.
