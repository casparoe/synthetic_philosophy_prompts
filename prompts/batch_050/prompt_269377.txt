I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on a question about the validity of consent. I want that question argued out on the page by two characters, with philosophical exactness rather than novelistic atmosphere, and I would like you to write the dialogue. Note that I have deliberately set the problem outside the domain of sexual consent, where such questions are usually fought; I want the arguments to live or die on the facts given. The situation, as the novel has established it by that point, is this.

A widow, Miriam, sells her late husband's private papers to a collector named Hale. She believes the collection consists chiefly of the letters he wrote her over their forty-year marriage. At the time of the sale she tells Hale that she has not reread the letters since the funeral and cannot bring herself to; her instruction is that he take the papers as they are and ask her nothing about their contents. Hale has already examined them. Among them are letters that make plain an affair her husband carried on for much of the marriage, and Hale knows that this is where the collection's interest and value lie. He tells no lie, because no question is put to him, and he says nothing of what he has read. Miriam accepts a price fair for what she takes the papers to be. A year later the affair becomes public when Hale exhibits the collection, and Miriam's position thereafter is that she never consented to this — to the sale of that.

The dialogue takes place some months after the exhibition, between Hale and Ruth, Miriam's grown daughter, who has taken up her mother's cause because Miriam will no longer speak of the matter.

1. Both characters must be fully competent. Hale is not a villain and Ruth is not a scold; the novel fails if the reader can convict Hale quickly. His case has real force: Miriam was an adult in command of her faculties, under no pressure, selling what was hers; she understood the act of sale perfectly; she expressly declined the very information she now claims to have lacked; and if consent is void whenever the other party knowingly profits from a false belief he did nothing to create, then no transaction between strangers is ever valid, since ordinary life runs on uncorrected assumptions. Ruth's case must be equally strong: what her mother understood herself to be parting with was the written record of a faithful marriage, and that understanding was false in a way that was not incidental to the transaction but, on Hale's own private accounting, its entire point; silence chosen because speech would have ended the sale is not the absence of a lie but its functional equivalent; and a refusal to reread old letters is a refusal of grief, not a waiver of the standing to know what one is selling.

2. The back-and-forth must be real. Each speaker answers the other's actual previous point, concedes where concession is due, and presses where pressing is possible. Each should be driven to abandon at least one opening move: Ruth's first framing — that Hale deceived her mother — cannot survive the fact of his perfect literal truthfulness, and she must say what deception is if it is not false assertion; Hale's first framing — that she contracted to sell papers and papers were what he took — cannot survive his own concession that the sale would have collapsed on the spot had the letters been read aloud at the kitchen table, and he must explain why a fact's power to kill a deal does not make it part of the deal.

3. The dialogue should itself discover where the disagreement lies, in the characters' own mouths and without any narrator's gloss. Among the candidates they test: whether engineered silence can be deception; whether the falsehood concerned the thing sold or only its meaning; whether an explicit refusal of information waives the right to it, and if so whether it waives the right against the world or only the claim to be told. At least one of these should turn out, in the course of the argument, to be a red herring, and the characters should recognize it as such. Do not let the surviving fault line be fixed by authorial fiat; let it emerge from which arguments actually break.

4. No philosophers, no cases, no terms of art. These are not academics. Every move must be made through examples drawn from ordinary life — buying a house, selling a car, accepting a marriage proposal, taking a job under a misapprehension — and the examples must do argumentative work, not decoration.

5. The ending: no winner. The dialogue should close with the two of them agreeing, perhaps reluctantly, on the single question on which everything turns — and, if they can honestly manage it, on what sort of consideration would settle it; failing that, on why they cannot agree even about that.

6. Length: roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words of dialogue. After the dialogue, give me, for my own use rather than for the page, four or five sentences stating as precisely as you can the fault line the dialogue uncovered and the strongest surviving move on each side.
