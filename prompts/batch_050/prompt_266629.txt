I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a character. The book is about a guy who reads Pascal's wager in the Pensées (I was using the old Trotter translation, the bit usually numbered fragment 233) and basically takes it seriously: his reasoning, paraphrasing, is that once an infinite payoff is on the table, the smallness of your chance of getting it stops mattering, since any nonzero shot at an infinite good swamps whatever finite thing you stake, and he adds that you don't even get to sit the bet out because you're already playing. So my character starts reorganizing his whole life around remote catastrophes, and my writing group is completely split on whether he's the fool of the book or the only honest person in it. Which made me realize I don't actually understand the normal practice he's violating. We buy smoke detectors even though house fires are rare. We vaccinate kids against diseases they'll probably never meet. Somebody funds a seed vault in Svalbard and a NASA office that watches for asteroids, and we call that prudent. But when my uncle won't fly because of a one-in-millions crash risk, or a neighbor builds a bunker in his backyard, we tell them to get a grip, you can't live your life like that. Same shape of argument both times -- tiny probability, enormous stakes -- honored in one place and ridiculed in the other, and nobody I've asked can state the rule that seperates them.

So: what does that everyday practice presuppose? An unstated probability floor, below which stakes simply don't count no matter how big? Bounded stakes, i.e. nothing is actually infinitely bad, so there's always a probability small enough to license ignoring it? A distinction between well-measured small risks (crash statistics, actuarial tables) and speculative ones someone just asserts? Or something about division of labor -- individuals may ignore what institutions exist to track? Reconstruct the implicit decision theory, and be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, since I suspect several different theories would license the same pattern of mockery and prudence. Then tell me whether it's coherent, and what would have to change if we dropped it: if we took Pascal-style expected-value reasoning seriously across the board, do we all owe my bunker neighbor an apology, and if instead we adopted a hard cutoff at small probabilities, which currently sensible things (the smoke detector? the asteroid office?) become irrational on paper?

And the novelist's question underneath it all: if the practice turns out to rest on nothing firmer than an unspoken agreement not to follow certain arguments where they lead, is my character irrational, or just more consistent than the rest of us -- and which version makes him more tragic?
