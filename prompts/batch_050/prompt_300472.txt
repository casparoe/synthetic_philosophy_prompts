I work for a mid-sized publishing company that recently licensed a large language model, trained in large part on the collective written output of the public — journalists, forum posters, hobbyist translators, fan-fiction writers, and, presumably, many of our own authors — and then used it to make redundant a third of our editorial staff. In the union meetings and late-night discussions this has provoked, I keep reaching for Marx, and I find myself stuck on a genuinely puzzling question: what would he actually have made of this particular configuration? The familiar move is to say he would celebrate the automation and condemn the ownership, but the details here seem to strain his framework in interesting ways. The machine in question is not a power loom: it is built out of the sedimented, mostly uncompensated labor of millions of people who never thought of themselves as producing a capital good, its "machinery" is partly a frozen artifact of past living labor rather than embodied in iron, and the commodity it produces competes directly with the very workers whose activity trained it. Does the labor theory of value even get a grip on a product whose marginal cost of production approaches zero? Would Marx say the value of AI-generated text is determined by the labor embodied in the training data and compute, and if so, what follows for his predictions about the falling rate of profit in industries where constant capital of this kind dominates?

So here is my request: give me your best reconstruction of Marx's position on this scenario, treating him as a serious theorist to be extrapolated rather than a stock character. I would like you to address at least: whether he would classify a trained model as constant capital, fixed capital, or something his categories don't cleanly cover; whether he would regard the unpaid collective labor absorbed into the training data as a new form of primitive accumulation or enclosure of a commons; what he would predict about firms' incentives to automate cognitive work given his account of the profit rate's dependence on living labor; and whether he would expect this kind of automation to liberate workers from drudgery or to complete their subsumption, and under what conditions each. Where you think his published views genuinely underdetermine the answer — because the technology violates an assumption he took for granted — say so explicitly rather than papering over it, and identify the assumption. I am less interested in a verdict on whether Marxism is true than in how well the machinery of his analysis survives contact with a machine made of other people's words.
