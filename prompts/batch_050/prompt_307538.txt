I'm preparing to lead a session of a political theory reading group on Shirley Jackson's short story "The Lottery," and I want to read it against the modern philosophical case for sortition — the proposal, now defended seriously by a number of democratic theorists, that political representatives or officials should be chosen by lottery rather than by election. The pairing will strike some members of my group as perverse, since sortitionists present the lottery as an instrument of equality, impartiality, and protection against corruption, while Jackson's lottery selects a villager to be stoned to death. But I think that tension is exactly what makes the story philosophically interesting rather than merely gruesome, and I'd like your help developing the reading before the session.

Here is the angle I want explored. The sortitionist's central fairness claim is procedural: when every eligible person has an equal ex ante chance of being selected, no one is privileged and no one is excluded by birth, wealth, or party. Jackson's village lottery satisfies that condition almost perfectly — every household draws, every member of the chosen household draws, and the outcome is genuinely random — and yet the story makes us watch Tessie Hutchinson insist, once the marked slip is in her hand, that the process was not fair. Read the story as a sustained examination of that gap: the difference between equal chances and just outcomes, between a procedure no one can complain about in advance and a result someone can complain about after. Then go further. Consider what the story shows about randomness as a way of diffusing responsibility — no one in the village chose the victim, so no one seems to bear the guilt, and even the children are handed stones — and ask whether an analogous worry applies to lottocracy, where officials chosen by lot might be accountable to no one, having been answerable to no electorate to get there. Consider also the moment the story hinges on: Tessie is a willing, joking participant until the instant she becomes the target, and I want to know whether that instant — when an equal member of the procedure is converted into its victim — shows something about equality under sortition that a formal argument cannot.

Please be honest in both directions. I don't want the story flattened into an anti-sortition pamphlet: the villagers' lottery distributes a horrific burden, whereas political sortition distributes offices and power, and a good critic has to say plainly where that disanalogy means the story does no work against the actual proposal, and where the reading strains against the text (for instance, Jackson's villagers treat the lottery as tradition and ritual, not as a fairness device they chose). I'd also like your verdict on the strongest question I plan to put to the group: does the story give the sortitionist a genuine philosophical problem — say, that procedural fairness can be perfectly realized inside an unjust institution — or only a visceral feeling that borrows its force from the murder? End by saying what, if anything, the story contributes to the sortition debate that the journal articles don't.
