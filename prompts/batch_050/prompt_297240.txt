I am preparing a fifty-minute seminar on the unilateralist’s curse for municipal policy analysts who understand expected value but have no background in game theory. By the unilateralist’s curse, I mean the selection problem that arises when several people can each independently initiate a shared, difficult-to-reverse action: if even one person judges the action worthwhile, it occurs, so the outcome is driven by the most favorable judgment rather than by the median or aggregate judgment. Please plan how I should teach the concept rather than simply explain it to me.

The lesson should be built around this fictional case: during an approaching storm, eight district engineers independently receive slightly different forecasts about whether the town’s dunes will be breached. Each has emergency authority to open the sea gate. Opening it would prevent catastrophic flooding if the storm is severe, but would permanently damage the harbor ecosystem if the storm remains mild; once opened, it cannot be closed in time. Every engineer is competent, sincere, and using a reasonable decision rule, yet the more engineers have authority, the more likely it is that at least one forecast crosses the threshold for opening the gate.

Specify the order in which the ideas must be introduced and explain each dependency: private judgment under uncertainty, decentralized initiation, irreversibility, selection of the most optimistic estimate, and the distinction between an individually reasonable choice and a collectively defective decision procedure. Indicate what features of the sea-gate case are essential, which should be simplified or omitted, and how much numerical illustration is enough to make the selection effect intuitive without turning the session into a statistics lecture.

Include a misconception map. At minimum, address the beliefs that the curse depends on irrational overconfidence, that it is just another name for groupthink, that adding cautious decision-makers always makes action less likely, that it is equivalent to diffusion of responsibility, and that it implies that individual initiative should generally be suppressed. For each misconception, state when it is likely to arise and exactly where in the teaching sequence I should head it off.

End with one check for understanding that would show whether participants grasp the mechanism rather than merely the label, and identify what I should deliberately leave out at this level while telling the audience that it has been left out.
