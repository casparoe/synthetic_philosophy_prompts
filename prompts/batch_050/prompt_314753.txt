I teach a second-year undergraduate course in political philosophy, and in two weeks I have a single ninety-minute seminar on Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument from Anarchy, State, and Utopia. The students will have read the relevant chapter, but last year's cohort mostly came away thinking the argument was either obviously decisive or obviously silly, and almost none of them could state precisely what it was supposed to show. Plan how I should teach it. I do not want a defense or a refutation of the argument itself; I want the explanatory plan.

Specifically:

1. The dependency order. What has to be in place conceptually before the Wilt Chamberlain case can do its work — presumably the distinction between historical and end-state principles of justice, the notion of a patterned principle, and the idea that holdings can arise through uncoerced transfers. In what order should these be introduced, and why that order? Is there anything in the argument that requires students already to accept Nozick's account of just acquisition, or does the case stand alone against patterned principles regardless of what one thinks about initial acquisition? I want them to see that the argument is narrower than its reputation.

2. The carrying example. Should I use the basketball case itself or build a parallel one? What features must the example have to function — voluntary transfers, a starting distribution that counts as just by the pattern's own lights, many small repeated transactions, no significant third-party effects — and what must it not accidentally suggest: that Wilt deserves the money, that the fans are irrational, that the resulting inequality is enormous? If you recommend a substitute example, specify it in detail and say what it gains and loses relative to the original.

3. The misconception map. List the misreadings I should expect — for instance, that the argument is about desert; that it assumes the pre-transfer distribution was just in some absolute sense rather than merely by the pattern's own standards; that it proves redistribution is impermissible full stop rather than that maintaining a pattern requires continuous interference with consented-to transactions; that it depends on the fans' preferences being beyond criticism; that Nozick takes actual existing holdings to be just. For each misreading, say where in the session's sequence it will predictably form, at exactly which point I should head it off, and how.

4. The check. One question or exercise for the final fifteen minutes that would show whether students have grasped what the argument does and does not establish — ideally something forcing them to apply the historical-versus-patterned distinction to a fresh case rather than recite the basketball story back to me.

5. What to leave out. Which complications — the Lockean proviso, rectification of past injustice, whether taxation is genuinely on a par with forced labor — should I explicitly bracket at this level, and what should I tell students about why they are being bracketed, so that no one feels the hard parts have been quietly hidden?
