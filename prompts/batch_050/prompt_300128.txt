I am a retired physiologist who has come late to philosophy, and our reading circle has just finished Thomas Mann's The Magic Mountain. I would like help preparing the introduction I am to give at our next meeting, and I want it organized around a debate I have been working through: the contest between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease. By naturalism I mean the view associated with Christopher Boorse, on which disease is a value-free matter of departure from species-typical biological functioning; by normativism I mean the family of views on which calling something a disease essentially involves a value judgment about what is bad for the person, with social norms doing part of the classificatory work.

Here is what draws me to the pairing. The Berghof sanatorium runs on a diagnostic gray zone: Hans Castorp arrives healthy, is found to have a small tuberculous lesion and a mild fever, and stays seven years. The institution's whole economy — its thermometers, X-rays, rest cures, and ritualized temperature charts — depends on treating the borderline case as a patient, and the patients, Hans included, come to prefer the status of the sick to the duties of the healthy down in the flatland. As a physiologist I note that latent tuberculous foci of exactly the sort Hans carries were historically common in people who lived and died without illness, which raises a real problem for the biostatistical approach: if a lesion is statistically normal for the species, is it still disease?

Please develop the reading in both directions, and be strict about the following. First, show where each theory genuinely illuminates specific scenes and arrangements — the diagnosis scene with Hofrat Behrens, the culture of measurement, Hans's courtship of his own fever, the contrast between Settembrini's enlightenment humanism and the sanatorium's romanticizing of sickness — rather than merely decorating the novel with vocabulary from the debate. Second, identify where each theory is embarrassed by the book: for the naturalist, the dependence of the sick role on what the community will pay to treat and will excuse; for the normativist, the novel's suggestion that disease has a physiological reality indifferent to what anyone values — the lesions and fevers proceed whether or not the sanatorium culture dignifies them. Third, say plainly where the novel resists the philosophical frame altogether: Mann is writing about time, eros, death, and the education of a young man, and the ending sends Hans to the trenches of the First World War, which may undercut the entire sanatorium question as a privileged refuge. An honest reading should concede that. Finally, close by stating what, if anything, a novel of this sort can contribute to the naturalism-normativism debate that the journal literature cannot — I am skeptical of claims that fiction "does philosophy," but I am willing to be persuaded.

Aim for something I could deliver in about twenty minutes. No quotations from the text are needed; scene references will serve our group better, as several members read different translations.
