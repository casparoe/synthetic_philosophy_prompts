Exam question: "State Arrow's impossibility theorem, specifying each of its conditions precisely. Then assess the claim, associated with William Riker, that the theorem shows that talk of 'the will of the people' is incoherent. Does the theorem support that conclusion?"

Student's answer: "Arrow's theorem states that when there are three or more alternatives, no method of aggregating individual preferences into a social preference ordering can satisfy all of the following conditions at once. (1) Universal domain: the method must work for every conceivable profile of individual preferences, whatever those preferences may be, including intransitive or incomplete ones. (2) Pareto: if every individual prefers x to y, then society must rank x above y. (3) Independence of irrelevant alternatives: the social ranking of x against y may depend only on how individuals rank x against y, not on their attitudes toward any third alternative. (4) Non-dictatorship: no single individual's preferences automatically settle the social ranking. Arrow proved that any rule satisfying the first three conditions must be a dictatorship.

The theorem is best understood as a generalization of Condorcet's paradox: just as majority rule over three alternatives can cycle, Arrow showed that every aggregation rule will, for some preference profiles, deliver cyclical and hence incoherent social preferences.

Riker drew the conclusion that populist democracy -- the idea that elections discover a pre-existing will of the people -- is incoherent, since Arrow shows there is no coherent general will to be discovered. Riker concluded that democracy is therefore an illusion and that elections serve no genuine function. This final step, however, does not follow from the theorem. Arrow constrains aggregation rules; he does not show that individual preferences are unreal or that elections are pointless. Democracy can still be defended on the narrower ground that elections allow citizens to remove rulers without violence, and that function does not require a coherent general will -- so the theorem discredits at most one interpretation of democracy, not democracy as such."
