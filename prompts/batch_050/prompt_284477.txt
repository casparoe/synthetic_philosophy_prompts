I'm teaching a unit on infinite games and expected utility in my decision theory seminar, and one of my better students came to office hours with an argument that I suspect is broken but that I've had trouble diagnosing cleanly. Consider the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars (so positive payoffs on odd tosses, negative on even ones, growing in magnitude), with a counterparty idealized as able to cover arbitrarily large amounts. Her argument runs as follows. (1) The value of a game to a rational agent is the sum, over all possible outcomes, of each outcome's probability multiplied by its payoff, whenever that sum can be computed. (2) Listing the outcomes of the Pasadena game in their natural order (first heads on toss 1, then toss 2, and so on), the relevant series is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. (3) So the game's value is ln 2 dollars. (4) But the very same outcomes, with the very same probabilities and payoffs, can be listed in other orders, and by rearranging the terms of this series one can make it converge to any real number whatsoever, or diverge. (5) The value of a game cannot depend on the order in which one happens to list its outcomes, since the outcomes themselves are unchanged. (6) So the game is equally worth every amount and therefore determinately worth no amount; the very notion of its value is incoherent. (7) But there is nothing special about this game in the reasoning above: any evaluation involving summing over outcomes is hostage to the order of summation. (8) Therefore expected-value reasoning is illegitimate in general — even for ordinary finite games like a coin flip paying +2 on heads and −1 on tails, where listing order is equally conventional.

Step (8) is obviously a bridge too far — it would throw out all of decision theory over one pathological game — so something between (1) and (7) must give, but each step has a surface plausibility that I want explained rather than just denied. Please identify exactly where the argument fails: which step should be rejected, why it looked compelling, and what precisely distinguishes the Pasadena case from the finite case and from games like the St. Petersburg game, so that the diagnosis doesn't itself prove too much. Then address the residue: once the faulty step is repaired, does it follow that the Pasadena game simply has no expected value while remaining fully well-defined as a game, and if so, what resources does a rational agent have for evaluating it — can dominance-style reasoning, bounded utility, or restrictions on admissible orderings deliver a verdict where expectation is silent, or must decision theory simply confess a gap?
