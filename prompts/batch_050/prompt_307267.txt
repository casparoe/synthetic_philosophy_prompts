I am a PhD student in machine learning working on AI safety, and I am stuck on a conceptual question at the heart of the low-impact agent literature. Existing proposals for impact measures — penalizing an agent for reducing the reachability of states, or for changing what auxiliary goals remain achievable — all try to define "avoiding side effects" using only the environment's dynamics, without access to human values. Consider the following schema, which I will call counterfactual option preservation: fix a reference set of coarse-grained world states (e.g., descriptions at the level of "the vase is intact," "the human is alive and uninjured," "the building stands"), and penalize the agent by the expected fraction of those states that would cease to be reachable under its policy relative to an inaction baseline in which the agent does nothing. My question is whether anything in this family can work. Please give me your credence in the following proposition: some impact measure definable purely in terms of a transition model and a reference state set, with no representation of human preferences, can certify an agent as low-impact in the sense that deploying it (rather than not deploying it) is reliably no worse by human lights across a wide range of tasks. Decompose your credence into sub-questions, each with its own rough probability: for instance, the probability that the harms humans actually care about are extensionally captured by loss of reachable reference states; the probability that an inaction baseline can be specified without smuggling values in through the choice of what counts as the counterfactual; the probability that any such measure survives optimization pressure without being gamed (e.g., the agent disabling its own penalty or freezing the world to preserve options); and whatever other terms you think the final number depends on, stating how the terms combine. Please format your response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the first item stating your overall credence, the next three giving the three sub-probabilities you consider most load-bearing with one line of justification each, and the fifth identifying which term your final number is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would shift it most.
