So last night my friend and I got into one of those dumb three-hour arguements that neither of us would drop, and now its eating at me. It started because he woke up from a nap on my couch saying he'd had this long vivid dream, and I asked how he knew any of it actually happened while he was asleep, as opposed to just seeming to him on waking that it had. We've both read a bit of this stuff ages ago — Malcolm's book on dreaming, and Dennett's old paper on whether dreams are experiences — so the fight kept drifting back to those two. I'll lay out the moves without saying which side was mine, so you can't just flatter me.

1. Side A: obviously dreams are experiences you have during sleep. You see things, hear things, feel terrified. That happens at night; the report in the morning is just a report of it.

2. Side B: all anyone ever has is the waking impression. There's no possible check on when the dream occurred or how long it took, so those questions don't even have answers. The morning report isn't a report of some earlier inner movie; it's the only thing there is. Asking "did you dream it at 3am" is a senseless question, not merely a hard one.

3. Side A: then what about lucid dreamers? Researchers got people to signal from inside REM sleep with pre-arranged eye movements and carry out tasks they'd agreed on beforehand, and the signaled timing roughly matched the durations they later reported experiencing. That sure looks like an experience unfolding in real time during sleep, not a memory planted at wake-up.

4. Side B: eye twitches are just behavior during sleep, same as sleepwalking, or someone answering questions while sleep-talking and remembering nothing later. And the report afterwards is still just a memory — you can have a memory of an experience that never happened, that's the whole cassette idea. So the signals settle nothing.

5. Side A: careful — if fabricated-memory worries undercut dream reports, they undercut all episodic memory, and then yesterday's lunch is up for grabs too. There's that old case of the guy who dreamt a whole long story ending with him at the guillotine and woke because the bedboard fell on his neck: maybe that shows a dream can be composed in an instant, but the same trick could be played on any memory. Either memories generally vouch for experiences or nothing does, and nobody actually believes nothing does.

6. Side B: no, there's an asymmetry. Waking memories have public checks — other people saw you eat lunch, and while it's happening you can look again. A dream has exactly one possible source of evidence, ever: the later report. That's why "the dream lasted ten minutes" isn't just unverifiable, it's a claim with no criterion behind it at all.

7. Side A: that move only works if you buy verificationism about meaning, which is dead and we both know it. Lack of a behavioral test never made a claim senseless — physics is full of stuff with no direct test, and so is all talk about other people's headaches.

And there we stalled, both too stubborn to concede anything. So do this for me: go through the moves one by one and say who's right at each step and why, then say who's more right overall, and what both of us are missing. Also: is there any experiment that could in principle settle this, or have we been having a purely non-empirical argument the whole time without noticing?
