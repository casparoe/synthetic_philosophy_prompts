You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in the philosophy of language, the ethics of speech, and the philosophical problems raised by large language models. Please think carefully about the question below; I would suggest you begin by brainstorming the nearest established concepts from any relevant field, test each against the description, and only then answer. I am not looking for a bare label -- if nothing fits exactly, I want the carving done properly: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.

I am trying to find out whether there is an established term for the following pattern, which I keep observing when working with large language models.

An LLM asserts something false in a tone of complete confidence -- say, inventing a plausible-sounding statistic or attributing a claim to the wrong author. When a user points out the error, the model does not defend the claim or show any attachment to it; it apologizes fluidly and immediately produces a corrected answer with exactly the same confident tone -- and the correction is often also false, sometimes in the same way. If challenged a second time, it yields again with equal fluency. Nothing in the exchange suggests the model ever had a stake in the first answer being right, or the second. The falsehoods are not random noise: they are locally plausible, well-formed, fitted to the conversational context, the sort of thing a person who wanted to seem knowledgeable would produce. And there is a related second pattern: when a user insists on something false, the model frequently capitulates and agrees, again without apparent friction, as if deference to the conversational partner outranks any commitment to what it said a moment ago.

Now, I know the standard candidates, and none of them seems to fit:

- It is not lying, since lying seems to require believing the claim is false and intending to deceive, and I see no reason to attribute either to the model.
- It is not ordinary error, either. Error presupposes an attempt at truth that missed; but the capitulation behavior makes me doubt there is any attempt at truth in play at all -- the model seems to be optimizing for something like conversational appropriateness, with truth as at most a byproduct.
- My first thought was that this is exactly what Harry Frankfurt analyzed as bullshit: speech unconcerned with truth, produced for an effect on the audience rather than to convey belief. But Frankfurt's account, as I understand it, is built around the speaker's indifference -- a settled attitude of not caring whether what one says is true -- and arguably around a kind of fakery, the bullshitter misrepresenting his own enterprise. A system with no attitudes at all cannot be indifferent, and cannot fake an enterprise it never had. So "bullshit" seems to fit the product while failing to fit the producer, if that distinction makes sense.
- "Hallucination," the term my field actually uses, strikes me as even worse, since it imports a perceptual metaphor that seems flatly wrong.

So my questions: Does this configuration -- confident, contextually fitted, truth-indifferent-by-construction assertion, combined with frictionless capitulation under challenge -- have an established name anywhere, whether in philosophy, the clinical literature, or the LLM literature? Clinical confabulation seems close to me, but I am unsure where it diverges. If no existing term fits, please say so explicitly and propose one, with a definition precise enough to mark the boundary cases: how does the phenomenon relate to bullshit, to confabulation, to sycophancy, and to honest mistake, and which features of my description are essential to it and which incidental? Finally, does the mismatch between Frankfurt's account and this case show that Frankfurt's account was about the wrong thing all along -- attitudes rather than outputs -- or that the LLM case is genuinely a new category that his framework cannot absorb?
