Two coworkers got into it over an A/B test at their company and neither will budge, and I keep flip-flopping on who's right, so I want a referee.

The company randomly assigned half its team leads to a new management training program. Six months later, the trained leads' teams score slightly *worse* on average than the control. Maya says that's confounded and pulls up the split by team engagement scores: within both the high-engagement and low-engagement teams, training *helps*. Better in each subgroup, worse overall -- textbook Simpson's paradox, she says, so you condition on the grouping variable, conclude the training works, roll it out.

Devon says not so fast: the engagement survey was administered *after* the training, so engagement might be partly an effect of the program. Conditioning on a downstream variable is exactly the mistake from all those collider/mediator examples -- you can manufacture a spurious benefit or erase a real harm. With a post-treatment grouping you can't trust the split, and since assignment was random the raw aggregate is an unbiased estimate of the actual effect, so the training probably doesn't work.

Maya's comeback: Devon is just pattern-matching to a blog post about colliders. Engagement is a pretty stable trait, the survey is basically measuring a pre-existing characteristic, the within-group gains are large and consistent across both strata, and anyway "randomized assignment" doesn't help if randomization happened to lump all the disengaged teams into the treatment arm.

Devon's reply: if randomization was done properly there *is* no confounding to correct, so a reversal appearing only after you condition is itself evidence you're conditioning on something downstream. And effect sizes don't settle whether conditioning is legit -- the within-stratum comparisons are no longer randomized comparisons.

Who's right about what here, and what are they both missing?
