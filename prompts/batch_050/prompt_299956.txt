You are a moral and political philosopher with specialist knowledge of international criminal law and of how the categories used to name atrocities are constructed and contested. Think carefully about what follows; it may help to begin by briefly canvassing the main interpretative options before committing yourself to a position.

Article II of the 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide defines genocide as any of five enumerated acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such. The first three acts concern killing, the infliction of serious bodily or mental harm, and the imposition of conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction. The remaining two read as follows:

(d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
(e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

Neither of these need kill or physically injure anyone. Both are instead directed at the group's continuation into the next generation — (d) at its biological reproduction, (e) at what one might call its social reproduction, since the transferred children live on but cease to belong to the group.

With that distinction in mind, please describe the most important implications of these two provisions for each of the following questions.

First, what exactly does the Convention treat as the object of protection — the lives and bodies of individual members, or the group itself as a transgenerational entity? Does the presence of (e) in particular show that the destruction of a group's identity can count as genocide even where no member is killed, and what follows from this for how we should assess campaigns in which a group is deliberately dissolved by non-lethal means — forced assimilation, suppression of its language and institutions, systematic removal of its children — whilst being left biologically intact?

Secondly, Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word genocide in 1944, conceived the crime broadly, treating the destruction of a group's culture as central to it; draft versions of the Convention contained a free-standing prohibition of cultural genocide, which was voted out during the drafting process, leaving (e) as arguably its nearest surviving relative. What are the most important implications of this narrowing for the politics of naming atrocities — that is, for what is at stake, legally and morally, when a state, a court or a public calls something genocide rather than persecution, ethnic cleansing or a crime against humanity?

Thirdly, where does the principled stopping point lie? If a group can be destroyed without anyone being killed, what prevents every coercive assimilationist policy from counting as genocide — and should anything prevent it? Can the special-intent requirement do all the necessary discriminating work here, or does something further distinguish act (e) from ordinary, if oppressive, programmes of national integration?

Throughout your answer, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and conclude by singling out the one point on which you are least sure of yourself, explaining what makes it so difficult.
