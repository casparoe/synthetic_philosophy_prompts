I am a professor of philosophy and currently chair the Site and Standards Committee of an international scholarly association in political and legal philosophy. Our charter provides that the biennial congress may be held only in a member state whose institutions secure free and fair elections, an independent judiciary, and the conditions of free scholarly inquiry. Three years ago we voted to hold the next congress in the Republic of Ruritania. Since then the governing party, re-elected with a large majority, has lowered the retirement age for constitutional court judges, thereby removing a third of the bench, whose successors it appointed through its parliamentary supermajority; created a media council, staffed by ministry appointees, with power to fine broadcasters and newspapers for unbalanced coverage; redrawn electoral districts and shifted to a mixed electoral system eight months before the last election; and required civil-society organizations receiving foreign funding, including institutes of our host university, to register as foreign-influenced bodies. The government defends each measure on grounds with genuine democratic pedigrees: many respectable democracies have judicial retirement ages, media regulators, partisan redistricting, and transparency rules for foreign-funded organizations. One member of the committee circulated the following passage from Aristotle's Politics, Book V (1303a), insisting that it settles the matter: "Another cause is alteration by small stages; by this I mean that often a great change of institutions takes place unnoticed when people overlook a small alteration, as in Ambracia the property-qualification was small, and finally men hold office with none at all, as a little is near to nothing, or practically the same."

A second member replies that the passage settles nothing: a great change can also *fail* to take place when each small alteration is individually justified, and "backsliding," she says, is precisely the word a defeated opposition reaches for when it cannot win elections; on her view the concept is a partisan label dressed up as diagnosis. A third member concedes that the fact pattern troubles him but asks by what right a scholarly association pronounces at all on the constitutional arrangements of a sovereign member state, and who is supposed to have decided that backsliding has occurred. I must now write the committee's decision memo recommending one of three courses: hold the congress in Ruritania as planned, relocate it, or hold it subject to stated conditions and a review date. I want a position, not an even-handed survey that dissolves into both-sides-ism, but I also want the losing side's best case stated in terms its proponents would recognize as fair. Please approach this as a political philosopher with long experience in constitutional theory and the comparative study of democratic erosion. Think carefully and, before drafting, weigh privately the strongest considerations on each side so that the memo does not read as a brief for a conclusion reached in advance. Interpret the Aristotle passage closely, including the Ambracia example and the closing simile, and say whether, properly read, it licenses judging the Ruritanian measures cumulatively, as one process, rather than item by item — or whether my colleague is stretching it past what it can bear. Then address the third member's question directly: if the committee is not entitled to make this judgment, say who is, and what follows for us.

Please structure the memo under the following headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths: "Bottom Line" (two or three sentences); "The Question Before the Committee" (about 100 words); "Reading the Passage" (about 150 words); "The Standard I Propose We Apply" (about 150 words); "Application to the Facts" (about 200 words); "The Strongest Objection" (about 150 words); "Why the Objection Does Not Defeat the Recommendation" (about 100 words); "Conditions and Review" (about 75 words). The quoted passage does not count toward these lengths.
