My philosophy of language prof put this summary of Grice in her lecture slides and I copied it down: "Grice's point was that what a speaker means often goes beyond the literal meaning of their words. When you say something, your listener works out extra conclusions by assuming you're following the Cooperative Principle and its maxims -- be truthful, be relevant, be brief, don't say more than needed. These extra conclusions are called implicatures. Since implicatures depend on the context rather than the words themselves, the same sentence will implicate different things on different occasions. Implicatures are part of what the speaker means, but unlike what is said, they can be cancelled without contradiction. Grice showed that most of what we communicate in ordinary conversation is implicated rather than said, which proves that literal meaning plays only a minor role in communication."

Most of this sounded right to me when she said it, but then a friend in the class told me the bit about "don't say more than needed" is doing double duty and the last sentence oversells it. I honestly can't tell which parts are standard Grice, which are a common simplification, and which (if any) she'd push back on if I brought them up in office hours. Can you audit it piece by piece? And does the conclusion in the last sentence actually follow from the parts that are accurate?
