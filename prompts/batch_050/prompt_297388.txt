So last night my roommate and I got into this stupidly long argument that started from a news story about a squatter who ended up legally owning a house, and it's still bugging me. The thing is, our legal system (we're in the US, but I think a lot of this generalizes) has this whole cluster of rules about how you come to own stuff that nobody owned before, or that the previous owner has stopped paying attention to. Adverse possession: if you openly, continuously, and exclusively occupy someone's land for long enough -- like years, sometimes decades -- without hiding it, you can actually become the legal owner, even if you knew it wasn't yours. First-possession rules for found objects and wild animals. Water rights in the western states going to whoever diverted the water first for productive use, not to whoever owns the land along the river. All of this has a vibe, and my roommate's claim was that the vibe is basically just Locke: you mix your labor with the unowned thing, that's what makes it yours, the law is Locke's theory of acquisition in a costume. My claim was that he's projecting -- the law doesn't care about labor at all, it cares about keeping land in productive use and having clean title records, which is a totally different, utilitarian-ish justification, and a squatter who just camps and gardens a little wins just as much as someone who pours sweat into the place.

Here's where we got stuck, and where I'd like some actual help. First: what theory of original acquisition does this cluster of practices *actually* presuppose, if any? I want someone to distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires to make sense and what it merely fits well with. Because on the one hand, the law demands a bunch of stuff Locke doesn't seem to care much about -- openness, notoriety, a fixed statutory period, sometimes paying the property taxes -- and it pointedly does *not* demand the one thing Locke supposedly cares about most, namely labor. On the other hand, "labor" is doing *some* work in explaining why it's the possessor and not the original absentee owner who wins, right? So is my roommate half-right, or is the labor part fully replaceable by incentives-and-record-keeping talk?

Second, and this is the part that really broke our argument: Locke has that famous proviso, that your acquisition is only legitimate if you leave enough and as good for others. As far as we can tell, nothing anywhere in these doctrines cares about that at all. You can adversely possess the *last* undeveloped lot in town; the first appropriator can take a huge share of a river even in a drought year. So does that mean the practice presupposes a Lockean acquisition theory with the proviso quietly amputated? Or does it show the practice was never Lockean to begin with? If it helps, consider the case we invented at like 2am: a settler in a desert valley fences off the one reliable spring, improves it, maintains it openly for twenty years -- clearly owns it under the actual law, and under labor-mixing, but the proviso seems to scream no. If we rebuilt adverse possession and prior appropriation to take the proviso seriously, what would concretely have to change, and would anything recognizable of the original doctrines survive? Also feel free to tell me if the whole bundle just doesn't presuppose any single coherent theory -- my roommate insisted it has to, but that seemed to me like his philosopher's prejudice more than anything.
