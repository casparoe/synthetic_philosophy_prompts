I run an internal philosophy reading group at a software company — about fourteen engineers, none with formal philosophy training, meeting for 75 minutes in a conference room with whiteboards, index cards, and sticky notes, but no laptops allowed. We are finishing a unit on epistocracy versus democracy: roughly, whether political authority should be distributed equally regardless of competence, or weighted toward the informed and the reasonable. Design a structured in-person game that makes participants feel the force of both sides of this dispute before either side is named.

The game must have two phases, in this order. Phase one should make the pull of epistocracy visceral: the group makes a sequence of collective decisions by strict one-person-one-vote under time pressure, where each decision has an objectively correct answer that can be revealed within the session, where some participants reliably have better information or a better decision procedure than others, and where the group's wrong calls impose a real (small, non-monetary) cost on everyone — so that the stronger performers experience the specific frustration of being outvoted by people they can see are guessing, and the guessers are later confronted with what their votes did. Phase two should then let the group adopt a competence-weighted system of their own design — weighted votes, a certification exam, a restricted franchise, whatever they choose — and it must be constructed so that the certification mechanism itself becomes the problem: whoever writes or grades the test acquires quiet power, the test ends up encoding the test-writers' blind spots, some genuinely good performers get disenfranchised, and some merely confident ones get certified. In other words, phase two should stage in miniature the standard democratic reply — that any procedure for identifying the competent is itself a political decision, subject to capture, bias, and error — and participants should run into that reply themselves rather than have it announced to them.

Please specify all of the following:

(a) Complete rules for both phases, including the actual decision problems. These must be questions whose correct answers the facilitator can verify live in a room with no internet: estimation, logic, and calibration tasks are fine; trivia requiring outside knowledge is not.

(b) A scoring or penalty scheme that creates genuine stakes without money and without humiliating anyone.

(c) The exact mechanism by which phase two's certification test gets written, administered, and graded, designed so that its bias emerges naturally from the participants' behavior rather than being scripted by the facilitator.

(d) A minute-by-minute timeline that fits the 75 minutes including the debrief.

(e) A debrief plan that maps specific moments from the session onto the philosophical dispute — which moments correspond to the epistocrat's core argument and which to the objection about who certifies the certifiers — and that has participants describe what they felt at each moment before any terminology is introduced.

(f) The two or three ways this will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (for example, a charismatic participant dominating the test-writing, or players gaming the estimation tasks once they understand the structure) and how to handle each.

(g) An honest note on which features of the real dispute the game cannot reproduce — for instance, that actual political decisions rarely have verifiable right answers, and that real disenfranchisement is not an exercise one can walk out of after an hour — and what the facilitator should say to keep participants from overgeneralizing from the activity.

Write it as a workshop design document I could hand to a colleague to run without me in the room.
