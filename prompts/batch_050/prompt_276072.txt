For the past year I have been teaching myself Islamic theology out of books and lectures, with no formal training, and I run a small monthly study circle for friends who are doing the same. We have just finished a stretch on the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite dispute over whether moral truths are knowable by unaided reason or whether they exist only because God commands them — the so-called Islamic Euthyphro. Our habit is to end each stretch with a self-administered exam, and it is my turn to write it. I want one essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, that cannot be passed by reciting the slogan that the Mu'tazila side with reason and the Ash'arites with revelation.

To force past the slogan, I would like the question built around a constructed case of my own devising, which you may restate or sharpen: suppose God issues a command that strikes every believer as gratuitously cruel — say, commanding that a harmless stranger be made to suffer with no redeeming purpose and no afterlife compensation. The question should make the writer work out, for each school, what this case does and does not show. Concretely, I want it to probe: (1) whether the Ash'arite can still meaningfully call God just in this case, or whether justice collapses into whatever God happens to will, so that praising God's justice becomes empty; (2) whether the Mu'tazilite's insistence that reason grasps good and evil independently commits them to a moral order standing over God, and how that sits with divine sovereignty; (3) why anyone is obligated to obey God at all on each view — gratitude and benefit on one side, sheer ownership and power on the other — and whether either answer avoids reducing obligation to mere prudence or mere force; and (4) where each side's standard reply to these worries succeeds and where it merely relocates the problem.

Please deliver three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, including the constructed case. Second, a model answer of roughly 500 words written to the standard of the best student in our circle. Third, a marking scheme out of 25: five must-hit points with the marks each carries, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way a weaker answer fumbles it — for instance, treating the Ash'arite position as simple arbitrariness, or assuming the Mu'tazilite view straightforwardly secures God's justice without addressing the sovereignty cost.

Finally, since I am grading without a teacher to consult, I would like the model answer to end with a candid epistemic note: how confident you are in its characterizations of each school's position, and the single claim in it you are least sure of, with a sentence on why that point is shakiest.
