I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reporting a story on how universities are responding to chatbots that can produce fluent student essays on demand. In the course of my reporting I've noticed something that strikes me as philosophically odd, and I'd like help thinking it through. The concrete practices: many professors and whole departments are reverting to in-class, handwritten, closed-book exams and live oral examinations, on the explicit grounds that a take-home essay no longer certifies that the student understands anything, since the essay might be the product of a machine that (they insist) understands nothing. Meanwhile, the very same institutions continue to treat a student's own fluent output -- the essay, the problem set, the seminar contribution -- as essentially conclusive evidence of comprehension; nobody requires the student to demonstrate anything beyond the competent performance itself. So within a single institution, fluent performance is treated as proof of understanding when a human produces it, and as no evidence of understanding whatsoever when a machine produces it. My sources mostly shrug when I ask what justifies the asymmetry.

Here's the conceptual background as I understand it, and please correct me if I've mangled it. There's an old, intuitive picture on which genuine skill flows from understanding: first you grasp the principles, then competence follows. Against this, some thinkers describe Darwinism and Turing-style computation as "strange inversions" of that picture -- natural selection builds exquisite functional competence (flight, camouflage, metabolism) with no comprehending designer anywhere in the loop, and a computer executes competent symbol-manipulation without, at least arguably, any comprehension of what the symbols mean. On this inverted view, competence is the basic phenomenon and comprehension, where it exists at all, is a late and local achievement built out of layers of competence, not its source.

What I'd like from you: reconstruct the theory of understanding that each practice presupposes. What view of the competence-comprehension relation does performance-based assessment in education (and, for that matter, performance-based licensing of pilots, doctors, and lawyers) commit us to -- does it tacitly assume the old comprehension-first picture, or is it actually neutral between the two pictures? And what conception of understanding does the dismissal of machine fluency presuppose -- does it require that there be some inner fact of comprehension that no amount of behavioral competence could settle, and is that conception coherent or does it smuggle in something like a Cartesian theater? Be careful to distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of understanding would license it equally? Finally, suppose the strange-inversion picture is right and comprehension just is, at bottom, a particular organization of competences. What would assessment then have to look like -- does any behavioral test certify understanding, does the return to oral exams actually solve the problem the professors think it solves, and does the asymmetry between the human and machine cases survive, or must one side of the practice give?
