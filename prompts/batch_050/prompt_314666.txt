For my upper-level seminar on epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, I would like you to write a piece of satire that only someone who genuinely understands the literature could produce.

The task: draft an excerpt, roughly 600 words, from the official customer-service training manual of a fictional retail bank whose corporate leadership has fully embraced the thesis that whether a customer *knows* the bank is open on Saturdays depends on how much rides on that belief for the customer. The manual should instruct tellers, in polished deadpan HR prose, on how to assess each inquiring customer's practical situation before deciding whether to affirm that the customer knows the Saturday hours, merely believes them, or should be advised to go double-check the posted sign. The humor must arise from the actual machinery of the view — stakes-sensitivity, the salience of error-possibilities, the idea that knowledge should be able to rationally license action — rather than from invented absurdities.

Requirements:

1. Include at least three worked scenarios in the manual's characteristic voice: a customer depositing a trivial birthday check, a customer whose mortgage will default if the deposit misses the Saturday cutoff, and one harder case that forces the manual to confront a genuine awkwardness for the theory (e.g., the teller's own stakes, two spouses whose situations differ, or a customer whose circumstances change mid-conversation). The manual should handle the hard case with confident bureaucratic language that quietly exposes the problem.
2. Include a short appendix attributed to the bank's retained epistemology consultant, whose fee structure gives them a stake of their own in whether the advice was correct.
3. The irony must remain deadpan and legible: at no point should the text wink, admit it is satire, or state the standard objections outright, yet a careful reader should finish with a felt sense of what is genuinely puzzling about letting practical interests do epistemic work — and also, ideally, a sense of what the view's defenders find compelling about the everyday cases.
4. Do not name or quote real philosophers; invent the consultant and any internal doctrine names.
5. Maintain consistent corporate-manual formatting: numbered sections, a stated policy rationale, and the tone of an institution that has never once doubted itself.
