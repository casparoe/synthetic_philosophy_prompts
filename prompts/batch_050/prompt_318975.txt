I'm a software engineer at a company that builds immersive virtual-reality products, and I've been asked to write the philosophy section of an internal design-ethics review. I have no formal philosophy training. Our products are not games: they are long-session "life enrichment" environments in which users pursue simulated careers, friendships, and creative projects, some of them for twenty or thirty hours a week. The obvious philosophical starting point is Nozick's experience machine, but the classic debate seems to assume an all-or-nothing decision — plug in for the rest of your life or stay out — whereas our products differ from the thought experiment in at least three ways: immersion is partial and revocable rather than lifelong; the environments are shared, so users interact with other real people instead of a private script; and some in-world work produces real outputs, such as published writing or shipped code.

My memo can only carry three or four substantive arguments into the review, because each argument translates into engineering work for a team with limited capacity over the next two quarters. So: which implications of the experience machine debate genuinely matter for designing products like ours, and which are overrated or fail to transfer from the thought experiment to partial immersion? Please rank the three or four implications that should make it into the memo and defend the ordering. The criteria I care about are: whether an implication, if sound, would force a change in product design rather than only in marketing; whether it survives the three differences from the classic setup listed above; and whether it depends on one contested theory of well-being (hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective-list theories) or holds across several of them. Please be explicit about trade-offs among these criteria where they pull in different directions. Also flag any implications commonly drawn from the thought experiment that you think do not actually follow — several of my colleagues, for instance, argue that it merely refutes the claim that pleasure is the only thing that matters, which nobody at the company holds anyway. Finally, for your top-ranked implication, state what would move it down the list: an empirical finding, a philosophical argument, or a design feature that would neutralize it.
