I am preparing a short paper for a graduate seminar on population ethics, and before I submit it I would like an audit of the positions I have committed myself to. Please respond as a specialist in population axiology who has thought carefully about the formal structure of these debates; work through the logical relations among my commitments step by step rather than reaching for a summary verdict.

First, some context. In Book IV, Chapter 1 of The Methods of Ethics (1874), Henry Sidgwick considers whether utilitarianism should direct us to maximize the average happiness of a population or its total, and concludes — against what he takes to be the assumption of the political economists influenced by Malthus — that the utilitarian optimum is the point at which the product of the number of persons living and their average happiness is greatest, so that an addition of people whose lives are on the whole happy can be good even if it lowers the average. My own view is built around an endorsement of that conclusion. Here are my commitments, as stated in the paper:

(a) The relation "is at least as good as, all things considered" is transitive and complete over outcomes.
(b) Mere addition: adding to a population further people whose lives are well worth living, without affecting the existing people, never makes an outcome worse.
(c) The Repugnant Conclusion is false: for any population of people with excellent lives, there is some much larger population whose lives are barely worth living such that the larger population is worse, not better.
(d) Sidgwick's conclusion in the passage described above is essentially correct: total welfare, not average, is what matters for ranking outcomes that differ in population size.
(e) The procreation asymmetry: there is a strong reason not to bring into existence a life that would be miserable, but no reason of comparable kind to bring into existence a life merely because it would be happy.
(f) Future people can be wronged by our choices even when those very choices determine which people will exist, as in standard non-identity cases such as a policy of resource depletion.

What I want from you is an audit, not an exposition of the field. I do not know which, if any, of these commitments pull against each other, and I have no prior view about which I would abandon. Please identify which pairs or subsets of (a)–(f) are in genuine tension, which pairings might look problematic but in fact cohere on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity, indicating for each what would minimally have to give. In doing so, please distinguish carefully between points on which there is broad agreement among philosophers working in this area (for example, which formal results are established) and points that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the received one.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, ordered from the most severe problem to the least, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph.
