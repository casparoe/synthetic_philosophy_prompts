My partner and I recently set up a joint giving fund, and we have hit a practical version of a problem I now realize has a name in moral philosophy: moral trade. She is a committed animal welfarist; I give mostly to global health and poverty relief. Neither of us thinks the other's cause is worthless, but we disagree substantially about how much good the typical donation to each does. The obvious proposal on the table is a bargain: she redirects some of what she would have given anyway to my preferred charities, I do the same for hers, and we both end up with more of what we value than if we had each given independently. It is usually motivated by an analogy to gains from trade in economics, sometimes formalized with the Nash bargaining solution over our respective utility-like valuations.

I have more or less talked myself into accepting the view that such bargains are not merely permissible but often obligatory for agents with genuinely conflicting values who must act together or can influence each other's actions -- that refusing to trade when both parties could thereby advance their own moral aims is a kind of moral inefficiency with no principled defense. Before I make this the governing principle of our household, I want a serious account of what would count against it. Please do not just list objections; organize the defeaters by how decisive they would be if true, and for each one say what the world -- or the theory -- would have to look like for it to hold.

Some angles I am unsure how to weight, which you should feel free to reframe or supersede. First, whether the analogy to economic trade survives the fact that there is no common currency of moral value: the Nash framework assumes something like interpersonally comparable utility, and I am not sure what takes its place when the parties disagree about the good itself rather than about means to it. Second, whether the view collapses under moral uncertainty in a different way -- if I should already be giving credence to my partner's worldview, the bargain may be redundant or even a way of laundering a duty I already have. Third, whether there are cases the view cannot describe: trade with parties whose values I take to be abhorrent rather than merely mistaken, where fulfilling my side of the bargain seems to make me complicit in something I should be trying to prevent. Fourth, whether the position is actually insulated from defeat in practice -- whether any proposed counterexample gets absorbed by redefining what counts as a genuine bargain, a fair division, or a party worth trading with, and if so whether that unfalsifiability is a defect in a view of this kind or just a sign that it is a norm of practical reason rather than an empirical hypothesis.

I would also like to know which defeaters a committed proponent of moral trade would themselves concede as damaging, rather than ones only its critics press, and which of the above you expect such a proponent to dismiss as misunderstanding the proposal. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each part of your analysis, and single out explicitly the single claim in your answer you are least sure of and why.
