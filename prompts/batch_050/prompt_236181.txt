I lead a small formal-verification team, and a bottleneck has developed in our review pipeline that I want to fix with an actual procedure rather than tribal knowledge. Our spec language and proof assistant are intuitionistic by default, because we extract executable code from existence proofs -- a proof that a witness exists has to actually hand us the witness. The problem is that our reviewers are strong engineers but not logicians, and our proof authors keep submitting arguments that are perfectly fine classically but cannot be extracted from. Review meetings then collapse into the same argument: one reviewer says "this step uses proof by contradiction, reject it," another says "it's fine, the theorem is true, just trust the math," and a third says "can't the proof assistant just be told to allow excluded middle this once?" Last week we bounced back a proof three times before I realized the flagged step was actually constructively valid, and in the same batch we waved through a proof whose existence claim, on close inspection, rested on a case split of the form "either P or not P" where nobody could compute which disjunct holds. I need to stop being the appeals court for every line item.

Please write a triage procedure my reviewers can run on any proof, in roughly thirty minutes, that classifies each suspicious step as constructively valid, constructively repairable, or essentially classical. It should be a sequence of tests applied in order, each with what counts as a passing answer: for example, distinguishing proving a negation by assuming the statement and deriving absurdity (fine) from proving a positive existence claim by assuming its negation and deriving absurdity (not fine); checking whether an instance of excluded middle is over a decidable proposition, in which case it is harmless, and making the test for "decidable in this context" concrete enough that a non-logician can apply it; and identifying the less obvious places classical reasoning hides, like double-negation elimination over an existential, or deriving an "or" from a "not (not A and not B)". Where a step fails, the procedure should say what a repair typically looks like -- restructuring around a decidable predicate, weakening the claim, supplying an explicit witness or algorithm -- and where it should instead stop and escalate to me, because the question is genuinely subtle rather than mechanical. Be explicit about which distinctions from the underlying logic have to survive in the checklist and which you are deliberately flattening, and flag the failure modes: where this procedure will wrongly pass a proof, and where it will wrongly reject one.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a proof that a comparison-sorting routine meeting our spec exists, done by assuming no such routine exists and deriving a contradiction from the spec's consistency clause. Second: the classic classroom argument that there exist irrational numbers a and b with a^b rational, which proceeds by considering a particular candidate and splitting on whether it is rational. Third: a proof that every input to a function either triggers a certain exception or doesn't, offered as a lemma -- state what the reviewer must check before passing it. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict a reviewer will find counterintuitive or annoying, and say how the reviewer should be trained to handle that reaction rather than overriding the checklist. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure cannot settle no matter how well it is written, so I know which decisions will keep landing on my desk.
