I'm writing a long post for a mathy blog about the two-envelope paradox and I'm sick of summaries that just list "the resolutions" as if they're all answering the same question. I want a decision tree that sorts the resolutions by what they actually commit you to, so a reader can walk down it and find out which one they believe.

Setup for reference: two envelopes, one has twice the money of the other, you pick one and get the option to switch. The dodgy argument says the other envelope holds either double or half, so switching gains you 5/4 in expectation. The tree's job is to sort responses to that.

Some requirements:

- Each branching question has to be phrased as a concrete case or claim the reader can react to without knowing any of the literature -- no "do you accept the Nalebuff conditioning principle?", more like "if someone offered you a deal where the expected value literally doesn't exist because the mean is infinite, do you think rational choice theory should still tell you what to do?"
- Include the variant where the amounts follow a doubling setup with infinite expected value (priors on the pair (1,2), (2,4), (4,8)... weighted so the expectation diverges), because the tree should split responses that only work for finite cases from ones that survive it.
- Include the randomized-switching strategy (you look at your amount, then switch with a probability that shrinks as the amount grows, and this genuinely gets you the bigger envelope more than half the time). I want to see which leaves have to say something awkward about it, since it sure looks like switching has real value there.
- One leaf should be the modus tollens reading: the paradox is a reductio of unbounded utility or of expected-value maximization itself, not a puzzle with a solution. I want to see where on the tree that branches off from the "it's just a mistake about priors" people.
- Also make sure the tree separates the "no proper probability distribution makes switching good conditional on every amount you'd see" diagnosis from the "the variable x shifts meaning mid-argument" diagnosis. People constantly mush those together and they're different claims.
- Aim for 6 to 9 leaves. Each leaf gets a short punchy name, one sentence stating the position, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Mark any combinations of answers that are unoccupied and say why -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And if two leaves can't actually be separated by any question, flag that instead of forcing it.

Audience is people who know what expected value is but havent read anything on this paradox. Don't cite anyone, I just want the logic.
