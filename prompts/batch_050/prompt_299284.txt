I'm in the second year of a politics degree and we're doing Locke's chapter on property in the Second Treatise. In seminar prep I got stuck on a bit of §28 that isn't the usual acorns-and-apples stuff everyone quotes. Locke writes: "Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

The servant bit bothered me, so I tried to turn my worry into a proper argument and now it looks like it proves way too much, which usually means I've broken something. Here it is. (1) Locke's whole account only works if the person who ends up owning the thing is the person whose labour got mixed into it — that's the point of property in one's own person, the labour is "the unquestionable property of the labourer". (2) The servant who cut the turfs is a man like any other, so his labour is his property, not his master's. (3) But Locke flatly says the turfs become the master's property. (4) So on Locke's own account you can acquire property through labour you never performed, as long as the labourer stands in the right relation to you. (5) If that's allowed, then any sufficiently clever arrangement of relations — wages, rents, whatever — lets the non-labouring party soak up the products of everyone else's labour, and the labour-mixing principle stops ruling anything out; it justifies exactly the unlimited accumulation the proviso was supposed to restrain. (6) That's absurd, so something between (1) and (4) has to give.

My instincts are that I'd rather give up (1) than (2), since giving up self-ownership feels like it sinks the whole Treatise, but honestly I can't see which step is actually the dodgy one. Tell me which premise you'd reject and what rejecting it costs — and if you think the argument is actually sound, say what that would imply for how Locke should be read. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, no preamble, then the reasoning. And flag where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong: what reading of the passage, or what distinction, would change your mind if it turned out to be right?
