I'm preparing a position statement for an interdisciplinary reading group on the foundations of probability and economics, and before I circulate it I'd like a stress test. Please take on the role of a philosopher of probability who is also fluent in the Keynes–Ramsey–Savage literature, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary of the debate. My view is broadly Keynesian, but I've borrowed pieces from other traditions, and I have a nagging feeling some of the pieces don't fit. Here is what I currently hold:

(a) Probability is, at least in many central cases, a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis: given the same evidence, there is a fact of the matter about how much confidence is rational, so probability is not merely a record of someone's personal betting dispositions. (b) Many important probabilities are non-numerical, and some pairs of probabilities are simply not comparable as greater or lesser; moreover, the weight of evidence behind a judgment is a genuinely separate dimension from its probability, and ignoring it distorts decision theory. (c) Ramsey's critique of Keynes essentially succeeds: talk of "logical relations of partial entailment" is obscure, and the only operational foundation we actually have for probability is coherent betting behavior, so something like subjective Bayesianism with Dutch book constraints is the right working account. (d) For decisions under risk, expected utility maximization in the Savage style is the correct normative framework, and departures from it are irrational. (e) Keynes was right that the long-run decisions that drive a market economy — investment, innovation, accumulation — are made under uncertainty that is not reducible to any probability distribution, and that the classical habit of pretending otherwise is a deep methodological error. (f) Ambiguity aversion of the kind Ellsberg's urn cases elicit is not a bias to be debunked; it can be a reasonable response to thin evidence.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Go through the set and tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which apparent conflicts dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely. Rank the genuine tensions by severity — a forced choice between two of my deepest commitments counts as more severe than an awkwardness that a small qualification repairs. For each genuine tension, state what would have to give on each side and what the cheapest revision costs me in terms of what I clearly care about. Finally, if there is a single revision that would resolve the most tension at once, name it and be honest about whether the resulting position is still recognizably Keynesian or whether I've quietly become a Bayesian with unusual rhetoric. If you think the set is more coherent than I fear, say so, but only after you've actually tried to break it.
