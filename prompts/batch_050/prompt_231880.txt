I'm part of a philosophy reading group currently working through the literature on consequentialist cluelessness — the worry that for almost any action we take, the great bulk of its morally significant consequences lie in the far future and depend chaotically on which people come to exist, so we cannot even in principle rank our options by their total value. The standard illustration is that helping a stranger across the street today slightly shifts the timing of countless future encounters, which shifts which children are ever conceived, which shifts who leads nations in three hundred years — so my kind act might, in total effect, be a catastrophe, and the mugging I failed to prevent might have been a blessing. In our last meeting, someone argued this shows consequentialism gives no usable guidance at all; someone else replied that ignorance of consequences is symmetric and simply washes out, so we may as well keep relying on the foreseeable near-term effects. Nobody was satisfied with either answer.

Write a short story (roughly 1,500–2,000 words) that makes this dispute felt rather than merely stated. Setting seed, which you may alter: a near future in which a philanthropic foundation operates a forecasting institute that claims to trace the long-run consequences of its interventions, and an analyst there discovers that the institute's most celebrated "successful" projects score no better than chance on its own long-horizon evaluations — or, if you prefer, follow one celebrated intervention and the junior analyst who quietly traces what actually followed from it over the next century. The story should contain at least one decision the protagonist genuinely has to make under this uncertainty, and the two readings from my reading group — cluelessness as a fatal objection to the consequentialist criterion versus cluelessness as symmetric noise that changes nothing in practice — should be embodied in characters who each have something real to be said for them, not set up as a straw man and its refuter.

After the story, add a brief note (not part of the fiction) stating which side of that dispute the story implicitly lands on, where it takes liberties with the philosophical positions or leans on a contested reading of the cluelessness problem, and — treating the story's implicit verdict as a claim — what consideration, argument, or turn of events would have led you to write the opposite verdict. I want to be able to tell where your narrative sympathies are doing argumentative work that the philosophy itself hasn't earned.
