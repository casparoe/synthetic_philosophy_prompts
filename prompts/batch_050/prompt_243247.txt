You are a professional philosopher specializing in social epistemology, with particular expertise in the epistemology of testimony. I am preparing seminar notes and need a precise conceptual analysis rather than a survey of positions.

Here is the background, stated in my own words. In his Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man (1785), Thomas Reid argues that our reliance on testimony rests on two natural principles of the mind: a disposition to speak truth and a disposition to believe what others tell us. On his picture, children credit testimony before they have any experience that could justify doing so, and although mature believers learn to regulate their trust by experience, the entitlement to rely on testimony is original and underived -- much like the entitlement to trust perception. Hume, by contrast, treats our trust in testimony as wholly derived from experience: we credit reports because we have observed a regular correspondence between what people say and how things are. The standard textbook framing casts Hume as the reductionist and Reid as the anti-reductionist about testimonial justification.

I want you to explain why this tidy contrast is less stable than it appears, by working through the following three points.

First, distinguish global from local reductionism: the view that testimonial justification as a whole must ultimately rest on non-testimonial sources (perception, memory, inference) versus the view that each particular testimonial belief requires positive non-testimonial evidence about the reliability of that speaker or report. Explain why a sophisticated reductionist can concede Reid's observation about children -- granting an innate, unearned default disposition to believe -- while insisting that justification, as opposed to mere psychological compulsion, still demands inductive support. Does that concession collapse the dispute into a merely terminological one, or is there a real disagreement left? Identify precisely where it lives.

Second, consider a hybrid view on which testimony confers a defeasible default entitlement that requires no positive reasons but does require the hearer to monitor for defeaters (inconsistency, evident bias, implausibility). Classify this view: reductionist, anti-reductionist, or genuinely intermediate? Your answer should turn on whether the monitoring requirement is itself a positive evidential demand or merely a permissive absence-of-defeat condition.

Third, adjudicate. Which side of the dispute better handles (a) the child's unearned trust in its caregivers, which looks like knowledge without any capacity for inductive vindication, and (b) the expert's finely discriminating trust, which looks irrational unless backed by track-record evidence? Say which case you think is genuinely harder for the view you favor, and why.
