I have recently become the director of a small family foundation, following the death of the founder, and the board has resolved that our grantmaking should henceforth be cause-neutral: we accept proposals from any area — global health, animal welfare, education, scientific research, policy work — and rank them solely by expected moral value. We decide roughly ten grants per quarter, with a staff of myself and two program officers, neither of whom has training in philosophy; the board signs off on our rankings but expects them to be defensible. Almost immediately I have run into the problem that our staff do not share one moral theory, and neither, if I am honest, do I: some proposals look excellent under nearly any plausible view, others look excellent only if, say, animals matter nearly as much as humans per unit of welfare, or only if the far future dominates the calculation.

I would like you to turn the philosophical literature on moral uncertainty into a procedure my program officers can actually run on a proposal. Specifically: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how to handle a proposal whose value is high under some credible moral views and negligible or negative under others; and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than analysis. In designing it, please be explicit about which distinctions from that literature must survive the translation to a checklist and which can safely be dropped — for instance, whether we should simply average expected value across moral views weighted by our credence in them, or instead treat the views like voters in a legislature whose verdicts must be reconciled without a common currency; how to compare units of value across theories that do not obviously share a scale; whether and how to credit a grant for the information it produces or the options it keeps open; and what to do about interventions whose effects are so remote that any estimate seems like guesswork. If some of these problems have no procedural solution, say so plainly and tell me where the procedure will misfire rather than papering over it.

Then run the procedure on three proposals of the kind we actually receive, showing each step: first, a bednet distribution program with well-documented mortality effects; second, a corporate campaign pressing food companies toward cage-free eggs, whose case rests heavily on the moral standing of chickens and on very large animal numbers; third, a speculative research grant aimed at risks several decades out, whose expected value is enormous if a small-probability scenario is real and roughly zero otherwise. Assume, if you like, that the staff credences over the relevant moral views are given to you at the outset, and state what credences you are assuming. I would like at least one of these applications to produce a ranking I will not like — my instinct is that bednets should beat the speculative grant, and I suspect the procedure may not cooperate — so that I can see whether the procedure is disciplined or merely decorative. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where an applicant could game it, where it will silently smuggle in a moral theory while pretending to be neutral, and what questions I should expect to keep deciding by hand no matter how well the checklist is built.
