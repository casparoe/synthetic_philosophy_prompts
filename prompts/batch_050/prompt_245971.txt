I'm a political theorist at a small policy institute that has just received funding for a three-year research project on secession and self-determination, and I'm setting the agenda. The normative literature strikes me as having stalled around a few familiar fronts: whether the right to secede is primary (held by any sufficiently defined territorial group that votes for it) or merely remedial (triggered only by serious injustice), what could possibly settle the boundaries of "the people" entitled to decide without begging the question at stake, whether supermajority or turnout thresholds for independence referendums can be given a principled rather than ad hoc defense, and whether internal self-determination -- autonomy arrangements, federalism -- is a genuine alternative to secession or just a way of avoiding the question. Which two of these (or which other questions I'm not listing) are most worth our three years, given that our comparative advantage is conceptual analysis rather than empirical casework and that we want our conclusions to matter for real disputes like Catalonia or a future Scottish referendum, not only for the journal literature? Please defend the ordering explicitly: tell me which questions are upstream of which in the sense that answers elsewhere depend on them, which are tractable with the tools analytic political theory actually has, and which you would deprioritize because even a settled answer would change little in practice. I'd also like to know what finding, partway through, would make you change the ranking.
