I've been going back and forth with a friend for months about the old question of whether quantum mechanics shows that elementary particles aren't individuals, and we've hit a wall. The setup is standard: swap two electrons and you get the same state up to a phase that cancels in every observable, and the Bose-Einstein / Fermi-Dirac counting works as if permutations simply aren't distinct possibilities, unlike classical Maxwell-Boltzmann counting. The received view says this means quantum particles lack individuality: there is no fact of the matter about which particle is which, maybe not even a fact that there are two things rather than some smeared collective stuff. The dissenters (the weak-discernibility line associated with Saunders, and van Fraassen before that) say the statistics are perfectly compatible with particles being individuals -- the formalism just doesn't assign physical significance to the labeling, and that tells you something about the theory's descriptive resources, not about how many things there are.

Neither of us can move the other, and the literature seems equally stuck: people keep publishing, nobody converts.

My working hypothesis is that this was never a disagreement about physics. Both sides accept the same formalism and the same predictions. What's really contested is whether "identity" and "individuality" retain any meaning once you've drained out every possible observable consequence -- whether the question "are there two things here or one" survives the observation that no experiment could ever depend on the answer. One side treats that as a genuine metaphysical question with an answer supplied by the formalism plus something like Leibniz's principle; the other side thinks it's a leftover instinct from classical pictures, a bug in our intuitions rather than a feature of the world.

So tell me: is that diagnosis right? Is this a substantive metaphysical dispute, or are the two camps using 'individual' and 'identity' in different senses -- one meaning roughly 'cardinality of the ontology', the other 'has a thisness beyond its qualitative profile' -- and talking past each other? Or is the hidden disagreement about what metaphysics is even allowed to infer from a physical theory, in which case the particles are just the showcase and the real fight is methodological?

I want a diagnosis with reasons, not another survey of the positions: what each side needs the words to mean, exactly where they stop engaging each other, and what -- a theorem, an experiment, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question -- could actually produce movement. And if your honest answer is that nothing could, because this rests on bedrock committments neither side can argue the other into, say that plainly and explain why.
