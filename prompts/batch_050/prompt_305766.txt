So I've been defending Raz's line on the rule of law from "The Rule of Law and its Virtue" and I want to stress-test it. His move, roughly, is that the rule of law is a formal or instrumental virtue of a legal system, the way sharpness is a virtue of a knife: a sharp knife is genuinely a good knife, and good qua knife, but that says nothing about whether you should use it to slice bread or to hurt someone. Likewise a legal system that is prospective, public, clear, stable, and actually enforced is genuinely better *as law* -- it's law's specific excellence, since law is supposed to guide conduct through rules -- but fully compatible with the regime doing horrible things through those very rules. Conformity to the ideal is one virtue among others and not itself a moral one.

That's my position too, or at least my default: the rule of law is instrumentally and formally good, not inherently morally good, and a wicked state can score high on it.

What would count against this? Sort what you give me by how decisive it would be -- I'd like at least: (a) arguments that the formal requirements smuggle in substantive moral content whether Raz likes it or not (e.g., that treating people as addressable, reason-responsive agents under public rules already respects dignity in a way that's morally loaded, so the knife analogy breaks down), (b) the empirical claim that genuinely wicked regimes basically never sustain high rule-of-law conformity because unpredictability is too useful to them, so the "compatible with great evil" claim is an empty modal possibility, and (c) the Fuller-style line that the desiderata form an internal *morality*, not just an efficiency standard. For each, say whether it would actually defeat the position or just embarrass it, and what Raz's defenders would themselves concede as a real hit versus what only critics think is a hit.

Also: is the position at risk of being unfalsifiable? It feels like every time someone produces a clear, stable, public, well-enforced wicked law, the Razian just shrugs and says "yes, and? that was the whole point." If the view has been insulated like that, tell me whether that's a vice for a conceptual claim about what the rule of law *is* (as opposed to an empirical hypothesis), because I'm honestly not sure conceptual theses owe us the same kind of exposure.

End with: what single argument or consideration would most change your verdict, and where is your own reasoning in the answer most likely to fall apart? And please write the whole thing in American English.
