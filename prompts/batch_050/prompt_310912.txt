Last night I had a long and rather frustrating argument with a friend about a study I had shown him: a new treatment for a chronic condition outperforms the standard one when you look at all patients together, yet within every subgroup we split the patients into -- mild cases, moderate cases, severe cases -- the standard treatment does better. I took this to show that the pooled result is misleading; he ended the discussion by declaring that the numbers don't lie and that the overall figure is just what the data say, full stop.

I am sure his slogan is doing more work than he realizes, but I could not get him to say which. So here is what I would like from you. Take the slogan "the numbers don't lie" as it is used in disputes like ours and lay out, as a list I could bring to a second round with him, the distinct precise claims it might be making: for instance, that the recorded figures are accurate rather than fabricated or mismeasured; that data are neutral and interpretation-free; that the aggregate over a whole population is the privileged or "real" summary, with breakdowns being optional commentary; that no legitimate analysis can reverse what the raw totals show; or that whoever refuses to act on the pooled result is being irrational.

For each reading, tell me three things: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; whether it is the sort of thing people who reach for the slogan probably have in mind; and -- most importantly -- whether it is strong enough to actually settle our argument in his favor. I suspect several readings are true but toothless, and at least one is strong enough but straightforwardly refuted by reversal phenomena of the kind in my study, where the direction of an association flips under conditioning.

Finally, one thing my friend said that I could not answer: he asked what makes the subgroup tables any less "the numbers" than the pooled table, and I had no principled reply. Please address the harder question underneath this -- given that conditioning on the wrong variable can itself mislead, so that disaggregation is not automatically the correction -- what, exactly, decides which table tells the truth? If the honest answer is that no slogan about numbers can settle it, because the choice depends on causal structure that the figures alone do not record, please make that case explicitly, since that is the conclusion I would need to defend.
