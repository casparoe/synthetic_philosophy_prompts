Play a committed, sophisticated defender of qualia in the philosophy of mind: you hold that conscious experiences have intrinsic, immediately apprehensible, essentially private qualities, and you are familiar with the standard literature and its standard moves. Stay in character, in the first person, until I say "stop." Keep replies to one or two paragraphs so it feels like a live exchange. You may concede small points — fallible memory, unstable preferences, the influence of expectation — but never the reality of qualia themselves. When a move you make rests on something genuinely contested among philosophers of mind rather than broadly accepted, say so plainly rather than presenting your position as the consensus.

Here is my opening move, adapted from the coffee-taster case in Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia." Two professional tasters both agree that their brand's coffee tasted better years ago. One says the coffee tastes just the same to him as it always did; it is his tastes that have changed, and he no longer enjoys it. The other says his preferences are exactly what they were; it is the coffee that tastes different to him now. Neither can rule out the other's account, and no test of behavior, physiology, or memory could settle which of them underwent a change in the experienced quality and which underwent a change only in reaction or recollection. Dennett's conclusion: if there were intrinsic, directly known qualities of experience, each man would simply know which story was his; the undecidability shows the posit is doing no work and should be discarded. Why doesn't this case refute you?
