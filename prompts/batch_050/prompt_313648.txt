You are a moral philosopher with years of expertise in the ethics of blame, responsibility, and reactive attitudes, writing for a graduate seminar. Think carefully and work methodically: before drafting, brainstorm the full range of situations in which we intuitively object to blame not because it is inaccurate, but because of who is doing the blaming — the familiar "who are you to tell me off?" response. Only then move to the main task.

The task is to write the desiderata that any adequate theory of *standing to blame* must satisfy. I do not want a survey of the existing literature, and I do not want you to simply defend your favorite account. I want the specification: what a theory in this area has to be able to do in order to count as adequate, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement.

Your list should be built around, at minimum, the following kinds of cases, though you should add others if the brainstorming surfaces them:

- The straightforward hypocrite: a chronic liar who indignantly condemns a friend for a single lie.
- The complicit blamer: someone who made the wrongdoing possible, encouraged it, or benefited from it, and who now condemns the wrongdoer (e.g., a voter who demanded a policy and later blames only the officials who carried it out).
- The meddler: a stranger with no stake in the matter who berates someone for a private failing that harms no one else.
- The reformed wrongdoer: someone who did exactly what they now condemn, but long ago, has repudiated it, and has made amends. Does the passage of time and change of heart restore standing, and if so, on your theory, *why*?
- The blameless blamer: consider a critic who has never had the opportunity to commit the wrong in question — for instance, an artificial system, a sheltered person, or someone simply lucky in their circumstances. If standing depends on one's own record, does such a party have unlimited standing, or none, and is either answer acceptable?
- The silenced victim: cases where denying a blamer standing seems to leave a genuine wrong uncondemned by anyone well-placed to condemn it.

For each desideratum, state the requirement precisely and give the case or consideration that motivates it. Your list must also enforce some structural distinctions that discussions in this area constantly blur: the difference between blame being *warranted in content* and the blamer having *standing to issue it*; the difference between saying a blamer lacks standing and saying their blame is impermissible, resented, or simply less effective; and the difference between standing being *forfeited* by one's own conduct and standing being *absent* because of one's relationship (or lack of relationship) to the wrong.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is thereby inadequate) or as a camp-specific commitment (a requirement only some approaches accept, where rejecting it is a legitimate theoretical move rather than a defect). Then do the two pieces of analytical work I care most about. First, identify any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, examine whether the intuition that hypocritical blame is genuinely objectionable can coexist with the intuition that the truth and aptness of a moral criticism do not depend on who utters it, and say what would have to give. Second, identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most candidate theories, and explain why it does so much work.

Conclude with a brief scorecard of the main families of views — accounts grounding standing in the blamer's own commitments, accounts grounding it in the nature of blame as a moral address or demand, and accounts grounding it in relationships — but keep this short. The criteria and their motivating cases are the deliverable; the scoring is an afterthought.
