I'm prepping a reading group on the unilateralist's curse -- roughly: when lots of well-meaning people each have the power to unilaterally pull some irreversible trigger (publish the paper, ship the feature, release the dataset, send the email), the trigger tends to get pulled even when it shouldn't be, because whoever's most optimistic just goes ahead and does it.

The standard telling treats this as basically an information problem. Everyone decides on their own private evidence, nobody adjusts for the fact that the most optimistic estimate wins, so the obvious fixes are: pool your evidence, deliberate together, and everyone shade their confidence down to correct for the selection effect. I want a case that puts pressure on that story.

So: construct a thought experiment where the curse bites anyway, even though everyone involved (a) knows about the unilateralist's curse and has explicitly discussed it, (b) has honestly shared all their evidence with each other, and (c) genuinely wants the same thing -- no hidden agendas, no clashing values, nobody being careless or ego-driven. The trigger still gets pulled when it shouldn't be.

Constraints:
- Realistic setting: a research lab, a company, an open source project, something along those lines. No demons, no perfectly rational agents, and no stipulating that people behave irrationally.
- The scenario itself should be one paragraph.
- The bad outcome has to fall out of the structure. If the story secretly depends on one person screwing up or withholding something, it's cheating.

After the scenario:
1. Walk through the reasoning of the person who ends up pulling the trigger, step by step, so that each step looks locally defensible.
2. Show why each standard fix fails in this case: sharing evidence, everyone discounting their own optimism, deferring to the group's judgment.
3. Name the single structural feature doing the work, and say whether removing it collapses the case back into the boring private-information version of the curse.
4. The patch test: suppose the group adopts the obvious remedy -- nobody acts unless everyone consents. Does that just generate the mirror-image curse, where clearly good actions systematically never happen because someone always has a reason to hold out? And can your scenario be tweaked to exhibit both curses in the same setting, or do they require different structures?
