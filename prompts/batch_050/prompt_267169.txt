I manage a forty-person engineering organization and, twice a year, I chair the committee that runs our incident reviews and calibrates performance ratings. These two processes keep colliding in a way I can no longer ignore. Last cycle we had two engineers who each merged a risky change on a Friday against our stated policy. One merge caused a nine-hour outage that made the trade press; the other sat in production unnoticed for three months and was quietly fixed. The incident-review process correctly said the two decisions were identical in quality, but when calibration came around, the committee rated the first engineer substantially lower, and honestly I felt the pull of that too. The same thing shows up everywhere: a manager who skips load testing and gets away with it is 'pragmatic'; one who skips it and melts down on launch day is 'reckless.' When I raised this, a colleague told me philosophers have a name for the whole problem -- moral luck -- and that there's a literature on whether and when it's defensible to judge people by outcomes they didn't control, including a 'control principle' that says we're only properly assessed for what was up to us, and attempts to distinguish luck in outcomes from luck in circumstances and luck in one's own disposition.

I want you to turn whatever that literature actually establishes into a procedure my committee can follow. The deliverable is a checklist or ordered set of tests we can run on any case in under thirty minutes, during a calibration meeting, by people with no philosophy background. It should tell us: how to separate the assessment of the decision from the assessment of the outcome; when a bad outcome is legitimate evidence about the decision (because a competent decision-maker would have foreseen the risk) versus mere noise; what to do with the near-miss case, where the lucky engineer's policy violation only surfaced because we went looking; and whether the procedure should treat the person who made a good decision with a bad outcome differently from the person who made a bad decision with a good one. For each step, say which distinction from the moral luck literature it is preserving and which subtleties you deliberately dropped as unusable in a meeting room -- and flag the points where the procedure runs out and someone simply has to exercise judgment, rather than letting us pretend the checklist settled it.

Apply the finished procedure to three cases, at least one of which should produce a rating the committee will hate: first, my two Friday-deploy engineers; second, a senior engineer who championed a database migration that failed for reasons genuinely unforeseeable at the time, costing us a quarter of engineering time; third, an engineer who was sloppy about access controls for a year, never got breached, and whose sloppiness we only discovered by accident. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: I suspect there is something in the calibration room -- the fact that the outage engineer's mistake is *memorable*, that leadership watched it happen -- that no checklist will neutralize, and I'd rather have that named than smoothed over.
