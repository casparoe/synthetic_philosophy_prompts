Last night a close friend and I fell into an argument about Descartes' cogito that carried on until well past midnight, and we parted without having moved one another an inch. Her position was that cogito ergo sum is a genuine piece of reasoning: Descartes, on her telling, infers his existence from his thinking, and the argument's force depends on a bridging premise to the effect that whatever thinks exists. Mine was that this reading both misconstrues the insight and wastes it — that the certainty of the cogito lies not in any inference but in the performance itself: the thought that I exist verifies itself in the very act of being entertained, in much the way certain utterances are self-guaranteeing independently of any derivation. We disagreed, in particular, about three things. First, whether Lichtenberg's objection — that the most one is strictly entitled to is the impersonal claim that thinking is occurring, on the model of saying that it is raining — damages only the inferential reading or both readings alike. Second, whether the cogito, on either construal, could ever reach a persisting subject rather than a momentary episode of thought, and what follows for Descartes' subsequent identification of himself as a thinking thing if it cannot. Third, whether the thought I do not exist, entertained now, is best modelled as a straightforward contradiction or as something structurally closer to a Moore-paradoxical sentence, such as the claim that it is raining while I do not believe that it is — impossible to assert truly, yet not inconsistent in form.

Write a dialogue of roughly 700 to 1000 words between two philosophers — one defending my friend's inferential reading, one defending my performative reading — that reconstructs and sharpens this dispute. Both interlocutors should be equally competent, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than deliver set speeches. The defender of the inferential reading should press the charge that a mere performance, lacking propositional structure, cannot be true or false and therefore cannot amount to knowledge — and that the performative reading smuggles in the first-person subject it claims to earn. The defender of the performative reading should press the counter-charge that any inference from thinking to existing requires a premise whose certainty is more dubitable than the conclusion it is meant to secure, and should answer the Lichtenberg objection directly rather than dismiss it. The dialogue may allow one side to concede ground on the Moore-paradoxicality question if the exchange genuinely earns the concession, but it should end with the central disagreement intact — ideally with both parties having arrived at a precise, shared statement of exactly what they still disagree about.
