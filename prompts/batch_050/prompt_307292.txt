I would like to know whether there is an established name for a distinction I keep needing when I think about music, and, if not, I would like one coined carefully. Consider two cases I experience regularly. In the first, I listen to a slow piece for solo cello and I call it heartbroken. The description seems to fit the music itself, the way one might say that a face, a posture, or someone's way of walking looks heartbroken. Yet I do not feel heartbroken in the slightest. I feel absorbed, perhaps quietly exhilarated, and if someone offered to switch the piece off I would protest. In the second case, a different piece genuinely changes my condition: I put on a certain recording after a difficult week and find myself actually low, heavy, close to tears, and I have to turn it off and make tea.

When I describe the first case to friends, I get two kinds of pushback. Some insist I must really be sad and simply failing to notice it; others say the music cannot literally be sad, because a sequence of sounds has no inner life, so calling it heartbroken can only be shorthand for its tendency to make listeners sad -- which it manifestly is not, since it does no such thing to me. I suspect that at least three different claims are being run together in these conversations, but I lack the vocabulary to prise them apart.

My questions, then, are these. First: is there an established term for the relation in the first case, where the emotion seems to be a quality of the music rather than a state of any listener, and what do the main accounts say such a quality could possibly consist in, given that organized sound has no mind to feel with? Second: does the literature treat the second case, in which music genuinely produces a feeling in the listener, as the same phenomenon, a different phenomenon, or a contested one -- and is the relation between the two cases itself a subject of disagreement? Third: is there a named puzzle for the fact that people deliberately seek out music of the first kind, and occasionally even of the second, apparently valuing the sadness involved rather than merely tolerating it, and what are the leading resolutions of that puzzle?

Please note that I will not be satisfied with a bare list of labels. If the nearest established concepts fit my two cases only imperfectly, I want to know exactly where the fit fails. And if there is no established term for the precise configuration I have described, please coin one, define it, and give its boundary cases -- for instance, whether it would also apply to calling a grey seascape melancholy or a piece of choreography defiant, and where it would cease to apply.
