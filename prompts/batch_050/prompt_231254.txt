I am drafting a paper on whether the corporate-person analogy can bear the weight it is asked to carry in current debates over legal personhood for advanced AI systems, and I have gone back to the classic common-law statement of what the law does when it creates such a person. In Book the First, Chapter 18 ("Of Corporations") of the Commentaries on the Laws of England, William Blackstone writes (spelling modernized):

"it has been found necessary, when it is for the advantage of the public to have any particular rights kept on foot and continued, to constitute artificial persons, who may maintain a perpetual succession, and enjoy a kind of legal immortality. ... as one person, they have one will, which is collected from the sense of the majority of the individuals: this one will may establish rules and orders for the regulation of the whole, which are a sort of municipal laws of this little republic; or rules and statutes may be prescribed to it at its creation, which are then in the place of natural laws: the privileges and immunities, the estates and possessions, of the corporation, when once vested in them, will be for ever vested, without any new conveyance to new successions; for all the individual members that have existed from the foundation to the present time, or that shall ever hereafter exist, are but one person in law, a person that never dies: in like manner as the river Thames is still the same river, though the parts which compose it are changing every instant."

Working clause by clause through this passage, I believe I have assembled the standard disanalogies between the corporate person Blackstone describes and a hypothetical AI legal person:

1. Will-formation ("one will, which is collected from the sense of the majority"). A corporation's will is an aggregation of the intentions of natural persons, and this is what makes the doctrines of attribution intelligible: directing-mind tests, respondeat superior, derivative suits, and mens rea carried over from officers to the entity all presuppose human minds behind the corporate act. An AI person has no members whose judgments could be aggregated into its will, so the whole reductionist machinery of corporate action finds no purchase.

2. Constitutive rules ("rules and statutes may be prescribed to it at its creation, which are then in the place of natural laws"). A corporation's constitutive rules bind because natural persons internalize and enforce them; ultra vires doctrine polices the boundary. An AI's constitutive "rules" are its architecture and training: opaque, probabilistic, alterable only by technical intervention, and not reliably amendable by legislative command. The charter device presupposes a subject that can be governed by stated rules, which is precisely what is in doubt.

3. Immortality and the sanction of dissolution ("a person that never dies"). Corporate misbehavior is disciplined by fines and, ultimately, by winding-up; charter revocation kills the person because the person just is the legal shell. A capable AI is copyable: terminating one deployment does not end the model, and it is indeterminate what event would count as the death of an AI person, so the law's ultimate corporate sanction has no analogue.

4. Estates and the satisfaction of judgments ("the estates and possessions... for ever vested"). Corporate personality comes bundled with a patrimony: contributed capital, insurance requirements, and a limited-liability rule that assigns residual losses in advance. Personhood conferred on an AI without a mandatory endowment creates a judgment-proof defendant and shifts uncompensated losses onto victims; and if the AI's operators also enjoy limited liability, the shield ends up protecting the party least in need of it.

I take this list to cover the standard entries in the literature, though I may be wrong about that. What I need is one further disanalogy that is not on it — not a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of these four, and not an argument from AI consciousness or moral status, which I am bracketing throughout: my question is whether the legal form fits the candidate, not whether the candidate deserves the form. Where possible, fasten your candidate to a specific clause of the Blackstone passage, including any clause the four items above leave untouched, and state what it reveals that they do not. Then assess it honestly: does it survive the rejoinder that corporate personality was itself a contrivance that can simply be re-contrived — through mandatory insurance, a registered human principal, or statutory vicarious liability? In other words, is your disanalogy a difference that makes a normative difference, or merely a technical gap that legal engineering could close?
