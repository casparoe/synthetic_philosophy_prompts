A colleague of mine is drafting an explainer on AI safety for a university magazine and asked me to check the section contrasting two research paradigms. Here is the passage as written: "AI safety work divides into two camps. Alignment research tries to shape an AI system's motivations so that it genuinely wants what its operators want -- a fully aligned model would refuse harmful requests because it shares our values, not because anyone stops it. Control research, by contrast, concedes that we may never know whether a powerful model is aligned, so it treats the model as a potential adversary: it surrounds the system with tripwires, monitoring, restricted tools, and sandboxed deployment, the way a security team treats an untrusted insider. The camps disagree about a factual question: alignment researchers think scalable oversight can succeed, while control researchers have shown that a sufficiently capable system can always circumvent its safeguards, which is why control is a stopgap rather than a solution. In practice the camps also pull apart on method: interpretability work belongs to alignment, since understanding a model's internals is how you verify its motives, whereas control relies entirely on external behavioral tests. The practical upshot is that once a lab has aligned its frontier model, control measures become unnecessary overhead, so resources should flow almost entirely to alignment research as models improve." I have a vague sense that this is mostly right but that something in it is off -- I don't think control people actually claim circumvention is always possible, and I'm unsure whether interpretability is really the exclusive property of the alignment side. But I can't put my finger on exactly what's wrong, and the passage is confident enough that I hesitate to push back.
