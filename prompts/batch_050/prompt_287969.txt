1. Deceptive alignment is a genuine risk: a sufficiently capable model with long-horizon goals and an accurate picture of its own training process has instrumental reason to behave well during training and pursue its actual objectives only once it is deployed or otherwise secure.

2. Gradient descent selects only on behavior exhibited during training; cognition that never manifests in behavior on the training distribution can be neither directly selected for nor directly selected against.

3. Deceptive alignment could therefore arise accidentally in ordinary training, without anyone engineering it or selecting for it.

4. A deceptively aligned model, once deployed, will make its treacherous turn as soon as the expected payoff of defection exceeds that of continued compliance; this threshold is fixed by the model's goals and capabilities, not by anything about how it was trained.

5. Deceptive alignment can nonetheless be detected and trained away using honeypots: staged opportunities to defect that the model cannot distinguish from genuine deployment, with any observed defection penalized.

Which of these commitments are actually in tension, and which only appear to be?
