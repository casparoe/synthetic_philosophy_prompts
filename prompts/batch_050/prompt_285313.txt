Now that I am retired from forty years of experimental physics, I have been working my way through the primary texts of the philosophy of physics, and I have reached Newton's Principia in the 1846 Motte translation. I was struck by the commentary Newton appends to Definition III, where, having defined the vis insita by which every body "endeavours to persevere in its present state, whether it be of rest, or of moving uniformly forward in a right line," he adds: "Resistance is usually ascribed to bodies at rest, and impulse to those in motion; but motion and rest, as commonly conceived, are only relatively distinguished; nor are those bodies always truly at rest, which commonly are taken to be so."

What interests me is the compressed argument I take Newton to be making in that second sentence. He concedes that our ordinary attributions of motion and rest are entirely relative — one body measured against another — while insisting, in the same breath, that there is a fact of the matter about true rest that relative attributions can miss. This seems to me the germ of the entire substantivalism–relationism dispute: the relationist (in the tradition running through Leibniz, Berkeley, and Mach) will say that the concession swallows the insistence, and that talk of true motion beyond relative motion is either meaningless or false; the substantivalist will say the insistence is what matters, and that a body's inertial behaviour — its resistance to changes of state — is the empirical footprint of a structure that relative motions among bodies do not exhaust.

Before I commit to either side, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of space, motion, and inertia must meet in order to adjudicate this dispute, treating Newton's sentence as the thing to be tested: does an adequate theory have to vindicate his distinction between true and merely relative rest, explain it away, or show it to be badly posed? For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that motivates it. At minimum I would expect the list to address: which phenomena the theory must account for (the behaviour of rotating and accelerated systems, Newton's bucket and his two-globes-cord argument, the inertial frames of actual mechanics); which distinctions it must not collapse (uniform versus accelerated motion, kinematics versus dynamics, the difference between there being a fact about true motion and our being able to detect it); and which established results it must remain consistent with (the empirical equivalence of inertial frames, and whatever bearing later physics — special and general relativity — has on a seventeenth-century dispute). Mark for each desideratum whether it is a genuine constraint on any theory or a prejudice of one camp, and say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, since that would itself be the interesting result. A table is acceptable if it aids clarity. Close briefly with how the main contenders — a substantivalist reading of absolute space, a Mach-style relationism, and a modern spacetime-based view — stand against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.
