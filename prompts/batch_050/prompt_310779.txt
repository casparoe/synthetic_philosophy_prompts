In my country, important constitutional questions are sometimes decided by national referendum, and afterward politicians on all sides say that "the people have spoken," treating the result not merely as a tally of votes but as the discovery of what the nation wants. Yet the same political system also requires supermajorities for some changes, lets constitutional courts strike down popularly approved measures, and lets elected representatives decide most questions without consulting anyone. I am trying to figure out what view of "the will of the people" this whole arrangement presupposes. Does treating a referendum result as the people's voice assume that there is a single collective will that exists before the vote and is merely revealed by it, rather than an aggregation of millions of conflicting preferences whose "result" depends on how the question was worded, which options were on the ballot, and who turned out? And if it assumes that, how is that consistent with the courts and supermajority rules, which seem to presuppose that the people can be wrong about their own fundamental commitments? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, say which rival views of popular will the practice quietly rules out, identify where it is genuinely underdetermined (i.e., where several theories would license the same institutions), and tell me what would have to change if we adopted a purely aggregative view on which there is no popular will beyond the mechanics of counting votes.
