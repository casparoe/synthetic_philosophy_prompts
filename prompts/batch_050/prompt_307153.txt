I've been reading about corporate criminal liability for a seminar, and I keep running into what looks like a deep inconsistency in how we actually treat companies, and I'd like help working out what theory of moral agency, if any, our practices presuppose. Consider the standard toolkit. When a large company does something seriously wrong -- an automaker installs software designed to cheat emissions tests, a bank opens millions of unauthorized accounts -- the response is typically to prosecute or fine the corporation itself, often for sums no individual could ever pay. Since a company cannot be imprisoned, enforcement has developed substitutes: deferred prosecution agreements that require the firm to reform its "culture," accept an outside monitor, fire executives, and express remorse. Meanwhile the public talks about companies as agents all the time: we say the company lied, that it knew, that it decided to cut corners, and people organize boycotts and feel the same kind of resentment toward a firm that they'd feel toward a person. Yet at the same time, everyone concedes that the fine is ultimately paid by shareholders, employees, and customers, most of whom did nothing wrong -- and some legal systems (notably the UK's identification doctrine) refuse to attribute a crime to a company at all unless a sufficiently senior "directing mind" personally had the guilty mental state, while US federal law happily imputes the misconduct of even fairly junior employees to the corporation.

So: what does this mixed practice actually presuppose about whether a corporation can be a bearer of moral responsibility? Does fining a company and demanding cultural reform presuppose that the firm has something like intentions, knowledge, and character of its own -- irreducible to those of its employees -- or is the practice equally compatible with a purely instrumental view on which corporate liability is just an efficient tax-and-deterrence scheme aimed at the deep pockets, with the talk of remorse and culture being theater? Note the tensions I'd like untangled: if the practice genuinely presupposed group agency, would punishing innocent shareholders be any less troubling than punishing the cellmates of a guilty person? If instead the practice presupposes that only individuals can truly be blamed, why do we bother with corporate prosecutions at all rather than exclusively prosecuting the responsible executives? And is the demand that a corporation show remorse coherent on either picture -- can an entity feel remorse through a court-appointed monitor?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) behind the practice, distinguishing carefully between what it strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- that is, where several rival accounts of corporate agency would license exactly the same conduct. Then tell me what would concretely have to change if we adopted each of the rival views: on a strong group-agency view, should companies be subject to punishments aimed at the entity itself (charter revocation as corporate "death penalty," mandated board turnover) rather than fines that pass through to bystanders? On an eliminativist view, should corporate criminal liability be abolished altogether in favor of individual prosecution plus civil penalties openly acknowledged as pricing? And on some middle position -- say, that corporate "intentions" are constituted by decision procedures and incentive structures rather than by any conscious subject -- which parts of the current toolkit make sense and which are superstition?
