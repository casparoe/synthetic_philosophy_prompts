So in my welfare econ reading group we keep going around in circles on whether "better than" has to be transitive, and two people are leaning hard on rival analogies. One guy (a math postdoc, predictably) insists betterness works like height: taller than is transitive basically by definition, because there's one underlying quantity being compared, so if A is better than B and B better than C, then A better than C is just arithmetic, and anyone who resists is like someone claiming a tower of blocks that loops back on itself. He says the spectrum arguments just confuse a measurement problem (we cant tell small differences apart) with a metaphysical one. On the other side, someone in philosophy says "better than" is more like "looks the same color as": you can line up paint swatches so each neighboring pair is indistinguishable and yet the ends clearly differ, and value works that way because what matters changes as you move along the spectrum, so local comparisons dont add up to global ones.

Can you audit both analogies? Lay out explicitly what maps onto what in each (the underlying quantity, the comparison relation, the observer), identify where each breaks down, and figure out what each one quietly smuggles in that "better than" may lack -- I'm thinking especially of the single-dimension assumption and the idea that comparisons are observer-independent. Which disanalogies actually matter for the transitivity question versus which are just noise? And once the disanalogies are on the table: does the height analogy's conclusion (transitivity is non-negotiable) survive, and does the swatch analogy actually establish intransitivity or only that we can be *fooled* about it? If neither analogy carries the load its user wants, whats a better one -- or is the right lesson that "better than" just isn't much like any familiar relation and the analogies should be retired?
