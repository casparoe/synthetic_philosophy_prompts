I'm making a handout for a reading group on objections to the conceivability arguement against physicalism (the zombie one: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me lacking any experience is conceivable, hence metaphysically possible, hence physicalism is false). Here's what I've got so far:

1. The phenomenal concept strategy — the conceivability of zombies comes from the weird way we think about experience (direct, recognitional phenomenal concepts that don't hook into our physical/functional concepts), not from any gap in the world. Zombie worlds are conceivable but impossible.

2. Zombies aren't actually conceivable — we just conceive sloppily. Imagine the physical facts in full detail and consciousness comes along for free; the "zombie" you're imagining is really a Dennett-style zimbo and you've smuggled in a difference without noticing.

3. Conceivability is a lousy guide to possibility in general — people have found all sorts of impossible things conceivable (a false mathematical proof can seem coherent for years), so conceivability evidence is too weak to overturn a metaphysical thesis.

4. Question-begging — whether a zombie world is genuinely conceivable-in-the-relevant-sense is exactly what's disputed between the dualist and the physicalist, so asserting its conceivability just restates the anti-physicalist premise rather than arguing for it.

5. Denying the disanalogy with water/H2O — the argument only works if consciousness is special, i.e. if there's no two-intension story explaining why zombies seem conceivable without being possible the way there is for watery-XYZ. A physicalist can just insist the cases are analogous and put the burden back on the dualist to show otherwise.

What I want from you: one more objection that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording or special case of any of these (e.g. "we might be unreliable conceivers" is just 3 warmed over, and "conceiving a zombie already assumes phenomenal ≠ physical" is 4). One ground rule: don't give me the zombie-Chalmers worry (a zombie would write the same books about consciousness, so our beliefs about experience would be explanatorily cut off from experience itself). I know that one and left it off on purpose because it seems to bite epiphenomenalism more than the conceivability argument itself — if you disagree with that call you can say so, but it doesn't count as your answer.

Then tell me honestly whether your new objection is actually any good, and how it compares to what you think is the strongest item on my list.
