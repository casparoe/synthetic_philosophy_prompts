Exam question: "State the tautology objection to the theory of natural selection as precisely as you can. What exactly is alleged to be tautological, and why is that supposed to be a problem for the theory's explanatory or predictive status? Then explain the propensity interpretation of fitness and how it is meant to answer the objection. Student's answer:

"The tautology objection targets the principle 'survival of the fittest.' The charge is that 'fittest' is defined in terms of survival and reproductive success -- the fittest are simply those who in fact survive and leave the most offspring. But then the principle reduces to 'those who survive, survive' (or 'those who reproduce most, reproduce most'), which is true by definition. A tautology is compatible with every possible outcome, so it explains nothing and predicts nothing. On this objection, natural selection is not a testable empirical theory but an empty logical truth dressed up as one, and critics have pressed some version of this since Darwin's own time.

The propensity interpretation, associated with Robert Brandon and others, replies that fitness is not actual reproductive success but a probabilistic propensity: roughly, an organism's disposition to leave a certain number of offspring in its environment, the way a fair coin has a propensity to land heads half the time. Formally, fitness is identified with the expected number of offspring rather than the actual number. Because expectation and outcome can come apart -- a highly fit organism can be struck by lightning and leave zero offspring -- 'survival of the fittest' is not true by definition. It becomes a statistical generalization: fitter organisms tend, on average, to outreproduce less fit ones, but luck can override fitness in any particular case. And this also makes the theory falsifiable, since we can now measure the propensity by counting how many offspring organisms of each type actually produce in a population and checking that the types we take to be fitter really do produce more on average.

My assessment is that the propensity interpretation fully answers the objection. Once fitness is a propensity rather than realized success, there is a genuine empirical fact about which organisms are fitter, fixed by their traits and environment and independent of what actually happens to them, so the circle is broken. Measurement is then straightforward: observe the reproductive output of many organisms of a type and average them -- the average actual output just is the type's fitness, so the propensity is empirically accessible, and critics who say fitness can't be measured without circularity are refuted by this averaging procedure. The only residual worry is small populations, where chance events dominate and the fitter type can easily go extinct, but that's a practical complication for fieldwork rather than a conceptual problem, so the tautology objection fails."
