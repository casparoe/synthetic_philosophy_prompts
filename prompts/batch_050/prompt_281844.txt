A colleague in another department, having read a popular article on the science of consciousness, summarised what he took to be the "illusionist" position to me as follows: "Illusionists about consciousness, like Keith Frankish and the late Daniel Dennett, hold that phenomenal consciousness — the felt, qualitative character of experience — does not exist. We are under a systematic introspective illusion of having such experiences, rather as we are under an illusion when we see a mirage. Once we accept this, the hard problem dissolves: there is nothing mysterious to explain, only the question of why brains misrepresent themselves in this way, which is a straightforwardly empirical matter. Consciousness itself, on this view, is real enough — it is just the brain's ordinary information-processing — but the 'inner glow' that philosophers obsess over is a fiction, and so a sufficiently sophisticated robot that shared our introspective machinery would be exactly as conscious as we are, which is to say, not phenomenally conscious at all."

I am reasonably familiar with the literature and my sense is that this is close but not quite right — in particular, the opening claim that phenomenal consciousness "does not exist" strikes me as a more contentious characterisation than illusionists themselves typically offer, and I suspect the remark about robots misdescribes the position's implications. But I would value a careful audit. Please indicate, sentence by sentence or claim by claim, what an illusionist such as Frankish would accept, what he would qualify, and what he would reject outright; note any conflation of distinct theses (for instance, between denying phenomenality and denying experience); and say whether the closing inference about robots follows from the corrected view. At most 700 words.
