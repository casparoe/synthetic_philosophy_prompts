I am the senior editor of a popular history magazine, and I have a recurring problem with the causal language in our features. Roughly once a week, a draft claims that some event "caused" a later one -- a assassination caused a war, a harvest failure caused a revolution, a treaty caused a famine -- when the historical record shows several independently sufficient conditions in play, or a backup condition waiting in the wings, or a contributing factor that was neither necessary nor sufficient on its own. My writers are good historians but have no philosophy background, and I cannot referee every sentence myself. When I push back, they either overcorrect into mush ("many factors played a role") or dig in on a monocausal story.

I want you to design a decision procedure my writers can follow, unaided, when assigning causal weight in prose. Ground it in what philosophers of causation actually say about overdetermination, preemption, and partial or contributory causation -- counterfactual dependence and its known failures, the idea of an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition, and the difference between a cause that was preempted and one that genuinely operated -- but translate it so that someone who has never heard those terms can apply it correctly. The deliverable is a reusable document, roughly: a short set of questions to ask about the historical record, in a fixed order; a mapping from the possible answers to a small inventory of sanctioned locutions ("caused," "triggered," "made possible," "hastened," "was among the sufficient conditions for," "would have happened anyway, but by another route," and so on -- refine this list as you see fit); and rules for when a sentence must name more than one cause and when a single-cause sentence survives scrutiny.

The procedure must explicitly handle at least these hard cases, and I want a worked application of it to each, including the exact sentence you would print: (1) two conditions, each alone sufficient, both operative, as with two independent fires that reach the warehouse simultaneously; (2) an operative cause and a backup that would have produced the same outcome slightly later, as when a dying regime faces both an invasion and an already-planned coup; (3) a factor that is neither necessary nor sufficient but whose removal changes which sufficient set is complete, as when drought, debt, and one bad harvest jointly precede a revolt; and (4) a case where the writer's preferred narrative is monocausal and your procedure refuses to license it -- for this last one, show me the sentence the writer wanted to print, the sentence the procedure forces, and how I should justify the forced sentence to an unhappy author.

State plainly which distinctions you chose to preserve in the simplification and which you deliberately dropped, and why the dropped ones are safe to drop for general-audience history writing but would not be for, say, a causal claim in an epidemiology paper. Mark the points where the procedure runs out and an editor must make a judgment call, and give me a rough sense of how often you expect that to happen. Finally, include a one-paragraph preamble I can circulate explaining to my writers why "many factors played a role" is a failure of the procedure rather than a safe default.
