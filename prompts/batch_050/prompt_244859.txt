Now my opening move. Take "Stealing is wrong" and "If stealing is wrong, then cheating on your taxes is wrong." Assert both, conclude "cheating on your taxes is wrong," and you've got a valid modus ponens -- and that validity looks purely formal. But on your view "wrong" can't mean the same thing in the antecedent of the conditional as it does when asserted on its own, since the conditional as a whole expresses disapproval of... something, but not straightforwardly of stealing. So the argument looks like a plain fallacy of equivocation on your account, and equivocation is not a small problem, it's the oldest mistake in the book.
