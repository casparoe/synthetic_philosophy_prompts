I'm taking a business ethics elective right now (not a philosophy major, forgive me in advance) and I keep tripping over the same pattern in the case studies we read, and I can't figure out if there's a recognized name for it or if I need to invent one. Two examples. First: a delivery company has a bunch of accidents because drivers are skipping breaks and cutting corners. You dig in, and the person who set the delivery quotas used industry-standard benchmarks, the person who designed the routing software hit the targets they were given, the regional manager who approved everything was looking at numbers that all looked normal. Second: a bank mis-sells accounts to thousands of elderly customers, and the decision chain has four people in it, each of whom signed off on something that, on its own, looked like a routine tweak. In both cases the outcome is clearly blameworthy -- it's not just bad luck or an accident, somebody ought to answer for it. But when you try to pin it on any actual human, each one has the same defense, and it's not the usual bystander excuse where everyone assumes someone else will handle it. It's stronger than that: almost anyone competent put in that seat, with those incentives and that information, would have done the same thing. So punishing the person feels like picking a scapegoat by lottery, but blaming the company feels like yelling at a filing cabinet, because the company doesn't feel anything and the fine just comes out of money nobody in particular owns.

Here's the part that really bugs me: in these cases it feels like the replaceability of the person is doing all the work. If the manager had been some villain cooking the books for fun, fine, blame them, problem solved. But the worse the organization's design is, the *more* innocent every individual inside it becomes, because the more true it is that anyone would have done the same -- so it seems like a badly designed company can launder blame away entirely just by making its roles fungible. That can't be right, but I can't say where it goes wrong. It's close to the idea of responsibility diffusing across a crowd, but the cases I care about aren't really about diffusion, since even a single decision-maker would get off as long as the role guaranteed the decision. It also seems related to how people talk about corporations as if they were persons with intentions, but I'm not asking whether companies *really* have minds -- I'm asking whether this specific configuration, collective fault with no individually culpable node even in principle, has its own name in the literature.

So, answer as a philosopher who specializes in responsibility and organizational ethics, and actually think through the boundary cases rather than just pattern-matching me to the nearest famous term. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- roughly "yes, this is called X" or "no established term exists, but here's the closest and what I'd coin instead" -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question. If there is an established concept, tell me exactly how my two cases fit or misfit it and what seperates it from plain diffusion of responsibility and from scapegoating. If there isn't, coin a name, define it precisely, and tell me which of my two cases you'd exclude from it and why.
