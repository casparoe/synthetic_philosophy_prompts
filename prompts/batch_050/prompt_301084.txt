Two colleagues, Nadia and Theo, are disagreeing about a case I described to them. An intensive-care nurse and a hospital data analyst read the same reliable medication report stating that 8 mg is the correct dose for a patient. The nurse must act immediately, and an error would be catastrophic, so she checks the original chart before administering the dose. The analyst needs the figure only for a low-stakes staffing projection and relies on the report without checking further. Assume that the report is accurate, both believe the same proposition for the same reason, and neither has any misleading evidence.

Nadia argued: (1) Before checking the chart, the analyst knows the dose is 8 mg, but the nurse does not, because the nurse’s higher practical stakes make the shared evidence insufficient for knowledge. (2) This is not epistemic contextualism, since the truth of a knowledge attribution does not vary with the speaker’s conversational context; rather, the nurse’s objective practical situation affects whether she knows.

Theo replied: (3) If stakes alone can remove knowledge, then an urgent page could destroy the nurse’s knowledge and a cancellation could restore it while her belief, evidence, and the truth of the proposition remain unchanged. That makes knowledge implausibly unstable.

Nadia answered: (4) Some instability is acceptable if knowledge must be secure enough to guide action. Greater risk rationally demands more assurance, and if knowing a proposition is the standard for properly treating it as a basis for action, then a change in what action demands can change whether one knows.

Theo responded: (5) Decision theory explains the case without pragmatic encroachment: high stakes increase the expected cost of error and therefore justify additional checking, while knowledge itself remains fixed. Nadia’s standard also seems to imply that no one could appropriately rely on a justified but uncertain belief in an emergency where further checking is impossible.

Nadia’s final move was: (6) If justified belief can license action without knowledge, then knowledge loses its central connection to rational action. Theo’s allegedly pure invariantism merely relocates stakes-sensitivity from knowledge to practical reasoning instead of eliminating it.

Please adjudicate the exchange in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: first, assess Nadia’s initial claim that the analyst knows but the nurse does not; second, assess her distinction between pragmatic encroachment and contextualism; third, assess Theo’s instability objection together with Nadia’s reply; fourth, assess Theo’s decision-theoretic alternative, including the emergency objection; fifth, assess Nadia’s final rejoinder, say which position is stronger overall, and identify any important distinction that both parties may be overlooking.
