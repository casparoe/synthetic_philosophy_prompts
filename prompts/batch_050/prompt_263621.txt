Construct a thought experiment that separates two diagnoses of the paradox of hedonism. The first diagnosis is psychological: consciously aiming at one's own enjoyment interferes with the states that produce it, since attention turns inward, absorption in the activity is lost, and the pleasure itself is thereby diminished. The second diagnosis is constitutive: happiness is partly a matter of caring about things other than one's own happiness, so a person who cares about nothing except his own good feeling is less happy in virtue of that structure, even if his felt pleasure is entirely undiminished. Build a single scenario, realistic and no longer than a paragraph, involving an ordinary person in an ordinary pursuit — no experience machine, no designer drugs, no stipulated brain states — in which the two diagnoses deliver different verdicts about the same life: one must say the person is doing as well as hedonism could ask, and the other must say he is not. After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: state the verdict each diagnosis delivers, identify the feature of the case on which the disagreement turns, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Then add one further detail that would flip the verdict of one diagnosis while leaving the other untouched, and explain why. Conclude with what the case shows about the paradox itself — whether it is an empirical claim about attention and motivation that sufficient technique or self-deception might dissolve, or a conceptual constraint on what happiness can be at all — and how a defender of the diagnosis that fares worse would attack the case's central stipulation.
