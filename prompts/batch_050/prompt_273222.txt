A while back I ended up in a long comment-thread argument about modal metaphysics, of the kind that starts out polite and ends with both sides accusing the other of confusing language with the world. The specific dispute was over origin essentialism: the thesis, associated with Kripke's work on naming and necessity, that a person's origins are essential to them -- that I could not have existed had I developed from a different sperm and egg, even one produced by the same parents on a nearby occasion. My interlocutor treated the thesis as close to obviously true, on the grounds that a person originating from different gametes would simply be someone else, the way a sibling is someone else. I find myself much less sure, and I want to pressure-test my uncertainty against a careful decomposition.

Here is the proposition I'd like a number on, stated as precisely as I can manage: for an ordinary human being, in every metaphysically possible world in which that very individual exists, the individual developed from the same zygote (or, more loosely, the same sperm and egg pair) that they actually developed from. My own credence sits around 0.35, and what I want from you is not a survey of the literature but your credence and, more importantly, its derivation. Concretely: break the question into the sub-questions your number actually depends on. Candidates I'd expect to matter: whether de re modal claims about individuals have determinate truth values at all, or whether cross-world identity is partly a matter of stipulation we choose for convenience; whether the intuition behind the thesis survives contact with Sorites-style pressure (if a one-nucleotide difference in the sperm still yields me, why not two, or fifty, and where does the line sit, and what does the absence of a principled line do to the thesis); and whether the argument illicitly slides from a claim about how we refer to people across possible worlds to a claim about what those people metaphysically require. Put a rough probability on each sub-question resolving in the thesis's favor, say how you combine them into your overall credence, and identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to -- i.e., the one where, if I could flip your view, your overall credence would move the most.

One more request, since I'm explicitly trying to locate disagreement rather than just collect opinions: if your credence lands far from my 0.35, tell me where you think the disagreement most likely lives -- whether you think I'm overweighting the vagueness objection, underweighting the sibling intuition, or importing an epistemic standard that de re modal claims can't be expected to meet. And if you think the whole question as I've stated it dissolves on inspection -- say, because there is no fact of the matter beyond our conventions for trans-world identification -- then say so, assign your credence to that deflationary meta-claim instead, and explain what, if anything, would count as evidence against it.
