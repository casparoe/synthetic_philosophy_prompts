My Indian philosophy textbook summarizes the Mīmāṃsā theory of knowledge like this: 'The Mīmāṃsakas argued that the Vedas have no author, human or divine, reasoning that anything composed by a person could contain that person's errors, so only an authorless text can be fully trusted. From this they derived their doctrine of intrinsic validity: every cognition and every statement is valid simply in virtue of being cognized or uttered, so testimony never needs a reliable speaker behind it — a liar's assertion is as much a source of knowledge as a sage's, and the Vedas are just the limiting case. Error, on their view, is therefore impossible; what we call error is only a cognition we have not yet managed to confirm through outside evidence.' Something here strikes me as off — I had understood that this school actually insists on the trustworthiness of the speaker for ordinary testimony — but I can't locate the mistakes precisely. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts are accurate, which parts conflate distinct theses (and how would Kumārila and Prabhākara each state the corrected point, where they diverge), and does the textbook's apparent conclusion — that Mīmāṃsā epistemology leaves no room for lying, doubt, or the detection of error — actually follow from anything the school held?
