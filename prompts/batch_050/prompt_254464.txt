For my decision theory seminar I want some classroom material that doesn't read like a textbook, so: write me a dialogue between two philosophers, Ana and Beat, who carpool to campus every morning along a highway with two indistinguishable exits, one right after the other. The catch is that once they're on the road they can never remember whether they've already passed the first exit (don't ask why, it's a philosophy department). Taking the first exit means sitting in traffic for hours (payoff 0), taking the second gets them straight to campus (payoff 4), and sailing past both means circling the ring road till noon (payoff 1).

Before setting off they've done the math and agreed: the best policy is to take whatever exit they find themselves at with probabilty 1/3. But now they're actually sitting at an exit, and Beat is arguing that, right here and now, exiting with probability 2/3 is plainly better -- and that if the car pulls off the highway more often than the plan says, that's the plan's problem. Ana thinks this is confused, or at least that sticking to the policy they chose at breakfast is still rational even when it doesn't look that way from the driver's seat.

Let them actually fight it out. I want them to get into what probability they should assign to this being the first exit, whether the breakfast agreement has any authority over the people currently at the ramp, and whether the driver at the exit is even the same agent who did the planning -- or two agents who happen to share a body and a payoff matrix. Both should be sharp; no strawmen, and no tidy conversion where Beat just says "ah, you're right" at the end. If anything I'd like the last exchange to be genuinely uncomfortable for both of them. Keep it to maybe 800 words.
