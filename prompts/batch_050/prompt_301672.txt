Below is a draft of a short essay I'm working on for a blog about democratic theory. I'd like comments on it -- especially on whether the central distinction actually holds up or whether one side collapses into the other. Also flag anything that seems confused or where I'm strawmanning populists. Don't worry about tone/style, I mostly care about the argument.

---

Two ways of speaking for the people

Populists are usually described as claiming to represent the will of the people against a corrupt elite. But I think this description hides two very different things populists might be doing, and the difference matters for how worried we should be.

Call the first mandate populism. The mandate populist says: the people already exist, they already want specific things (lower immigration, say, or leaving the EU), and the elite has refused to deliver. On this picture, the will of the people is a fact that can be read off -- from referendums, polls, election results, common sense -- and politics is just implementation. The populist is a kind of megaphone or delivery mechanism.

Call the second constituent populism. The constituent populist's real work happens earlier: they tell you who the people are in the first place. The hardworking majority, the real Americans, the ordinary folk betrayed by cosmopolitan elites. Before there's any will to implement, there's a boundary being drawn -- who's in, who's out, whose grievances count as the people's grievances and whose are special pleading. The "will" is then just whatever this constructed group is said to want.

My claim: mandate populism is mostly democratic, if crude -- it's arguably just majoritarianism with attitude. The genuinely anti-democratic move is constituent populism, because whoever gets to define the people can't lose. If an election goes against them, the voters weren't the real people. If protests erupt, the protesters aren't the real people. The category does all the work, and it's immune to falsification, whereas a mandate claim can at least be checked -- did the people actually vote for this?

So critics of populism who fixate on "there is no single will of the people" (pluralism! society is diverse!) are aiming at the weaker target. The stronger target is the prior question: by what right does anyone get to draw the boundary of the people, and what would it take for that drawing to itself be democratic?

---

Specific things I'd like comments on: (1) Does mandate populism actually survive contact with reality, or does any real populist movement always end up doing the constituent move when the supposed mandate isn't there? (2) Is it fair to call mandate populism "mostly democratic"? (3) The last paragraph implies the boundary-drawing could in principle be done democratically -- is that coherent, or am I promising something impossible?
