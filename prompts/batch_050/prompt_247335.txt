Here's the exam question (short answer, ~1 page, 20 points):

Consider the sentence: It is raining, but I don't believe that it is raining. There is obviously no contradiction in the weather being one way and my beliefs being another, so this sentence could easily be true. And yet there seems to be something deeply wrong with it. Explain what exactly is wrong with it, and be careful to say whether the problem lies in the sentence itself, in asserting it, or in believing it. In your answer, distinguish the "omissive" form (p, and I don't believe that p) from the "commissive" form (p, and I believe that not-p), and say whether the same diagnosis covers both.

And here's the student's answer:

This is a version of Moore's paradox, associated with G.E. Moore. At first it looks like the sentence must be contradictory, but that can't be right: it might in fact be raining while, as a purely psychological matter, I fail to hold that belief. Since both conjuncts can be true at once, the sentence itself is consistent, and the paradox has to be located elsewhere.

The standard move is to note that assertion normally expresses belief. When someone asserts the first conjunct, they represent themselves as believing that it's raining; when they assert the second conjunct, they represent themselves as lacking that belief. So anyone who asserts the whole sentence represents themselves as both believing and not believing the same thing. That makes the sentence unassertable, though not false. A related observation, which Wittgenstein made, is that in the first-person present tense, "I believe it's raining" functions less like a report on the speaker's mind and more like a tentative assertion about the rain itself, which is why the Moore sentence sounds so bizarre.

The commissive form is a different and worse case. "It is raining and I believe that it is not raining" is a genuine contradiction, because anyone who believes that it is raining cannot also believe that it is not raining — belief is closed under consistency. So unlike the omissive form, where only the assertion is defective, the commissive sentence is defective in itself, and the two forms do not recieve the same diagnosis.

In sum, Moore's paradox is a purely pragmatic phenomenon about speech acts and the norms governing them. It tells us something about what we are permitted to say, not about what may be the case or what may be thought. Someone who silently believes "it's raining but I don't believe it" without ever opening their mouth commits no offense against logic at all — the whole problem lives in the assertion.
