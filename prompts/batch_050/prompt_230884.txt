I am a first-year master's student in philosophy, and I want to turn a recent family dispute into my thesis. My mother died four months ago, and my sister has signed us up for a service that builds a chatbot from the deceased's texts and emails. Our argument is not about whether the bot comforts her -- it plainly does. It is about whether the bot can wrong our mother herself, especially since it does not merely replay her words but generates new utterances in her voice, including things she never said and might never have endorsed. Please help me write a short thesis prospectus (one semester, roughly 10,000 words) on this question: is there an ethically significant line between a simulation that replays or recombines what a dead person actually wrote and one that extrapolates novel speech, and if the line matters, does it matter because of interests the dead retain, or only because of effects on the living? The prospectus should state the question precisely; explain why the existing debates leave it open (the posthumous-harm literature is mostly framed around wills, reputations, and broken promises rather than ongoing interactive simulation, while discussion of griefbots tends to focus on mourners' welfare and consent); lay out the approach -- testing candidate accounts of posthumous interests against constructed cases that vary replay versus generation, prior authorization, and divergence from the person's known values -- and say why this conceptual approach promises more than the obvious alternatives, such as an empirical study of grief outcomes. State what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and give an honest assessment of scope for one semester. I will supply citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments without a bibliography. Throughout, please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely settled in the posthumous-harm debate and what remains contested among those who work on it, and do not present any contested position as the consensus.
