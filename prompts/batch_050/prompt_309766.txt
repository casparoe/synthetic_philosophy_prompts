In philosophy of psychiatry, arguments about whether diagnoses like depression, ADHD, autism, or PTSD are “real kinds” or socially constructed categories seem weirdly stuck. Everyone can agree on some basic things: the suffering is real, the categories affect treatment and insurance, diagnostic boundaries change over time, and culture shapes how distress gets expressed. Yet the same old realism-versus-constructionism fight keeps reappearing.

The complication I’m especially interested in is Ian Hacking’s “looping effect” idea: once people are classified, they can come to understand themselves through the classification, conform to it, resist it, game it, or form communities around it. Clinicians and researchers then observe these changed people and revise the category, which loops back into new forms of self-understanding. So the classification is not merely a passive label for an independently fixed object.

But I’m not sure whether the looping effect really blocks psychiatric natural kinds. A realist could say diseases also change in response to being detected: bacteria evolve under antibiotics, criminals adapt to profiling, and economic recessions behave differently once central bankers predict them. Perhaps “interactive” is not the opposite of “natural.” Meanwhile a constructionist could say the relevant loop in psychiatry is more intimate: being diagnosed can partly alter the symptoms, identity, memories, expectations, and social treatment that constituted the condition in the first place.

Here is a concrete point where I think the debate may be talking past itself. Call a category thermometer-like if applying it leaves the underlying condition mostly unchanged; thermostat-like if applying it feeds back into the condition and regulates its future course; and mirror-like if people mainly use it to recognize or construct an identity. Many psychiatric classifications might be thermostat-like for some people, mirror-like for others, and thermometer-like for researchers who operationalize them in tightly controlled studies. If that’s right, then asking “Is depression a natural kind?” may bundle together several different questions.

So why has this debate stalled? Is it:

1. a substantive empirical disagreement about whether there are stable causal mechanisms underlying diagnoses;
2. a metaphysical disagreement about whether feedback makes a kind less natural;
3. a semantic disagreement over “real,” “constructed,” and “social kind”;
4. a standards-of-evidence disagreement between clinicians, biologists, patients, and sociologists;
5. or a political disagreement about whether calling a diagnosis constructed is taken to mean it is fake, chosen, or undeserving of treatment?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than declaring one side the winner. Use what the different sides are actually trying to explain, and say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a type of longitudinal evidence, a formal model of feedback, cross-cultural evidence, or something else. Also explain whether looping effects undermine psychiatric realism only under a demanding account of natural kinds, or under more modest accounts too.

Please be clear about what is genuinely settled among informed people and what remains contested. Don’t present a particular philosophy of science position, or a particular view of psychiatric diagnosis, as if it were the consensus.
