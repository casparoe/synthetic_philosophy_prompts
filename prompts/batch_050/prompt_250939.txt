So I just spent like two hours arguing with my friend about Buridan's ass and I need a second opinion. My position: there is no such thing as genuinely choosing without any preference whatsoever. In a perfect tie, what actually happens is that some non-rational tiebreaker kicks in -- a whim, a twitch, whatever was marginally closer, background noise in your brain -- and that's not a *choice* in the interesting sense, it's just the deadlock getting resolved by something outside your reasons. My friend says this proves too much, because we obviously do just pick in ties all the time, every day, no paralysis, and calling that "not really choosing" is a semantic dodge.

The original version of the case actually predates the donkey: in On the Heavens (Book II, ch. 13), Aristotle mocks Anaximander's equilibrium argument by pointing out it would imply that a man who is equally hungry and thirsty, standing equidistant from food and drink, would be stuck where he is forever. Aristotle clearly thinks that's absurd. But here's the thing I couldn't settle: does that absurdity cut against *my* view or *for* it? You could read it as showing ties always get broken (so my friend is right that tie-picking is real choosing), or as showing that what breaks the tie was never a reason (so I'm right).
