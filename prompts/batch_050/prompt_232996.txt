You are a professional philosopher with expertise in metaethics and moral psychology, advising me on an essay I am drafting. I want to read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the debate about internal and external reasons. Please think carefully, brainstorm the main options first, and then give me the reading.

The crux I care about: Stevens seems unreachable by reasons. When Lord Darlington orders the two Jewish refugee maids dismissed, and when Miss Kenton is implicitly offering him a different life, nothing in Stevens's deliberative repertoire can get a grip — every consideration is processed through the professional ideal of the great butler and what he calls dignity. On Williams's internalism, the claim that Stevens has reason to refuse, or to leave, is true only if a sound deliberative route runs from his actual motivational set to refusing or leaving; but his set is constituted by the very norms the reasons would have to work against. So address three things:

1. Does the novel commit itself to external reasons? The narration is built so that the reader sees what Stevens cannot; does that structure presume there were reasons there whether or not Stevens could reach them, or does the book in fact vindicate Williams by treating such judgments as, at best, an optimistic bet about what deliberation from within Stevens's outlook could have achieved?

2. What should the internalist say about Stevens's late recognition on the pier? If his regret shows the motivational set finally shifting, does the reason to have lived otherwise appear only then, vindicating internalism — or does the scene make better sense if the reason was there all along and merely blocked? Stevens looks like the hard case for Williams: an agent whose deliberative capacities are deformed by the very thing he needs reasons against. Say whether you think he is that case, and whether the novel suggests an escape route the journal literature doesn't.

3. What does the book add that the articles don't? I am particularly interested in whether it shows something about deliberative imagination as a moral capacity — that a sound deliberative route can fail not for lack of relevant desires or beliefs but for lack of the imaginative resources to take a consideration seriously — and whether that observation supports a more permissive internalism or is just externalism under another name.

Work from the novel's actual scenes and its frame structure (the motoring trip, the letters from Miss Kenton) rather than speaking in generalities, and where the text resists the philosophical reading, say so plainly. No citations or bibliography needed; I know the literature.
