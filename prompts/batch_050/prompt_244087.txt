I sit on the honors review board of a national military academy, and we have to decide whether to strip a retired general of a decoration over something he did as a cadet: he falsified a training report, and a classmate got expelled over it. Ordinarily this would be simple, but here's the wrinkle. Fifteen years ago, after a rough deployment, the general voluntarily underwent an experimental memory-suppression treatment for PTSD. It worked, broadly -- he lost most of his autobiographical memory from before his thirties. He now sincerely remembers nothing about the academy. But his private diaries from his forties (which his family handed over) show his younger self remembered the cadet years clearly, including a guilty passage about "the report thing." Our charter says the board can only sanction someone if they're "the same person" as the offender, and a working group once glossed that as psychological continuity. So: the general remembers being the man who wrote the diaries, and the man who wrote the diaries remembered being the cadet -- but the general doesn't remember being the cadet. Depending how you cash out continuity, both verdicts seem available. Draft the board's written opinion: recommend revoke or don't, say what test of personal identity the board should adopt going forward, give the losing side its strongest run, and address whether it matters that the memory loss was self-chosen.
