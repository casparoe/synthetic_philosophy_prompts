I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and a recurring editorial problem has become acute enough that I want it systematized. Several times a month I receive pitches, preprints, or university press releases claiming that some new theoretical result or experiment shows the quantum wavefunction to be real, or unreal, or that it rules out a whole class of interpretations of quantum mechanics. My editors are excited by these stories; my experience is that most of the claims collapse on inspection, but a few are genuinely significant, and I currently have no defensible way of telling which is which in the two hours I typically have before I must file or kill a pitch.

What I am asking for is a triage procedure I can actually run, built on the psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic distinction as drawn by Harrigan and Spekkens and on the logic of results like the 2012 theorem of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph, which purports to show that, given certain assumptions, models treating the quantum state as mere knowledge of an underlying reality cannot reproduce quantum predictions. The procedure should be a sequence of questions in order, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and where to look for it in a paper or press release. I need it to force the following discriminations, because these are exactly where the press releases blur things: whether a claim concerns the ontological status of the quantum state or merely confirms, yet again, a prediction of standard quantum mechanics that no serious interpretation denies; whether a no-go result rules out a precisely circumscribed class of models or is being inflated into a verdict on an entire interpretation; which auxiliary assumptions (such as preparation independence, no-superdeterminism, or free choice of measurement settings) the result requires, and whether the pitch quietly drops them; whether the view being "ruled out" is one anyone actually holds, and whether the major interpretations — collapse theories, many-worlds, pilot-wave, and the various epistemic or statistical readings — even fit cleanly into the ontic/epistemic dichotomy or fall outside its intended scope.

The procedure must also say where it terminates: which questions, if answered a certain way, mean the story is dead regardless of the result's technical merit, and which questions are irreducibly judgment calls that I should flag to my editor rather than let the checklist answer for me. Where the distinction between a metaphysical claim and a statistical claim resists a checklist, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical pitches: first, a preprint claiming to rule out all psi-epistemic models via a Bell-style experiment with entangled photons; second, a press release from a major university announcing that researchers have "measured the wavefunction directly," proving it is a physical object; third, a theorist's claim that a new retrocausal model shows the wavefunction is neither ontic nor epistemic in the standard sense and that the entire debate rests on a false dichotomy. At least one of these applications should produce a verdict I will not enjoy delivering — a story my editors want that the procedure tells me to kill or to gut of its headline claim. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which errors it will systematically fail to catch, and what kind of expertise I would need to buy in for those cases.
