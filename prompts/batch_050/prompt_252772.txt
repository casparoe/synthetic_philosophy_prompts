I'm the registrar for my university's undergraduate prizes committee, which each year awards the one senior thesis prize in each department. Our current procedure is that a three-member faculty panel reads the shortlisted theses, meets to deliberate, and decides by consensus or, failing that, majority vote. The chair of the committee has proposed replacing this with a nine-member panel whose members read the theses independently, never discuss them, and submit ranked ballots that we aggregate by majority rule. Her case rests on what she calls the statistics of group judgment: if each reader is even somewhat better than a coin flip at identifying the strongest thesis, a larger panel of independent readers converges on the right answer with near certainty, whereas three people in a room are hostage to whoever talks most persuasively.

I've pushed back in our planning meetings, though I'm no longer sure I'm right. My points were these: (1) her arithmetic assumes each reader's errors are independent, but our readers were all trained in the same three graduate programs, rely on the same canonical markers of quality, and read the same letters of recommendation, so the "independent draws" assumption seems to fail exactly where it matters; (2) with three shortlisted theses rather than two, majority aggregation can cycle -- thesis A beats B, B beats C, C beats A -- and a larger panel makes that more visible, not less; (3) deliberation isn't merely a source of bias: readers routinely catch each other's misreadings, share subject-matter knowledge the others lack, and correct factual errors, so the meeting itself raises each reader's individual competence above whatever it was in isolation, which the chair's model treats as fixed. She replies that deliberation mostly converts independent guesses into correlated ones dominated by senior voices, that cycling is rare enough to handle with a runoff rule, and that on my own logic I should want the nine members to deliberate, not the three -- so my arguments really support expanding the panel while keeping the meeting.

I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo for next month's vote, recommending either the status quo, the chair's proposal, or some specified hybrid (e.g., independent first-round balloting followed by a deliberative second round). Please write that memo: a clear bottom-line recommendation, an analysis that takes seriously both the conditions under which larger independent panels outperform smaller deliberative ones and the conditions under which they don't, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument against whichever option you recommend -- including whether the chair's closing point (that my objections argue for expansion-with-deliberation rather than the status quo) is actually sound. Roughly 600-900 words; the committee members are academics but not statisticians, so any formalism should be explained in prose.
