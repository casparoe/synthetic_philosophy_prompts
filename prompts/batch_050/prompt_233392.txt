In the Scholium to the Definitions of the Principia, Newton argues that rotation can be detected without any reference to other bodies: if two globes connected by a cord revolve about their common center of gravity, the tension of the cord reveals their endeavor to recede from the axis of rotation, and hence the quantity of their circular motion -- and this, Newton says, would hold even in an immense vacuum where nothing external existed to which the globes' motion could be compared. This is the less-discussed companion to the bucket argument, and it is in some ways the cleaner one, since it dispenses with the messy dynamics of fluid and bucket.

Construct a thought experiment that puts maximal pressure on this argument, engineered to the following specifications:

1. The scenario must be a universe containing Newton's two globes and cord and nothing else of consequence -- no distant stars, no background matter -- and it must be describable in a single paragraph using only Newtonian mechanics (no general relativity, no quantum effects, no perfect predictors or demons).

2. The experiment must be engineered so that the substantivalist and the relationist are forced into opposite verdicts about a single, specified observable -- the tension in the cord -- in a case where the two theories cannot both be rescued by redescribing the same facts. In particular, the case must block the relationist's standard first move of treating "rotation" as merely rotation relative to some tacitly assumed background, since by stipulation there is no background. Design the scenario so that this blocking is achieved by the structure of the case rather than by fiat.

3. The scenario should isolate exactly one variable -- the presence or absence of absolute space as a bearer of inertial structure -- while holding fixed everything the two theories share (the intrinsic properties of the globes, the cord, their mutual distances and relative motions at each instant).

After presenting the scenario, do the following:

- State the observable prediction each side is committed to, and walk through the reasoning that generates each prediction from the theory itself rather than from a caricature of it.
- Identify the single feature of your construction that does the philosophical work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed -- in particular, whether it collapses back into the standard dialectic in which the relationist simply concedes that Newtonian mechanics requires more structure than bare relative motions (Sklar's maneuver) and the debate shifts to whether that structure must be reified as a substance.
- Anticipate the strongest counterattack from the side your case comes out worst against: if your case embarrasses the relationist, give the relationist's best reply (for instance, the sophisticated relationist who denies that inertial structure requires substantival space, or the Machian who denies that your universe is physically possible rather than merely conceivable); if it embarrasses the substantivalist, give the substantivalist's best reply. Say whether the reply succeeds or merely relocates the disagreement.

Finally, say in two or three sentences whether Newton's globes argument, as applied to your constructed universe, is best understood as an empirical argument, a metaphysical argument, or an inference to the best explanation -- and whether your verdict on that question changes which side you think wins.
