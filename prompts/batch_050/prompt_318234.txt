I am a PhD student in epistemology, and alongside my dissertation I coordinate the intake workflow for a small university-affiliated archive that catalogs video evidence submitted by journalists, researchers, and citizen witnesses. My dissertation touches on what I take to be the deepest problem with synthetic media, which I will state in my own terms so we can work from it directly: the damage done by convincing fakes is not primarily that particular false videos get believed, but that recordings lose what I think of as their backstop function. As long as events might have been recorded, speakers are constrained in what they can deny or assert, and a recording can settle a dispute on its own. Once any recording can plausibly be dismissed as fabricated, the backstop fails in both directions at once: genuine footage no longer settles anything, and denials of real events become cheap. My archive now faces this operationally, and our current practice is ad hoc.

Please turn this diagnosis into a written intake-and-assessment procedure my team (graduate students with no forensic training, at most one hour per item) can actually run on each submitted video: the questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and at which points the procedure issues a verdict (catalog as evidence, catalog with a downgraded epistemic status, hold pending review, or decline). The procedure must respect a distinction we keep collapsing: between a video being fabricated, being genuine but miscontextualized (real footage attached to a false claim about when, where, or what it shows), and being genuine and correctly described but unverifiable by us. Say explicitly where the procedure must hand off to a human judgment call and why that step cannot be proceduralized. It should also be honest about what no intake procedure can do, given that detection tools give probabilities and that finding no sign of manipulation is not proof of authenticity.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a clip of a public official making an incriminating statement, submitted anonymously with no provenance during an election period; (2) bystander footage of an alleged assault whose metadata checks out and which two independent witnesses corroborate, but where our archive cannot independently confirm the metadata; (3) footage our staff strongly believes to be genuine and correctly described, on which the procedure nonetheless forces a status we will find unsatisfying. For the third case, do not soften the result: show the procedure giving an answer we will not like, and explain why that is the procedure working rather than failing.
