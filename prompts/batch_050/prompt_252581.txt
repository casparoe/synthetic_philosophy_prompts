Since I retired from the biology department a few years back I've been falling down the philosophy-of-mind rabbit hole, and recently I got stuck on the sphex wasp case — you know, the digger wasp that drags its paralyzed cricket to the burrow entrance, pops inside to check things out, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's down there, she comes out, repositions it, and checks again, over and over, never seeming to notice she's in a loop. What really unsettles me (and my wife, who has heard about this wasp at dinner too many times now) is the follow-up question: how would I know I'm not doing the same thing in some domain of my own life, where the loop just happens to look like thoughtfulness from the inside?

So here's what I'd like you to build for me. Construct a thought experiment in which a human being exhibits genuine sphexishness — they're running a fixed subroutine, not deliberating — but where this fact is completely invisible to outside observers, because the loop only ever gets triggered in a way that mimics perfectly sensible, reasons-responsive behavior. Some constraints: the setting has to be mundane and realistic (a kitchen, a commute, a lab bench — no trolleys, no mad scientists, no brain chips, no hypnosis, nothing a retired naturalist couldn't stumble into); the whole scenario should fit in one paragraph; and, crucially, the sphexishness must be revealed by a small environmental nudge analogous to moving the cricket — some trivial intervention that harms no one and that the agent never even registers as an intervention. I'd also like the loop to concern something that actually matters morally (how the person treats a colleague, say), not just where they leave their keys, because I want the stakes for responsibility to be real.

After the scenario, do the philosophy: walk me through how the case looks from a reasons-responsiveness picture of free will versus a sourcehood or leeway picture — does the person act freely, and are they morally responsible for what the loop makes them do? Tell me which feature of your case is doing the argumentative work and what happens to the verdicts if you remove it. Then, honestly, play defense for the other side: how would a compatibilist argue that my person is no worse off than any of us, given that we're all creatures of habit? And finally, tell me at what point, as you make the triggering nudge less and less peripheral, the case stops being about sphexishness and just becomes an ordinary story about somebody with a bad habit — or is there no clean line? I suspect there isnt, which is sort of the point, but I'd like to see it argued rather than asserted.
