I serve on the nine-member hiring committee at my college, and we are deadlocked over two finalists for a single tenure-track position. At our last meeting the dean proposed replacing our current practice -- open discussion until consensus emerges, with a formal vote as a formality -- with a straight secret-ballot majority vote taken before any substantive discussion. The proposal came out of a long argument I had with a friend over dinner, and I made the mistake of repeating her reasoning at the meeting. Her claim was that under plausible conditions a majority of individually fallible but better-than-chance judges is far more reliable than any individual member, and that our consensus ritual actively destroys the very thing that makes majority rule trustworthy: we listen to the same job talk, read the same three reference letters, and then spend two hours in a room where the two most senior members speak first, so by the time we "vote" our errors are no longer independent but correlated, and whatever epistemic magic the majority might have had has been engineered away.

I pushed back, and I still think I was partly right. Her picture assumes each member's judgment is better than a coin flip, which is doubtful when we assess candidates outside our own specialties -- our committee spans five disciplines, and on the candidate from the farthest field perhaps only three of us have genuine competence. I also argued that discussion is what *produces* competence: the senior members' comments are how the rest of us learn what to look for, so abandoning deliberation to protect independence might preserve the statistical conditions of the theorem while undermining its epistemic premise. But she had a reply that keeps nagging me: if discussion raises everyone's competence but correlates everyone's errors through the same anchors, we may end up confidently and unanimously wrong, whereas silent independent judgment at least guarantees that our mistakes cancel. Neither of us could say which effect dominates for a committee of nine, where asymptotic assurances are cold comfort anyway.

Please write a formal memo to the committee recommending a decision procedure for this and future searches. The memo should take a clear position among the live options: the dean's pre-discussion secret ballot, our current consensus practice, or some hybrid (e.g., an independent first-round ballot followed by discussion and a second vote, restricted voting by demonstrated competence, or expanding the committee). Ground the recommendation in the logic of the majority-competence argument -- stating its assumptions explicitly, marking where our small committee violates them, and explaining how correlated error changes the calculus -- while giving an honest account of the strongest point on the losing side, since whatever you recommend, at least half the committee will have backed the alternative. The memo should end with concrete procedural language the dean could adopt as written.
