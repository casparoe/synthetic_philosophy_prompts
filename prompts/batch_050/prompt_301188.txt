Here's something that's been bugging me since I started reading about consent for a seminar. The same legal system, sometimes the same month of my life, uses 'consent' in what look like three completely different ways:

1. I recently had minor surgery. Beforehand the surgeon had to walk me through the material risks and get a signature, and if she'd skipped a serious risk she could be on the hook for that even if the operation itself went flawlessly. The wrong is in the nondisclosure, not in touching me badly.
2. Yesterday I clicked 'I agree' on a 40-page software license without reading a word, like everyone does tbh, and as far as the law is concered I'm bound. Nobody asked whether I understood anything; the click is enough.
3. And then there's sex: in most jurisdictions, lying to get someone into bed -- saying you're single when you're married, or rich when you're broke -- is sleazy but not a crime; the lie leaves consent intact. But if a doctor touches a patient intimately while pretending it's an examination when there's no actual medical purpose, that deception destroys consent and it's assault. So deception about what the act is invalidates, deception about everything surrounding the act doesn't.

What theory of valid consent does each of these practices presuppose? The medical one looks like it treats consent as genuine informed authorization, worthless without actual understanding. The click-through one looks like consent is just a signal you emit, a bare performative, with comprehension beside the point. And the sex cases seem to presuppose that consent attaches to the act itself rather than to your reasons for agreeing to it, which is a weirdly specific commitment when you think about it.

Can one coherent theory of what makes consent valid underwrite all three, or is 'consent' just three different norms sharing a word? For each practice, tell me what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits comfortably with -- e.g. does medicine really need the strong 'understanding' theory, or could a weaker view still explain why the disclosure-and-signature ritual looks the way it does? And where is a practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it equally well? Finally: if we swapped the theories across domains -- ran software licenses on the medical theory, or surgery on the click-through theory, or sex on either of them -- what would concretely have to change in each practice?
