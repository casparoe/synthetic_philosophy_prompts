I'm an economist -- game theory, mechanism design, dynamic inconsistency, commitment devices, that whole toolkit. Recently I stumbled onto Kavka's toxin puzzle and I keep feeling like it's secretly one of our problems wearing a costume, but I can't quite get the costume off. Quick recap in case the details matter: some rich eccentric offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vile (painful, but not dangerous) toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money depends only on having the intention at midnight, not on actually drinking. And you know that come tomorrow, once the money's secured, you'll have no reason whatsoever to actually drink the stuff. Kavka's line, as I understand it, is that you can't win the money -- not because drinking is irrational, but because you can't even form the intention, since you already know you'll have no reason to follow through.

So here's what I want. Restate the puzzle and the main moves in the debate around it -- whether such an intention is rationally formable at all, whether intending-to-drink and drinking can come apart the way the puzzle needs, the whole thing about it sometimes being rational to make yourself irrational -- into the vocabulary I actually think in: extensive-form games against your future self, subgame perfection and credible threats, hyperbolic discounting and sophistication vs naivete, commitment devices, cheap talk, self-enforcing agreements, a principal trying to incentivize an agent he can't contract with. Be as precise as you can about what each piece of the puzzle becomes.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly (my guess is the incentive structure does), which ones you had to bend to fit, and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary. My suspicion is that "genuinely intending" as opposed to "announcing a plan" is the thing that won't translate, since game theory doesn't seem to distinguish forming an intention from just picking a strategy -- but maybe I'm wrong. And give me your verdict: is whatever's left over a real limitation of the game-theoretic toolkit, or is it a sign that the philosophers' puzzle depends on a notion (intention as something over and above expected-utility-relevant choice) that dissolves once you model the situation properly? Basically I want to know whether this puzzle tells economists something we're missing, or tells philosophers something they're overcomplicating.
