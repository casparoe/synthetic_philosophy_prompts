I teach political philosophy at a mid-sized European university, and this spring I was appointed, somewhat to my surprise, as the lone humanities academic on our national government's Advisory Council on Measuring Societal Progress. The council has been asked to rule on a proposal from the finance ministry that would make it official policy to place our country among the top five in the World Happiness Report within ten years, with a portion of each ministry's discretionary budget — five percent in the current draft — tied to improvement in our mean score on the ladder-of-life question that drives those rankings. A late amendment, inserted by a junior minister who has clearly been reading about behavioral insights, would additionally let the national statistics office experiment with the framing and ordering of the survey questions themselves if doing so could 'more accurately capture' our citizens' evaluations, a phrase several of us on the council find slippery. Our chair has asked each member to submit a short written opinion before the vote, and mine is due next week.

In preparing it, I have been rereading Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, in particular the early chapter where he sets out how the value of a pleasure or pain is to be measured. Bentham proposes that pleasures and pains be assessed along several dimensions — their intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, propinquity or remoteness, and, when considering acts rather than single sensations, their fecundity, purity, and extent across persons. What strikes me is how poorly a single self-report on an eleven-rung ladder seems to map onto this apparatus: the ladder arguably compresses intensity and duration into one gesture, says nothing about fecundity or purity at all, and yet Bentham's list is precisely the sort of thing a defender of the ministry's proposal might invoke, claiming that any cardinal measure of felt welfare is Benthamite in spirit and that an imperfect gauge beats none. I want my opinion to take that defense seriously rather than dismiss it.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the council, of roughly 800 to 1,000 words. It should reach a clear, actionable recommendation on the proposal as a whole — adopt, adopt with specified amendments (if so, name them), or reject — and should say explicitly what the council should do about the survey-framing amendment and the budget-tied incentive, since those are the two points on which the vote will actually turn. In the reasoning, test the ministry's plan against Bentham's dimensions: which of them, if any, a life-evaluation ladder genuinely measures; whether the gaps are a reason to fix the instrument, to supplement it, or to abandon the target; and whether tying money to the score corrupts the measure in a way Bentham's framework can or cannot anticipate. Finally, give an honest statement of the strongest consideration in favor of the side your recommendation rejects — not a token concession, but the version of that consideration you would find hardest to answer if a colleague pressed it on you at the meeting.
