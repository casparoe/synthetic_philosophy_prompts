I'm writing a paper for my jurisprudence seminar and I've gotten stuck on something, so let me lay out the actual case I'm working with. After German reunification, in the early 1990s, German courts prosecuted former East German border guards for shooting people who tried to flee across the Berlin Wall. The guards' defense was essentially: what we did was legal under the law that applied to us at the time -- the GDR border statute explicitly authorized the use of firearms to prevent illegal border crossings, and we were soldiers following lawful orders. The courts convicted them anyway, and in doing so reached for the Radbruch formula, the idea that positive law which reaches a certain threshold of injustice simply forfeits its character as law. As I understand it, this maps onto one of the two sides of the Hart--Fuller debate: Fuller (and Radbruch before him) thinks sufficiently evil law isn't law at all, while Hart thinks that move is a confusion -- the honest thing to do is admit the law was valid but monstrous, and then punish people under openly retrospective legislation instead of pretending the legality away.

What I want from you is an analysis that runs the debate through this episode in both directions, not just a summary of what Hart and Fuller said in 1958. On the theory-to-case direction: what exactly does each position say the German courts should have done, and be precise about what each one gives up. Hart's retrospective-statute solution has its own rule-of-law cost (it seems to violate the nullum crimen sine lege principle that Hart elsewhere treats as part of the minimum content of legality), so say how bad that cost is and whether Hart can just bite it. Fuller's side needs a principled threshold for when injustice voids legality, so test it: was every application of the GDR border law intolerably unjust, or only its application to unarmed civilians -- and if the latter, is the formula even doing the work, or is ordinary criminal-law reasoning doing it?

Then the case-to-theory direction, which is the part I actually care about for my paper. Two wrinkles I want you to take seriously. First, several of the defendants were young conscripts who would have been punished themselves for refusing orders, and others were senior officials who designed the system -- does the Hart--Fuller framing treat those cases the same, and should it? Second, and this is what has me stuck: some commentators argue the shootings were arguably unlawful even under East German law as written, since the statute contained proportionality requirements and the shootings were systematically covered up by the GDR itself, which suggests the regime didn't think its own law authorized them. If that's right, the Radbruch question never actually arises in these cases. Does that possibility dissolve the Hart--Fuller dispute here, or does it just relocate it -- and does the fact that courts reached for the formula anyway tell us something about what the dispute was really about? My sense is Hart's claim that the two sides mostly agreed on outcomes gets tested hard by this case, but I can't tell whether it confirms or embarrasses him.
