I run a small reading group on the ethics of future generations, and next month I am leading our session on population axiology. In preparation, I have been pressing one of our regulars — a sharp, rather doctrinaire member named Nadia — for her verdicts on a series of pairwise comparisons between possible worlds, each described by the number of people it contains and the quality of their lives (assume welfare is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, and that the worlds differ only in the respects stated). Her verdicts so far: between a world of two billion people with excellent lives and a world of two billion with merely decent lives, she says the first is strictly better; between four billion at good-but-unremarkable lives and eight billion at somewhat better lives, she says the second is better; between five billion very good lives and three billion moderately good ones, she says the first is better; and between four billion at high welfare and that same world plus three billion additional people whose lives are slightly better still, she says the enlarged world is better, remarking that the mere addition could only improve matters. I should add that early in our discussions she announced herself a Sidgwickian, citing the passage in Book IV, Chapter I of The Methods of Ethics where Sidgwick argues that the utilitarian end is reached not when average happiness is at its greatest but when the product of the number of persons and the average happiness each enjoys is at its maximum, so that the happiness gained by additional people is to be weighed against whatever the remainder lose.

Here is the comparison I intend to put to her next. World J contains five billion people, all at very high welfare. World K contains those same five billion, entirely unaffected, plus fifteen billion further people whose lives are worth living but only just — modest, straitened, yet genuinely positive. My questions: first, which candidate principles are jointly consistent with her four recorded verdicts (I have in mind at least the total view, the average view, critical-level views, and person-affecting or neutrality views, but please correct or extend that list); secondly, what does each of those principles say about J versus K, and where, precisely, do they diverge; thirdly, what is your best prediction of Nadia's actual verdict, giving the reasons for preferring it — in particular, how much weight her professed Sidgwickianism should carry against the pattern of her verdicts, and whether her remark about mere addition settles anything, given that in that case the addition raised both the total and the average; and fourthly, if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what single further pairwise comparison would most efficiently discriminate between the surviving principles.

Please structure the response along those four lines rather than treating it as an invitation to defend your own favourite axiology. Throughout, distinguish carefully between what is settled — the logic of the cases and what the standard views in fact imply — and what remains genuinely contested among people who work on population ethics, including how Sidgwick's own criterion is best interpreted; do not present any contested verdict as the consensus. Please write in British English.
