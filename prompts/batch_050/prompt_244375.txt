A popular philosophy-of-science book I've been reading gives the following account of Hume's problem of induction and Goodman's "new riddle," and I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it in an essay:

"Hume's argument in the Enquiry is disarmingly simple. When we infer that bread will nourish us tomorrow because it has nourished us in the past, we assume that nature is uniform; but that assumption cannot be demonstrated a priori, since its negation is conceivable, and it cannot be established by experience, since any experiential argument for it already presupposes it. Hume thus proved that inductive inference is rationally groundless, that science ultimately rests on blind animal faith rather than reason, and that we continue to make predictions only because habit leaves us no choice. Nelson Goodman's grue paradox sharpens the point into a refutation. Define 'grue' as applying to things examined before some future time t just in case they are green, and to other things just in case they are blue. Every emerald observed so far is grue, so all our evidence confirms 'all emeralds are grue' exactly as strongly as it confirms 'all emeralds are green' -- yet the two hypotheses make opposite predictions about emeralds first examined after t. Since we can cook up such predicates at will, any body of evidence can be made to confirm any conclusion whatsoever, and induction collapses into an arbitrary game of linguistic gerrymandering. Goodman's own verdict was that there is nothing to choose between the hypotheses except entrenched habit: we project 'green' rather than 'grue' only because our linguistic community happens to have used 'green' longer. So the upshot of Hume and Goodman together is that scientific prediction rests on nothing more than custom plus the historical accidents of vocabulary."

The broad shape of this feels right to me, but two things nag: I had thought Hume's conclusion was about what inductive reasoning *is* rather than a proof that it is worthless, and I had thought Goodman's point was about telling good predicates from bad ones rather than showing that evidence confirms everything equally. But the book is by a reputable philosopher, so maybe I'm the one flattening things. Please audit the passage: identify which parts accurately represent Hume's argument in Section IV of the Enquiry, which parts overstate or distort his conclusion, whether the definition of "grue" and the symmetry claim are stated correctly, whether Goodman's appeal to entrenchment is fairly described or made to sound more deflationary than it is, and -- most importantly -- whether the closing claim about what scientific prediction "rests on" actually follows from the accurate parts of either argument.
