I'm in a small philosophy of math reading group (mixed grads and one very patient set theorist) and we keep circling the same fight. Whenever someone asks whether 2 is literally an element of 4 — like, is the question even well-posed, given that on the standard von Neumann construction it is and on other equally good constructions it isn't — somebody reaches for the position analogy: being the number 2 is like being shortstop, or like being the white king's bishop, or like being the treasurer of a club. The shortstop is nothing over and above a slot in a web of relations defined by the rules of baseball, and 2 is nothing over and above a slot in the natural number structure. And the analogy gets wheeled in to do a specific job: dissolve the question. Asking whether 2 is a member of 4 is supposed to be like asking whether the shortstop position is itself a member of the second baseman position — not a deep fact one set-theoretic reduction gets right and another gets wrong, just a confused way of talking.

Last week our set theorist snapped and said the analogy is carrying the entire conclusion and nobody in the room has ever actually checked it. So: check it. Give me the explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the game, the rules, the positions, the players (if anything does), the identity of a position across different rulebooks. Then tell me where it breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the dissolving job from the ones that are just noise. Two pressure points I want addressed specifically: first, positions in games and organizations are slots that get *occupied* — someone plays shortstop, the position and its occupant come apart — whereas nothing occupies the number 2, so does the analogy smuggle in a position/occupant distinction arithmetic simply lacks, and does that make the dissolution easier or harder to defend? Second, chess squares and infield positions are to some degree interchangeable (symmetries of the board, the shortstop and second baseman being distinguishable only by convention), while the natural numbers are rigid — every number is pinned down uniquely by its structural relations alone — so is 2 actually more like a position than shortstop is, or does that rigidity quietly turn 2 back into the kind of self-standing object the analogy was meant to eliminate?

Finally: does the analogy have the resources to even state, let alone answer, the cross-structure identity questions (is the natural number 2 the same thing as the real number 2, the complex 2+0i?), or does it just declare them out of bounds by fiat? If after the audit the analogy still works, say what work remains for it; if it collapses, tell me what comparison the structuralist should actually be using, or whether the whole "numbers as mere positions" picture was only ever the analogy in disguise.
