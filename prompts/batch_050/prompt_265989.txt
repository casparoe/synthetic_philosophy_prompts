Consider the characteristic features of the modern corporate and consumer wellness industry, taken as a single practice: employers run wellness programs that set uniform, quantified health targets for all employees (step counts, sleep hours, biomarker ranges); wearable devices continuously score the body against these standards and treat any dip as a problem to be corrected; illness and low productivity are often framed, however gently, as failures of individual discipline; and health itself is presented as a baseline state of unimpaired functioning that everyone should aim to preserve indefinitely and in essentially the same way.

Contrast this with the outlook Friedrich Nietzsche developed from his own experience of chronic sickness. Nietzsche held that questions of diet, climate, and daily regimen are far more philosophically important than his contemporaries assumed, but that there is no single regimen suited to everyone: what nourishes one person may ruin another, and any rule of living must be fitted to an individual's particular physiology. He also credited his own long periods of illness with making much of his philosophy possible, treating suffering not as a pure loss but as something a strong nature can use as a stimulus, and he admired what he called a kind of "great health" that consists not in the absence of sickness but in the capacity to endure, incorporate, and profit from it. On his view, an anxious obsession with preserving one's health can itself be a symptom of weakness rather than of strength.
