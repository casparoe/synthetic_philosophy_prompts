So Hume's Of Suicide makes his case by going through three duties one by one -- duty to God, duty to society, duty to yourself -- and arguing suicide needn't violate any of them. Meanwhile Canada has been running a live experiment on this: MAiD legalized in 2016 for the terminally ill, expanded in 2021 to people whose death isn't reasonably forseeable, a further extension to mental-illness-only cases proposed and repeatedly kicked down the road, and recurring stories of people applying not because they're dying but because they can't get housing or treatment.

Analyze the Canadian MAiD regime through Hume's essay, in both directions. Which of the three duty-arguments does the original terminal-illness version vindicate, and which does the 2021 expansion quietly lean on? Hume's society-duty line is roughly that you're permitted to bow out once you've become a burden -- does that survive contact with cases where the "burden" is manufactured by the state failing to house or treat people, or does it collapse into a perverse-incentive story? And the mental-illness-only track seems to attack the duty-to-oneself argument from the inside: if the wish to die is itself a symptom of the condition that makes you eligible, whose self-interest is being served? Don't give me a book report on Hume -- I want the mapping, and an honest account of where the mapping breaks and where Hume just doesn't speak.
