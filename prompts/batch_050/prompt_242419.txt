I'm writing the term paper for my epistemology seminar on the regress problem, and I've become more interested in the state of the debate than in the trilemma itself. Roughly: foundationalists hold that inferential chains terminate in beliefs that are justified without support from further beliefs; coherentists hold that justification is a matter of a belief's fit within a sufficiently comprehensive and coherent system, so support runs in loops or holistically rather than linearly; and infinitists, following Klein, hold that the chain of reasons is infinite and non-repeating, and that this is a feature rather than a reductio. Each camp has a standard objection to the other two -- coherentism allegedly lets a belief be justified by a system that is internally elegant but cut off from the world; foundationalism allegedly dignifies arbitrary stopping points as "basic"; infinitism allegedly demands of finite creatures what they cannot deliver. These objections have been on the table, in essentially their current form, for decades, and the underlying puzzle for two millennia. As far as I can tell, the debate is a stable three-body orbit: nobody's objections get answered, nobody defects, and each new paper mostly restates its camp's charge against the others with fresh examples.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that this is not one disagreement but two running in parallel under a single name. One question is structural: what does the justification of a belief consist in -- what makes it the case that I am justified? The other is dialectical: what must I be able to produce when a challenger keeps asking how I know? On my hypothesis, foundationalism and coherentism are answers to the structural question, infinitism is really an answer to the dialectical one (a thesis about what genuinely giving a reason requires), and much of the apparent standoff comes from each side scoring points against a question the other wasn't answering. A friend in the seminar has a more deflationary version: she thinks the entire trilemma is an artifact of the spatial metaphors the debate runs on -- foundations, chains, webs, rafts, pyramids -- which treat justification as a load-bearing structure that must either rest on bedrock, hold itself up, or dangle forever, and that if you drop the engineering picture the three "options" stop looking exhaustive or even rivalrous.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A substantive dispute about the nature of epistemic justification, a partly verbal one about what counts as "giving a reason," or several well-posed but different questions wearing one label? Please diagnose it by engaging what each side actually argues -- not a survey of the three positions, but an account of why their objections perpetually fail to land: do the sides differ about whether justification must be accessible to the subject, about whether non-inferential justification is even intelligible, about what the skeptic's challenge demands, or about starting intuitions that admit of no further argument? Assess my two-questions hypothesis and my friend's deflationary one against your own diagnosis. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves part of it, an argument showing one horn misdescribes its own demand, a formal or empirical result from elsewhere (say, about probabilistic support or about cognitive architecture), or the conclusion that no progress should be expected because the impasse is the correct theory of the phenomenon.
