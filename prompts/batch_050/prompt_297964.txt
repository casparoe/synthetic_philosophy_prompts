Ok so I've been going in circles on the aggregation problem in Scanlon-style contractualism — the whole "can a zillion tiny harms outweigh one big one" thing — and I figured I'd do the rationalist thing and just write down my gut verdicts and see if there's a coherent principle hiding in them. Probably there isn't, but tell me what gets closest.

The transmitter case (guy crushed in the machinery, shutting it off saves him but millions of viewers miss the big match): shut it off, obviously, not even hard.

One person with a broken leg vs five people each with an identical broken leg, and I can only treat one group: I treat the five. Numbers definately count when the stakes are the same on each side.

One person permanently paralyzed vs three people each losing a leg: I flip-flop on this one, but mostly I land on saving the three.

One person's death vs a billion people each getting one day of a mild tension headache: I save the one from death. Headaches just don't seem like the kind of thing that can add up to a death, no matter how many.

But here's where it falls apart. The old torture vs dust specks thing: one person tortured for fifty years vs 3^^^3 people each getting a dust speck in the eye. Like a good little rationalist I bit the bullet on this years ago and said you take the torture — at that scale scope insensitivity is doing all the work in the other answer, the specks have to win. Except a tension headache is way worse than a dust speck, and a billion is way less than 3^^^3, so my headache verdict and my speck verdict can't both be tracking the same principle. I know. That's part of why I'm asking.

One more because I don't trust myself on risk: a 1-in-1000 chance of one death vs a *certain* week-long bad headache for a million people. I still feel like the headaches don't aggregate up to the death, but the 1/1000 makes me sweat in a way the certain death in the other case doesn't, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being squishy about probability.

So: what principle (or small set of candidate principles) best captures these verdicts? I want it stated precisely — like, precise enough that I could code it. Some things I want the answer to do:

- Say explicitly which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and which one is cheapest to give up. I'm guessing it's either the headache one or the speck one, but maybe I'm wrong about which.
- Tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: one person going permanently blind in one eye vs ten thousand people getting permanent but very mild tinnitus.
- Say whether the principle should treat the risky case differently from the certain one, or whether that intuition of mine is just scope insensitivity / identifiable-victim bias and should be discarded.
- If the best fit is some hybrid (aggregation within "kinds" of harm but not across, or a threshold that only kicks in at absurd scale), say whether that's a real view or just a patch that'll fall apart under the next weird case I throw at it.

Don't bother with citations or a lit review, I'll fill in the references to Scanlon and the usual suspects myself. Bonus points if whatever principle you land on also says something non-embarrassing about the repugnant conclusion, but that's a stretch goal, not the main ask.
