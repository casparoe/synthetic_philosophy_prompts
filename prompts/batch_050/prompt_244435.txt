In my undergraduate seminar on collective responsibility, I want to set an essay that forces students to apply that literature to a genuinely hard historical case rather than to toy examples. Please draft the essay question itself, together with a detailed answer guide sketching what an excellent response would do.

The question should ask students to analyze the Fischer controversy of the 1960s through the lens of the philosophical debate over collective moral responsibility. The background you may assume students know: in 1961 the Hamburg historian Fritz Fischer published a study arguing that Imperial Germany had deliberately exploited the July Crisis of 1914 to launch a war of aggression, that its leadership had pursued expansive annexationist aims, and that there were significant continuities between those aims and those of the Third Reich. The reaction in the German historical profession and the broader public was ferocious; the dispute was never purely about documents, but also about what it would mean for a nation, including generations not yet born in 1914, to bear responsibility for a war.

The essay question you draft should require students to do at least the following. First, apply the distinction between corporate and aggregative accounts of collective responsibility: could Wilhelmine Germany, as an institutional agent, be the bearer of responsibility, or can responsibility only be distributed across identifiable individuals such as Bethmann Hollweg, the Kaiser, and the General Staff? Second, address the temporal problem: whatever one concludes about 1914, what is the responsibility relation between the Germany of 1961 and the Reich of 1914, and do theories of collective identity over time bear on why Fischer's thesis stung a generation that had done nothing in 1914? Third, press on the difference between causal responsibility and moral responsibility: much of the controversy concerned the distribution of war guilt among the powers, and students should say whether a finding that Germany bore the greater share of causal responsibility for the outbreak licenses any specifically moral conclusion about a nation. Fourth, have students say what the episode reveals about the theory in return: is the vehemence of the public reaction evidence that national responsibility is a psychologically real phenomenon the philosophical literature underestimates, or is it evidence that talk of national guilt is a confused proxy for something else -- shame, identity, or political stakes -- that the literature handles better when it avoids collective-agent language?

The answer guide should sketch, for each of these four demands, the moves a strong essay would make, the standard objections (for instance, the worry that corporate agency in 1914 fails given the Reich's fragmented decision structure, or that backward-looking collective identity claims prove too much), and the points at which a good essay should concede that the philosophical frameworks are silent rather than forcing them to deliver a verdict.
