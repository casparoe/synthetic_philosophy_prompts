I'm a second-year grad student and I keep getting into the same argument with a friend who's in med school. She thinks whether something is a disease is basically a biological question that medicine settles, I think values are baked in all the way down, and every paper we throw at each other (biostatistical accounts on her side, normativist and Canguilhem-ish stuff on mine, hybrid views like harmful dysfunction floating in the middle) just restates the standoff. For my qualifying paper I want to force the debate into a cleaner shape, so: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of health and disease would have to meet. Not a survey of the existing theories -- I mostly know those -- but the spec. Which classifications it must get right (e.g. clear cases like diabetes or a broken leg; hard cases like aging, infertility, menopause, deafness, and the historical embarrassments like homosexuality's old diagnostic status that a theory has to be able to explain as errors rather than ratify), which distinctions it can't collapse (disease vs. illness vs. just being unhappy or unusual, being disvalued vs. being dysfunctional, something being abnormal in a population vs. pathological -- tooth decay is nearly universal and still seems like a disease), and which commitments it can't have (it can't make health purely a matter of what the surrounding culture happens to disapprove of, but it also can't pretend to be value-free while smuggling values in through the choice of reference class or what counts as a 'function').

To keep you honest, here are two test cases I made up that I want the desiderata to be able to handle, since the literature's usual suspects are worn smooth: (1) a genetic variant that cuts your lifetime heart disease risk in half but leaves you unable to digest a common protein, so carriers need a restricted diet -- one condition, one person, net effect arguably positive. Is the variant a disease, healthy, both, neither? A decent theory should say something principled here rather than shrug. (2) A slow demographic shift where, two centuries from now, everyone over 60 develops the same progressive joint stiffness, with no exceptions. Has it become normal aging and hence not a disease, or is the whole population now diseased? I suspect this is where statistical-normality-based accounts get into real trouble, but I want the requirement stated so it's clear why.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag which ones you think are genuine constraints any theory must meet versus which are really just one camp's prejudices dressed up as common sense (I have my suspicions about 'the theory must match clinical usage' as a requirement, since clinicians disagree and have been wrong before). The part I care most about: say whether some subset of these desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- if no theory can meet all of them, that's a result in itself and basically becomes the thesis of my paper. At the end, you can briefly run the main contenders (biostatistical naturalism, pure normativism, harmful-dysfunction hybrid, maybe a capability-flavored view) against the list, but keep that short -- the list is the deliverable, the scorecard is just to show the list has teeth. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most theories.
