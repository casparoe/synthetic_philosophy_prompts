You are a philosopher of social science with expertise in the debates over historical explanation; think carefully, and consider sketching several competing diagnoses before settling on the one the evidence best supports.

The debate over whether history admits of laws has been going since at least Popper and Hempel, and it seems to have frozen rather than resolved. On one side: the covering-law tradition, which holds that a historical event is genuinely explained only if it can be subsumed under some general regularity. On the other: critics who point out that the proposed laws are either trivial ("power corrupts"), riddled with exceptions ("revolutions follow prolonged immiseration"), or true only by definition, and who conclude that historical explanation must work through narratives, mechanisms, or particular causal chains instead. My impression is that both sides now talk past each other: covering-law proponents quietly retreat to "rough generalizations" or "mechanisms at a lower level," while critics keep attacking a strict nomological position almost nobody still defends. Meanwhile, comparative historical sociologists and agent-based modelers seem to just get on with producing something law-adjacent without settling the philosophical question.

Diagnose this impasse rather than adjudicating it. Is the disagreement substantive, or does it hinge on a verbal question about what counts as a "law" (strictly universal? ceteris paribus? scale-dependent? probabilistic)? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under one heading -- one about what makes an explanation satisfying, the other about what makes it reliable? And given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress -- a clarified taxonomy of regularities, a formal result about prediction in open systems, a changed research question -- or should we expect the debate to remain stalled, and why?
