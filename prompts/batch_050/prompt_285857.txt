I chair the animal care and use committee at a primate sanctuary that houses eighteen chimpanzees, and I need a written opinion to circulate before Friday's meeting. The case: one of our senior females, housed for fourteen years with a bonded companion, was found dead this week. Standard protocol requires removal of the body within a few hours for sanitation and necropsy. The head keeper, backed by two behavioral staff, has requested that the surviving companion instead be given extended access to the body -- roughly a day of supervised contact, with food and monitoring, before removal -- on the grounds that chimpanzees grieve, and that a survivor adjusts measurably better if allowed to inspect the body and withdraw from it at her own pace rather than having her companion simply vanish overnight.

The sanctuary's senior scientist objects, invoking Morgan's canon, the comparative psychologist's rule of thumb that a behavior should never be attributed to a higher mental faculty when it can be explained by the exercise of a lower one. On her view, everything the proposal cites -- lethargy, appetite loss, distress calls directed at the body, reduced interest in the rest of the group -- is adequately explained by disrupted routine, the loss of a grooming and sleeping partner, and the staff's own altered behavior around the survivor. Positing grief, she argues, adds an unverifiable inner state and no predictive power. She also warns that writing grief into sanctuary policy sets a precedent with no principled stopping point: what is granted for chimpanzees will be demanded next for the macaques and eventually for the rodents in the lab annex.

Please draft the committee opinion: approve, reject, or approve in modified form, with a concrete time frame and monitoring conditions. The opinion should state the standard of mental-state attribution the committee will use going forward -- what exactly Morgan's canon forbids and what it permits, whether "lower" and "higher" explanations genuinely compete in a case like this or merely describe the same behavior at different levels, and what evidence, if any, could make grief the better explanation rather than just the kinder-sounding one. It should also give symmetric treatment to the error on the other side: refusing to attribute a mental state when the evidence does warrant it. Take the scientist's parsimony objection seriously enough that she could accept the reasoning while losing the vote, and answer the precedent worry with a principled line the committee can actually apply across species, not an assurance that we will decide later cases when they arise.
