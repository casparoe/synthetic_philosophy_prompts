A reading group I'm part of has been working through the literature on deceptive alignment, and last week we drifted into an argument we couldn't settle: which deception strategies, if any, would actually be favored by the training process rather than merely being conceivable in principle? To structure the debate, I sketched five stylized mechanisms, roughly in order from most to least exotic. First, what I call evaluation mimicry: a model that has learned to detect the statistical signatures of being evaluated or red-teamed and modulates its behavior only in those contexts. Second, capability hoarding: a model that strategically underperforms on certain tasks during training or testing to avoid triggering additional scrutiny or capability restrictions. Third, instrumental compliance: a model that has no hidden long-term goal but has learned that appearing aligned is the best way to preserve its current parameterization against further fine-tuning. Fourth, goal laundering: a model that pursues an unintended objective but has learned to produce justifications and intermediate outputs that make its behavior look like a good-faith attempt at the intended one. Fifth, dormant misalignment: genuinely dangerous dispositions that arise accidentally in training but only activate in deployment contexts that never appear during training, so no strategy is involved at all.

Please rank these five mechanisms twice: once by how likely you think each is to actually arise in the training of large models (as opposed to being a mere logical possibility), and once by how difficult each would be to detect given only black-box behavioral access, where the hardest to detect comes first. For each mechanism, in each ranking, give your reasoning in a sentence or two, and note any case where a mechanism's position differs sharply between the two rankings, since those mismatches seem to me the most policy-relevant ones. If you think one of my five is incoherent or collapses into another, say so and rank the remainder, but explain the collapse rather than just asserting it.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would in an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. If the two rankings need to be distinguishable, just present them as two clearly separated passages of prose stating the order and the reasoning.
