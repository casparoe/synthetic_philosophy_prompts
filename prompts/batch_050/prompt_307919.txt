I'm a political science PhD student in a formal theory program, currently in a directed reading on early modern political thought, and my advisor and I keep circling the same disagreement. She reads the following passage from Pascal's Pensées (Section V, "Justice and the Reason of Effects," no. 294 in W. F. Trotter's translation) as an essentially cynical piece of advice to rulers; I read it as a proto-game-theoretic analysis of how political order actually persists:

The art of opposition and of revolution is to unsettle established customs, sounding them even to their source, to point out their want of authority and justice. We must, it is said, get back to the natural and fundamental laws of the State, which an unjust custom has abolished. It is a game certain to result in the loss of all; nothing will be just on the balance. Yet people readily lend their ear to such arguments. They shake off the yoke as soon as they recognise it; and the great profit by their ruin and by that of these curious investigators of accepted customs. But from a contrary mistake men sometimes think they can justly do everything which is not without an example. That is why the wisest of legislators said that it was necessary to deceive men for their own good... We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end.

I'd like you to adjudicate between us by doing something more systematic than picking a side. Restate the argument of the passage in the vocabulary I use every day — strategies, equilibria, coordination problems and focal points, common knowledge, path dependence, endogenous beliefs, enforcement mechanisms — as faithfully as you can. Take the passage's main claims one at a time: that tracing custom back to its origins dissolves its authority; that appeals to natural law as a basis for revolution are a game in which everyone loses; that law introduced without reason can become reasonable over time; and that order requires concealing origins rather than justifying them. For each, say what it becomes once translated, and whether the translation is clean or whether you had to smuggle in an assumption (for instance, treating "authority" as mere stability, or treating citizens as knowing the game they are playing) to make it fit.

Then give me the residue report: what in Pascal's passage has no expression in game-theoretic vocabulary at all? I have two candidates in mind but want your independent judgment. First, Pascal appears to hold that there is such a thing as genuine justice — elsewhere in the same fragment he says that natural laws doubtless exist but that corrupted human reason has corrupted all — so can an equilibrium-selection story even state that claim, or does translating it silently replace justice with whatever happens to be stable? Second, Pascal's conclusion is an instruction, conceal the origin, addressed to someone with a responsibility to preserve order; does the normative, first-personal character of that advice survive translation into a descriptive model, or does it shrink into just another parameter of the equilibrium? Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: does it reveal a defect of the game-theoretic framework — things a political theory ought to be able to say but can't — or does it show that Pascal's talk of true justice was doing less argumentative work than it appeared?
