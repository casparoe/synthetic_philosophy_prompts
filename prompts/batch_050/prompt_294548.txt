I recently joined the budget committee of the small coastal town where I live, and we've run into a disagreement that I suspect is really a philosophical dispute wearing a practical disguise. The town has a modest surplus, and two options are on the table. The first is to replace our aging seawall now, which would protect the current harbor district for a few decades. The second, pushed by a financial advisor we hired, is to invest the surplus in a long-term fund; by the time the seawall genuinely has to be rebuilt in roughly forty years, the fund would have grown enough to pay for a far better wall plus badly needed upgrades to the water system. The catch is that most of us making the decision, and most of the people who currently use the harbor, will be old or gone by then. The main beneficiaries would be people who are children now or haven't moved here yet.

The debate keeps circling one sticking point. One colleague argues that benefits arriving forty years from now are simply worth less than benefits today — in his words, later is just worse, and he says he'd feel this way even if the future project were guaranteed to happen and guaranteed to be bigger. Another colleague agrees we should be cautious, but insists the only respectable reasons are practical ones: the fund might underperform, the town might shrink, the wall might not be needed if the harbor silts up, and people in the future may well be wealthier than we are anyway. She says that once you've accounted for all of that honestly, valuing our comfort over future residents' safety purely because we happen to be alive earlier is arbitrary — like refusing to help someone because they live across town rather than next door, except with time instead of space.

My question is: who's got the better of this argument, and how should we actually run the numbers? Should we be applying some percentage by which future benefits automatically count for less, and if so, is there any principled way to choose that percentage, or are we just laundering a gut preference for ourselves into math? I'd also appreciate guidance on how to disentangle the legitimate worry that the future is uncertain from the seemingly illegitimate instinct that the future just matters less, because in our meetings the two keep getting conflated and I don't know how to separate them cleanly.
