A colleague of mine has been reading a popular book on animal behavior and the mind, and he summarized its most memorable chapter for me as follows. A digger wasp of the genus Sphex seems, at first glance, to behave with real intelligence and foresight: it paralyzes a cricket with a precisely placed sting, drags the cricket to a burrow it has prepared, leaves the prey at the entrance while it goes inside to inspect the chamber, and then comes back out and pulls the cricket in. But experimenters discovered that if the cricket is moved a short distance while the wasp is inside, the wasp will drag it back to the entrance and repeat the entire inspection routine -- and it will do this over and over, as many times as the experimenters care to move the cricket, never once varying the sequence, noticing the repetition, or simply pulling the cricket straight in. What looked like purposive, intelligent behavior turns out to be a rigid, mechanical subroutine. Philosophers have coined the term 'sphexishness' for this kind of built-in inflexibility, and the lesson my colleague drew -- which he says is the lesson the book itself draws -- is that the wasp case proves that behavior which feels from the inside like free, reasoned choice can in fact be entirely mechanical; and since human behavior is likewise produced by a biological machine, our sense of our own freedom is sphexishness on a grander scale, an illusion we cannot catch ourselves in because our routines are too complex to be reset by a moved cricket.

My colleague found this argument compelling, and I have to admit I did too, but I also suspect that something in it moves too fast, and I do not know enough to say exactly where. So: which parts of his summary are accurate, and which are overstatements, conflations, or misrepresentations? In particular, I would like to know whether the wasp literature really supports the picture of an endlessly repeating, perfectly inflexible routine, or whether the famous anecdote has been polished in the retelling; whether 'sphexishness' as philosophers actually use the term is a claim about determinism, about inflexibility, or about something else, and why the difference matters; what a careful proponent of the wasp example would say it does and does not establish about human beings; and whether my colleague's conclusion -- that our felt freedom is an illusion of the same kind as the wasp's -- survives once the summary is corrected, or whether it depends precisely on the parts that are wrong.
