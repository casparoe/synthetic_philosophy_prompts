I am a professor of philosophy, but before entering the academy I spent a dozen years working as a catastrophe modeller and underwriter for a reinsurance firm, and I still find myself thinking in that vocabulary: tail risk, model uncertainty, credibility, loadings, insurability limits, counterparty exposure. I am currently preparing a graduate seminar on the problem known in the decision-theory literature as Pascal's mugging — the puzzle in which an agent is confronted with a threat or promise of such astronomical stakes that even a minuscule probability of its being genuine appears, on the expected-utility calculus, to swamp every mundane consideration, so that maximising expected value seems to counsel handing over one's wallet, or worse. I would like to present the puzzle to my students in the idiom I know best from my former career, but I do not trust myself to perform the translation honestly, so I am asking you to do it for me.

Please restate the mugging problem, together with the standard lines of response to it — bounded utility functions, discounting or disregarding sufficiently tiny probabilities, refusing to grant the offered probability estimate in the first place — in the vocabulary of insurance and reinsurance practice: underwriting criteria, catastrophe loading, model risk, the distinction between risks one can price and risks one must simply decline to write, moral hazard, and adversarial counterparties who can observe your pricing rule before deciding how large a claim to make. Then give me the residue report: which elements of the puzzle map cleanly onto actuarial notions, which you had to distort to fit, and which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in the underwriting vocabulary at all. In particular, I want your view on what the residue signifies. Does the industry's settled practice of declining risks it cannot independently model capture, in operational form, what the philosophers are gesturing at when they resist the mugger — or does the residue expose a genuine gap in how insurance thinks about tail risk, given that the industry has its own version of being mugged whenever a counterparty sizes a claim after reading the models? Alternatively, if the untranslatable remainder turns out to rest on an idealisation no working risk professional would accept — taking at face value a probability asserted by an interested party — please say so, and tell me whether the puzzle is really about rational choice at all, or about when one is entitled to trust a stated probability.

Please write your answer in British English.
