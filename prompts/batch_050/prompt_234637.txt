I chair rapid expert-assessment panels for a public-health agency. Several times a month, typically under time pressure, we convene five to nine specialists to produce a collective judgment -- for example, the likely severity of an emerging pathogen, or whether a surveillance anomaly warrants escalation. I sign off on the result, and it feeds directly into decisions I do not control. Two recurring problems have convinced me we are systematically mishandling what I understand economists call informational cascades. First, our panels deliberate sequentially, senior people speak first, and I suspect later agreement is often deference rather than evidence. Second, when deciding myself, I rely on the observed behavior of other agencies -- a sequence of foreign counterparts adopting a recommendation, say -- without any principled way of telling when that sequence is genuinely informative and when it is a cascade in which each actor copied the last.

Please turn the theory of rational herding and information cascades into a procedure I can actually run, in two parts: (1) an elicitation protocol for my panels that preserves the evidentiary value of each expert's private information -- the steps in order, what to do when we lack the time for the full protocol, and which shortcuts are safe versus fatal; and (2) a decision rule for how much weight to give an observed sequence of others' actions when I cannot see their underlying evidence -- the questions to ask about the sequence, in order, and the answers that should make me discount it to nearly zero even when every participant is competent and honest. In translating the theory, be explicit about which distinctions must survive operationalization (I assume the public-versus-private-information asymmetry is one) and which can be safely dropped for a non-specialist user.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order: "The core distinction" (roughly 150 words; what separates a genuinely informative aggregation from a cascade); "The elicitation protocol" (roughly 400 words; numbered steps, with the failure each step prevents); "Reading other people's sequences" (roughly 350 words; the diagnostic questions in order, with stop conditions); "Worked applications" (roughly 400 words; apply both parts to three cases: a panel where the senior epidemiologist announces her conclusion before anyone else speaks; a chain of five foreign agencies successively adopting the same travel-screening recommendation within a fortnight; and a case where the procedure gives an answer I will dislike, such as instructing me to discard a near-unanimous panel consensus); "Limits and failure modes" (roughly 200 words); and finally "Settled versus contested" (roughly 150 words), in which you distinguish what is established in this literature from what remains genuinely disputed -- for instance, whether real-world agents herd as mechanically as the models predict -- without presenting a contested claim as the consensus view.
