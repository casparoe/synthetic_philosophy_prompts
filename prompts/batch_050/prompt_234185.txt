I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've ended up in a lunch-break argument at work that I can't settle, so I'm outsourcing it.

A colleague who's into the history of political thought was telling me about Jeremy Bentham's essay Anarchical Fallacies, his attack on the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. The bit that stuck: Bentham's view (I'm paraphrasing my collegue paraphrasing him, so treat this loosely) that rights are only real when an actual legal system creates and backs them — real rights come from real laws, and so-called natural rights that supposedly exist prior to any government are imaginary, which is where his famous sneer about nonsense upon stilts comes from.

That made immediate sense to me because it's basically how our codebase works. A comment that says "this function must return results sorted" does nothing unless there's a test or a linter enforcing it; without the enforcement it's decoration. So I tried to run Bentham's argument properly, and it seems to prove something absurd, and I can't find where it slips. Numbered so we can argue about specific steps:

(1) A claim about what someone is entitled to or owed is only meaningful if there's some actual mechanism behind it that gives it teeth — a law, a court, a contract, something. (This is the Bentham premise, as I understand it.)

(2) The overwhelming majority of ordinary moral talk has no such mechanism. "You ought to apologise to her", "he had a duty to tell me", "you promised" — no court enforces most promises, nobody fines you for ingratitude, there's no runtime for any of it.

(3) So by Bentham's own logic, everyday duty-and-obligation talk is imaginary in exactly the same way natural rights are — the same kind of nonsense, just dressed up less grandly.

(4) But Bentham himself plainly thought some things really are right and wrong — his whole career was spent insisting laws ought to be reformed to increase happiness, and that ought can't itself be backed by a law on pain of infinite regress (which law enforces the law that says laws ought to maximise happiness?).

(5) So either Bentham's own life's work is nonsense too, or the move from (1) to (3) is broken somewhere.

I refuse to believe (3). My colleague bites the bullet and says fine, ordinary moral talk is decoration too, just decoration we happen to like — and honestly he seems happy living there, which annoys me. But I also can't crisply say which step goes. I suspect (1) isn't quite what Bentham meant, but I can't articulate what the real criterion is if it isn't enforcement. If I had to choose, I'd rather keep the dig at natural rights and lose (1) than give up on ordinary oughts, but I want to know what I'm paying for that.

Which step do you reject, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it actually cost? Please answer in British English.
