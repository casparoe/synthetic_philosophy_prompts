I'd like you to take on the role of a careful historian of ideas with expertise in early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought. Please think through the dispute below step by step before delivering a verdict.

At my reading group we came across the following passage from George Berkeley's The Querist (first published in 1735), a book on money and the Irish economy written entirely as a series of questions:

"Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

Two members got into a drawn-out argument over it:

(1) Daniel said the passage shows Berkeley believed money is pure make-believe. If trade could carry on even with all the gold and silver gone, then money's value must rest entirely on shared confidence, so nothing real is lost when a government creates money out of thin air.

(2) Martha objected that he was reading one question in isolation. Berkeley's nearby queries, she said, tie money's usefulness to whether it helps "stirreth up industry," so the point isn't that money is fiction but that money is a tool whose job is to keep people working and trading -- which means creating money only helps if it actually gets people working.

(3) Daniel replied that this is a distinction without a difference: if mere tokens with no metal behind them can do the job, then the metal never mattered, and the state may replace gold with paper as freely as it likes -- which is just his point restated.

(4) Martha said that's exactly where he overreaches. The phrase about "the denominations being retained" means Berkeley is imagining the units and records staying stable, not the state printing at will; and a writer who elsewhere asks whether idle people deserve to be "expelled like drones" out of the state plainly thinks wealth comes from labor, not from the printing press.

Neither would budge, and I stayed out of it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about each specific claim, identify what -- if anything -- both parties are overlooking (for instance, does it matter that Berkeley is asking a question rather than asserting something?), and give your verdict on whose overall reading of the passage is closer to the mark.
