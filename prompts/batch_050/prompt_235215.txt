A friend and I argued late into the night over a piece of reasoning he defends and I insist must prove too much. His argument: (1) A being has moral status just in case it is sentient, that is, capable of experiences that feel some way to it. (2) Sentience is not all-or-nothing: it arises by degrees across phylogeny and development, and nature draws no sharp line between the sentient and the insentient. (3) One tiny step in neural organization — a handful of neurons more or less — cannot make the difference between having moral standing and having none at all. (4) A series of such tiny steps runs continuously from a human being down through a chicken, a honeybee, a nematode, and ultimately to a simple feedback device like a thermostat. (5) Since moral status tracks sentience, moral status must come in degrees too; so thermostats possess some small positive degree of it, and unplugging one wrongs it a little, just less than swatting a fly wrongs the fly. He cheerfully accepts the conclusion and now hesitates before adjusting his heating. I accept (1) through (4) more readily than I'd like, but the conclusion strikes me as a reductio of something — I just can't say of what. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: identify the step or premise where the argument goes wrong, explain why that step seemed plausible, and state what each of the main alternatives to rejecting it would cost me, given that I am more attached to (1) than to anything else in the chain.
