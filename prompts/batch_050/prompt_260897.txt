Is there a name for this? I keep bumping into a specific wrinkle in the old "we can't foresee the long-run consequences of our actions" problem. The standard reply I see to that worry is: fine, the far future is a black box, but near-term effects are predictable enough, so just do the locally good thing -- feed the hungry person, fund the vaccine, don't lie -- and treat the unknowable downstream stuff as a wash that cancels out across options.

Here's what's been bugging me. For a lot of these supposedly safe near-term goods, whether they were actually good seems to depend on the downstream stuff too. Saving a child's life is the canonical near-term win, but whether it was good, full stop, plausibly depends on what that child and their kids and their kids' kids end up doing over the next century or two -- which is exactly the black box we just agreed to ignore. So the near-term effect isn't a self-contained nugget of value with a chaotic tail attached; its value is partly *made of* the tail. Same with educating someone, or introducing two people who go on to start a company, or keeping a hospital open in a struggling town. The predictable fragment borrows most of its moral weight from the unpredictable remainder.

Which means the move "ignore the far future, just optimize the near future" doesn't merely set some value aside as unknown. It seems to dissolve the very near-term category it was supposed to protect, because almost nothing near-term carries its goodness entirely on its own shoulders. Maybe some things do? Preventing an agonizing death might be good no matter what happens later, though I've seen even that disputed.

So: does this specific failure mode have an established name? It's clearly a cousin of cluelessness, but the versions of cluelessness I've come across are about not being able to compare whole actions because their total consequences are unknown. Mine is narrower and weirder: it's about the value of the *foreseeable fragment* of a consequence inheriting the unknowability of the unforeseeable rest, so that "be a near-termist" isn't a stable fallback position rather than a place the problem just reappears. If there's no name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so it doesn't collapse into plain cluelessness on one side or ordinary moral uncertainty on the other? I'm also interested in the boundary cases: is there any prinicpled way to sort near-term goods into self-contained vs. hostage-to-the-future, or is the hostage structure basically everywhere once you look closely?
