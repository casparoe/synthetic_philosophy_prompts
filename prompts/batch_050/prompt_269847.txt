I run a monthly "decision-making club" at the software company where I work as an engineer — about fourteen attendees, all developers or data scientists, meeting for 75 minutes in a conference room. None of us has any formal philosophy background, and most have never heard of the Allais paradox or the independence axiom. For next month's session I want to run an activity on it, and I'd like you to design that activity in full.

The goal is for participants to feel the paradox in their own choices rather than learn about it as a curiosity. Specifically, I want them to (1) make a series of real choices with small but genuine stakes (company swag, snack budget, points toward an end-of-quarter prize — something concrete, not imaginary millions), (2) later endorse, in the abstract, the principle their earlier choices violated, and (3) only then have the collision pointed out to them. The paradox should emerge from what they did, not from anything I say up front, and they shouldn't hear the terms "Allais paradox" or "independence axiom" until the debrief.

Please include:

- The exact sequence of choice pairs, with the probabilities implemented physically (tickets in a bag, dice, a spinner — something they can watch resolve, not just numbers on a slide), since I think tangible randomization will hit harder than described lotteries.
- Rules that keep choices private and binding, so no one anchors on a neighbor or treats the whole thing as hypothetical.
- A timeline with rough minute marks, including the abstract-principle phase and the reveal.
- A debrief script: the questions I ask, in order, that walk them from "here's what you chose" to "here's the axiom you said you accept" to "so which one goes?" — plus how to map specific moments in the session onto the parts of the paradox.
- A plan for the failure mode I most worry about with this crowd: these are numerate people, and several will simply compute expected values and pick the higher-EV option every time, thereby "passing" the test and ruining the demonstration for themselves and possibly gloating about it. Tell me how to design around this — whether that's adjusting payoffs so EV-maximizing still produces the reversal, recruiting the EV-maximizers into the debrief, or something else.
- Two or three other things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: for instance, whether small-stakes choices can really tell us anything about how people would choose at life-changing stakes, or whether observed reversals in the room actually demonstrate a violation of independence versus something else (risk attitudes, transaction costs, distrust of the facilitator). If there are places where the debrief would overclaim what the activity proved, flag them.

Open your response with a single sentence stating the one design decision the whole activity stands or falls on, then give the full plan — no preamble, no restating of my request. Write in American English.
