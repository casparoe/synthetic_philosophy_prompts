A close friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about populism, and although we parted on good terms, the argument ended in a stalemate I would like help resolving. Her view is that populism is a single, coherent phenomenon with an identifiable essence, and that Jean-Jacques Rousseau diagnosed it in advance. At the height of the argument she read me this passage from Book II, Chapter I of The Social Contract, in G. D. H. Cole's translation:

"I hold then that Sovereignty, being nothing less than the exercise of the general will, can never be alienated, and that the Sovereign, who is no less than a collective being, cannot be represented except by himself: the power indeed may be transmitted, but not the will."

Her gloss was that populism is the political stance that takes this sentence seriously: the people is a unity, its will can be known, and whatever interposes itself between that will and political power — parties, parliaments, courts, the press — is an obstacle to be denounced or dismantled.

Over the course of the weekend I put a series of cases to her, and her verdicts were unhesitating and, as far as I could tell, consistent:

1. Hugo Chávez's Venezuela: populist — in her words, the paradigm case.
2. William Jennings Bryan and the People's Party of the 1890s: populist.
3. A system in which the major economic decisions are delegated to independent central bankers and technocratic commissions insulated from elections: not populist.
4. A legislator who openly describes politics as a continuing compromise among many legitimate, competing interests, none of which speaks for the whole of society: not populist.
5. A Leninist vanguard party that claims to understand the true interests of the working class better than workers themselves currently do: not populist — she called it the opposite error, contempt for the people's actual will in the name of its real one.

Where we stalled was over two further cases:

- A leaderless, decentralized citizens' movement campaigning to transfer most lawmaking from parliament to binding national referenda, on the grounds that parties, legislatures, and the media are "filters" that distort what the people actually wills. The movement pointedly refuses to anoint leaders or spokespeople, on the theory that any spokesperson would be one more filter.
- A charismatic president who declares that he personally embodies the will of the people, but who also insists that the constitution and an independent judiciary express the people's "deeper will," and who says the courts should overrule him — and be obeyed when they do — whenever he conflicts with them.

Here is what I would like from you. First, state at least three distinct rules that would each generate all five of her recorded verdicts, formulated precisely enough to yield predictions about new cases rather than merely redescribe the old ones. Second, say what each rule predicts for each of the two cases we stalled on, and mark exactly where the rules diverge. Third, give me your best prediction of how she would actually rule on each case, with your reasons — her invocation of the Rousseau passage should count as evidence here, since she plainly takes herself to be his faithful reader. Fourth, describe one further hypothetical case whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate among the rules you take most seriously, and say what each possible outcome would show. Finally, tell me whether the passage she read aloud actually supports the use she makes of it: on its own terms, does it vindicate the referendum movement, the self-described embodiment president, both, or neither? I have a suspicion that her proof-text cuts against her, but we were both too tired to settle it.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, not counting the quoted passage.
