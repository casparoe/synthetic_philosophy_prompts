I have been rereading Moby-Dick and would like a reading that places the novel in conversation with the dispute between Donald Davidson and conceptual-metaphor theory over what metaphor is. Davidson, in his paper "What Metaphors Mean," denies that a metaphor carries any second, metaphorical meaning: the words mean only what they literally mean, and a metaphor's power lies in what it makes us notice — it prompts us to see one thing as another rather than expressing a content that could be paraphrased without remainder. Lakoff and Johnson, in Metaphors We Live By, hold roughly the opposite: metaphor is not decoration or provocation but a systematic mapping between conceptual domains, and much of our abstract thought is constituted by such mappings, so a metaphor can bear real structure and content that paraphrase loses. Please take both positions seriously and state them as carefully as the reading requires.

Now the novel. Ahab's quarterdeck speech treats all visible things as masks or walls that a thinking agent must strike through to reach what stands behind them — a conception of the whale as inherently figurative. Starbuck answers that the whale is a dumb brute acting on blind instinct, and that organizing a life around vengeance against it is madness verging on blasphemy: the whale means nothing beyond itself. Around these two poles the novel stages scenes where interpretation multiplies without settling: the meditation on the whiteness of the whale, in which Ishmael finds whiteness legible both as purity and as terror, and the episode of the doubloon nailed to the mast, which each crew member reads differently, each reading disclosing the reader rather than the coin, down to Pip's unmoored version.

Please read the novel through this dispute, in both directions. Is Ahab a standing counterexample to Davidson — a man whose thought about the whale really is constituted by a metaphorical mapping (the whale as enemy, as agent, as veil) that he cannot think outside, which would be the Lakoff-and-Johnson point? Or is Starbuck the novel's Davidsonian, and does the catastrophe vindicate him — suggesting the whale had only a literal meaning all along, and that Ahab's figurative reading was never a content but a compulsion? Attend to where the novel resists each mapping: the doubloon episode seems to side with Davidson in showing that there is no correct paraphrase of a symbol, yet the Pequod's entire shared life runs on metaphors (the ship as world, the voyage as fate) that are plainly doing cognitive work, which favors the conceptual-metaphor view. Close by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that the papers do not — for instance, whether it recasts the dispute as one not only about semantics but about ways of living, with the two positions dramatized as two fates. Do not worry about exact quotations; work from the scenes as you remember them and paraphrase freely.
