I run a small reading group in practical ethics, and last month we spent three sessions on how one ought to act when one is genuinely torn between moral views -- not torn about the facts, but about the morality itself. I found that I have fairly confident gut verdicts on particular cases, but I cannot articulate what principle, if any, is generating them, and I suspect my verdicts may not be jointly coherent. I am not asking which moral theory is correct; I am asking what rule for deciding under moral disagreement-with-oneself best systematizes the following judgments.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) In a trolley-style scenario, I am about 70% confident in a broadly consequentialist outlook on which diverting the trolley is clearly right, and 30% in a view on which pulling the lever is straightforwardly murder; my verdict is that I must not pull, and the minority view wins outright rather than getting 30% of a say. (2) I assign maybe 15% credence to an absolutist prohibition on lying, yet I tell a harmless comforting lie to a distraught friend without a flicker of hesitation -- the minority view gets essentially no say here, which seems inconsistent with (1) unless the gravity of what is at stake on each side does the work. (3) When I am evenly split between a view on which I owe as much to distant strangers as to my own community and a view on which local obligations dominate, splitting my donation between two charities feels exactly right, whereas "half-pulling" the lever in (1) is obviously nonsense -- so sometimes I hedge between theories and sometimes hedging is not even an option. (4) I give a small but nonzero credence, a few percent, to the view that animal suffering matters enormously, and my response is to keep eating meat but buy the expensive certified-humane kind; this partial accommodation feels principled rather than merely weak-willed, though I cannot say why a few percent warrants that much concession while 15% in (2) warrants none. (5) When I am uncertain whether an act (say, donating a kidney to a stranger) is obligatory or merely admirable-but-optional, I treat the uncertainty as settling the matter in favor of not doing it; but when I am uncertain whether an act is permitted or forbidden, I treat the uncertainty as counting against it. Asymmetry between "required vs. optional" and "permitted vs. forbidden" seems to be doing real work for me.

What I would like is a principle -- or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, with an honest account of which verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon. Two obstacles I keep hitting may be relevant. First, the hedging cases (3) and (4) seem to demand weighing each view's verdict by my confidence in it, but the views do not deliver their verdicts in a common currency: one tells me how much welfare is at stake, another tells me that something is simply forbidden, and I do not know how to put a number of lives on one scale and a prohibition on the other without smuggling in an answer. Second, (1) and (4) seem to pull toward opposite rules: in (1) I let a minority view veto, in (4) I let it only trim. Please say also what your best principle predicts for a case I have not listed: I am 60/40 between two views that agree an act is permissible but disagree about whether it is very good or merely neutral -- should the 40% view temper how much I praise or pursue it, or does the agreement on permissibility make the disagreement inert?
