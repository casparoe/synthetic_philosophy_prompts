You are a moral philosopher who specializes in normative and moral uncertainty, writing the specification document for a working group. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming a wide set of candidate requirements before pruning to the ones that genuinely discriminate among theories.

I am designing a meta-level decision procedure for a philanthropic funder that must allocate a fixed budget across interventions whose worth is vouched for by rival moral theories — say, one intervention that looks best under any aggregative welfarism, one that looks best under deontological or rights-based views, and one that looks best under views that give special weight to existing relationships. Before I evaluate any particular theory of acting under moral uncertainty, I want the full list of desiderata such a theory must meet to count as adequate for this purpose.

Write the requirements list. For each desideratum, state the case or consideration that motivates it — not just its name. At minimum, your list should take a position on all of the following, but do not treat this enumeration as exhaustive:

1. Which stylized cases it must get right: at minimum, a case where two theories agree on the ordinal ranking of options but differ sharply in how much is at stake; a case where a theory commanding low credence promises enormous value; and a case where all options are impermissible according to at least one theory with non-negligible credence.
2. Which distinctions it cannot collapse: acting under moral uncertainty versus acting under ordinary empirical uncertainty; being uncertain between theories versus being uncertain within a theory; and the difference between a theory that is merely permissive of an option and one that requires it.
3. Which structural commitments it must declare: how (or whether) it compares the stakes that rival theories assign to the same outcome, and how it responds when the meta-level theory itself is one of the things the agent is uncertain about.

Then do three further things:

- Flag any pair of desiderata on your list that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension precisely. If your list contains a near-impossibility result in embryo — some triad of intuitive demands that cannot all hold — that is the part I most want to see developed.
- Score the main contenders briefly against the list: maximizing expected choiceworthiness, acting on whichever first-order theory you have most credence in, a parliamentary or bargaining model in which theories negotiate over the decision, and a consensus rule that restricts the funder to options no plausible theory forbids. Keep this short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard.
- Identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most contenders, and state explicitly what would change your mind about it: which argument or counterexample would move you to drop or weaken that requirement, and where in your list your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Close with a one-sentence summary of the specification, and then, as the very last line, give your answer to the single most discriminating requirement in machine-readable form: \answer{...}.
