So I've gone down the sortition rabbit hole and I've basically talked myself into the view that choosing officials by lottery is *more* democratic than elections, not less. The thing that really got me was this passage in Aristotle's Politics (Book IV, where he's talking about how to mix constitutions):

"I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one"

So on Aristotle's telling, elections aren't the democratic element at all -- they're the *oligarchic* one, because they systematically favor the notable, the rich, the already-famous, and lot is what actually equalizes political power. That seems to me to line up with everything we now know about campaign finance, incumbency advantage, the professionalization of politics, the fact that elected bodies look nothing like the population descriptively, etc. My position: if you actually care about democracy as rule by the people rather than rule by the people who are good at winning popularity contests, some form of lottocracy or allotted chamber beats elections, and the Athenians understood this better than we do.

Now I want to stress-test this before I embarass myself defending it at a workshop. What would count against it? Not the lazy "but random people are stupid" objection -- give me the strongest version of that (does it survive the fact that allotted bodies deliberate with expert input, and that elected officials are mostly generalists anyway?) and also the objections that aren't about competence at all. A few things I specifically want mapped: whether Aristotle's own framing cuts against me, since he's describing conventional opinion ("it is thought") rather than endorsing it, and his preferred constitution *mixes* lot and election rather than going full lot; whether elections have some democratic function that lot structurally can't replicate (accountability? the sanction of removal? the way campaigns force elites to at least pretend to care what people think? consent and authorization?); whether there's a difference between lottery being democratic in the sense of equal *chance* of office and democracy meaning something like *responsive* rule, such that my view equivocates on "democratic"; and what empirical results from citizens' assemblies, deliberative polls etc. would actually damage the view if they went the other way.

Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed lottocrat should concede it as counting against the view or can reasonably absorb it by design tweaks (term limits, stratified sampling, hybrid chambers, sortition for some offices and election for others). And end with an honest verdict: is the view actually exposed to evidence, or have its defenders (including me) built in enough escape hatches that nothing could count against it?
