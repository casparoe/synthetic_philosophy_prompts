After a recent health scare, I mentioned to a colleague that I'd been brooding about mortality, and he tried to reassure me by summarizing what he called the classic Epicurean argument. His version: since good and bad consist in experience, and the dead experience nothing, death cannot be bad for the person who dies; and since we look back on the eternity before our birth without any distress, consistency requires us to view the eternity after our death the same way. Therefore, he said, a person who dies at thirty, painlessly, is no worse off than one who dies at eighty, and fearing death is simply a confusion about what could harm us.

I'm sympathetic to the conclusion but suspicious of the summary. Which parts would a careful defender of the Epicurean position actually endorse? Are the two arguments he runs together really the same argument? And does his final inference — that an early death costs its victim nothing — follow from the parts that are accurate, or has something gone wrong in the last step?
