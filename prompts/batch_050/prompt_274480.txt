I am a PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about coercion and the criminal law, and before I commit to a position on blackmail I would like an audit of the commitments I currently hold, to see whether they are jointly tenable. I will state them as plainly as I can, with the motivations I would give for each.

(1) The paradox of blackmail is a genuine philosophical problem, not an artifact of confused framing. It is, on its face, puzzling that the conditional conjunction of two acts each of which is legal -- remaining silent in exchange for money, and disclosing truthful, lawfully obtained information -- should itself be a crime, and I am not satisfied by the deflationary response that the law may simply criminalize conjunctions of permissible acts without owing anyone an explanation.

(2) Where blackmail is wrong, it is wrong because it is coercive, and coercion in general is best analyzed by a moralized baseline account: a proposal is a threat rather than an offer when it proposes to make the recipient worse off relative to the baseline of what she is morally entitled to expect, and the threat/offer distinction so drawn is what does the normative work in distinguishing wrongful coercion from hard but permissible bargaining.

(3) People have no general legal duty to keep one another's secrets. Absent contract, fiduciary relationship, or some special undertaking, disclosing true, lawfully acquired information is within one's rights, and a free-speech regime worth the name protects the gossip and the journalist along with everyone else.

(4) Informational blackmail is nonetheless rightly criminalized, and not only in the cases where the threatener seeks some further wrongful act. The standard case -- pay me or I publish the photographs -- deserves criminal prohibition full stop.

(5) The criminal verdict should not turn on facts about what the threatener would otherwise have done. Whether the blackmailer is a habitual gossip who would certainly have published, or a discreet person who would never have breathed a word, should be irrelevant to whether the demand constitutes blackmail; the wrong is in the conditional demand itself, not in the counterfactual biography of the person making it.

(6) If the secret-holder herself initiates the transaction -- approaches someone who knows her secret and offers money for silence -- the resulting agreement is unobjectionable and should be both legal and enforceable.

My questions: Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? I suspect, for instance, that (1) and (4) look uneasy together, and that (3) and (4) do as well, but I would like to know whether those apparent tensions survive scrutiny. Conversely, I would like to know whether there is a real incompatibility I have not noticed -- particularly among (2), (4), and (5), where I have an uneasy feeling that my general theory of coercion may not be able to deliver the verdicts on cases that I want. For each genuine tension, please state exactly which commitments generate it, how severe it is, and what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the chapter. Finally, if you had to identify the single revision that restores the most consistency at the least philosophical price, which would it be?
