I teach philosophy of medicine at a mid-size university, and every October the student wellness office plasters the campus with flyers for Mental Health Awareness Week. This year's slogan, on roughly a hundred posters, is "Depression is an illness like any other -- you wouldn't blame someone for having diabetes." There's also a line urging students to "get checked" at the counseling center the way they'd get a physical.

Meanwhile, in my seminar we just spent two weeks on Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness. His line, if my students have him right, is that illness is something a body has -- a lesion, a chemical abnormality, something a pathologist could in principle point at -- so calling despair or hearing voices an illness is a metaphor that's gotten lose, and what psychiatrists treat are really what he called problems in living dressed up as medicine. One of my students, who volunteers with the wellness office, is annoyed: she thinks Szasz is peddling exactly the stigma the posters are trying to kill. Another student is convinced Szasz is obviously right and the posters are basically advertising for the counseling center. I want to to settle this in a way that isn't just "Szasz vs. public health."

So: audit the analogy. Treat "mental illness is an illness like any other" as an argument rather than a slogan, and tell me how far it holds.

Structure the answer like this:

1. Correspondence table (~200 words). What plays the role of the lesion, the symptoms, the diagnosis, the treatment, the cure? Flag the cells where you already have to strain to find a counterpart.

2. Where it holds and where it snaps (~300 words). Which features of bodily illness genuinely carry over to, say, major depression or schizophrenia, and which don't. Get into the weeds on the thing my students fought about: bodily diseases get called diseases because of what's found in the tissue, not because of how the patient behaves, whereas most psychiatric diagnoses rest on conduct and self-report. Does that asymmetry break the analogy, or does it just mean psychiatry is where the rest of medicine was before microscopes?

3. What the poster quietly imports (~150 words). When someone says "like any other illness," what extra cargo arrives unannounced? I'm thinking of: blamelessness, the idea that treatment is obviously the right response, that treatment means professionals, and (my own bugbear, following Szasz) that once it's an illness, coercing treatment starts to look defensible. Which of these does the analogy actually earn?

4. The syphilis test (~150 words). The standard objection my Szasz-sympathetic student couldn't answer: general paralysis of the insane used to be a psychiatric condition, then it turned out to be late-stage syphilis, and now it belongs to neurology. Doesn't that show today's mental illnesses are tomorrow's brain diseases? Give me Szasz's best comeback (my reading: that's the point -- once you find the lesion it stops being psychiatry) and then say whether the comeback actually works.

5. The verdict (~150 words). The poster was doing a job: getting distressed students to seek help without shame. Does that job survive the audit? My own suspicion is the destigmatization never needed the analogy in the first place, but argue it either way.

6. A better analogy, if one exists (~100 words). If "like any other illness" can't carry the load, what comparison would do the poster's work more honestly -- or is the honest move to drop analogies entirely?

Keep it in plain prose I can hand to undergrads (the table can be an actual table). And don't referee this to a draw -- I want to know which of my two students is closer to right.
