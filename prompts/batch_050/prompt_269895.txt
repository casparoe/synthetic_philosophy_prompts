I help run a philosophy circle that meets once a month at our public library. None of us has any academic background in the subject — we're a retired nurse, a bus driver, two software developers, a vicar, and so on — but we take it seriously, and every January we hold a light-hearted written "examination" on the previous year's big topic, which I have volunteered to set this time.

The topic is one I have been turning over privately for a while: could I have come into being from a different sperm and egg? My own thinking runs in circles, which is why I want to test the group on it. Part of me thinks the answer is obviously no. If my parents had conceived a month earlier or a month later, or — the case that first set me off — if a fertility clinic had selected a different embryo from the same laboratory batch, the child born would have been a sibling of mine, not me. But another part of me rebels at this. It seems absurd that whether I exist at all should hang on which microscopic cell happened to win a race, and I notice that the same sperm and egg can produce two people (identical twins), so the exact origins cannot be the whole story anyway. Someone at the library told me there is a position in the literature called origin essentialism, and I gather it vindicates the first of these intuitions, but I do not want an exam that rewards people merely for stating it.

Please write the following for me:

1. One essay question, answerable in 75 minutes by intelligent non-specialists, that forces a candidate to grapple with both sides of my circle rather than recite a position. In particular, it should make them engage with (a) the embryo-selection case, which tempts the strict view that one's exact biological origins are fixed, and (b) the twin case together with the question of how much an origin could vary — a different egg? the same egg a day later? — before the resulting person stops being you. The question should not use technical vocabulary, though candidates should be free to introduce their own.

2. A model answer of roughly 600 words, written in plain language, that takes a definite position while giving the strongest opposing consideration its due. It should explain, without jargon, why the twin case does not refute the strict view even though it shows origins alone do not settle identity, and it should confront honestly whether the strict view implies that a difference of a single cell decides whether you ever existed.

3. A marking scheme built around five things a good answer must do, with a stated number of marks for each (totalling 25) and, for each, one sentence on how candidates most commonly bungle it. Finish with a short paragraph explaining what separates a merely competent answer from an excellent one.
