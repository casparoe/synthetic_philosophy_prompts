So here's my situation: I did my undergrad at this business school twenty years ago, then weirdly ended up becoming a philosophy professor, and now the dean (who was my classmate) has asked me to give the "reflections" address at this year's graduation. Ten minutes, cap and gown, very warm room, families present. The catch is that I want to build the whole speech around Aristotle's distinction in the Politics between oikonomia (household management, getting what you need for living well, which has a natural limit) and chrematistike (money-making for its own sake, which has no limit because there's always more money), plus his thing about usury being especially unnatural since money breeding money is not what money is for. I genuinely think this is the single best frame for talking to business graduates about what they're about to do with their lives, and Aristotle is more even-handed about commerce than the cartoon version -- he's not against trade or wealth as such, he's against acquisition with no telos.

The problem is calibration. Half the graduating class has signed at banks, consulting firms, and PE shops. I don't want to give a smug humanities-professor speech that tells them their jobs are inherently corrupt -- that would be both unkind and, I think, a misreading of Aristotle, since on his view the problem isn't the activity but the absence of a limit, i.e. whether the money-making serves a life or becomes the life. But I also don't want to water it down into a graduation-speech fortune cookie ("Aristotle teaches us to follow our dreams!") because that betrays the text AND bores the room. The Aristotle material needs to do real work: it should structure the speech's argument, determine what I'm allowed to say and what would be cheating, and ideally give the graduates an actual diagnostic question they can ask themselves at their five-year reunion, not just a vibe.

So: please write the ten-minute address (roughly 1200-1400 words), warm but not sappy, funny in at most two places, addressed to students and their parents, with the oikonomia/chrematistike distinction explained in plain language (you can use an example, e.g. a shoe -- made to be worn vs. stockpiled to be flipped). One more request: Aristotle scholarship has contested bits here -- e.g., whether he condemns all retail trade or only unlimited acquisition, whether usury is the paradigm case or an extreme case, and I've seen people fight about how literally to take the "natural/unnatural" language. Please flag in a short note after the speech which of my framings above are broadly accepted and which lean on a contested reading, so I don't present something as settled Aristotle when it's actually one side of a scholarly argument. Don't water the speech down because of this -- just tell me where I'm standing on disputed ground.
