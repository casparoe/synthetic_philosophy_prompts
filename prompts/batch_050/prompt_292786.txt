Philosophers have this habit of arguing by inviting you to imagine things, and then treating the outcome of your imagining as evidence. Someone argues about personal identity by describing a teleporter that scans your body, destroys it, and rebuilds you on Mars out of fresh atoms — and the whole debate turns on whether you find that coherently imaginable and who you reckon steps out at the far end. Someone else argues about consciousness by asking you to conceive of a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that has no inner experience whatsoever, and treats your success or failure at picturing it as a datum about what consciousness could possibly be. Nobody in these debates claims any of it actually happens. All the work is done by what you can and can't get your head around.

What does that practice presuppose about the link between conceivability and possibility? Spell out the strongest version of the link the practice seems committed to (something like: if a scenario is genuinely conceivable, then it's possible in some robust sense, not just "possible for all I know"), and say which rival views it quietly rules out — e.g. the view that conceivability only ever tracks what's consistent with our concepts, or only tracks our ignorance. Also, is the practice even internally consistent? Philosophers who happily accept that we can coherently imagine water turning out not to be H2O while insisting that scenario is impossible will still lean on zombie-style conceivability arguments in the next seminar. Can both moves be made in good faith, or does someone have to give something up?

Here's a wrinkle I'd like addressed properly rather than in a footnote. Conceivability seems to be detail-sensitive. A teleporter is trivially easy to imagine at the level of a two-sentence summary; it gets harder the more physics and physiology you insist on filling in, and at some level of detail maybe nobody can tell whether they're imagining a genuine possibility or just a gap-riddled sketch they've filled in charitably, the way readers of fiction accept time travel because the genre tells them to. If that's right, then what the practice calls "conceiving" might often just be "holding together a partial description without noticing the cracks". Does that observation undermine the practice, or only force a more careful version of it — and what would the more careful version have to look like?

Finally: tell me what would actually have to change in philosophical practice if we swapped the presupposed view for the deflationary one, where conceivability only ever informs us about our own concepts. Presumably the zombie argument would still show something — but something much weaker. Distinguish, throughout, between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with, and flag any point where it's genuinely underdetermined and several theories of modality would licence it equally well.
