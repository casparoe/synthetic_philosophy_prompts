My sister has been using one of those griefbot services since our uncle died last year -- it was trained on his texts and emails, and honestly it freaked me out at first, but she says it helps. It's gotten me thinking about when this kind of thing is okay and when it isn't, and I've noticed my gut verdicts don't line up under any single rule I can state. Here they are:

(1) My sister's case: uncle never consented to it, but he was a private person whose data his next of kin legally controls, and only my sister talks to the bot. Feels fine, like reading someone's letters. (2) A startup scraping a recently dead actor's interviews and selling chatbot access to fans, against the family's wishes. Feels clearly wrong, a kind of theft. (3) A grieving mother keeps a bot of her deceased nine-year-old, who obviously never could have consented to anything. Feels sad and maybe unhealthy, but not *wrong* the way (2) is -- nobody's being wronged, I think. (4) A man builds a bot of his dead ex-wife who cut off contact with him years before she died; he uses it to have the reconciliations she refused him in life. Nobody's harmed, she's beyond harm, but it feels like a violation anyway. (5) A philosophy department builds a bot of Kant from his published works and letters for students to argue with. Feels completely fine, even charming. (6) The bot of the uncle spontaneously tells my sister "I forgive you" about a fight they had, something he never said in life. She's comforted; I'm bothered, though I can't fully say why, since it isn't lying to her exactly -- she knows it's a bot.

The problem: if I say consent of the deceased is the whole story, then (3) should be as bad as (2), and it isn't. If I say it's all about harms to the living and the dead can't be wronged, then (4) should be fine, and it really doesn't feel fine, and (2) needs some other explanation than "the actor is wronged." Time since death seems to do work between (2) and (5) but that feels more like a patch than a principle. Is there one principle -- or two clean ones -- that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I give up, and what does the best principle say about a case I haven't listed, like a court letting a griefbot of the deceased "testify" about their wishes in an inheritance dispute?
