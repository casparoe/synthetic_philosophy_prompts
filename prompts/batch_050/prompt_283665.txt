I am trying to settle on what a theory of analogy even owes us before I commit to reading more of the literature, and I'd like the specification itself rather than another theory. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of analogical thinking -- what it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect -- with the phenomenon motivating each requirement, a judgment on which are genuine constraints versus the signature commitments of particular research programs, and an explicit note where you suspect two desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied.

The phenomena I have in mind that the list should cover, at minimum:

- The triviality problem: any two things share infinitely many properties (both the atom and the solar system contain the letter "o"; both weigh less than the galaxy), so a theory must say what makes some mappings apt and others empty without smuggling in the answer it is supposed to produce.
- Asymmetry: "the surgeon is like a butcher" is an insult while "the butcher is like a surgeon" is a compliment; a theory must explain why analogy has a direction and what flips with it.
- The retrieval puzzle: remindings are often driven by surface features (a story about a tumor recalls a story about a fortress), yet the analogies we *judge* best are systematic and structural. A theory must explain how the same operation can be shallow on the way in and deep on the way out -- or deny that it is one operation, which is itself a position that needs defending.
- Slippage: in "if your house were my house, I'd sell it," the role-fillers slide into place effortlessly. A theory should explain how concepts stretch to accommodate a mapping without collapsing into meaning whatever the mapping needs.
- Children's and experts' analogies, one-shot analogical learning, the role of analogy in legal precedent, and why analogies persuade in argument even though everyone agrees they are not deductions -- including what separates a legitimate analogical argument from a fallacious one, if anything principled does.
- The strong claim associated with Hofstadter's work (in *Surfaces and Essences* and elsewhere) that categorization and analogy are a single cognitive mechanism differing only in degree: whether a theory must take a stand on this, and what evidence would bear on it.

For each desideratum, state the case for it in a sentence or two and mark it as non-negotiable or program-specific. Then tell me which single requirement you think eliminates the most existing accounts, and give a brief scorecard -- a few lines each, no more -- for structure-mapping theory, the pragmatic/goals-based account associated with Holyoak, and the fluid-concepts view associated with Hofstadter, against the full list. The scorecard is secondary; the list and its internal tensions are what I actually want.
