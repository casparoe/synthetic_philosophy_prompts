PRIME NUMBERS AND WAX: DOES MATHEMATICS EXPLAIN, OR ONLY DESCRIBE?

[1] The indispensability argument for mathematical realism says we should believe in mathematical objects because mathematics is indispensable to our best science, and we should be as committed to what our science posits as we are to electrons. A stronger, more recent version narrows the claim: if mathematics plays a genuinely explanatory role in science — if some physical fact is explained by a mathematical fact — then realism follows almost immediately, since we surely believe in whatever explains. Two cases are always wheeled out at this point: the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas and the hexagonal cells of honeybees. They are almost always treated as exhibits of the same kind. This essay argues they are not. In one case, the fact to be explained is itself partly mathematical; in the other, only the explaining apparatus is. I will argue that only the first kind of case could possibly support the indispensability argument — and that it faces a problem of its own.

[2] North American periodical cicadas spend either thirteen or seventeen years underground before emerging en masse to mate and die. Both numbers are prime, and the leading explanation appeals to primality itself: a prime-period cicada rarely coincides with the shorter cycles of periodic predators or parasites, and broods on different prime cycles almost never hybridize — a thirteen-year brood and a seventeen-year brood emerge together only once every 221 years. The fact doing the explanatory work here is a theorem of arithmetic: a prime shares no factors with any smaller number, so prime periods minimize dangerous intersections. Notice what evolution was selecting for. It was not selecting for thirteen-ness or seventeen-ness; it was selecting for primality, a property with no uniform physical description. Any purely biological restatement of "this cycle shares no divisors with likely predator cycles" would be an unwieldy, possibly infinite disjunction of conditions. The generalization exists only at the level of number theory. That, it seems to me, is what a distinctively mathematical explanation would have to look like.

[3] Now the bees. A hexagonal tiling partitions the plane into equal cells with the least total perimeter — a fact conjectured in antiquity but proved only around the turn of the millennium. The standard moral: natural selection found the wax-optimal design two thousand years before mathematicians could confirm it, so the optimization theorem explains the bees' architecture. But look at what is being explained: a shape, not a mathematical fact. And the explanatory chain passes through a contingent premise — that what selection optimized was wax per unit volume — which could simply be false. Experimental work in the last decade or so suggests bees deposit roughly circular cells and that the hexagons emerge on their own, as the warmed wax flows under surface tension. If that is right, no optimization was ever performed: a physical process settles into the optimum the way a soap film settles into a minimal surface, and the theorem describes the outcome rather than explaining why it came about. (There is an old story that a mathematician's computed cell angles diverged slightly from the measured ones until the discrepancy was traced to an error in his logarithm tables — the bees were right and the tables wrong. It is too good to check, and probably too good to be true, but it gets told for a reason: we want the bees to have been doing mathematics.)

[4] Here is a test to sharpen the contrast. Hold the physics fixed and vary the mathematics: if the explanation collapses when the theorem does, the mathematics was carrying explanatory weight. Cicadas pass. In a world where composite periods, not primes, minimized predator overlap, selection would have produced composite periods; the biology alone does not fix the answer, the arithmetic does. Honeycombs fail. In a world where some other tiling minimized perimeter, surface tension would still have produced whatever minimizes surface energy — the physics drives the outcome and the mathematics tracks it. I admit the counterfactual is strange, since it is hard to know what a world with different arithmetic even is. I think the strangeness is a feature: explanations that survive it bottom out in necessity rather than in the contingent layout of matter.

[5] Even so, the cicada case has a soft underbelly. Perhaps the mathematical character of the explanandum is an artifact of framing. We described the life cycle as "a period," measured it in years, and asked why it is prime; but evolution acted on insects, not integers, and a biologist who described the phenomenon in other terms might never encounter a number at all. The reply from paragraph [2] — that the explanatory generalization is multiply realizable and so lives at the mathematical level — may show only that mathematics is indispensable for stating the explanation compactly, not for the explaining itself. Whether that weaker conclusion is still enough to platonize, I am not sure. I suspect the honest upshot is this: of the two famous cases, the bees teach us about physics and the cicadas teach us about arithmetic, and only the cicadas give the realist anything to work with — less than she hoped, but more than nothing.
