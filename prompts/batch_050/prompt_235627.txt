A textbook I'm reading explains the course of the French Revolution on the model of a fever: an incubation period of accumulating grievances, a crisis in which the old regime collapses, a delirium of radical excess (the Terror), and a convalescence in which the patient returns, chastened, to something like normal politics. The author draws two conclusions from this picture. First, the Terror was not really anyone's doing -- it was a stage the revolution had to pass through, so asking whether Robespierre or the Committees could have chosen otherwise is like asking whether a patient could have skipped the fever's peak. Second, the Thermidorian reaction and the eventual settling-down were simply the fever breaking, not achievements or failures attributable to anyone. Audit this analogy. Give the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to what, and which correspondences are doing genuine explanatory work rather than decorative work? Where does the analogy break down -- in particular, does a revolution contain anything that plays the role of a body striving to return to health, and does a fever contain anything like agents deliberating and reacting to one another's moves? What does the picture quietly import about inevitability and responsibility, and who exactly is the patient -- the nation, the revolutionaries, the old order? Does the author's exculpatory conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Finally, if the fever model fails to explain why the revolution consumed its own leaders, is there a better comparison, or should the inevitability claim instead be argued directly in terms of class interests, ideological dynamics, or sheer contingency?
