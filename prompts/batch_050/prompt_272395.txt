My mother recently asked me to leave my job and move home to help manage her care, and she framed the request as something I owe her for the years she spent raising me. In working out what to say, I reread Jane English's essay "What Do Grown Children Owe Their Parents?," where she argues that parental sacrifices do not create debts to be repaid; instead, grown children owe their parents what friends owe one another, so that the obligation rises and falls with the continuing relationship of mutual concern rather than with the ledger of past favors. I want to figure out which model of filial obligation I actually accept, rather than which one I find comforting this week, so please build me a decision tree that sorts a person into a named position. Required leaves: the debt-or-gratitude model English attacks; English's friendship model; the view that what matters is the special good of the parent-child relationship itself, whether or not it resembles friendship; a Confucian-style view on which the parental role grounds duties of respect and care regardless of affection; and an impartialist view on which children have no special duties to parents at all. Phrase every branch node as a concrete case a respondent can answer without knowing the labels — for instance, whether a devoted but emotionally cold parent is owed more than an estranged friend would be, or whether a parent's sacrifice that the child never asked for still generates a claim. End with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions, flag any two positions your tree cannot distinguish by cases alone, and for each leaf state in one sentence the most awkward commitment it forces on someone in my situation.
