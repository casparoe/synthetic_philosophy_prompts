I got into a long argument with my friend last night (she's finishing a sociology PhD, I'm the skeptic) about whether "generations" are a real thing or just astrology for marketing departments. She kept citing stuff like how the Boomers share a genuine outlook, how Gen Z is measurably different on risk and dating and work, and I kept saying: you could draw the birth-year boundaries anywhere, slice the data differently, and get a different story; "cohort effects" are just age effects and period effects wearing a trenchcoat. We both realized we were stuck because we don't actually know what a *theory* of social generations would have to do to count as good -- as in, what would it take for "Millennial" to pick out something real in the way that, say, "the class of 2008 graduates" clearly does, or doesn't.

So before I concede anything, write me the desiderata. What must any adequate theory of social generations deliver? Cover things like: which canonical cases it has to get right (post-war boom babies, the '68ers, "digital natives" -- does it have to validate the familiar ones, or is a theory allowed to conclude most of them are fake?), which distinctions it can't collapse (generation vs cohort vs age group vs "generation unit" in something like Mannheim's sense, age vs period vs cohort effects), what it has to say about the boundary problem (why do cohorts supposedly start in one year and not the adjacent one? what mechanism could make a birth-year cutoff non-arbitrary?), whether it has to generalize beyond the rich West or whether it's fine if generations only exist where there's mass media and shared national events, and what it predicts about people at the edges or outside the "units." For each desideratum give the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag which ones are genuine requirements vs. which are just the hobbyhorses of one camp (demographers want X, cultural critics want Y, marketers want Z).

Then -- and this is the part I actually want to win the argument with -- tell me which pairs of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement knocks out the most of what's currently published under the label "generational research" (the Pew-style birth-year bands, the shared-formative-events accounts, the cohort-replacement-as-social-change stuff). Finally, don't sit on the fence: given the spec you've built, take a definite position on whether generation talk can be salvaged as anything more than a convenient fiction, and tell me which side of the argument with my friend I should actually be on. I'd rather have a committed answer with reasons than a tidy menu of options.
