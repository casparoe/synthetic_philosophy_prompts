So I'm reading this pop philosophy book on skepticism (won't name it, its by a pretty well known science writer) and it has a chapter on Robert Nozick's theory of knowledge. Here's how it summarizes the tracking theory:

"Nozick's idea is that knowledge is a matter of your beliefs tracking the truth. You know something when (1) it's true, (2) you believe it, and (3) if it weren't true, you wouldn't believe it -- your belief is sensitive to the facts. So I know I have hands because if I didn't have hands, I wouldn't believe I did. The payoff is that this lets Nozick explain why skepticism feels so compelling without giving in to it: you do know you're not a brain in a vat, because if you were one, you'd notice -- the vat-world would look different from this one, so your belief 'I'm not a BIV' tracks the truth. The cost is that Nozick has to deny that knowledge is closed under known implication: since he thinks deduction is an unreliable way of extending belief (long chains of reasoning are where mistakes creep in), you can know P, know that P implies Q, and yet fail to know Q. Most epistemologists think this is too high a price, which is why the tracking theory is now mostly of historical interest."

The bit about denying closure rings true from what I've heard, and the book's overall take is that it's a clever theory that didn't survive. But something about the brain-in-a-vat part felt off to me -- isn't the whole point of the vat scenario that you *couldn't* notice? Can you go through this summary and tell me what's a fair statement of Nozick's view, what's a subtle misrepresentation, and what's just backwards? In particular: what would Nozick himself say about whether you know you're not a brain in a vat, and is the book's explanation of *why* he denies closure the one he actually gives? And after the corrections, does the book's verdict -- clever but dead -- still follow?
