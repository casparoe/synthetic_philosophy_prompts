After a faculty workshop on sentencing reform last month, two colleagues of mine — Marta, a criminologist, and Daniel, a political economist — got into an argument about cannabis policy that continued over email for a week and has now landed in my inbox with a joint request that I referee it. I've tried and failed, so I'm handing the job to you. I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of law who has also spent serious time on the empirical drug-policy literature: someone who cares about conceptual distinctions but won't let either side win on vibes. Reason slowly and carefully; where an empirical premise matters and you can't verify it, say so and reason about it conditionally rather than pretending to certainty.

Here is the exchange as accurately as I can reconstruct it. Marta opened with the standard abolitionist case: prohibition has failed on its own terms, since usage rates barely respond to enforcement intensity, while criminal records destroy employment prospects and the illegal market finances violence; Portugal's decriminalization paired with treatment referrals shows you can remove criminal penalties without use exploding; so the conclusion is to legalize, regulate, and tax. Daniel agreed that prohibition failed and that criminal records are an unforced catastrophe, but insisted on a distinction Marta was glossing over: decriminalizing possession is not the same as creating a legal commercial market. A for-profit cannabis industry, he argued, inherits the alcohol industry's structure — a small minority of heavy users accounts for the bulk of consumption, so the industry's revenue depends on creating and retaining dependent users, and every public-health measure that works (higher prices, lower potency, marketing restrictions) is a direct threat to its business model. Portugal gets the decarceration benefits without building that engine; so the right policy is decriminalization without commercialization. Marta replied that decriminalization leaves supply illegal, which means the black market, its violence, its untested and adulterated products, and zero tax revenue all persist — a half-measure that keeps the worst features of both regimes — and that if commercialization is the worry, the answer is regulation: potency caps, advertising bans, taxes proportional to THC content. Daniel countered that this ignores political economy: once a legal industry exists it funds lobbying against precisely those measures, and alcohol is the standing proof that regulated legal industries defeat their regulators over time; the only reliable moment to prevent the industry is before it exists. Marta's final move was to say that this concedes her core point — his objection is not to legalization but to the for-profit form, and non-commercial models exist (state monopolies, buyer's cooperatives, home cultivation, the Uruguayan approach), so his position collapses into "legalize, but not commercially," which is a species of her view. Daniel's final move was that the concession actually proves his: "legalization" as it is actually campaigned for and enacted means the commercial model, because tax revenue and business opportunity are what make it politically viable, and the non-commercial variants poll terribly and rarely survive contact with legislatures; so the live policy choice really is decriminalization versus commercial legalization, and he was right about that choice from the start.

Please adjudicate the dispute. Structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order: **What the dispute is actually about** (one short paragraph identifying where the real disagreement lies, as opposed to where the two think it lies); **Move-by-move adjudication** (work through the six moves in the order given above — Marta's opening, Daniel's decriminalization/commercialization distinction, Marta's half-measure reply, Daniel's regulatory-capture reply, Marta's non-commercial-models move, Daniel's political-viability move — devoting roughly 100–150 words to each, with a verdict on who is right about that specific point and why); **What both sides are missing** (a short bullet list of considerations neither raised that bear on the verdict, empirical or conceptual); and **Overall verdict** (about 150 words saying who is more right on balance, and under what empirical conditions the verdict would flip). Do not adjudicate by averaging — if one side wins decisively, say so.
