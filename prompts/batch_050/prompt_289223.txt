I'm in a grad seminar on the philosophy of humour (yes it exists, yes everyone makes that joke) and last week we put the four big theories on the table -- superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation. Someone asked the prof whether any of this theorizing actually helps you understand why things are funny, and he shrugged and quoted E.B. White at us: "Humor can be dissected, as a frog can, but the thing dies in the process and the innards are discouraging to any but the pure scientific mind." His point was basically that our whole seminar is frog-killing: a joke is a living thing, analysis kills it, and whatever you learn from the corpse tells you little about the living animal. It got a laugh, obviously, but it's been bugging me because he was using it as an argument against the entire enterprise, and I can't tell how much weight the analogy can actually carry.

So audit it for me. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the frog, the scalpel, the death, the innards, the squeamish onlookers -- and then where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion (theorizing about humour is pointless at best, destructive at worst) from the ones that don't. A few things I keep circling: does the line conflate explaining a joke to someone in the moment with building a general theory of humour, and are those actually both threatened in the same way? A frog dies because dissection destroys the very structures keeping it alive -- is a joke "alive" in any analogous sense, or does that just assume what's in dispute? And crucially: which theory of humour would itself predict that understanding kills amusement? It seems like incongruity theory might (analysis resolves the incongruity, so the collapse of expectation never happens) and maybe relief theory (a pressure once discharged stays discharged?), while superiority theory might predict jokes survive dissection fine, even improve with savouring. Benign violation I'm genuinely unsure about -- does explaining remove the violation, the benignity, both, neither? If the frog line only lands given one particular theory, then using it to dismiss theorizing across the board seems like it begs the question. Also the counterexamples: wine critics enjoy wine more after training, magicians still delight in tricks they understand cold, comedy writers dissect jokes for a living and seem to laugh plenty. Does that sink the analogy, or just show the "pure scientific mind" clause was quietly doing all the work?

End with a verdict: how far does the frog analogy hold, what if anything can it legitimately be used to argue, and if there's a better analogy for what theory does to humour -- grammar to fluent speech? musicology to music? -- tell me what it is and what conclusion it would license instead.
