So this happened at a rationality meetup last month and it's been bugging me because I genuinely can't figure out who won. Two regulars, call them Alex and Beth, got into a long fight about moral luck, and I was just sitting there eating pizza. Here's the exchange as best I remember it, reconstructed from the group chat after. (1) Alex started with the classic setup: two drivers, both texting, both equally careless, one happens to hit a kid and the other doesn't. The only difference between them is luck, so they deserve exactly equal blame -- and the fact that one goes to prison for years while the other gets a traffic ticket is just society being arbitrary. (2) Beth said even if the blameworthiness is equal, blame and punishment practices were never purely about desert -- they also serve deterrence, reparations, giving victims some kind of resolution. Plus there's an epistemic asymmetry: actually hitting someone is evidence about your negligence in a way that not hitting anyone isn't, and we can't run everyone's counterfactuals. (3) Alex replied that the epistemic point proves way too much -- in his example we have identical evidence about both drivers (we know both were texting), the harm adds zero information about the negligence, so what Beth is really defending is giving victims an emotional outlet, i.e. justice-flavored vengeance. And if it's really about deterrence, deterrence of what? You can't deter bad luck. (4) Beth then said something I found more interesting: causing harm changes what you did, not just what you risked. Duties are partly owed to particular people -- in the world where the kid gets hit, there's now someone the driver wronged, someone he's on the hook to, and that obligation literally couldn't exist in the other world because there's no victim to owe it to. And Alex's egalitarianism is useless anyway: "equalize up" (punish every texter like a killer) is insane, and "equalize down" lets actual killers off with a ticket. (5) Alex's last move: the "you owe the victim" thing smuggles in the conclusion. Sure, reparations track outcomes -- obviously you can't compensate a person who doesn't exist. But that's a point about restitution, not about blame, and Beth keeps sliding between them. And if punishment is really just a social tool, fine, but then let's stop moralizing about desert at all, which Beth definitely doesn't want.

I'm curious what people here make of this, because I've read the Nagel/Williams-adjacent stuff and I still keep flip-flopping. Please go through the five moves one at a time: for each, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or secretly two claims stapled together where one is right and one isn't, and why. Then give an overall verdict -- who was more right in the actual exchange as it happened, not who'd win if they were smarter. And lastly: is there some consideration or distinction both of them completely missed that would've dissolved part of the fight? I have a sneaking suspicion the blame-vs-liability conflation is doing a lot of work on both sides but I can't quite nail it down.
