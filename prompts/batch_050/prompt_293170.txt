Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on expected value fanaticism. The two parties are, on one side, a Fanatic who holds that rational choice must follow expected value even when that means preferring a minuscule probability of an astronomically good outcome to a certainty of a merely very good one, and who regards the awkward verdicts this generates (Pascal-style muggings, paying any bounded sum for a St. Petersburg gamble, sacrificing the near-certain for the vanishingly improbable) as bullets worth biting; and, on the other side, an Anti-fanatic who holds that some such verdicts are reductios of the expected-value rule and that rationality must permit — at least sometimes — discounting, truncating, or ignoring sufficiently small probabilities of extreme stakes. Neither party is a strawman: the Fanatic concedes the verdicts are counterintuitive, and the Anti-fanatic concedes that every proposed fix (bounded utility functions, risk-weighted expected utility, lexically thresholded probabilities, abandoning continuity or completeness) carries real theoretical costs.

The document they produce should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. These should include: which standard axioms, accepted jointly, suffice to force fanaticism; agreement that certain quick dismissals fail (for instance, that simply capping utility at some bound merely relocates the problem rather than solving it, or that appealing to the fact that nobody ever faces such cases does not touch the normative question); agreement on which cases in the literature are genuinely hard for whom; and agreement about what the dispute is not about.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state a residual disagreement in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own position. Likely candidates include: whether fanatic verdicts in single-shot cases are acceptable bullets or counterexamples; whether the source of the problem is unbounded value, the aggregation of many tiny probabilities into decisive weight, or the expected-value rule itself; and whether fanaticism should be fixed in the axiology (what outcomes are worth) or in the decision theory (how to respond to outcomes of known worth).

3. A cruxes section. For each crux: (a) a neutrally stated sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; (b) a concrete case or argument both parties agree in advance bears on it — for example, a sequence of choice problems in which probabilities shrink and stakes grow in tandem until one side's verdict becomes indefensible to them, a case where the expectation is undefined rather than merely extreme (in the manner of the Pasadena game), and a long-run argument from repeated independent trials and whether it legitimately transfers to the one-shot case; and (c) each side's prediction, recorded before the analysis, of what examining that case will show.

4. A final short section in which the parties state whether any part of their dispute is verbal — for instance, whether they disagree about what rationality requires or only about which theorems follow from which axiom sets — and say which part, and why.

The writing should be precise enough that both parties could sign it without reservation, and neither side's position should be stated more persuasively than the other's.
