I'm a high school junior serving a one-year term as the student voting member of my school's Discipline Review Board, a student-faculty committee that hears challenges to school rules and recommends whether the administration should keep, revise, or withdraw them. We try to decide cases consistently with our own past rulings. Our record so far:

1. We struck down an attendance policy announced in September that counted the previous spring's absences against students.
2. We upheld a rule against "disrespectful conduct," reasoning that a student of ordinary caution who avoids anything a reasonable classmate would call disrespectful can steer clear of it.
3. We struck down a dress-code rule that was enforced almost exclusively against transfer students, even though its written text was neutral.
4. We struck down a library rule that existed only in a staff handbook students were never shown.
5. We upheld a rule requiring lab partners to co-sign each other's data logs, even though complying was burdensome, because any student who actually did it could be sure of staying out of trouble.

The new case: the school has begun running all essays through a purchased authorship-verification tool whose criteria are undisclosed — the vendor's contract forbids sharing them, and the administration argues secrecy is needed to prevent gaming. Flagged students receive sanctions unless they can prove authorship, and reviewing teachers defer to the tool in nearly every case. Several students have shown, with drafts and notes, that flagged work was genuinely their own. The underlying rule itself — submit only your own work — is public, prospective, and perfectly clear.

Our civics unit connected this cluster of concerns to rule-of-law ideals (public, prospective, clear, and faithfully administered rules), which you may draw on but don't have to. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate to the board before Thursday's vote: recommend a verdict that is consistent with our record, identify the closest analog among our precedents, and say honestly what principle we'd be adopting under each possible verdict. If the record genuinely underdetermines this case, name the further hypothetical precedent that would reveal which principle we are actually running.
