For my upper-level undergraduate seminar on practical reason, I want a decision tree, usable as a one-page handout, that takes a student from the bland starting claim that reasons can be cited both in explaining and in justifying action to a named position in the debate between internalism and externalism about reasons. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: five to seven branch nodes, each phrased as a question the student can answer without knowing the labels — for instance, whether a consideration counts as a reason for someone only if sound deliberation from their actual motivations could lead them to act on it; whether a desire based on a false belief (wanting to drink the liquid in the glass, believing it to be gin when it is in fact petrol) gives one a reason to drink it; whether someone entirely without scruples still has reason not to betray a friend, and, if so, whether failing to respond to that reason makes them irrational; and whether the motivational set that matters is the agent's actual one or some idealized version, with the further question of what fixes the idealization without smuggling in the very values at issue. The leaves should include at least strict actual-motivation internalism, procedural internalism of the sort associated with Williams (a reason must be reachable by sound deliberation from the agent's subjective motivational set), constitutivist views on which some reasons are internal to agency itself, and straightforward externalism; each leaf should carry one concrete case that forces the choice. Note any unoccupied combination of answers and say briefly whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot reliably separate rather than papering over the gap. Close by stating how confident you are that the tree carves the debate at its joints, and single out the branch you are least confident belongs on the main trunk.
