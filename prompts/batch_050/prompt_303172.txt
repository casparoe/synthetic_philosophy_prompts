I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship in political philosophy and need a proposal of roughly two pages, written in my voice in the first person. Here is my half-formed idea, which I need you to turn into a rigorous document.

The debate over reparations for historical injustice — with slavery and its aftermath as the paradigm case — is usually framed as a contest between backward-looking accounts (reparations as the correction of a completed wrong) and forward-looking ones (reparations as an instrument of present-day equality or reconciliation). My suspicion is that both sides share a presupposition that does far more work than anyone acknowledges: that the injustice in question is finished. I want to investigate a sense in which such an injustice might still be underway — not merely in its effects, as in the familiar "continuing disadvantage" argument, but in the way that extant legal practices (inheritance law, property title, rules of citizenship and standing) mechanically transmit the distributions the injustice produced, so that the wrong is arguably re-enacted each time those rules are routinely applied. If that idea can be made precise, several standard objections to reparations claims — the non-identity problem, the fading of claims over time, the difficulty of identifying liable and entitled parties — may look very different, since all of them trade on treating the wrong as a completed event in the past.

Please proceed as follows. First, adopt the persona of an experienced political philosopher who has served on fellowship selection committees and knows what separates a fundable proposal from an admirable one. Before drafting, think carefully through the hardest objection to the core idea — namely, that any persisting effect of a wrong can be redescribed as a "continuing" wrong, so that my distinction between a wrong's transmission and its mere effects may turn out to be merely verbal. Let your considered answer to that objection shape the framing of the whole proposal, though the document itself should present only the finished case.

Structure the proposal with exactly these sections, in this order:

1. The Question (one short paragraph). State the research question so that it admits of a determinate answer, and distinguish it explicitly from the standard continuing-effects claim so a skeptical reader cannot conflate them.
2. Why the Existing Positions Leave It Open (about half a page). Explain how backward-looking and forward-looking accounts each respond to the non-identity problem and to time-decay objections, and why both responses presuppose rather than defend the claim that the injustice is complete. Refer to positions by description rather than by citation.
3. The Central Conjecture (about a third of a page). State precisely what would have to be true for the "ongoing transmission" picture to succeed: above all, a principled, non-circular criterion separating the repetition of a wrong from the persistence of its effects.
4. Method and Work Plan (about a third of a page). Say what philosophical work will actually be done, month by month: conceptual analysis, construction of test cases, confrontation with legal practices such as limitation periods and laches that embody judgments about when wrongs are "over," and revision of the criterion in light of counterexamples.
5. What a Result Looks Like — and What Failure Looks Like (about a quarter page). Describe what vindication of the conjecture would establish for who owes, who is owed, and when claims lapse; then state honestly what findings would count as the project collapsing (for instance, if the transmission/effects distinction cannot be drawn without smuggling in the conclusion) and what partial results would still be worth publishing in that event.
6. Risks to Scope (two or three sentences). Give a candid assessment of whether the project fits twelve months and what would be cut first if it does not.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by author name; I will supply references myself. Keep the prose plain and declarative, avoid rhetorical throat-clearing, and do not oversell: committees reward proposals that name their own weak points.
