I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and my newsroom has been tying itself in knots over how to handle AI-generated text, so I want help figuring out what we actually believe, philosophically speaking. Here's our practice as it's really written down. One: our style guide forbids saying a chatbot "said," "believes," "claimed," or "wants" anything -- we have to write that it "generated" or "produced" text, on the grounds that the words only mean something once a person reads them, the machine doesn't mean anything by them. Two: the same style guide cheerfully lets us say the model "hallucinated" a source or "made up" a citation, which sounds to me like exactly the inner-state talk rule one banned. Three: when an error slips into a piece drafted with AI help, the correction runs under the magazine's name and the editor gets the blame; nobody dreams of saying the model was responsible. Four: our lawyers say pure AI output can't be copyrighted at all, only the human-edited parts, because copyright needs an author and software can't be one.

Now the thing thats been bugging me. Say someone installs a coin counter at an arcade in Panama City that takes both US quarters and Panamanian quarter-balboas, since the two coins are the same size and weight. Is it a quarter detector or a balboa detector? There seems to be no answer inside the machine -- it depends entirely on what the owner cares about, what the repair manual says, what counts as a mistake versus a success. The machine's aboutness is something we project onto it for our own purposes. But if that's true of the coin counter, why isn't it true of us? When I read an anonymous wire report I extract meaning from the words without knowing or caring what passed through the writer's head; arguably the meaning lives in my uptake, not locked inside the author. A philosopher friend pushes this further and says human meaning is just as projected as the machine's, and the conviction that people have meaning-from-the-inside while artifacts only borrow it is a superstition we cant justify.

So, concretely: what theory of meaning does my newsroom's tangle of rules presuppose? Does it assume a deep line between words that are meant by somebody and words that merely look meant, and if so where exactly does that line run and why? Does rule two contradict rule one, or is there some way to render them consistent? If the presupposed theory really is the superstition my friend describes, what would honest practice look like instead -- bylines for models, corrections addressed to software, copyright for machines, or something subtler? And the question I actually care about: does the practice need any theory at all, or is it compatible with several rival ones, such that we're just dressing up editorial convenience as principle?

Please answer in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
