I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm commissioning a humor column for our magazine's annual philosophy issue. The target this year is P. F. Strawson's account of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility -- specifically his distinction between the participant stance (resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the demands that come with treating someone as a fellow adult) and the objective stance (treating someone as a problem to be managed, treated, or worked around, as we do with small children or the seriously ill).

Write a parody of a corporate HR handbook, roughly 700 words, for a company that has officially abolished blame and adopted the objective attitude toward all employees, all the time. Format it as sections with headers (onboarding, performance review, conflict mediation, promotions, termination), in the chipper, euphemistic register of real HR documents. The comedy should come from getting Strawson right: performance reviews that read like clinical notes, a complaint form that routes every grievance to the wellness team, the awkward discovery that certain everyday courtesies (apologizing, thanking, being offended) presuppose the participant stance and therefore need policy replacements, and the fact that full exemption from reactive attitudes is, as Strawson thought, something we cannot really will ourselves into -- let that leak through somewhere without breaking the deadpan.

Please act as a professional satirist with serious training in moral philosophy. Before writing, briefly brainstorm which Strawsonian points each handbook section will carry, so the piece has a coherent intellectual architecture rather than a series of disconnected gags -- but submit only the handbook itself. The piece should be legible and funny to a general reader while making a specialist nod rather than wince, and it should end in a place that leaves the reader genuinely wondering whether anything essential is lost when resentment is retired.
