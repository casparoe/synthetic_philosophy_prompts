Eight months ago I left a procurement job in Canada to run a donor-funded health-infrastructure program in a country I will leave unnamed. Last month I learned that our local implementing partner — the colleague I rely on most — routinely gives what she calls appreciation gifts to ministry officials: cash envelopes at New Year, school fees for a director's daughter, a contribution toward a clinic generator after an official fast-tracked our permits. When I objected, she was genuinely bewildered. In her community, she said, withholding such gifts would be the corrupt act — a refusal to honour a relationship on which everyone depends. Our donor's compliance manual, meanwhile, treats corruption as any misuse of entrusted authority for private benefit, which would make at least some of her gifts reportable.

Before I decide what to report, what to tolerate, and what to write into our program's own code of conduct, I want to stop leaning on definitions and ask what an adequate account of corruption would have to do in the first place. Write me the desiderata: the requirements any theory of corruption must meet to be usable for this kind of judgment, with the case or consideration motivating each one. The list should cover, at minimum, the cases a theory must classify correctly (quid-pro-quo bribery; post-hoc gratitude payments; speed money for services one is already owed; nepotism; perfectly legal lobbying and revolving-door appointments; gifts given to an institution rather than to a person; arrangements where the benefit flows to a third party or an entire village rather than to the official); the distinctions it may not collapse (illegal versus corrupt, individual versus institutional, intent versus function, the corrupt act versus the corrupt agent); and the empirical facts it must accommodate (that gift exchange is constitutive of trust in many societies, and that anti-corruption crackdowns have sometimes merely raised bribe prices or destroyed informal accountability without replacing it).

Then tell me which desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable — I have in mind especially the tension among a standard that travels across cultures, one that is sensitive to local norms, and one that still permits me to condemn clientelism — and which items are genuine requirements versus the prejudices of a particular camp, whether donor-compliance culture or the economists' principal-agent framing. Close with a brief scorecard of the main contenders (public-office definitions, principal-agent accounts, institutional-corruption views, impartiality-based views) against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I will do the final scoring on my own.
