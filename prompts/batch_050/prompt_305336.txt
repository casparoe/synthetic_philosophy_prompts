Last week I sat in on an argument between two colleagues in the math department common room, and it got stuck badly enough that I'd like a referee. You are a philosopher of mathematics with a firm command of the Cantorian orthodoxy and its critics; please think through the dispute carefully, charitably reconstructing each side before judging either.

The dispute was about Hilbert's hotel — the fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms that can still accommodate a new guest by shifting everyone one room up, and infinitely many new guests by shifting everyone to double their room number.

Anna opened by saying the thought experiment proves that actual infinity is incoherent as a notion of size. Her argument: any coherent concept of magnitude must obey the principle that a whole is strictly greater than any proper part of it. The hotel's room set can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the room set minus one room, so applying "same size" there forces us to deny that principle, which she called not a surprising discovery but a reductio. She said Cantor didn't dissolve the paradox, he legislated it away by redefining "size."

Bruno replied that she's just rediscovering Galileo's observation about the natural numbers and the perfect squares, and that Dedekind simply took the property she calls paradoxical as the definition of an infinite set. A definition, he said, cannot be refuted by an intuition; the correspondence shows the concept is coherent, and her preferred principle is simply a theorem about finite sets that she is overgeneralizing.

Anna's counter-move was to concede the formal consistency but deny its significance. She said: of course you can define a consistent relation and call it "cardinality"; the question is whether it deserves to be called a measure of how many things there are. The whole-part principle, she argued, is partly constitutive of what "how many" means, so bijection-based cardinality is at best a homonym of size, and the hotel story borrows all its intuitive force from the ordinary meaning it abandons.

Bruno then shifted ground: he granted that "size" talk may be misleading but said the real payoff is operational — the hotel's manager never does anything impossible. Each guest moves to a specific finitely-numbered room after finitely many steps; there is no supertask, no completed infinity of actions, just a rule. So even a moderate finitist should accept the story as harmless, and Anna's objection reduces to a distaste for the completed totality, not a contradiction.

Anna's final move: precisely — the hotel is harmless only if you quietly read it as a potential infinity (for every guest, a rule assigns a room), but it is always *presented* as an actual completed totality, and that presentation is what does the philosophical work and what she rejects. She said the story is either a finitist platitude in costume or an equivocation on "all the rooms." Bruno said that charge would bankrupt all of real analysis, and she said maybe so, and that was the end of round one.

Please adjudicate, using exactly this structure:

- Round-by-round verdicts (roughly 600 words): go through the exchange in order — the opening reductio, the Galileo/Dedekind reply, the "homonym of size" counter, the supertask-avoidance move, and the potential-vs-actual closing charge — and for each, say who has the better of that specific exchange and why, independent of who is winning overall.
- What both are missing (roughly 250 words): at least one consideration, distinction, or deflationary resolution that neither party raised, which would move the dispute forward.
- Overall verdict (roughly 150 words): state which side's position survives the exchange in better shape, or whether the honest result is a score draw, and identify the single claim on which the whole dispute actually turns.

Do not resolve the disagreement by definitional fiat (e.g., simply declaring cardinality well-defined); both parties already know the definitions and disagree about what they show.
