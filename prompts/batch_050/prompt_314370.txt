I am preparing a seminar session on the dogmatism paradox, and before we evaluate any proposed solutions I want a specification of what a solution would have to accomplish. For orientation, the puzzle as I understand it runs roughly as follows: if you know that p, then any future evidence you receive that tells against p must be misleading evidence, and it seems you are thereby in a position to rationally discount or dismiss it in advance; but a view that licenses dismissing future counterevidence on the strength of current knowledge appears to ratify exactly the closed-mindedness we condemn in the dogmatist. The puzzle is standardly associated with Saul Kripke's remarks on knowledge as presented in the literature on rational change of view, and it connects to contemporary debates about higher-order evidence, misleading evidence, and whether knowledge can be rationally retained in the face of apparent defeat.

Write the desiderata that any adequate resolution of this paradox must satisfy, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. I want the list to cover, at minimum, the following territory, though you should reorganize, subdivide, and add to it as you see fit: which intuitions the resolution must preserve (for instance, that a competent inquirer who genuinely knows something is not thereby irrational, and that an inquirer who refuses to look at unfavorable data is); which commitments it must avoid (such as licensing the disregard of evidence simpliciter, or collapsing into the skeptical consequence that we almost never know anything, or entailing that knowledge once gained is rationally impregnable); how it must handle the distinction between discounting evidence because one knows it is misleading and ignoring evidence one has not examined; how it interacts with cases where the counterevidence concerns one's own reliability rather than the proposition directly; and what it must say about situations in which knowledge is genuinely lost through new evidence. For each requirement, state why it is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one camp in the debate, and indicate which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are contestable. I am especially interested in whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list itself demonstrates that no solution can satisfy everything we pre-theoretically want, say so explicitly and identify which requirement should be abandoned and at what cost. Then, briefly and without leaning on the details of any particular author's formulation, indicate how the main families of response fare against the list: the view that apparent counterevidence against a known proposition simply fails to be evidence against it, the view that one can rationally know p while being required to remain open to evidence against p, and views that resolve the tension by distinguishing grades or kinds of justification. Do not supply citations or a bibliography; I will locate the references myself.

A note on form: write the entire response in American English, in continuous prose throughout, the way one would write a letter. Do not use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables; where the structure of the desiderata matters, convey it through the organization of the prose itself.
