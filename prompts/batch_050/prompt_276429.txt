Construct a thought experiment in which two conscious episodes are phenomenally indistinguishable from the inside, yet one intuitively counts as a pleasure and the other does not. The case should force the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: if pleasures share no common phenomenal quality, then whatever makes something a pleasure must lie elsewhere -- but where? Constraints: the scenario must be psychologically naturalistic (no direct neural stimulation, no stipulated brain states; the difference must be something a real person could live through, e.g., a shift in context, attention, or standing desire); the phenomenal identity of the two episodes must be argued for, not merely asserted; and the case must remain gripping without presupposing that pleasure is either a feeling or an attitude. After the scenario, walk through how a phenomenalist (pleasure as a shared felt quality) and an attitudinalist (pleasure as a sensation one intrinsically wants or welcomes) each diagnose the case, identify which single feature of the scenario does the work, and explain how each theorist would try to resist the verdict -- including what would happen to the case if that feature were removed.
