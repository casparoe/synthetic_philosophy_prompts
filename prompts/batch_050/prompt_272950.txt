I organize a monthly reading group for a dozen or so software engineers who are interested in AI safety but have no philosophy background and, mostly, no prior exposure to the relevant literature. For our next session I want to introduce a distinction I came across in discussions of AI alignment: between a goal-directed agent, which selects actions because it predicts those actions will bring about some state of the world it values, and an approval-directed agent, which selects actions because it predicts that a designated overseer would approve of the action itself, with no direct concern for downstream consequences beyond what the approval signal captures. I understand the distinction tolerably well myself, but I've twice watched it fail to survive contact with smart engineers -- it either collapses immediately into 'approving the action just is the goal' or gets inflated into a complete safety proposal. So I don't want the distinction explained to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it in about forty minutes.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. What conceptual pieces have to be in place before the distinction can even be stated -- I'm guessing the group first needs a working picture of an agent as something that predicts consequences of candidate actions, but tell me what else, and in what order, and why each piece depends on the last. What single example should carry the whole thing? It needs to be an agent whose two versions behave identically in ordinary cases but diverge in a case the group can actually reason about, and it needs to avoid involving a human overseer who simply knows the right outcomes, since that smuggles the difference away. Tell me what the example must contain and what it must conspicuously lack, and why the obvious candidates -- a chess engine, a customer-support chatbot -- fail.

Then give me the misconception map: the errors this particular audience will form, in the order they'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I expect at least these three -- that approval direction is just goal direction with approval as the goal; that approval direction is inherently safer because a human is in the loop; that the distinction is about what the agent is told rather than about what the agent is trying to do -- but correct my expectations where they're wrong and add what I've missed. Finally, give me one question I can pose at the end that would reveal whether the group has grasped the distinction as a distinction between kinds of selection criterion rather than between kinds of reward signal, and tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level -- infinite recursion of overseers, for instance -- while saying openly that I'm leaving it out.
