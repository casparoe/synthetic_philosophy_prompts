I'm a TA for an upper-level political philosophy course and we're on the distributive justice unit. The professor's lecture ran through the equality-of-what debate (welfare vs. resources vs. capabilities) but the students mostly absorbed it as "three smart people with three takes," and on the last problem set a bunch of them just picked whichever metric felt intuitively nice without noticing what it commits them to. I want to fix that with a handout that forces them to locate themselves in the space before they know what they're signing up for.

So: write me a decision tree that sorts someone into a position on what the currency of egalitarian justice should be. Here's the constraint that I think will actually make it work -- every branch node has to be phrased as a concrete case the student renders a verdict on, not as a question containing the technical vocabulary. The cases I want used (you can add one or two more if needed, but these must appear): (a) someone who cultivated expensive tastes -- learned to love fine wine at an expensive course their rich parents paid for -- and now can't be made as happy on the same budget as everyone else; (b) someone with a disability who needs extra resources just to reach the same level of functioning, not the same level of happiness; (c) someone raised in circumstances that crushed their ambitions, who now sincerely reports being content with very little; (d) someone who'd rather spend their equal share on leisure (the surfing case) while others work. Phrase each so a student answers yes/no or picks between two verdicts without knowing which position the answer funnels them toward.

Leaves should include at least: equality of welfare, equality of resources, the capability approach, and luck egalitarianism as distinct from plain resourcism -- plus sufficientism if you can get there cleanly from the questions, since a couple students drifted that way already. Build the tree from the logical space first: say which combinations of answers are possible, then locate the actual positions in it, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves your questions can't genuinely separate (I suspect welfare and capabilities collapse together on the adaptive preference case unless you add something -- if so, say what extra question would pry them apart, or say it can't be done without begging the question). For any combination of answers that's unoccupied, note briefly whether that's because the position is incoherent, just unattractive, or historically unexplored. Aim for maybe 5-8 questions deep, and end with a short note for me on where the tree oversimplifies (e.g., whether Dworkin's view actually sits at one leaf or straddles two). If any of this rests on a confused premise -- like if "sort people into positions" is the wrong frame because the real debate is about the site of equality rather than the currency -- push back on that directly rather than quietly accommodating it.
