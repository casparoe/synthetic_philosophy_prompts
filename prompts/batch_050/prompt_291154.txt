At the pub last night two friends of mine got into a surprisingly heated argument about Quinn's self-torturer puzzle, and I stayed out of it because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. For anyone unfamiliar: you're fitted with a device with 1000 settings, and at each step you can turn the dial up one notch in exchange for £10,000. The catch is that the increase in pain at any single step is too small to notice, and you can never turn the dial back down. Step by step, each choice looks obviously worth taking. Taken all together, they leave you in constant agony (albeit very rich).

Maya's view was that the puzzle shows something deep: evaluating choices one at a time is rationally defective, full stop. Rationality, she said, has to attach to plans or policies rather than individual acts, and the rational move is to bind yourself in advance — pick a stopping point before you start and commit to it, because no step-by-step procedure can save you.

Dev thought the puzzle was rigged. His objection was that it smuggles in a dodgy assumption, namely that an imperceptible increase in pain is a costless one. Lots of tiny real costs add up to a huge one, and a rational agent should reason about the aggregate rather than being hypnotised by what they can feel at each margin. The mistake isn't taking the money; it's pretending imperceptible means free.

Maya replied that this misses the whole point. The agent literally cannot feel which setting they're on, so they can't implement aggregate reasoning on the fly — at no point does the next notch feel worse than the current one. The only thing that works is a rule like "stop at 200", and that rule is arbitrary, since 200 feels identical to 201.

Dev said arbitrariness isn't irrationality. Choosing a savings rate for your pension is arbitrary in exactly the same way, and any rule beats no rule. The puzzle only has teeth, he claimed, if you insist that every decision must be made by comparing neighbouring options — and nobody's forced to insist that.

Maya then said Dev had just conceded her entire argument: once you admit the decision has to be made at the level of policies rather than individual choices, you've agreed that stepwise rationality fails, which was her claim from the start.

Also, for context: the reason this came up is that Dev works at a company that keeps adding one more notification to its app every few weeks, each one A/B tested as producing no detectable change in user satisfaction but a small uptick in revenue, and he insisted that process was obviously fine, which is what kicked the whole thing off.
