Bit of background so this makes sense: I spent forty years as a condensed matter physicist, retired about two years back, and since then I've tumbled headfirst into philosophy and social choice theory. Lots of reading, some of it admittedly over my head, but I'm stubborn.

The thing that keeps nagging me is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — the result that any deterministic voting rule on ranked ballots with three or more alternatives, no dictator, and an unrestricted range of preferences is manipulable: somebody can always do better by lying about their preferences than by voting honestly. Coming from physics, where an impossibility theorem is basically an invitation to find the hidden assumption and relax it, I keep hunting for escape routes. Here are the ones I know about already, so don't feed me these:

1. Restrict the domain. If preferences are single-peaked over a left-right axis, the median voter's top pick wins and honest voting is a dominant strategy.
2. Randomize. Let the rule output a lottery over candidates; Gibbard's own later work shows you can get strategy-proofness back if you accept coin flips and hybrid schemes.
3. Change the ballot. Use cardinal or approval-style ballots instead of ordinal rankings, since the theorem only bites rules defined on rankings.
4. Drop down from dominant strategies to equilibrium. Aim for Nash implementation instead — let the strategic game produce the right outcome at equilibrium even though no rule is strategy-proof outright.
5. Make manipulation computationally hard rather than impossible — design rules where finding the profitable lie is NP-hard, so in practice nobody can pull it off.

What I want: give me one more escape route that is not on this list. Not a rewording, not a special case, not two of these stapled together. If you're about to hand me something that is secretly #3 wearing a hat, catch yourself and say so instead. Explain how it actually differs from the five above and which assumption of the theorem it leans on.

And then — this is the part I care most about — give me a committed verdict on whether your escape genuinely works or whether it just shoves the same problem through a side door (e.g. the manipulation reappears at the level of choosing the mechanism, or the equilibrium, or the utility scale). Don't lay out both sides and leave me to pick. Pick a side, argue for it, and if it turns out your own idea collapses under pressure, say that plainly too. I'd rather get one honest no than three hedged maybes.

Oh, and please argue it out here rather than pointing me at papers — my university library access lapsed with my pension, so a reading list won't do me much good.
