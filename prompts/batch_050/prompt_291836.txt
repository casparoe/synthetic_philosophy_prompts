Okay so I run a weekly D&D game, and last session I introduced a culture in my setting where, in-world, abandoning your parents once they turn sixty isn't just tolerated, it's genuinely the morally right thing to do — the fiction was supposed to be built so that this is simply true, and the players were meant to come around to it. They flat-out refused. Not "our characters disagree," more like "the world can't work that way, you can't just declare that." I went googling afterwards and apparently this is a known puzzle called imaginative resistance.

Explanations I've already found, so skip these:

1. The fiction can't make it true (Walton, I think?): moral facts supervene necessarily on the non-moral ones, so if the story's world is like ours in every non-moral respect, the moral facts come out the same no matter what the narrator insists — the storyteller just fails to establish the deviant moral fact.
2. Contagion worry (Gendler?): pretending to imagine things tends to leak into what you actually believe and feel, so we resist imagining whatever we don't want leaking out of the fiction.
3. The emotional implication one (Moran?): being asked to imagine something from the inside is being asked to feel along with it, and we balk at feeling along with cruelty even in a story.
4. The deflationary one: there's no special puzzle here, fictions can contain contradictions and deviant moralities alike, and apparent resistance is just readers being confused or the story being told badly.

Is there a fifth explanation out there? It should be genuinly its own thing, not a rewording or mashup of these four. And here's my real question: does your fifth explanation predict that my players would also balk if I declared that in my setting 2+2=5? Because everyone seems to resist the morality case way harder than the math case, even though both are supposedly necessary truths, and I'd want an explanation that explains that asymmetry rather than ignoring it.
