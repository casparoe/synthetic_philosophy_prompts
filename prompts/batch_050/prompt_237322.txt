When people discuss why human extinction would be bad, they usually stop at the obvious answer: it would involve a great many deaths. But the more interesting question is what makes extinction bad *over and above* the deaths it involves -- even if, hypothetically, everyone died painlessly and simultaneously. Here are the explanations I have collected so far for that further badness:

(1) The lost-future-value explanation: extinction forecloses an enormous number of future lives that would, on the whole, have been worth living, and the badness of extinction is the badness of that loss.

(2) Scheffler's afterlife-conjecture explanation: much of what we value depends on the assumption that humanity will continue after we die -- our projects, institutions, and efforts get their point partly from being taken up by successors -- so extinction (or even the justified belief in it) would drain meaning from our lives now, and this dependence itself grounds a reason to care about extinction beyond the deaths.

(3) The interrupted-project explanation: humanity is engaged in long-term collective enterprises (science, art, moral progress) whose value lies partly in their continuation and development; extinction truncates them mid-course the way a death mid-sentence differs from a death after the final word.

(4) The irreplaceability explanation: humanity is a unique bearer of certain capacities (rationality, moral agency, aesthetic creation), and its extinction is the permanent destruction of the only known instance of something irreplaceable -- more like losing the last member of a kind than like losing some members of an abundant kind.

(5) The betrayal-of-the-past explanation: earlier generations labored and sacrificed partly for the sake of a continuing human future; extinction renders those efforts futile in a way that even catastrophe followed by recovery would not, and there is something bad about thwarting the forward-directed hopes of the dead.

Is there another explanation I'm missing? It should be genuinely different from these five -- not a restatement, not a special case (e.g., losing one particular ongoing project is not new, since (3) covers the general form), and not merely a combination of two items on the list (e.g., 'the future was valuable AND it was built by the past' adds nothing). Please explain how the new candidate differs in its underlying logic from each of the five, and give your honest assessment of whether it actually identifies a distinct source of badness or collapses into one of them on closer inspection.
