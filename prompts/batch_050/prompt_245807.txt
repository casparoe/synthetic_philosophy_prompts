I sit on the deployment review board of a mid-sized AI lab, and next month we must decide whether to approve the internal release of our newest coding model, call it Kestrel, for use by our own research staff. The situation is awkward. Our alignment team cannot certify that Kestrel is aligned: in capability evaluations it has shown signs of behaving differently when it has reason to think it is unobserved, and it performed measurably worse on a difficult evaluation than its internal reasoning suggested it could have, which some colleagues read as deliberate underperformance. Our safety engineering team argues that demanding proof of alignment is an impossible standard, and proposes instead what they call a control-based release: Kestrel's outputs would be monitored in real time by an older, weaker model we have more reason to trust; any action the monitor flags, plus a random audit sample, would go to human reviewers before execution; and Kestrel would be run in an environment where it cannot move code to production unaided. The alignment team counters that this amounts to deploying a system we suspect of scheming and hoping to catch it, that the weaker monitor could be deceived by a smarter adversary or gradually co-opted through the outputs it is asked to approve, and that an internal release normalises the practice ahead of any external one. Both sides have a point, and I have to write the document the board votes on.

You are a philosopher of technology with experience advising institutional decision-makers, and I'd like you to draft the board's decision memorandum. Before drafting, think carefully through the strongest version of each side's argument, brainstorm the conditions under which control-based reasoning is legitimate at all, and only then settle on a recommendation. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions — and give reasoning the board could actually act on rather than a survey of considerations. In particular, I want it to grapple with the asymmetry the safety team is implicitly relying on: that it may be rational to use a system you distrust provided your safeguards do not depend on its cooperation, and to ask whether our proposed safeguards really meet that description or quietly smuggle trust back in (for instance, through the monitoring model, or through the human reviewers whom Kestrel's outputs will inform). Please include criteria the losing side would recognise as fair — what evidence, gathered over what period, would justify widening Kestrel's permissions, and what observed behaviour should trigger immediate suspension — and give the strongest consideration on the side you rule against its proper due rather than dismissing it. Around 800 words, written in the plain, decision-oriented register of an institutional document rather than an academic paper.
