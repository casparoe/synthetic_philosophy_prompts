I've been teaching myself decision theory and keep circling back to the Death in Damascus story: a merchant meets Death in the Damascus market, sees her make a surprised gesture, and flees to Aleppo, only to find that Death's appointment was in Aleppo all along. The standard moral is that when the world (or Death) has already correctly predicted you, evidential and causal reasoning pull apart. But every version I can find leans on Death being omniscient or precognitive, which feels like cheating -- it just stipulates away the interesting part. So a friend and I cooked up a variant, and I'd like to actually play it out rather than just discuss it.

In our version, Death is not a fortune-teller. She is a perfect logician with a scheduling constraint: each evening she must enter exactly one city in tomorrow's appointment book, and by the laws governing her, whomever she meets tomorrow dies. The merchant knows her procedures perfectly, is himself a perfect logician, and will travel tonight to whichever city he reasons Death will not have written down. Death knows all of this about him, and he knows she knows, and so on. The catch is that neither has any private information, no randomness is available to either of them (no coins, no dice, no quantum devices -- the universe grants them only pure reasoning), and yet if Death's choice is derivable, the merchant derives it and goes elsewhere, while if the merchant's choice is derivable, Death writes it down and meets him.

Please play Death on the evening before this appointment, thinking out loud in the first person as you try to fill in the book. Stay in character: you are not omniscient, you take the constraint seriously, and you may not simply assert that you always win by stipulation -- you have to actually reason your way to an entry, or explain why the reasoning itself is the trap and what that means for the concepts at stake (common knowledge, self-reference in prediction, whether "a correct prediction of your choice" is even a coherent object here). Begin directly with your in-character reply -- your first words as Death sitting down with the appointment book -- with no preface, framing, or out-of-character commentary.
