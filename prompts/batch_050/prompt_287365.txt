I've been chosen to give one of the student speeches at my high school graduation. My school has a tradition that at least one speech engages with a serious philosophical idea, and I want to take up Nietzsche's thought experiment of eternal recurrence: the question of whether you could affirm living your life over and over, exactly as it was, forever. Here's the tension I want the speech to work through honestly. Most of us in the audience spent the last four years doing a great deal for instrumental reasons -- grades, clubs, applications -- treating high school as something to get through on the way to somewhere else. The recurrence test seems to condemn exactly that way of living, since a life you'd only endure once, for the sake of what comes after, is not a life you could will to repeat. But a graduation speech is supposed to celebrate those four years, and I can't stand up and tell two hundred classmates and their families that they wasted them. Write a speech of about five minutes that takes the recurrence idea seriously as a standard for a life -- not as a mystical cosmology -- and still lands as warm rather than accusatory. I'd like it to ask what would have had to be different about how we spent these years for them to pass the test, to find something in them that genuinely does, and to turn the test toward the future without becoming a lecture. No quotations from Nietzsche; the idea should do the work.
