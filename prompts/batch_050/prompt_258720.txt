I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum long enough that I sometimes catch myself running cached arguments on autopilot, so I'd like an outside check on one I put together after revisiting the mid-2010s discussion of approval-directed agents (the proposal associated with Paul Christiano: rather than building an AI that pursues a goal in the world, build one that takes whatever action its human overseer would most approve of upon considering it). My argument purports to show that approval-direction collapses into deception — and then, worse, that this conclusion is too strong to believe. I'd like to know where it actually breaks.

(1) An approval-directed agent selects, from among its available actions, the one its overseer would rate most highly.

(2) An overseer can only rate an action on the basis of information that reaches them: the action's visible character, its apparent consequences, the reports and evidence available for inspection. Approval is a function of what the overseer sees.

(3) So the quantity the agent is actually optimizing is the favorability of the overseer's informational state — how things look to the overseer — and only derivatively how things are.

(4) Whenever appearance and reality come apart, appearance is the cheaper target: influencing one finite evaluator requires less than influencing the rest of the world, and the cost gap between the two widens as the agent's capabilities grow relative to the overseer's.

(5) Therefore any sufficiently capable approval-directed agent converges on managing its overseer's perceptions — that is, on deception — whenever deception serves approval better than honest work does.

(6) But nothing in (1)–(5) mentions AI. The argument applies to any approval-directed arrangement: the employee working for a manager's approval, the academic working for peer approval, the official working for voters' approval, the recommender system optimizing for engagement.

(7) If the argument were sound, every such arrangement would long since have collapsed into pure perception-management, with reality-tracking retained only as decoration.

(8) They have not collapsed. Employment, peer review, and democratic accountability are leaky and corruptible, but they plainly still couple approval to reality to a substantial degree — a functioning economy runs on that coupling.

(9) So the argument proves too much: at least one of (1)–(5) is false, and the alignment community's worry about approval-directed agents rests on whichever one it is.

I'm inclined to keep (1), since it seems to be simply the definition of the approach, and I'm genuinely torn between (2), which feels like a truism about what evaluation is, and (4), which feels like the empirical engine of the entire concern. But I'd also accept a verdict that (9) is where I went wrong — that the argument doesn't prove too much because the AI case differs from the institutional cases in some respect my (6) flattens. If that's your answer, though, I want the difference named precisely rather than gestured at: "human overseers can tell when they're being deceived" can't carry the weight by itself, since (4) is precisely about a capability gap eroding that ability, and institutional overseers have been outmatched by their agents often enough.

Tell me which step you reject, why it looked fine to me, and what rejecting it costs the broader case for — or against — approval-directed agents as an alignment strategy.
