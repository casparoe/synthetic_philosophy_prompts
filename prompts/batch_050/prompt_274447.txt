I've been thinking about how much morality can demand from ordinary people, and I want a consistency check on my current views:

1. There's no absolute cap on moral sacrifice. If the stakes are genuinely enormous, morality could require someone to give up almost everything.
2. Everyone is still entitled to a personal-life floor: at least one serious project, some close relationships, and some time that isn't constantly optimized for helping others.
3. When lots of people could solve a problem, nobody should have to contribute more than roughly double their fair share just because other people refuse to help.
4. If you're the only person left who can prevent a catastrophe, your obligation can shoot far past that fair-share limit.
5. Demandingness gets less objectionable when institutions make everyone contribute predictably, even if the total sacrifice is the same as it would be for one isolated person.

Which of these actually clash, and which only seem to? Please rank the tensions by how serious they are, and suggest the least costly revision to the whole set. I especially want to know whether my personal-life floor secretly introduces a cap that #1 denies, and whether #3 and #4 disagree about what to do when everyone else defects. I cant tell if I'm being nuanced or just contradicting myself.
