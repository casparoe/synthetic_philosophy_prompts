You are an epistemologist with expertise in formal epistemology and the rationality of outright belief, helping me design teaching materials for a graduate seminar. Write a decision tree titled "What do you actually think about outright belief under uncertainty?" that takes a student from the starting commitment "it can be rational to believe propositions that are merely highly probable rather than certain" to a named position on the lottery and preface paradoxes.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. The branch nodes I care about are: whether rational outright belief is closed under conjunction (the agglomeration question that separates the two paradoxes); whether the lottery and preface cases should receive the same treatment or different ones (and if different, which feature distinguishes them -- e.g., that the preface author's beliefs were formed on evidence while the lottery beliefs are purely statistical); whether purely statistical evidence can ever rationalize outright belief, as opposed to evidence that makes the truth normal or to be expected; and whether the right response is to keep outright belief but restrict its logic, or to retreat to credences alone. Phrase every node as a question answerable without knowing where it leads -- ideally anchored in a concrete case, such as a fair million-ticket lottery, the historian's preface to a carefully researched book, or a court asked to convict on attendance statistics alone.

The leaves should include at least: a threshold ("Lockean") view that keeps agglomeration; a view that keeps outright belief but denies closure under conjunction; a radical probabilist view on which there are only credences and the paradoxes dissolve; and a view on which outright belief requires evidence that makes the proposition normal rather than merely probable. For any combination of answers your tree allows that no one seems to occupy, note it explicitly and say briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really separate. Aim for six to nine branch nodes, and think carefully about node order so that the earliest questions do the most classificatory work.
