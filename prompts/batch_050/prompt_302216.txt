I'm on the five-person board of our 40-unit housing co-op, and we're deadlocked over parking. Years ago we adopted a policy everyone agreed was fair -- spots assigned by need, rotating every two years -- but we have no real way to enforce it, and by now people just park wherever. Our building manager has proposed a fix: he assigns all the spots himself, at his own discretion, and fines anyone who ignores him. It would definitely work, because he controls the garage gate and everyone knows it. Two board members argue that a rule people actually follow beats a fair one that exists only on paper, and that after a year of obeying the manager, people will just accept his assignments as how things are -- which is basically what happened with the old policy in reverse.

This keeps reminding me of the stretch of Pascal's Pensées (in the Lafuma numbering, the fragments around 103-110) where he argues roughly that justice without power gets trampled, power without justice gets called tyranny, and that since we can't make what is just strong, we end up treating what is strong as just -- his point about why we follow the majority being not that they have more reason but that they have more force.

Draft the written opinion I'll bring to Thursday's board meeting. I want a clear recommendation (adopt the manager's plan, reject it, or adopt some modified version), with the reasoning laid out, and it should actually grapple with the Pascal material: is our situation the one he describes, and does his diagnosis count for or against the proposal? Also give the strongest version of the case for the manager's plan rather than a strawman. And please be careful to separate what's pretty well established in this territory (e.g., that unenforced rules tend to lose their authority) from what's genuinely contested (like whether enforceability is part of what makes a rule just, or a seperate pragmatic matter) -- don't present a contested view as the consensus.
