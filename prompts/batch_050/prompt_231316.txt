So I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene in my current book. Two philosophers, old friends, end up snowed in together at a cabin in the Catskills over a long weekend, and on the second night, after a bottle of wine, they get into it about panpsychism. I need the actual argument they have, and I want it written as a full dialogue I can steal the moves from.

Here's the setup. Both of them accept panpsychism in some form -- they've both long since conceded to each other that the hard problem is real and that experience of some rudimentary kind goes all the way down. So no rehashing the zombie argument or anything like that, please, they've heard it all. Where they disagree is on the combination problem.

Mara is a micropsychist: she thinks tiny subjects of experience (electrons, quarks, whatever physics bottoms out in) combine to form unified subjects like you and me, and she thinks this can be made sense of -- she's sympathetic to something like phenomenal bonding, the idea that subjects can literally stand in relations that constitute a higher-level subject. Daniel is a cosmopsychist: he thinks the only fundamental subject is the universe as a whole, and that subjects like us are aspects or partitions of it, because he thinks William James's old objection -- that no collection of separate experiences ever just adds up to a further experience over and above them -- is basically fatal to the bottom-up picture.

What makes the scene dramatically interesting to me is that each of them has a loaded gun pointed at the other. Daniel presses Mara's combination problem; Mara fires back that cosmopsychism just has the mirror-image problem -- how does one big cosmic subject get carved up into many little ones with private inner lives (the decombination problem, some people call it) -- and that explaining how one subject splits into many is no easier than explaining how many merge into one. Neither should win cleanly. Daniel should have a real answer to the decombination worry (maybe something about how wholes grounding their parts is at least familiar from priority monism in metaphysics, whereas subject-summing is allegedly unprecedented), and Mara should have a real answer to why that's special pleading.

Requirements, since my last attempt at this scene read like two Wikipedia articles arguing:

- Real back-and-forth. Each character must respond to the other one's actual last point, not pivot to their next prepared speech. At least a couple of times someone should concede something small and then build on the concession.
- They should get into the weeds at least once on what "combining" even requires -- is it that the many subjects must still exist inside the unified subject, or that they must be replaced by it, or that the phenomenology must be derivable? The argument should shift depending on which reading they take, and they should notice it shifting.
- Both of them should be smarter than a strawman. Daniel isn't a hand-wavy mystic and Mara isnt a naive "it just emerges" person.
- No narrator, no stage directions beyond minimal parentheticals like (pours more wine) if you need a beat. First names as speaker labels.
- End it the way a real late-night argument between friends ends: not with agreement, but with them having located, out loud, the exact point where they disagree -- a single question they can both state and can't settle. I'd like that last bit to feel earned rather than announced.
