A public-policy institute has two fellows who have been arguing past each other for years, and it has now ordered them to produce a joint statement or lose their funding. The first is a libertarian paternalist in the tradition of Thaler and Sunstein: she designs default-enrollment rules for pension plans, cafeteria layouts, and organ-donor registries, and holds that choice architecture which steers people toward their own good while formally leaving every option open is not merely permissible but often obligatory. The second is a classical-liberal legal scholar who regards soft paternalism as paternalism all the same, and who thinks the state's proper answer to self-regarding foolishness is argument and information, not architecture. As a condition of sitting down together, both insisted that Mill's On Liberty serve as their common text, and both have agreed that any joint statement must take a position on this passage from Chapter IV, which each cites against the other:

"Considerations to aid his judgment, exhortations to strengthen his will, may be offered to him, even obtruded on him, by others; but he himself is the final judge. All errors which he is likely to commit against advice and warning, are far outweighed by the evil of allowing others to constrain him to what they deem his good."

Draft the joint statement they would produce. It should have four parts.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. It must include: a shared reading of the quoted passage that locates Mill's line between aiding judgment and constraining choice precisely enough to do real work later in the document, rather than gesturing at a spirit both can praise; the empirical points both sides accept, such as that defaults and framing reliably move behavior and that there is no architecture-free way to present options; and whatever doctrinal points genuinely command both signatures -- for instance, whether both accept that an unnoticed nudge and an explicit incentive can differ morally even when they change the same behavior to the same degree.

Second, a disagreements table: each surviving disagreement stated in a single sentence of neutral wording, followed by one column each for how the nudger and the classical liberal would state their own position, phrased so that each would sign their own cell. At minimum, the table must settle whether the quoted passage classifies a pension default as a consideration aiding judgment, an exhortation, or a quiet evasion of the person's final say -- and if they cannot agree on that classification, the table should record it as a disagreement about the passage itself, not merely about policy.

Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one side to revise its view. For each crux, name a concrete case or finding both agree would bear on it -- for example, evidence about whether defaults reshape preferences over time or merely bypass them, or about what happens to people's decision-making competence when they are repeatedly spared hard choices -- and write down in advance what each side predicts that case or finding shows.

Fourth, a closing paragraph signed by both, stating what each would count as a genuine defeat of their own position: not a concession, but the evidence or argument that would actually move them.

If some part of their dispute proves to be verbal -- if, say, "freedom of choice" means preserved options to one fellow and unmanipulated deliberation to the other -- the statement should say so explicitly and record which disagreements survive once the words are disambiguated.
