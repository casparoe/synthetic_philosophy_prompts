Last week a friend and I got into a long and surprisingly heated argument about how legal texts should be interpreted, and we both ended up leaning on a passage from Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", while reading it in opposite directions. Here is the passage in dispute: "It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say." My friend reads this as a flat textualism: only the enacted words ever count. I think Douglass is saying something subtler, but I couldn't articulate what, so I tried to figure out my own position by checking my gut on cases, and now I want to know what principle, if any, I actually hold.

My verdicts: (1) A testator's will leaves everything "to my children," and a diary entry surfaces proving he privately hoped his estranged son would be cut out; the son inherits, and the diary should count for nothing. (2) A city's 1920 ordinance bans "vehicles" from the park; everyone at the time would have pictured horse-drawn carriages and early motorcars, but I think a modern e-scooter is covered, because the word does the work, not the mental picture. (3) A statute is genuinely ambiguous between two readings, and published floor speeches from the debate show the legislature publicly understood it the first way; I think those speeches may break the tie, because they were out in the open, not hidden in anyone's breast. (4) A clause was drafted in deliberately general language because the drafters privately disagreed about its reach and papered over the disagreement; I think later interpreters must fight out the reach on the general language's own terms, and neither faction's private hope binds anyone. (5) A regulatory agency has, for eighty years, read a statute in a way I concede the text does not naturally bear, and the whole industry has structured itself around that reading; I find myself unwilling to disturb it, even though this verdict sits badly with (1) and (2), since here something other than the enacted text is winning. My friend says (5) proves I don't really believe the Douglass principle at all, and that (3) proves my friend doesn't either.

So: state as precisely as you can the principle Douglass's passage actually commits him to — is it that secret intentions are inadmissible while public ones are admissible, that only the text is ever evidence of anything, or something else — and then state the principle (or small set of candidate principles) that best captures my five verdicts, saying which clause of each principle is motivated by which case. Do Douglass's principle and mine coincide, or does the passage permit the tie-breaking use of public debate in (3) while forbidding the diary in (1), and if so, what feature of the passage does that turn on? Which of my verdicts would a clean version of the principle force me to surrender — I assume (5), but tell me honestly — and what does the resulting account predict for a case I didn't raise: a constitutional provision whose framers used a term of art from their legal culture that has since drifted in ordinary speech, where enforcing the modern meaning would reach conduct the enacting generation would have found obviously outside it?
