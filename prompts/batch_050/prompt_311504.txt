So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about who gets blamed when AI systems screw up -- the self-driving car that kills a pedestrian, the trading algorithm that tanks a market, that kind of thing. Philosophers apparently call this the "responsibility gap": the manufacturer never intended that specific outcome, the operator couldn't have predicted it, and the machine itself doesn't seem like the sort of thing you can meaningfully blame, so responsibility seems to fall into a crack between all the candidate parties.

Here's my angle: I keep thinking Aristotle would have had surprisingly sharp things to say about this, given his account of voluntary action in the Nicomachean Ethics -- roughly, an action is voluntary and blameworthy when it originates in the agent and isn't done from certain kinds of ignorance, plus his whole thing about praising and blaming people for their character rather than isolated acts. So, put yourself in his sandals for me:

1. Would Aristotle think there really is a responsibility gap for autonomous systems, or would he try to dissolve it -- say, by treating the machine as a tool and tracing responsibility back through to whoever designed, trained, or chose to deploy it?
2. His view that we're responsible for our character because we shape it through habit is interesting here, because an ML system's "character" is literally shaped through training. Would he extend that thought to how we should think about the people who trained it, or would he resist the analogy?
3. He also thought you can be responsible for harms done in ignorance if the ignorance was itself your fault (his drunk example). Does that give him a way past the "nobody could have predicted what the model would do" defense that every tech company reaches for?

Give me his likeliest verdicts with the reasoning, and -- important -- tell me honestly where his framework definately creaks or breaks when applied to modern ML systems, because I don't want to pretend a 4th century BC guy magically solves 21st century tech policy. No need for citations or a bibliography, just a well-argued reconstruction I can build the column around.
