I’ve been asked to give the opening remarks at the launch of a small research centre where I used to work. The centre studies organisations whose rules are supposed to be self-correcting, and its unofficial emblem is Escher’s Drawing Hands. The founding example is a hospital project I helped with: nurses can propose changes to the triage protocol, the revised protocol changes how the staffing algorithm works, and the algorithm then changes which nurses are available to revise the protocol. Everyone calls this a tangled hierarchy, because each level can reach into the level that appears to control it. My own contribution was arguing, rather unfashionably, that there also has to be an inviolate level: a small constitutional layer specifying who may suspend the loop, what evidence counts as an emergency, and which safeguards neither the nurses nor the algorithm are allowed to rewrite.

The audience will be an awkward mixture of systems theorists, software people, hospital administrators, former colleagues, and members of my family who have heard me complain about this project for years. Please write a five-minute speech in American English that uses the tangled-hierarchy/inviolate-level distinction as its organising idea rather than merely mentioning it. It should explain Drawing Hands accessibly, make one genuinely funny joke about the danger of becoming the inviolate level oneself, and end by dedicating the centre without pretending that any safeguard can stand outside history forever. I want something warm and occassionally self-deprecating, but philosophically exact enough that the specialists won’t wince.
