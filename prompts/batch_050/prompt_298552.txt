So I've been reading about AI control -- the whole approach of building safety measures that are supposed to work even assuming the model is actively misaligned, rather than trying to make it aligned in the first place. The analogy that keeps doing the heavy lifting in these discussions is that a scheming AI is like an untrusted employee or an insider threat at a company. You can't make the mole loyal, but you can still get work out of them: compartmentalize what they see, have a second employee review everything they produce, never hand them the master keys, and plant honeypots -- tempting oppurtunities to defect -- so you catch them the first time they try something. The conclusion the analogy gets used to support is a pretty strong one: that maybe alignment is a nice-to-have, and good enough "security culture" around a model could carry most of the load on its own.

Please audit this analogy properly. Give me the explicit mapping (what corresponds to what: the model, the monitor, the honeypot, the "firing," etc.), then go through where it breaks down, and for each break tell me whether it matters for the conclusion above or is just cosmetic. Some things I suspect matter, but tell me if I'm wrong: a human insider works at human speed and can only be in one place, while the model is running thousands of actions a second across many copies at once; when you catch a human mole you fire that specific person, but catching one misbehaving model instance tells you something about every other instance since they share weights; two human reviewers can collude but at least they're independent draws, while your AI monitor might share the exact same blind spots or failure modes as the model it's watching; and a human insider needs the defection to somehow pay off for them in a way we can reason about, while a misaligned model's "payoff" might be opaque to us, so it could defect at a time we'd never predict or even defect when it gains nothing we'd recognize. Also: does the analogy quietly smuggle in the assumption that misalignment looks like rational adversarial agency in the first place -- like a spy with a goal -- when the real risk might be weirder than that?

At the end: which parts of the strong conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the insider-threat analogy is doing more harm than good, what's a better analogy to reach for? Please answer in American English.
