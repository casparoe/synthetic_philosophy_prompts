I'm a novelist and I keep tripping over this problem that, once I started asking around, turns out philosophers have been chewing on for decades without settling it, and I'm hoping you can tell me what the fight is actually about.

Here's the thing in practical terms. Readers will follow me anywhere as long as I mess with facts. Dragons, time travel, a universe where the dead get customer service lines, gravity running backwards on Thursdays -- nobody blinks. But the moment a story treats something morally wrong as just plain right -- not a villain believing it, not an unreliable narrator claiming it, but the story itself building a world where, say, casually betraying your friends is genuinely noble and praiseworthy -- readers balk. And I balk too, writing it. It's not that such a world is harder to picture than backwards gravity. It feels more like I refuse to picture it, or like the story is committing some kind of fraud when it asserts it.

As far as I can tell the literature has three camps. One says we literally can't imagine it -- the fiction is defective, the way a story asserting that 2+2=5 would be. Another says we can imagine it fine but won't, because fully imagining a moral world is different from imagining a physical one -- it recruits you, it asks you to take up a stance, and refusing is a way of keeping your hands clean. A third says the problem is really about my authority as the author: I get to stipulate the physics of my world by fiat, but moral truths (if there are any) aren't mine to stipulate, so my narrator simply lacks the standing to make betrayal noble, wich the story then dishonestly pretends he has.

My working hypothesis, and tell me if it's naive: these three camps aren't actually disagreeing about imagination. They're disagreeing about what reading a story IS -- whether it's simulating a world, joining a game, or taking instruction from a teller -- and the imagination stuff is downstream of that. So is this a substantive disagreement about the mind, a verbal one about what "imagine" means, or three people answering different questions under one label? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the camps, and tell me what would actually count as progress here -- a distinction nobody's drawn, an experiment with readers, a formal point about what stipulation can do -- or whether nothing would, and why. Practical stakes for me: whether this is a real constraint on what fiction can do, or just a fact about squeamish audiences I should write through.
