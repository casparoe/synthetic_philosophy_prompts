I am a novelist at work on a piece of counterfactual historical fiction: the premise is that a single well-documented nineteenth-century event comes out differently, and the plot traces the consequences across two generations. When I described the project to a friend who trained as a historian, she waved it off with a line I have since heard repeated elsewhere: history knows no subjunctive mood. She took it to settle the matter -- as if the sentence itself showed that asking what would have happened is a confused enterprise, and that my novel therefore rests on a mistake. I suspect she has conflated several different claims under one aphorism, but I cannot cleanly separate them, and the separation matters to me practically: if some reading of the slogan is sound, I want to know whether it merely disciplines how counterfactuals should be constructed or whether it really does condemn the whole genre.

Would you distinguish the precise theses this slogan could be expressing? I can gesture at candidates -- that counterfactual claims about the past lack truth-values altogether; that they have truth-values but we could never know them, since history offers no controlled reruns; that the causal fabric is so densely interwoven that altering one thread unravels everything, leaving no determinate world to describe; that major outcomes were overdetermined and would have arrived by another route anyway, so the divergence is idle; or that counterfactual reasoning is simply not the historian's method, whatever its merits elsewhere -- but I do not trust my list to be exhaustive or my formulations to be the sharpest available. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or something that collapses under scrutiny, and identify which reading my friend's dismissal actually requires in order to reach her conclusion. Finally, if the strongest defensible reading falls short of her verdict, tell me what it would instead demand of a novelist who wants to write counterfactual history honestly rather than whimsically.
