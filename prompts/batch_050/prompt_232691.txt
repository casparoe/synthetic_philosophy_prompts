Had a massive argument with a friend last night about loss aversion — she thinks most of what behavioural economists call the endowment effect is perfectly fine, I think it's mostly bias. Her verdicts on our cases so far:

1. Priya won't sell a gig ticket she bought for £40 even when offered £150, though she admits she'd never pay £150 for it herself. Friend: bias.
2. Priya won't sell her grandmother's ring for £2,000, but also wouldn't pay £2,000 for an identical one. Friend: legitimate preference, not a bias.
3. Priya has sat in her workplace pension's default fund for six years and can't give a single reason for preferring it to the alternatives. Friend: bias.
4. Priya turns down a 50-50 bet, lose £100 / win £180, saying a loss would simply sting more than the win would please. Friend: legitimate.
5. Priya won't sell the flat she bought two years ago at a price she conceeds she wouldn't pay for it today, citing moving costs and that it's become her home. Friend: legitimate.

New case: Priya won't sell a signed first edition her dad gave her for her birthday last month, even at three times the going price, though she says she wouldn't pay the going price for it herself. Her dad is alive and well, and she's not especially sentimental about books in general.

What will she say about this one? More importantly: work out which rule she seems to be running (sentimental attachment, time-in-possession, whether there's a real cost or a genuine asymmetry behind the gap, what she'd endorse ex ante, ...), which other rules fit all five verdicts just as well, and which of those rules diverge on the new case. Then tell me the follow-up case that would best settle which rule she's actually using.

Finish with your prediction as \answer{bias} or \answer{legitimate}.
