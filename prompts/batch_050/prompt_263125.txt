I teach a second-year philosophy of mind seminar, 22 students, one 80-minute session. Design an in-class simulation that makes the combination problem land before I ever say panpsychism or combination problem. The thing I want them to feel is the gap between information being jointly integrated in a system and there being one subject for whom the whole thing is experienced.

Here’s the basic setup I have in mind, which you can refine but should preserve:

- Split the class into teams of 5 or 6. Each student privately gets one simple phenomenal contribution to a hidden scene -- a color patch, a texture, a tone, a smell-free scent substitute, etc. -- plus maybe one relational fact like louder than or to the left of.
- Only the owner gets to experience their contribution directly. They can communicate under tight rules, but cannot simply hand over the stimulus.
- The team must reconstruct one composite scene from the private parts. Run several rounds with different information structures: decentralized relay, a central human integrator, and finally a non-person integrator such as a shared board or sealed-envelope procedure.
- The teams should be able to succeed at the reconstruction task. The philosophical pressure should come only after functional success, when you freeze the game and ask: who, if anyone, experienced the whole scene? If the central integrator did, what happened to the contributors’ experiences? If the team did, what exactly is the subject of that experience?
- Include one moment where someone is tempted to answer the group experienced it, and build the debrief around forcing them to say what that could even mean.

Give me a genuinely runnable activity: prep and materials, sample hidden scenes, exact rules and message limits, a minute-by-minute timeline, roles, scoring or points that create a small stake without real money, and the transition into debrief. The debrief should map particular moments onto functional integration, phenomenal unity, privacy of experience, subject-summing, and the difference between representing a whole and experiencing that whole. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- students treating it as a teamwork game, peeking, equivocating on experience versus information, etc. -- and how to handle those problems. Be honest about what the simulation cannot reproduce, especially the fact that the students are already full-blown subjects rather than little proto-experiential parts, so it cannot establish panpsychism by itself.
