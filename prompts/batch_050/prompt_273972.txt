I recently ended up in a long disagreement with a colleague in my department about the philosophical zombie argument. After I pressed him on why the conceivability of a zombie world should tell us anything about whether physicalism is true, he closed the exchange by saying, "If you can genuinely conceive of it, it's possible — that's the only test of possibility we have." I have heard versions of this line in many places, always doing argumentative work, and I suspect it is nowhere near as clear as its users assume. I would like a careful unpacking of what it could mean.

Please set out the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing, treating the two key terms separately. On the conceivability side, distinguish at least between merely imagining a scenario, entertaining it without detecting a contradiction, and something stronger like ideal or fully informed conceivability. On the possibility side, distinguish logical, metaphysical, and merely epistemic possibility. For each combination that amounts to a real thesis someone might hold, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or straightforwardly false, and give a brief reason. I would also like the analysis to engage test cases: I can apparently conceive of water without H2O, of Goldbach's conjecture being false, and of a round square if I do not think too hard; it matters to me which readings survive these cases and which are embarrassed by them.

Finally, return to my colleague's use of the line: say which precisification, if any, would actually support the inferential move from zombie conceivability to the falsity of physicalism, and whether that reading is one anyone is in a position to know applies.
