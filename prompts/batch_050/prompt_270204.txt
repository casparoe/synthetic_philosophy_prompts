My roommate read Gödel, Escher, Bach over the summer and cornered me with this argument that supposedly shows nobody is actually self-aware. I've poked at it for a week and can't break it, but the conclusion is obviously nuts, so tell me which step dies and what the funeral costs.

Setup: think of Escher's Drawing Hands, the lithograph where two hands are each drawing the other into existence. It looks like a perfect self-contained loop, but of course neither hand is really drawing anything -- there's an invisible third level doing all the work, namely Escher, the paper, the ink, and that level isn't drawn by anything in the picture. Call that the inviolate level. Now the argument:

(1) Being self-aware means the system's picture of itself -- the "I" -- is part of the system and feeds back on it, like the two hands: my thought about me changes the me the thought is about, which changes the thought, and so on.

(2) Every apparent loop like this only works because there's an inviolate level underneath that does the real work and stays outside the loop. That's the whole lesson of the Escher print.

(3) So a self-aware brain must have an inviolate level too. In the brain's case that's just the substrate: neurons, chemistry, whatever implements the whole thing.

(4) The inviolate level is by definition not reachable from inside the loop -- if the loop could reach back down and redraw it, it wouldn't be inviolate.

(5) So the "I" can never be aware of the level that actually produces it. My self-model cannot include the machinery that runs the self-model.

(6) But genuinely being aware of something means being aware of what it actually is, and what I actually am just IS that substrate. A picture of me that leaves out the part doing the picturing isn't a picture of me at all -- it's a picture of a fictional narrator, like how the hand in the print "thinks" it's drawing when really Escher is.

(7) Therefore nobody's "I" is an awareness of anything real. Self-awareness is impossible, full stop.

The part that kills me is each step feels like a truism and then suddenly (7) tells me I'm not aware of myself, which I demonstrably am, since I'm doing it right now while being annoyed at this argument.

Where does it break? If forced, I'd most like to keep (1) and (5) -- the idea that the self-model can't swallow its own machinery seems like the one deep insight here -- but I'm willing to be talked out of either if the price is right. Also, whatever step you reject: explain why it looked obviously true, because it did, and that's embarassing.
