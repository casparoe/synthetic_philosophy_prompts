For an undergraduate philosophy seminar, write a dialogue about Moore’s paradox between two equally competent philosophers, Dr. Vale and Dr. Iben.

They are discussing the following case: Amara looks out the window at a heavy rainstorm and sincerely tells her colleague, It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining. When challenged, Amara explains that she regards the evidence as decisively supporting the claim that it is raining, yet does not feel the settled practical confidence that she associates with believing it.

Dr. Vale defends the view that Amara’s utterance is necessarily defective: sincere assertion constitutively involves presenting oneself as believing what one asserts, so Amara must be irrational, insincere, confused, or using belief in an unusual sense. Dr. Iben defends the view that the utterance can be both sincere and rational: assertion may answer to the evidence while belief requires a higher threshold of practical commitment, so the apparent paradox results from treating these epistemic attitudes as one.

Write twelve to sixteen exchanges. The philosophers should respond directly to one another’s arguments, consider at least one objection concerning the transparency of belief and one concerning the social function of assertion, and distinguish any substantive disagreement from a merely terminological disagreement about the word belief. Do not resolve the exchange by making either philosopher careless, dogmatic, or obviously mistaken, and do not turn the dialogue into a lecture.

After the dialogue, add a brief seminar note identifying where the exchange takes liberties with, or leans on a contested interpretation of, Moore’s paradox. In that note, separate points that are broadly settled among philosophers familiar with the issue from questions that remain genuinely contested, without presenting either character’s position as the consensus view.
