So I cover science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and I'm writing a column about a result in logic that I think deserves way more attention than it gets: David Lewis's triviality results about conditionals. The short version for my lede is that the most natural thing you could want from the word "if" -- that the probability of "if A then C" just equals the probability of C given A -- provably cannot work for ordinary truth-conditional conditionals, on pain of collapsing your whole probability space (basically everything ends up probabilistically independent of everything, or your language can only express a handful of propositions). My editors are fine with the column but they want a sidebar: a "which way out of triviality are you?" decision tree readers can walk through.

Write me that decision tree. Start the reader at acceptance of the triviality result itself (the tree isn't for people who think Lewis's proof is just wrong -- though you can include one early node asking whether they even accept that the proof goes through, with a leaf for the small band of holdouts). Then branch on the commitments that actually separate the live responses: do indicative conditionals have truth conditions at all, or do they only have assertability conditions? If they lack truth conditions, is "if" doing something like expressing a conditional belief or supposition (the Adams-style expressivist/suppositional route)? If they keep truth conditions, do they take "if" to be a genuine two-place connective whose semantics is context-sensitive in a way that blocks the proof's assumptions (the Stalnaker-ish route, which famously has to give up principles like the equivalence between "if A then if B then C" and "if A and B then C")? Or do they deny that "if" is a connective at all and treat if-clauses as restrictors on modals and adverbs of quantification (the Kratzer line, where bare conditionals get treated as implicitly modalized)? And leave room for the deflationist holdouts who say the indicative conditional is just the material conditional and the apparent counterexamples are pragmatics doing overtime.

Each branch node should be phrased as a question a smart reader can answer without knowing any of the names -- e.g., ask them what they'd say about a concrete case, like whether "if the die lands even, it lands on two" is straightforwardly false when the die in fact lands on five, rather than asking "do you accept truth conditions for conditionals?". Aim for 5-8 leaf positions, give each leaf its standard name plus one sentence on the bullet it has to bite (e.g., the expressivist owes a story about conditionals embedded inside other conditionals; the restrictor theorist owes a story about probability judgements about bare conditionals). Also: flag any two leaves your tree can't cleanly separate by questions alone, and if there's a combination of answers that's logically available but seemingly undefended by anyone, note it and give your best guess why -- incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored. Keep the tone at the level of a good pop-science explainer, not a journal article.
