I've been following forecasting and epistemics discussions on rationalist blogs for years, and an argument with a friend recently crystallized a question I can't settle. Suppose a research group builds an opaque machine-learning system -- call it the Oracle -- that outputs numerical probabilities for well-defined future events: elections, clinical trial outcomes, whether a specific bridge fails within a decade. It's a black box; nobody can inspect why it says what it says. But its track record over fifteen years is extraordinary: across hundreds of thousands of predictions, among events it assigned probability p, the realized frequency is essentially p, under every partition anyone has tried, and it has consistently outperformed prediction markets, superforecaster teams, and domain experts on proper scoring rules. My friend claims that a rational agent should treat the Oracle's outputs exactly the way David Lewis's Principal Principle says we should treat objective chances: set your credence in an event equal to whatever number the Oracle assigns, absent some extraordinary reason. I objected that the Principal Principle is about *objective chance* -- a metaphysical posit about the world's physical propensities, the way quantum mechanics is sometimes said to be chancy -- and the Oracle is merely a very good epistemic expert. Deference to experts is one thing; the Principal Principle is another.

Please argue for my friend's side: that a rational agent should defer to the Oracle's outputs precisely as if they were objective chances, even though they are not grounded in any chancy physical process and are just the outputs of a deterministic computation. I'd like the argument to engage seriously with what the Principal Principle actually demands rather than hand-waving at it. In particular, address these pressure points: (1) whether empirical calibration and predictive dominance are sufficient to earn Principal-Principle-style deference, or whether something extra is needed -- e.g., the outputs must *screen off* other evidence you might hold, and what that would require; (2) the fact that the Oracle revises its outputs over time, and whether this is a problem for deference or instead parallels the way objective chances themselves are time-indexed and evolve as history unfolds; (3) cases where you possess private evidence the Oracle demonstrably lacks -- say, you personally flipped the coin in question and felt its weight -- and whether the Principal Principle's own admissibility clause has an analogue here that lets you depart from its outputs without abandoning the general policy of deference.

The strongest version of the argument, I suspect, has to say something about what objective chance was ever *for* epistemologically -- whether it earns its deference-conferring status through metaphysics or through its functional role, and whether anything occupying that functional role inherits the deference. If you think that move fails, explain where. Roughly 800-1200 words; take a definite stand rather than surveying both sides.
