I volunteer at our local natural history museum on weekends (mostly dusting cases and stopping kids from licking the ammonites) and there's something that's been bugging me for months that I don't have a word for. In the fossil hall we've got this famous sequence of snail shells, layer after layer of them from the same rock formation, and each layer's shells look almost exactly like the ones above and below it. But at some point in the stack the little label changes names -- same genus, different species. And here's the thing: presumably every snail in that sequence could have interbred with its parents' generation and its kids' generation, and with their parents and kids, all the way down. No snail ever looked at its mate and thought, wrong species. The line on the label exists only because we, millions of years later, are looking at the whole stack at once. Nobody was ever standing at the boundary. So the boundary seems... made by us? But also not totally arbitrary, because the shells at the top genuinely are different from the ones at the bottom.

My brother-in-law, who breeds canaries and considers this qualification enough to hold forth, told me it's the same as the situation with, say, herring gulls around the arctic where neighbors can interbreed but the ends of the chain can't. But I don't think that's right, or at least it's not the interesting version. In the gull case there's at least a *place* where things break down -- you can point to the map. In my snail case there's nothing to point to. Every individual lived its whole life on the right side of whatever line we draw. It's like the line only exists backwards, from our vantage point.

I read a bit about the "species problem" online and it mostly seems to be about which definition to use for living things (interbreeding vs. family tree vs. physical similarity, etc.), which feels adjacent but not quite it -- my question is about drawing lines inside one continuous lineage over time. Is there an established name for this specific puzzle, or for the naming practice itself? Is it secretly just the old heap-of-sand paradox with a geological twist, or does the temporal element change something real? And if there's genuinely no name for it, what would you call it -- I'd want a definition that makes clear whether the same thing is going on when people argue about when Latin "became" Italian, or whether that's a different animal entirely.
