So I'm leading my reading group's session on Molière's Le Misanthrope next week, and I want to frame it around Rousseau's attack on the play in the Letter to d'Alembert. As I remember it, Rousseau's complaint isn't just that the play is funny at Alceste's expense -- it's that Molière had to make the sincere man ridiculous to get a Parisian audience on side, so the theater ends up teaching the audience the exact corruption it pretends to satirize: laugh at honesty, admire Philinte's smooth complaisance, go home unchanged. He also gripes that the whole plot is incoherent, since a genuine hater of falseness would never fall for Célimène of all people.

What I want from you: is Rousseau actually right about this play? Where does the text support him -- I'm thinking the sonnet scene with Oronte, Célimène's after-dinner portrait gallery where she skewers all her absent friends, the way Alceste's retreat to his desert at the end is staged -- and where does the play fight back? For instance, is Alceste really sincere, or is his honesty itself a kind of vanity and a way of controlling people, which is basically what Philinte and Célimène keep hinting at? If the play itself knows Alceste is partly a poseur, does that defuse Rousseau's reading or just confirm his deeper worry that the stage can't present virtue without making it a joke? And then zoom out: Rousseau's general claim is that spectators leave the theater with their passions flattered rather than corrected, so even good satire morally achieves nothing. Does Le Misanthrope -- the very play he picked as his example -- refute that theory, or is it his best exhibit? I'd like a reading that takes both the play and the Letter seriously and doesn't just hand the win to either of them.
