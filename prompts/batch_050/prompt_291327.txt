In my free-will seminar we discussed a Frankfurt-style case: Jones decides entirely on his own to shoot Smith, while Black, a nefarious neurosurgeon, stands ready to force the decision via a brain chip if Jones shows any sign of wavering. When I suggested Jones is blameworthy, a classmate shut me down with the line: "You can't be responsible for what you couldn't have avoided." I suspect that one sentence is smuggling several different claims into our debate, and that nobody in the room separated them. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, including at least: (a) a claim about counterfactual dependence, i.e., that Jones's deliberating made no difference to what happened; (b) a claim about alternative possibilities, i.e., that no course of action available to Jones would have avoided the shooting; (c) a claim about sourcehood or control, i.e., that the decision did not originate in Jones in the way responsibility requires; and (d) a conditional reading of "couldn't have avoided," i.e., that even if Jones had chosen otherwise, the outcome would have been the same. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or simply false; whether it survives Frankfurt-style cases (including the worry that Jones's telltale prior sign is itself an involuntary "flicker of freedom"); and whether it is the reading my classmate actually needed in order to establish that Jones is not blameworthy. Conclude with a verdict: should a careful philosopher adopt one precisification of the slogan, or retire it in favor of the separated claims? Please write your answer in American English.
