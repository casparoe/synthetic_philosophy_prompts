I retired three years ago after a career in marine geochemistry, and I have been spending my newfound leisure working through the "big history" literature my grandchildren keep recommending -- Harari's Sapiens, Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, and most recently Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything. I was startled to find that these authors, though ostensibly describing the same long arc of human prehistory, seem to belong to opposed camps. The Diamond-and-Harari side, as I read them, treats the adoption of agriculture as a kind of trap: sedentism, grain surpluses, and population growth more or less mechanically generated hierarchy, states, and disease, with environmental endowments determining which societies fell in first. The Graeber-and-Wengrow side replies that this is a just-so story: that early farmers experimented freely for millennia, that hierarchy was a political choice rather than an ecological necessity, and that the conventional narrative mistakes an impoverished archaeological record for evidence of inevitability. My reading group of fellow retirees has been arguing about this for months, largely past each other.

I would like you to draft the document our group should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed partisan of the materialist-inevitability camp and a committed partisan of the Graeber-Wengrow camp. Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims -- statements about the archaeological and ethnographic record, about what the rival narrative gets right, and about what a good grand narrative owes its readers -- that both could sign without wincing. Follow this with a disagreements table in which each remaining dispute is stated in neutral wording, with a one-line formulation of each side's position phrased so that its own side would accept it. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party. I suspect one crux is whether cereal agriculture and durable hierarchy can be shown to be separable in the record rather than merely separable in principle, and another is whether "the state" names one kind of thing across the cases; but you should identify the cruxes yourself rather than take my guesses on faith. For each crux, name a concrete kind of evidence, case, or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict or concede if it came out against them. Where part of the dispute strikes you as verbal -- two camps using the same word, such as "equality" or "freedom," for different things -- say so explicitly and say which part. As the last line, give a one-sentence summary of where, in your judgment, the genuine disagreement ultimately resides.
