So I've been chewing on this idea I came across a while back, roughly the African ubuntu thought that a person becomes a person through other people -- like your selfhood, maybe even your moral status, is constituted by your relationships and your community rather than being something you just have on your own, as a self-contained individual. I got into it through a podcast, then an argument with my brother, and honestly the more I sit with it the more I believe it. The Western atomistic "individual first, relationships second" picture strikes me as getting things backwards: we're born into a web, we're shaped by it, the very capacities that make us persons (language, empathy, having a self-narrative at all) come from other people. So my position is something like: personhood is relational through and through, not just causally but constitutively.

Here's what I want from you: what would count AGAINST this view? Not a list of generic objections from a textbook, but a real map of where the view is exposed. I keep poking at hard cases myself -- the hermit who leaves society and seems to stay a person, or even becomes a deeper one; the infant or the person with severe dementia who can't reciprocate relationships; the person raised by a genuinely cruel or conformist community, where "becoming a person through your community" starts sounding like a threat rather than a comfort. Does the view collapse into "the community gets to decide who counts as a person," and is that as bad as it sounds? Also: could the view be falsified or weakened by empirical stuff, like if it turned out feral children or isolated AI systems developed person-like capacities without any community? And does it stand or fall with bigger claims about what anything IS -- like, does it need some general metaphysics where relations are prior to things, and if that turns out to be wrong, does ubuntu personhood go down with it?

Please structure the answer like this: first a section called "The view at its strongest" (maybe 150 words, steelman it better than I did), then "Defeaters, ranked" (the longest part, 400-500 words, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of relational personhood would concede it as a real hit or wave it off as a misunderstanding), then "What the view actually predicts" (about 150 words -- what does it commit to that the individualist rival doesn't, so I can tell whether it's even risking anything or has been qualified into mush), and finally "Bottom line" (short, like 75 words: is this view genuinely vulnerable or suspiciously unfalsifiable, and is that a problem for a view of this kind).
