A friend sent me this argument and I think it proves too much, but I can't pin down where. The background is Chesterton's fence -- the idea that you shouldn't tear down a fence until you've figured out why someone put it up.

(1) You shouldn't remove a long-standing rule or institution until you understand why it exists.
(2) The reasons long-standing institutions exist are mostly tacit and distributed: nobody designed them, they were selected for effects nobody intended, and many of their functions only show up when they're gone. So complete understanding of why one exists is in principle unavailable to any would-be reformer.
(3) Therefore no reformer is ever in a position to justifiably remove a long-standing institution.
(4) But every institution was once new. Whoever first established it replaced some earlier arrangement -- including the arrangement of simply having no rule there -- and by (2) they couldn't have fully understood the functions of what they were replacing either, since it was just as old and tacit.
(5) So the founding of every institution was illegitimate by the same standard, and the only legitimate social state is whatever came first, which traced all the way back is no institutions at all.
(6) That conclusion is absurd.

So which step goes wrong, if any? The argument feels valid to me, which makes me think one of the premises has to go, but I'd rather not give up (1) since the basic insight behind it seems obviously right. Tell me which premise is cheapest to reject and what I'm actually paying for rejecting it -- e.g. if (2) goes, am I committed to thinking reformers can in principle have the relevant understanding, and is that a bullet I should want to bite?
