I'm giving a talk in two weeks at our philosophy of psychiatry workshop, and I want to go in armed. My thesis is the boring-sounding but actually contentious one: delusions are beliefs, full stop — not a sui generis attitude, not imaginings mistaken for beliefs, not some in-between state we need a new name for. My argument, roughly: (1) the best reason to deny they're beliefs is that they fail the functional norms of belief — they're not responsive to evidence, they often don't generate appropriate emotion (the Capgras patient who believes his wife is an impostor but isn't alarmed), and patients frequently don't act on them; (2) but ordinary beliefs massively fail these norms too — political beliefs, religious beliefs, my colleague's unshakeable conviction that his book is underappreciated — and nobody says those aren't beliefs; (3) so the right conclusion isn't that delusions aren't beliefs but that belief is a category with loose normative standards, and partial functional integration is enough; (4) the rival attitudes people propose can't explain the conviction and the distress anyway.

The audience is the problem. There's a clinical neuropsychologist who works on the two-factor theory and will know the Capgras literature cold, including the autonomic response stuff and the cases where brain-damaged patients have anomalous experiences without forming delusions. There's a Bayes-brain person who I suspect thinks a one-factor account is basically right — anomalous prediction error plus ordinary inference gets you the delusion, no second "belief evaluation" deficit needed — and I don't know whether that helps me or hurts me. There's a phenomenologist who keeps citing Sass and says things like "delusion as transformation of the field of experience" in the coffee breaks. And there's a philosopher sympathetic to the view that delusions flunk every rational standard belief has, so they're beliefs only in a deflated sense — which might be a friendly amendment or might gut my thesis, I genuinely can't tell.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of these four people would actually phrase them (the clinician will be concrete and case-driven, the phenomenologist will be vague but dangerous). For each: what should I concede, what should I hold, and where my reply is weakest. I especially want to know whether my step (2) — the "ordinary beliefs are irrational too" move — survives the obvious comeback that delusions fail the norms in a different *way*, not just to a different degree, and I want the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, which I think has to do with double bookkeeping but I haven't been able to formulate crisply.
