I am a robotics and reinforcement-learning engineer, fluent in the vocabulary of Markov decision processes, transition and observation models, model-based planning, learned world models, partial observability, reward specification, and non-stationary environments, but I have never studied philosophy of mind or classical symbolic AI. I keep encountering references to the "frame problem" as something that was once considered a deep difficulty and is now, depending on whom you ask, either solved by learning or quietly forgotten.

Please do the following. First, reconstruct the frame problem as it arose for logic-based agents -- including the ramification problem (an action's knock-on effects), the qualification problem (the indefinite list of conditions under which an action succeeds), and the distinct worry that the hard part is not tracking what changes but knowing what is relevant to think about at all -- restating each as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary: as claims about transition functions, state and action spaces, world-model accuracy, planning under a finite compute budget, and the cost of model error. Use, as your running example, the classic thought experiment of the robot that correctly deduces it must pull its wagon out of a room to save its power source from a bomb, yet fails to infer that moving the wagon moves the bomb sitting on it; translate that failure precisely into a statement about what the agent's model and planner did and did not represent.

Second, give me the residue report. Which parts of the original problem translate cleanly into questions about model class, abstraction, and planning depth; which parts you had to distort to fit into the MDP formalism; and which parts -- if any -- have no expression at all in terms of transition models and reward functions. For whatever does not survive, say whether the loss reflects a genuine gap in the MDP vocabulary or whether the original claim was less precise than it appeared.

Third, and briefly: does the modern practice of learning a world model from data actually answer the frame problem, or does it merely relocate it -- into distribution shift, latent-variable choice, and the question of which abstractions the model was induced to learn? Where you think the honest answer is "we no longer know whether we have solved it or renamed it," say so.
