I found this explaination of costly signaling in a science book and I'm trying to figure out how much of it to trust:

“Costly signaling theory says a signal is believable when it would be too expensive to fake. A peacock’s tail, a degree from an elite college, an extravagant wedding, or a donation made publicly can all advertise some hidden quality -- health, intelligence, commitment, or generosity. According to the handicap principle, the signal has to handicap the sender by wasting resources; if it benefits them independently, it isn’t really a signal. Cost is what guarantees honesty, because weak or deceptive senders can never afford to imitate it. So an expensive gift is better evidence of love than a thoughtfully chosen cheap one, and conspicuous consumption is usually a rational way to prove wealth.”

My instinct is that this gets the broad idea right but smuggles in some stronger claims. Is it a fair summary? In particular, does the theory really require waste, does cost by itself make a signal impossible to fake, and does it imply that the more expensive option is always more credible? Please separate what a careful costly-signaling theorist would accept from what they'd qualify or reject.
