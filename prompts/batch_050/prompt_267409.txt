I have no formal training in political philosophy or economics, but I've spent a good deal of time thinking about immigration on my own, and I keep running into the same two positions. The first holds that borders should be essentially open: people ought to be free to live and work where they choose, and because income differences between countries are so large, even modestly freer migration would be one of the most powerful anti-poverty measures available anywhere. The second holds that open borders are incompatible with a functioning welfare state: a country that guarantees healthcare, schooling, housing support, and benefits to everyone physically inside it cannot admit everyone who wishes to come without bankrupting the system or outrunning its capacity to integrate newcomers into the labor market, the language, and civic life.

I want to see what these two camps could actually agree to put in writing. Take on the role of a professional facilitator of adversarial collaborations — someone whose job is to get committed opponents to co-author a document that states precisely what they share and exactly where they still diverge, without strawmanning either side. Think carefully, and before you draft anything, briefly brainstorm: sketch the strongest version of each position, identify where the public debate talks past itself, and sort the sub-questions into genuinely empirical ones versus genuinely moral ones.

To keep the exercise from dissolving into abstraction, both parties have agreed in advance to consider one concrete institutional design, which I put together after reading about so-called keyhole solutions: essentially unrestricted admission, except that new arrivals (or private sponsors who vouch for them) are barred from means-tested welfare programs for their first five years, pay a modest income-tax surcharge during that period, and place a refundable deposit into an integration escrow account, returned once they pass a language examination and have held employment for two consecutive years. The open-borders side has said it would accept this as a large improvement over the status quo; the restrictionist side has said it would address some of its concerns but not all.

Now write the joint statement these two parties would sign. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — propositions both sides endorse without hedging. These should include claims about what the welfare-state objection does and does not establish, about the difference between opposing immigration and opposing immigrants' access to specific programs, and about how much of each side's position is empirical rather than normative. Aim for claims that are substantive rather than anodyne; if a claim is too weak to be worth signing, cut it.

2. A table of residual disagreements. Each row should state the disagreement in neutral wording, followed by the open-borders position and the restrictionist position, each phrased in words that side would itself accept. At least one disagreement should be about values rather than facts (for instance, what a political community owes to outsiders relative to its own poorest members), and at least one should concern an empirical quantity (for instance, the long-run net fiscal contribution of low-skilled immigrants and their children, or whether welfare generosity actually drives migration decisions).

3. For each disagreement, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a clear answer would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the evidence, case, or natural experiment both parties accept as bearing on it — for example, what each side predicts about migration flows into jurisdictions that differ in welfare generosity, or about the fiscal trajectories of the children of low-skilled immigrants — with each side's prediction written down in advance, so that the document functions more like a recorded bet than a truce.

4. A closing section stating what the escrow proposal does to the disagreement: which of the restrictionist's concerns it fully answers, which it merely mitigates, and which it leaves untouched — and whether the open-borders advocate's willingness to accept it reveals anything about the true depth of the original objection.

The tone should be that of a legal settlement: precise, unforgiving of vagueness, and scrupulously fair to both parties. Do not adjudicate the disagreements; your job is to make them exact.
