so I keep circling back to the ticking time bomb debate and there's something in it that feels less like a hypothetical and more like a live design question about how we actually run things. Here's the setup I want analyzed. Most legal systems with torture bans treat the prohibition as exceptionless on the books -- no necessity clause, no ticking-bomb carve-out written into statute, and the international convention explicitly says no emergency whatsoever justifies it. And yet the same societies seem to quietly rely on the idea that if a *real* ticking case ever happened, the interrogator would just break the law and then get rescued by the messy back half of the system: a prosecutor declining to charge, a jury refusing to convict, an executive pardon, a judge finding some excuse. Meanwhile proposals like Dershowitz's torture-warrant idea -- where you'd get a judge to sign off before the fact -- get treated as more monstrous than the informal arrangement, even by people who'd expect the informal arrangement to actually be used.

Act as a professional moral philosopher who also understands how legal systems actually function (not just how they're supposed to), and think carefully before answering; maybe sketch the rival reconstructions before committing. What I want to know: what does this two-layer structure presuppose, philosophically? Some candidate readings I can see: (1) it presupposes that rules and their enforcement are different moral objects, and you can coherently will a rule you expect to be broken; (2) it presupposes something like rule-consequentialism where the ban's value is in never being *ratified* ex ante even if breached ex post; (3) it presupposes that judges/juries/pardons are a kind of moral filter that can be trusted with discretion the legislature can't be trusted to write down; (4) it's just incoherent buck-passing and the system presupposes nothing -- it's in denial. Reconstruct which view(s) the practice actually needs versus which it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it's genuinely underdetermined. Under (3) shouldn't we want the filter to be trained or at least acknowledged rather than pretending discretion doesn't exist?
