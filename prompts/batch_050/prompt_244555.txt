I'm an economist, mostly of the Hayek-adjacent institutional sort -- prices as information, dispersed and tacit knowledge, mechanisms that aggregate what no single participant knows, conventions nobody designed. I recently got into a long argument with a philosopher friend about an idea he called free-floating rationales. Roughly: the claim that there can be reasons for things without anyone ever having had or formulated those reasons. His stock examples: there is a reason bird wings have the shape they do, even though no bird and no designer ever reasoned it out; there are reasons behind social norms like queuing or property conventions that most participants couldn't articulate and nobody chose; on this view, natural selection and cultural evolution are processes that find, track, and refine reasons while containing no reasoner at all. The fight, as far as I could tell, is over whether this is literal or a manner of speaking -- whether reason-talk applied to mindless processes picks out something real in the world, of whether it's just causal-history talk dressed up in normative clothing, a metaphor that quietly misleads.

The thing is, this all sounded extremely familiar to me, because economists say structurally similar things constantly: the market knows things no market participant knows, the price system solves allocation problems nobody set out to solve, a convention can serve a function that's opaque to everyone who follows it (my friend brought up the fence whose purpose nobody remembers and therefore nobody should tear down, which is basically an old institutionalist point). So here's what I actually want. Restate the free-floating rationales debate, both the defenders' and the skeptics' main claims, in the vocabulary I genuinely work in: prices, signals, incentives, dispersed knowledge, equilibrium, selection pressure, revealed preference. I know this vocabulary cold, so don't simplify it. But be honest in the translation: don't just smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call the result a translation, and don't round the philosophers' claims off into whatever economic claim they happen to resemble.

Then, the part I care about most: the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in economic vocabulary? My own suspicion is that the biggest casualty is normativity -- a price system can aggregate information without that information being correct, and a convention can be stable without being good, whereas a rationale is supposed to be a *reason*, something that justifies and not just something that happened to persist. Is that a real loss, or does my framework already contain a covert stand-in for the normative part (fitness, efficiency, whatever survives selection) and just refuses to admit it? And where there is residue, tell me which diagnosis you buy: that my vocabulary is missing something it needs, or that the philosophers' reason-without-reasoner talk is less substantive than it sounds once you try to cash it out.
