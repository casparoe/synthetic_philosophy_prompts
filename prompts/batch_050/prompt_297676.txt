A friend I know from years of reading and occasionally commenting on rationalist forums (we met in a LessWrong comment thread, of all places) and I have been arguing about whether the state should license parents, in the spirit of LaFollette's old proposal. I am against licensing; she thinks my own commitments force me into it. To prove it, she took the principle I keep invoking against her — what Joel Feinberg called the child's right to an open future — and built the following argument, which I have to admit is uncomfortably clean. (1) Every child has a right against her parents that they not raise her in a way that closes off, before she can choose for herself, the major life options she would otherwise have had. (2) An option counts as closed off in the sense of (1) whenever the parents' choices make it the case that the adult the child becomes could not realistically choose that option, regardless of whether the closing works through physical constraint, withheld education, or psychological formation. (3) A sufficiently insular upbringing — controlled schooling, no uncensored contact with outsiders, one worldview taught as unquestionable fact — makes it psychologically unrealistic for the resulting adult to leave the community or adopt a secular life, even though she formally remains free to do so. (4) When a practice violates a serious right of a child, the state may coercively restrict that practice, as it does with other grave harms to children. (5) Therefore the state may prohibit, or make conditional on a license, insular upbringings of the kind in (3). So far I nod along. But then: (6) the principle in (2) cannot stop there, because every act of upbringing is an act of narrowing — choosing English as the language of instruction forecloses native fluency in Mandarin, raising a child in a militantly secular household makes it psychologically unrealistic for her to ever take monastic vows seriously, not starting violin at five forecloses the concert stage — and there is no option-neutral way to raise anyone, since the values a child is formed with determine which open options she would ever want to take. (7) So by (1)–(4), essentially all parenting stands in need of state authorization, and prospective parents should be licensed with their upbringing plans reviewed for option-narrowing. (8) But (7) is absurd, so something upstream has to go.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? My preference, if I get one, is to keep (1) and (3): I genuinely do think some upbringings wrong children in exactly this way, and I don't want a diagnosis that rehabilitates the cloister. I am most willing to sacrifice (2) or (6), but every weakening of (2) I have tried either lets the cloister back in or leaves the licensing conclusion untouched, and (6) honestly just looks true to me — which is precisely what worries me, since in my experience the premise that looks most obviously true is usually where the trap is. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs me. If the argument is actually valid and sound and I should just accept licensing, say that too rather than protecting my preferred conclusion. And since this is the sort of question where confident-sounding philosophy is cheap: say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the one step or claim in your own answer where you are least sure you have it right.
