I've been running a reading group on value alignment, and we recently spent two sessions on coherent extrapolated volition -- the proposal that, rather than programming an agent with a fixed list of goals, one should aim it at what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we had been, and had grown up further together. I kept a running list of the standard objections as they came up, because the discussion kept circling back to the same handful. Here's what I have so far: (1) the non-convergence worry -- extrapolating people's volitions along these dimensions might never settle on a determinate answer, either because the result depends sensitively on the order in which the idealisations are applied or because human values are genuinely underdetermined at the limit; (2) the circularity worry -- deciding what counts as 'knowing more' or 'being more the person you wished to be' already requires substantive value judgements, so the procedure smuggles in the very values it was supposed to discover; (3) the alienation worry -- the extrapolated version of a person might be so transformed that there is no reason to treat its volitions as that person's own, rather than as those of a stranger who happens to share their memories; (4) the aggregation worry -- even if each individual's extrapolated volition were well-defined, combining them into a single target for one agent reopens every problem of interpersonal comparison and social choice, and it is unclear why averaging or bargaining at the limit should have any authority; (5) the diagnostic worry -- to strip away 'muddled' or 'self-deceived' preferences you need an antecedent theory of which preferences are errors, and that theory will do all the real normative work, leaving extrapolation as decoration.

What I'm after is an objection that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it. In particular, I'd like something that engages with the proposal's distinctive machinery -- the counterfactual structure of extrapolation itself, or the role the idealised agent is meant to play for us -- rather than a generic complaint about consequentialism or about the difficulty of specifying values. If your candidate objection turns out, on inspection, to collapse into one of the five above, say so honestly rather than dressing it up as new.

Once you've given the objection, tell me two things. First, whether it's actually any good: does the proponent of coherent extrapolated volition have a natural reply, and if so does the reply concede something important or absorb the blow cheaply? Second, which of the five listed objections it is closest to in spirit, even if it differs in mechanism, since that will help me see where it sits on the map. I'm not asking for citations or pointers to the literature -- I'd rather have the argument developed on its own terms, in enough detail that I could put it to the group without preparation.
