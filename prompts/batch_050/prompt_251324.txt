I'm a third-year doing a joint major in philosophy and CS, and this semester I'm in a history of ethics seminar where we've been slowly working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, while on the other side of my schedule I'm TAing an intro reinforcement learning course. These two classes are colliding in my head in a specific way and I want help sorting it out.

The thing that got me: Sidgwick ends up in this place where practical reason seems divided against itself. Rational egoism (maximize your own good) and utilitarianism (maximize the general good) both rest on axioms he takes to be self-evident, their demands genuinely conflict in particular cases, and he can't find any deeper principle of reason to adjudacate between them. His only candidate for reconsiling them was the empirical hope that the universe happens to be set up (say, by a benevolent God) so that duty and self-interest always coincide, which he didn't think he was entitled to just assume. My prof calls this the dualism of practical reason and says it basically haunted Sidgwick for the rest of his life.

Here's the actual request. Restate this whole situation in the vocabulary I use in my other life: multi-objective optimization. I mean the real technical terms — objective functions, Pareto frontiers, scalarization, weighted-sum methods, lexicographic preferences, reward specification, that kind of thing. My gut says Sidgwick's problem is just "agent has two objectives and no weight vector," and the theological move is like hoping the environment's reward function happens to be arranged so the frontier collapses to a single point. But my prof (who knows zero ML) insists something essential would be lost in that translation, without being able to tell me what. So: translate as faithfully as you can, then give me a residue report. What survives cleanly? What only survives if you fudge it? And is there anything in the dualism that has no counterpart in optimization-speak at all? I'm especially wondering about the "both are rational" part — Sidgwick isn't saying an agent happens to have two preferences, he's saying each verdict has the authority of reason behind it, and I can't figure out how to even express that in a framework where objectives are just given from outside. If there is a residue, is it a defect of the optimization vocabulary, or does it show Sidgwick's problem was less substantive than it sounds?
