I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I need an impartial assessment of one student's exam answer. I should mention, by way of context only, that last week I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is a decisive blow against epiphenomenalism or merely an uncomfortable consequence its defenders can absorb. I will not tell you which side I took, and I do not want you to settle that dispute; I want the answer below graded strictly on its own merits, since my written comments will be returned to the student.

The exam question was:

Define epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and assess whether the paradox shows epiphenomenalism to be false.

The student's answer:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that phenomenal properties — what it is like to have an experience — are caused by physical processes in the brain but themselves cause nothing physical. The view is motivated by causal closure: physics is causally complete, so if qualia are non-physical, they cannot affect the physical world on pain of systematic overdetermination.

The paradox of phenomenal judgment arises because our judgments about our own experiences are themselves physical events. When I judge that I am seeing red, that judgment is a neural state, or an utterance, and as such it has a complete physical cause. On epiphenomenalism the experience of red plays no role whatsoever in producing the judgment. So the connection between my experiences and my judgments about them appears to be a massive coincidence: I judge that I have experiences, and I do have them, but the judgments would have been the same even if the experiences had been absent or entirely different. A zombie duplicate of me, lacking all experience, would accordingly fall silent on questions of consciousness, since nothing would prompt it to report experiences it does not have.

The paradox, in my view, amounts to a strict self-refutation. If epiphenomenalism were true, then our beliefs about consciousness would be caused by something other than consciousness, which contradicts the belief's content, since the belief is precisely that consciousness is what prompts it. A theory that entails that its own believers could not form the belief in it thereby refutes itself.

One response open to the epiphenomenalist is the common-cause reply: a single brain state causes both the experience and the judgment, so the judgment reliably covaries with the experience even though the experience does not cause it. But this reply concedes the central problem, since it admits that the judgment's accuracy is guaranteed only by the physical common cause and never by the experience itself."
