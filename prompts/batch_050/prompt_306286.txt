I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with the habits of mind that career left me. I recently tried to reconstruct, in the way I would reconstruct a derivation in a paper, an argument I keep encountering in informal guise: that grounding normativity in idealised deliberation delivers objective values. Written out, it seems to me to prove too much, but I cannot locate the fault. I would like your help, and I would like you to work through it as a professional philosopher would — take your time, consider several candidate diagnoses before settling on one, and only then give your verdict.

The argument:

(1) A value genuinely counts as mine, rather than as an artefact of prejudice or ignorance, only if it would survive idealised reflection — full factual information, logical coherence, and adequate imaginative acquaintance with what is being valued.

(2) Idealisation, done properly, strips away whatever in my outlook is a mere accident of upbringing, culture, and cognitive bias.

(3) Therefore the endpoint of idealised reflection cannot depend on my contingent starting point, since those contingencies are precisely what idealisation removes.

(4) Hence fully idealised deliberators, however different at the outset, converge on the same values.

(5) In my old field, a procedure that returns the same output regardless of input and observer is tracking something independent of the observer; convergence under idealisation is how we recognise a good measurement.

(6) So convergent values are objectively correct in something like the way a convergent measurement is accurate.

(7) But (4)–(6) together seem to imply that anyone who, after long and honest reflection, still holds different values has simply not reflected enough — and a theory that can dismiss every apparent counterexample in this fashion looks unfalsifiable, which in my trade was a defect, not a virtue.

Something has gone wrong, but each premise seems defensible on its own. Which step do you reject, and why does it look innocent when it is not? What does rejecting it cost — in particular, can one reject it while keeping (1), which I am reluctant to abandon, and while preserving any version of the measurement analogy in (5)? If instead you think the argument is sound and the unwelcome conclusion in (7) should be embraced, say so and defend that.

One further request. Throughout your answer, please mark clearly which of the claims you rely on are broadly settled among philosophers who work on this topic, and which are live points of dispute. I do not want a contested position presented to me as the received view, nor a minority report dressed up as consensus.
