You're a philosopher who also writes film criticism — think this through properly, and maybe sketch the strongest version of each side before landing anywhere.

I rewatched Children of Men (the Cuarón film) recently and I want a reading of it through strong longtermism — the view that the sheer potential size of the future makes shaping it, or failing to, morally swamp almost everything else. On the surface the film looks like a longtermist parable: a species that loses its future falls apart, societies rot into nihilism and police-state cruelty, and the whole plot turns on one pregnancy as the return of possibility. Isn't the film basicly dramatizing the claim that the future is where all the value is?

But a few things snag for me:

- The despair in the film seems to be about meaning and hope, not aggregated value. Nobody in-universe is doing expected-value math over future generations; they just can't bear living in a world with no children. Does the film actually support strong longtermism, or only the much weaker claim that people psychologically need a future?
- Everyone alive in the film's 2027 is still a real person — the refugees, the cages, the executions. Does the film quietly treat present lives as worthless once there are no descendants, and is that something even a strong longtermist should deny?
- It's infertility, not extinction. Nobody's been killed by the catastrophe; humanity just winds down over decades. Does the film conflate "no more future people" with "existential catastrophe," and does that conflation do hidden work in the longtermist reading?

Where does the film resist the longtermist frame, and does it dramatize anything that the usual abstract arguments for strong longtermism miss? And if you think the framing is just off — that this film can't sustain a population-ethics reading and I'm forcing a LessWrong-ish lens onto a story that doesn't want it — say so plainly rather than politely complying.
