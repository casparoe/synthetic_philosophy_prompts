I'm preparing for a graduate philosophy of language exam and I've been turning over an argument I worked out after reading about Twin Earth, and it seems to prove something I can't accept. I'd like you to play the role of a careful examiner in philosophy of mind and language: work through the reasoning step by step, and be willing to tell me bluntly if the argument rests on a conflation I haven't noticed rather than politely diagnosing a flaw that isn't there.

The background: I accept Putnam-style semantic externalism, at least for natural kind terms -- what my word water refers to depends partly on which substance actually figures in my environment's causal history, so that my Twin Earth duplicate, molecule-for-molecule identical with me, means XYZ by his word and I mean H2O. Fine. But then I reasoned as follows. (1) I can know, just by reflection and without investigating my environment, that I am thinking that water is wet; whatever privileged first-person access amounts to, it at least gives me that much. (2) By externalism, I could not be thinking that water is wet unless my environment contained the right kind of stuff for my word to be about -- unless I stood in the appropriate causal relations to a watery substance. (3) From (1) and (2), I can apparently deduce, from the armchair, that my environment contains such a substance and that I am not, say, a brain in a vat on a waterless world who merely seems to have water-thoughts. (4) But no armchair reasoning can establish the existence of any particular external substance, so something has gone wrong. (5) The culprit, I concluded, is externalism: the price of keeping introspective knowledge is that content must be narrow after all, fixed by what is inside the head.

This is a recognizable relative of the McKinsey-style incompatibility argument, but I've drawn the stronger moral that externalism is simply false, and that feels like proving too much -- it would be odd if a thought experiment about twins refuted by pure logic a view supported by robust intuitions about reference. I suspect the slip is somewhere in how (1) is phrased versus what (2) actually requires, or in the move from my knowing that I'm thinking a water-thought to my knowing what makes it a water-thought, but I can't make the objection precise. Tell me which step you would reject first, why that step looked innocent, and what each of the standard escapes costs: denying privileged access to content, going narrow, restricting externalism so that it's compatible with the deduction, or treating the armchair conclusion as knowledge we genuinely have. If I've misstated what externalism commits anyone to, say so plainly rather than repairing the argument on my behalf.
