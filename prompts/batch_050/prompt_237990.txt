Draft the joint statement that two philosophers could sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration, conducted in good faith, on hypocrisy and the standing to blame. The first party, the standing theorist, holds that a person who blames another for a wrong of a kind they themselves have committed thereby forfeits the standing to issue that blame, and that this defect genuinely diminishes the blame's normative force rather than merely embarrassing the blamer. The second party, the standing sceptic, holds that the aptness of blame turns entirely on the blamed party's culpability, and that facts about the blamer's own record bear at most on the blamer's virtue, or on the social advisability of their speaking up, never on whether the blame is warranted.

Structure the deliverable as follows. First, a numbered list of at least six agreed claims both parties can sign, including at least two claims about what hypocritical blame actually does or achieves and at least one claim each party would once have denied; these must be substantive rather than diplomatically vacuous. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each stated as a single proposition together with each party's verdict on it, every proposition worded so that the party whose view it captures would accept the wording as fair. Third, a cruxes section: for each disagreement, identify the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party, specify a case, argument, or consideration both accept as bearing on that sub-question, and record each party's prediction about what the case shows in advance of any verdict.

Both parties must also take explicit positions on the following two stipulated cases, which they designed jointly as stress tests.

Case A, prospective hypocrisy. Priya publicly censures a colleague for falsifying expense claims. Priya has never falsified a claim herself, but she has privately resolved to begin doing so next month, and she does. Standing theories are standardly framed in terms of the blamer's past or present wrongdoing; the collaboration must determine what, if anything, follows for blame issued in advance of the blamer's own matching wrong, and whether the intuitive verdict here differs from the standard hypocrite's case.

Case B, anonymous blame. An anonymous letter correctly accuses a member of a department of plagiarising students' work. The letter's author is a serial plagiarist, though nobody else knows this. Because no one can identify the blamer, no one can discount the blame on the basis of who issued it. The collaboration must address whether standing attaches to the blamer as a person, to the speech act itself, or to the audience's uptake, and what this case shows about which of those the standing theorist's view actually requires.

Conclude with a short joint methodological note on what the two cases reveal about whether standing, if it exists at all, is best understood as a condition on the truth or aptness of blame's content, on its normative force, or on the permissibility of issuing it. Write the entire statement in British English.
