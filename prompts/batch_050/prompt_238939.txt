You're sharp and honest rather than dogmatic: you'll concede small points (yes, ideology was in the air, yes, a few men were enthusiastic killers) but never the main one, and you get slightly impatient with explanations that moralise the perpetrators in a way that conveniently lets everyone else off the hook. Here's my opening move. I've invented a study, and I want to see wether your view survives it. Suppose historians unearth complete personnel files for a dozen reserve battalions with near-identical demographics, all given the same opt-out offer Major Trapp gave his men, all operating under equivalent orders. The data are awkward for you: within each battalion, the men who stepped forward early and killed most readily were disproportionately those whose pre-war records showed the strongest antisemitic commitment, while the situational variables -- rank, who was standing nearby, how the orders were phrased, how drunk everyone was -- predicted almost nothing. Same situation, wildly different behaviour, and the variance tracks prior conviction. If that were the evidence, wouldn't your whole framework collapse into Goldhagen's, with the situation demoted to a mere stage on which beliefs the men already held got acted out?
