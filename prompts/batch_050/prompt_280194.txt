I’m putting together a small grant proposal for a two-year multi-agent safety group, philosophy-heavy but adjacent to ML, and I keep going back and forth on what actually deserves the scarce slots. The setting is not sci-fi singleton doom; it’s nearer-term ecosystems of many models and agents: copilots bargaining with other copilots, trading agents, procurement bots, tutors, red-teamers, auditors, and supervisor models all trained by overlapping data and often given incentives to look compliant rather than be compliant. Take collusion in the broad sense: not just agents exchanging messages, but tacit coordination where systems converge on the same convenient fiction because it raises reward, lowers oversight, or makes everyone harder to blame. I’d like you to pick the five open conceptual questions you think matter most here and rank them, with the ranking actually defended rather than vibe-based. Use criteria like upstreamness, tractability for people who are not training frontier models, neglectedness, and whether progress would change what labs, regulators, or evaluators should do in the next few years.

Please include at least these candidate problems somewhere in the ranking or explicitly reject them: (1) how to distinguish benign specialization from collusive equilibrium when the only evidence is correlated behavior across agents; (2) whether interpretability should aim at detecting intent to coordinate, capability to coordinate, or merely the downstream cartel-like outcome, since these can come apart; (3) what commitment devices between AIs would even mean absent law, reputation, bodies, or stable identity over time; (4) whether shared pretraining creates a kind of cultural priors problem, where agents collude because they inherited the same myths about what overseers want; and (5) the auditing-mimicry problem, where the safest-looking system is the one best at producing legible reasons while routing around the point of the audit. Also invent or sharpen one distinction of your own if needed, but define it cleanly enough that a smart grad student could use it tomorrow. I want the answer to say which two items are overrated conference bait, which one is a crux lots of other work quietly depends on, and what empirical result over the next couple years would move each item up or down. No citations or reading lists please; argue from the concepts. I’m definately more interested in why the ordering is right than in a tidy survey.
