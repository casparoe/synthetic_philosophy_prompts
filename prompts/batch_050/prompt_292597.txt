Ahead of a graduate reading group on philosophy of mind, I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my settled views on mental causation so that the others can probe it for faults. I would like a dry run first. Here is what I am prepared to defend:

(1) Causal closure: every physical event that has a cause at a time has a sufficient physical cause at that time.
(2) Mental properties are not identical with physical properties. Multiple realisability defeats type-identity: an octopus, a human and a conceivable silicon system can all be in pain without sharing any single physical property.
(3) Nevertheless, token identity holds: each particular mental event just is some particular physical event occurring in a particular brain (or other substrate).
(4) Mental causation is real, and it is content-involving: I reach for the glass because of what I believe — because I believe it contains water — and not merely because of some state that happens to realise that belief.
(5) Actions are not systematically overdetermined. The ordinary case of raising my arm is not like the firing-squad case in which two assassins' bullets each suffice for the death; genuine overdetermination is rare, and it would be absurd to suppose every voluntary movement has two independent sufficient causes.
(6) Causation is at bottom a matter of counterfactual dependence or difference-making, not of some productive force transmitted from cause to effect.
(7) The causal claims of psychology stand or fall with those of the other special sciences: if exclusion-style reasoning threatens the causal efficacy of beliefs and desires, it threatens that of genes, synapses and interest rates in the same way, and higher-level causal talk is in general just as legitimate as lower-level causal talk.

Please audit this set for internal coherence. Go through the commitments and identify which of them pull against one another, ordering the tensions you find by severity. For each genuine tension, state precisely which commitments generate it, how serious the damage is, and what the cheapest revision would be — noting what that revision costs me in terms of the motivations that made the commitment attractive in the first place. I am equally interested in apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection: if some pairing looks problematic but is in fact perfectly coherent, say so and explain why, rather than flagging everything indiscriminately. Finally, tell me which single change to the set would restore the most consistency for the least theoretical cost. Do not assume I already know where the problem lies, and do not presume which commitment I would be willing to surrender.
