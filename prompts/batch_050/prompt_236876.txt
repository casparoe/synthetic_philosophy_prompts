I'm a second-year law student, and I've run into a puzzle that keeps recurring in different forms. Last month, in a discussion section about regulatory offenses, we were considering a municipal ordinance that fines shopkeepers for selling alcohol to minors even when the shopkeeper checked what looked like convincing ID and had every reason to believe the buyer was of age. I said this seemed unfair -- the careful shopkeeper and the negligent one get the same fine, and surely what you deserve should depend on what was going on in your head. Another student shut me down with the maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*, delivered as though it were a refutation: an act doesn't make someone guilty unless the mind is guilty, so the ordinance is illegitimate, end of discussion. Since then I've noticed the same maxim deployed against felony-murder rules, against certain drug-possession statutes, and even in a newspaper op-ed about environmental crimes, always with the same conversation-ending confidence.

The trouble is that the maxim seems to be doing different work in each of these settings, and I can't tell what it's even claiming. Taken one way, it looks like a straightforward description of the criminal law, in which case the ordinance we were discussing -- a real law, on the books, being enforced -- is an immediate counterexample. Taken another way, it looks like a definition of what the word "crime" properly means, in which case the ordinance wouldn't count as criminal law at all, which seems like an evasion rather than an argument. Taken a third way, it looks like a moral principle about when punishment is just -- but then I'd want to know precisely what the principle demands. Must the offender have known the facts? Known the law? Intended the outcome? Been in a position where they could have avoided the outcome through reasonable care? These give wildly different verdicts on our ordinance, since the careful shopkeeper presumably fails the first but passes the last.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this maxim might be expressing -- descriptive, definitional, interpretive (as a presumption courts apply when reading statutes), and normative -- and, for the normative versions, spell out what exactly the "guilty mind" requirement amounts to under different precisifications? For each reading, I'd like a verdict: which are trivial, which are plausible and defensible, and which are simply false as claims about how the law works. Most importantly, tell me which reading my classmate would have needed in order to actually refute my worry about the ordinance, and whether that reading is one anyone should accept. I want to be able to go back to the discussion section and press the right point rather than just trading Latin for intuitions.
