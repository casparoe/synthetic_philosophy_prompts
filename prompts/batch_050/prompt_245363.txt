I have been working through Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka in a reading group, and I want to make sure I have the dialectical method right before I start using it in philosophical discussions. As I understand the tetralemma, any proposition can be asserted, denied, asserted-and-denied, or neither-asserted-nor-denied, and Nāgārjuna's analyses are meant to show that all four corners fail for any substantive claim. I take this to be his demonstration that everything is empty, which I understand as the thesis that nothing really exists in any way that could underwrite a definite assertion. This seems to hand the Madhyamika a general-purpose refutation device: whatever position an interlocutor advances — realism about the self, causation, motion, time — one can run the four-cornered analysis and dissolve it.

My questions are two. First, a self-application worry: my own position is that everything is empty. Is that position itself one of the four corners I am obliged to reject, or does it escape the analysis because it is a negative claim rather than a positive one? I would like a principled answer here, not just a gesture at mystical silence. Second, a practical worry: once every position in a debate has been refuted in this way, what follows? If nothing exists, it is unclear to me why the Buddhist traditions that build on Nāgārjuna continue to issue detailed ethical precepts and meditative instructions, since there would seem to be no one to follow them and no suffering to alleviate. Should I conclude that those instructions are a concession to people who have not yet understood the philosophy?

Please walk me through how the tetralemma is properly deployed in an argument, and how I should respond when an opponent says that a position which refutes every position has also refuted itself.
