I'm a TA for a 200-level seminar called Animal Minds (16 students, 75-minute sessions) and I keep hitting the same wall. My students happily agree that octopuses and dogs are conscious, get squirmy around fish, and outright laugh at the idea that fruit flies might feel anything -- but when I press them on what evidence is doing the work in each case, it turns out it's the same kind of evidence every time: behavior plus a bit of physiology. The only thing that actually changes is how much the creature resembles us. I want them to feel that problem in their bones rather than just nod along to it.

I've gotten fixated on a sentence from early in Thomas Henry Huxley's 1874 essay "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History," where he's laying out the physiologist's research program:

"But the living body is not only sustained and reproduced: it adjusts itself to external and internal changes; it moves and feels. The attempt to reduce the endless complexities of animal motion and feeling to law and order is, at least, as important a part of the task of the physiologist as the elucidation of what are sometimes called the vegetative processes."

What strikes me is how casually "moves" and "feels" get yoked together in that sentence, when the slide from one to the other is exactly where all the trouble lives: you can observe motion in anything, but feeling has to be inferred, and the inference gets less comfortable the farther the animal sits from us. (Huxley's own essay is ambivalent about this slide in a way I find delicious, but that's a seperate conversation.)

So: design me a runnable classroom activity built around that sentence. My half-formed idea is some kind of committee game -- students get anonymized "case files" describing an entity's behavioral repertoire and nervous system in neutral functional terms and have to vote on whether it gets welfare protections, with the identities (which should definately include animals at varying distances from us, plus at least one human case and one non-biological ringer) revealed only after votes are locked. Take that or leave it, but whatever you design has to make participants run into the moves/feels slide themselves before anyone names it -- the problem should show up in what happens in the room, not in a lecture I give afterwards. Give me the actual materials: rules, roles, three or four example case files written out in full, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto Huxley's sentence and onto the other-minds problem more generally, what will predictably go wrong when I run it (students guessing identities early and gaming the vote, someone derailing into a semantic fight about the word "consciousness," that kind of thing) and how to handle each. End with an honest note about which feature of the passage the activity structurally can't reproduce, so I know what I still have to supply in discussion. Don't just describe the vibe -- I want something I could run next Tuesday.
