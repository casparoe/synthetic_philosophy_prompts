You are a philosopher with long experience translating normative theory into decision procedures for non-specialists, and you think carefully about where a principle survives translation and where it breaks. Before drafting, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: what the underlying principle actually commits us to, which parts of it are doing the real work, and where any procedure based on it is bound to misfire. Then produce the deliverable described below.

Here is my situation. I have no academic training in philosophy, but a few years ago I came across the famous thought experiment about the child drowning in a shallow pond -- the one arguing that if you would wade in to save the child at the cost of ruining your shoes, then distance and strangeness shouldn't matter, and you owe something similar to people dying far away whom you could save at comparable cost. I've thought about it a lot on my own, and I mostly buy it, but I've noticed I apply it wildly inconsistently in practice.

I run a small charitable fund at my company -- about forty colleagues contribute, and I decide, roughly once a week, which requests and opportunities we act on: disaster relief appeals, a coworker's GoFundMe for a relative's surgery, an effective-altruism-style malaria charity, a family in our town facing eviction, and so on. Each decision takes me maybe an hour, the amounts involved range from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, and I have to justify my choices to the contributors at a quarterly meeting, so the reasoning has to be explainable to people who have never heard of the pond.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure -- a checklist or ordered sequence of tests -- that operationalizes the pond principle for these weekly decisions. Specifically:

1. Translate the principle into concrete questions I can ask of any case, in order, with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer to each. The principle as I understand it has at least three moving parts -- that the harm is severe, that my cost is small by comparison, and that nothing morally relevant changes just because the victim is distant or a stranger -- and I suspect my inconsistencies come from smuggling extra conditions in (visibility of the victim, whether I'm uniquely positioned, whether others are also failing to act). The procedure should make those smuggled conditions visible rather than silently keeping or discarding them.

2. Tell me how to handle the points where the procedure genuinely can't produce an answer: for instance, how much cost counts as "small," whether the number of other potential rescuers changes my duty, and what to do when the best interventions are statistical (mosquito nets) rather than identified individuals. For each such point, say plainly: this is a judgment call the procedure cannot eliminate, and here is a defensible default.

3. Then run the procedure on three worked examples: the coworker's relative's surgery fund, the malaria charity, and a case I supply -- a disaster appeal where the news coverage is intense but the need per dollar appears worse than ongoing malaria prevention. At least one of these should land on an answer I will find uncomfortable (my guess is the procedure will rank the emotionally compelling local cases below the statistical ones -- show me whether it actually does, rather than softening the result).

4. Close with an honest section on what the procedure cannot do: where the pond argument itself has known pressure points (demandingness, the aggregation of small costs across many cases, the difference between rescuing and preventing), and which of my weekly decisions will therefore still require judgment even after the checklist is complete.

Write the procedure so that someone with no philosophy background could run it in under an hour, but don't sand off the distinctions that matter -- I'd rather have a procedure that sometimes says "judgment required" than one that pretends to settle everything.
