I'm a novelist working on a book in which Mozi, the Mohist philosopher, is somehow transported to the present day and must navigate contemporary institutions with his doctrine of impartial care (jian ai) intact. One episode has him volunteer with an organization that triages disaster relief, and I want to dramatize a genuine clash of intuitions rather than a cartoonish one, so I'd like your help thinking through his likely positions before I write the scene.

Here's the specific scenario I'm building around. The organization uses an algorithm to allocate scarce resources — say, a limited stock of antiviral medication during an outbreak — across hospitals in different regions. The algorithm is deliberately blind to whether recipients are kin, compatriots, or strangers; it optimizes purely for expected lives saved per dose. Mozi, I suspect, would approve of the impartiality. But complications pile up quickly. The algorithm gives no special weight to children of the relief workers themselves, which causes a mutiny among the staff. It also turns out that allocating purely by efficiency means some remote communities get nothing at all, because delivering doses there is too costly — so perfect impartiality in principle yields outcomes that look, in practice, like neglect of the worst-off. And a Confucian character in the novel (a modern scholar who defends graded love, the view that our obligations diminish with relational distance) argues that the staff mutiny reveals something deep: that care which refuses all gradation is psychologically unsustainable and perhaps not even desirable, since particular loves are the seedbed from which any concern for strangers must grow.

My questions: First, how do you think Mozi would actually respond to each of these three complications — the mutiny, the neglected remote communities, and the Confucian seedbed objection? I'd like answers grounded in the logic of his actual position (including his tendency to defend impartial care on consequentialist grounds — that universal concern produces order and benefit where partiality produces strife), rather than generic speculation about what a nice person would say. Second, is there a point in this scenario where you think the Mohist position genuinely runs out of resources — where even a sympathetic reconstruction of Mozi would have to either bite an implausible bullet or quietly smuggle in graded concern? That failure point, if it exists, is where I want the dramatic climax of the chapter to land.
