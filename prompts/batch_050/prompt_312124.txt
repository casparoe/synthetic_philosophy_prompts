Since inheriting a small logistics firm this spring, I have been making consequential decisions far faster than I used to, and I have started keeping a private ledger of when ending a search early felt legitimate and when it did not. My gut verdicts are these. Buying a laptop: I took the first model meeting my specs within budget, twenty minutes, no regrets. Signing a lease: I took the fourth apartment I viewed because it cleared my bar and the deadline loomed; fine. Hiring a dispatcher: I made an offer to the third interviewee, clearly competent, knowing a stronger candidate might have applied the following week; acceptable, though this is the one I keep returning to. My surgeon: I would be appalled to learn she used the first workable technique rather than the best available one; stopping early seems inexcusable. Marriage: a friend described her fiancé as having cleared every item on her list, and something in me revolted at applying apartment-search logic to a person. Retirement savings: I chose the default low-cost index fund after one evening, and trying to optimize felt like a trap rather than a duty. Jury service last year: settling on "he probably did it, let's go home" would have been abhorrent.

What principle, if any, sorts these the way my gut does? The hard pair for me is the dispatcher versus the surgeon and the juror: all three affect third parties, yet I excused myself and would not excuse them. Please state a principle precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed (a pilot deciding whether to divert in deteriorating weather, a grant committee skimming a large applicant pool); and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. At most 400 words.
