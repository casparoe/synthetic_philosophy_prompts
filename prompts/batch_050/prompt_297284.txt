In a long-form magazine essay on the prospects of artificial intelligence that a colleague has circulated at work, the author presents the case against machine minds as follows:

'Turing showed that a computer can do anything achievable by following explicit rules, and any rule-governed reasoning can in principle be written down as a formal system. Gödel then proved, in 1931, that every logical system rich enough to contain arithmetic includes true statements that the system itself can never prove — and which any competent mathematician, once shown the statement, can simply see to be true. From this Penrose draws the decisive consequence for artificial intelligence: a human mathematician can always step outside the formal system and grasp a truth that eludes it, whereas the machine cannot, so human mathematical understanding cannot be reproduced by any computer program, however advanced. Since computation therefore cannot account for the mind, consciousness must depend on non-computable physics, which Penrose locates in quantum processes within the brain's microtubules. In short, Gödel demonstrated that minds are not machines, and Penrose has supplied the physics of what they are instead.'

Something about this troubles me, though I cannot put my finger on exactly where it goes wrong. The essay is written by a science journalist with a mathematics degree, and the overall shape of the argument — incompleteness, the mathematician stepping outside the system, the conclusion about machines — is one I have encountered elsewhere, so I do not want to dismiss it out of hand. But the closing sentence feels stronger than anything Gödel himself would have claimed, and I have a vague memory that his own stated view was more cautious and hedged.

Please audit the passage sentence by sentence. For each claim, tell me: whether a logician or philosopher of mathematics would accept it as it stands; whether it is a defensible popular simplification that loses something important (and if so, what precisely is lost, in terms of the assumptions the actual theorems require); or whether it misattributes or overstates a result. In particular, I would like to know: whose argument this actually is, as distinct from what Gödel proved; what has to be assumed about the formal system, and about the mathematician, for the 'stepping outside' move to work; and what the strongest defensible version of the anti-mechanist conclusion would be once the passage is corrected — including whether it rules out the possibility that a mind is a machine whose specification we happen not to know.

Finally, assess the two inferences the essay draws at the end: that no computer program could reproduce human mathematical understanding, and that consciousness must therefore involve non-computable physics of the microtubule kind. Do either of these follow from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, and where would a defender of Penrose say the real argumentative weight lies?
