I'm teaching the philosophy of economics week of my phil of science seminar and I want to build the session around the 2008 financial crisis as a test case for Friedman's methodology of positive economics. The students will have read the essay, so they know the famous moves: assumptions shouldn't be judged on "realism" at all, only on the accuracy of the theory's predictions, and the more significant a theory the more unrealistic its assumptions will tend to be -- the billiards players acting as if they solve the physics, leaves arranging themselves as if each one maximizes its light.

Here's what I want from you as a careful case analysis. Take the standard critique that surfaced after the crash: macro models with representative agents and rational expectations, no financial sector worth the name, efficient-market assumptions baked into both the models and the regulatory culture, Greenspan's congressional testimony where he admitted to finding a flaw in his worldview, the Queen's why-didn't-anyone-see-it-coming question at the LSE. The critics' complaint was basically that the assumptions were fantasy -- people aren't hyperrational calculators, markets aren't self-correcting, there were no banks in the models. Map that critique onto Friedman properly: is the "unrealistic assumptions" charge one Friedman is committed to dismissing, and if so, does the crash give the critics a *better* complaint in Friedman's own terms, namely predictive failure? Or is there a problem with that too -- I'm struck by the thought that these models weren't really in the business of making conditional predictions about crises at all (a crisis is almost by definition out-of-sample, and the models were being used more as reassurance and as guides for institutional design during the so-called Great Moderation), in which case Friedman's criterion may not even get a grip on them. If the criterion can't be applied, is that a defense of the models or the deepest problem with them?

Then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to Friedman's position? Is it a confirmation (the models failed predictively, exactly as his criterion demands), an anomaly, or a kind of case he never anticipated? One wrinkle I'd like pressed: Friedman wrote for a context where the theory doesn't much change the phenomena (planets don't read physics), but financial models get used by the actors inside the system being modeled -- risk models shape the risks taken. Does prediction-first methodology survive that, and where exactly is Friedman silent rather than refuted? I don't want a hatchet job in either direction; if the honest answer is that the crash neither vindicates nor refutes the F-twist but shows its limits, say that and locate the limits precisely.
