I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long piece on the psychology of sad art. Two of my sources gave me essentially the same analogy, and it is doing real argumentative work in my draft, so I'd like it audited before I publish.

A psychologist of emotion told me that our enjoyment of tragedy is a case of what he called benign masochism: we enjoy sad films the way we enjoy roller coasters, chili peppers, and ice-cold showers. On his picture, the negative experience is real but discounted because we know, at some level, that we are safe; the pain is the price of admission, and the pleasure outweighs it. A literary scholar independently reached for the same image and drew a stronger conclusion from it: once you see that tragic sorrow is just roller-coaster fear in a cinema seat, the old philosophical puzzle about why we deliberately seek out art that makes us miserable simply dissolves. There is nothing left to explain, she said, beyond the arithmetic of costs and benefits.

Please treat the roller coaster analogy as an argument and audit it accordingly. Concretely:

1. Give me the correspondence table. In the case of sitting through a tragedy that moves you to tears, what plays the role of the safety harness, the manufactured danger, the adrenaline, the rider's knowledge that the ride will shortly end, and the queue of people paying to be frightened?

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't. Three candidates I want assessed, among any others you find: on a roller coaster, the fear seems to depend on some felt possibility of genuine danger, whereas the weeping cinema-goer fully believes that no one is dying; on the ride, the fear and the thrill seem merely to coexist and could in principle be separated, whereas in tragedy the sorrow seems to be a constituent of what we value rather than a cost we grudgingly tolerate; and enjoyment of a given roller coaster tends to fade quickly with repetition in a way that rereading or rewatching a beloved tragedy apparently does not.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports. In particular, does it smuggle in a ledger model of experience -- separable pleasures and pains that can be summed to a net balance -- and does the phenomenology of tragedy actually fit that model? If the sorrow and the satisfaction are not independently measurable entries, what happens to the cost-benefit arithmetic my second source relies on?

4. Deliver the verdict. Does the strong conclusion -- the puzzle dissolves, nothing remains to be explained -- survive the audit? Does some weaker conclusion survive instead? And if the analogy was carrying more weight than it can bear, what would be a better one for the phenomenon as it actually presents itself?

I'm not looking for a bibliography, reading list, or names to drop; I want the analysis itself, stated in terms an educated general reader could follow.
