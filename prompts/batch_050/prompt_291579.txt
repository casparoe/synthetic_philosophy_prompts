I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've got a podcast interview coming up with a philosopher who defends the higher-order thought theory of consciousness -- the view that a mental state is conscious because the subject has a suitable (noninferential, assertoric, non-observational) representation of themselves as being in that state. My problem: she's done a hundred interviews where she just explains the basics and name-drops the inner-sense comparison. I want to actually press the theory.

Draft me 8-10 interview questions that engage the real pressure points. I definately want to hit these, in whatever order you think builds best:

- The targetless/misrepresentation worry: if a higher-order thought can represent a first-order state that doesn't exist (or gets its content wrong), what fixes the phenomenology in those cases -- the higher-order state, the first-order one, some mix? Does the theory commit her to conscious experience with nothing underneath it?
- The "rock" style objection: becoming conscious of a rock doesn't make the rock conscious, so why should becoming conscious of a mental state make it conscious? She'll say the theory only explains state-consciousness, not creature-consciousness -- I want a follow-up that pushes on whether that's a real answer or a re-description.
- Animals and infants: higher-order thought seems to require possessing concepts of mental states, and there's decent reason to think lots of creatures lack those. Does she bite the bullet? Her likely move is that thin or recognitional concepts are enough -- help me push on whether that move quietly collapses the theory into a first-order view.
- The noninferential condition: if I consciously think about my nonconscious headache, why doesn't that make it conscious? Is the "noninferential" rider principled or is it patched in to block counterexamples?
- What would actually distinguish this empirically from first-order or global-workspace rivals -- prefrontal lesion studies, no-report paradigms, that whole literature. End with: what evidence could count against the view, and if nothing could, what does that say?

For each question give me the follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, not the reply I hope for. And one more thing: when you draft these, mark which of these objections are genuinely open disputes -- where even people who work on this disagree about whether HOT has an adequate answer -- versus which ones defenders broadly consider handled and critics mostly accept the response to. I don't want to frame a question as a devastating gotcha when the field thinks it's settled, or treat something as settled when it's actually contested.
