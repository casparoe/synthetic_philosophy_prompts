So I'm in a seminar on the ethics of memory right now and we've just done Margalit's The Ethics of Memory alongside Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, and my prof keeps pushing us to test the theories against fiction. I picked The Giver by Lois Lowry (yeah the middle school book, but honestly it's perfect for this): a community that's engineered away war, hunger, grief, even color, by offloading ALL collective memory onto one person, the Receiver, and by giving everyone else pills that blunt feeling and desire. Jonas becomes the new Receiver, gets the memories, and it ruins him for the community.

Here's the essay I have to write and I'm stuck: read The Giver as a contest between Margalit and Rieff. The obvious reading is that Lowry is a Margalit partisan -- Jonas only becomes capable of real, "thick" relationships (with Gabriel, and arguably with the Giver himself) once he has memory, and the community's memorylessness is what lets them "release" babies and old people without horror. Rieff looks like the villain's manifesto. But I think that reading is too easy. Rieff's case for forgetting is therapeutic and forward-looking -- the point is that memory-cults keep wounds open -- and the community DID achieve peace and the end of starvation, which Rieff could say is forgetting working exactly as advertised, without any ressentiment festering. So: does the novel actually refute Rieff, or just refuse to look at his strongest case?

And the thing I really can't figure out, and want the reading to take seriously: Rieff defends forgetting as something a person or community does to itself after experience, but in The Giver the forgetting was done generations ago, so no one alive ever consented or even knows what was lost -- Jonas literally cannot grieve what he never had until the Giver hands it to him. Does that intergenerational version of forgetting break Rieff's argument, or is all collective forgetting like that (nobody consents to being born into a culture that stopped commemorating something)? Also, is the Receiver himself a Margalitian figure -- the one who remembers on behalf of everyone who can't -- and is that arrangement (one rememberer, a whole society of forgetters) something Margalit's framework can even make sense of, since he thinks shared memory is partly what constitutes a community in the first place? If the "community" in the book isn't really a community by Margalit's lights, does the novel accidentally concede Rieff's point that you can have peace without moral community, and the real question is just whether peace is worth it?

End with where the novel genuinely resists both of them, if it does -- I want to argue that Lowry smuggles memory back in through color and music and physical pain rather than through history or testimony, which is a version of memory neither Margalit nor Rieff really theorizes, but I'm not sure that holds up. Be honest about where I'm overreading.
