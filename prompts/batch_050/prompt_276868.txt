I am a Year 12 student, and for our school philosophy society I have volunteered to defend David Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism at next term's debate evening. The argument, as I understand it, rests on four claims: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to benefit from that absence, but the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is deprived of it. When I presented this to my friends at lunch, their objection was predictable: they said the fourth claim is just asserted, not argued for, and that ordinary people clearly do think it is a shame, in some sense, when a happy life goes uncreated. My teacher suggested that the best way to settle who is right is not to trade intuitions about the abstract claims but to construct a case in which the fourth claim does all the work, so that we can see what denying it actually costs. I have tried to build such a case myself and failed twice, so I am asking for help.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The scenario must be one in which accepting Benatar's fourth claim and denying it deliver different verdicts, and in which that claim is the only thing separating the two verdicts: someone who accepts the first three claims but rejects the fourth must be committed to saying there is a genuine moral reason (perhaps not an all-things-considered duty, but at least a real pro tanto reason) to bring a happy person into existence, while someone who accepts all four is committed to saying there is not. The scenario must be realistic rather than science-fictional: a prospective parent or couple making a decision, a doctor advising one, or something of that sort, with no imagined machines that manufacture possible people, no oracles with perfect knowledge of the child's future, and no stipulation that the child would be extraordinarily happy or miserable. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and do not stipulate the verdicts within it; they have to fall out of the premises.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: state what verdict follows if the fourth claim is accepted, what follows if it is rejected, and why the first three claims alone are not enough to generate either verdict. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed -- does the disagreement between the two positions disappear, or does it merely relocate? Finally, since I will be facing an opponent rather than an empty chair, tell me how someone who rejects the asymmetry would attack the case itself rather than the abstract claim: is there a way to deny that my scenario is the right test, for instance by insisting that the moral reason to create happy people exists but is simply too weak to matter?
