For more than forty years, political philosophers have argued about a single qualifying clause in John Locke's chapter "Of Property" in the Second Treatise of Government: the requirement that appropriation from the common stock be legitimate "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others." The interpretive options are well mapped. On a weak reading, associated with Robert Nozick, the proviso merely forbids appropriations that leave others worse off than they would have been had the resource remained unowned, and is in practice almost never violated. On stronger egalitarian readings, associated with left-libertarians such as Michael Otsuka, the proviso requires something like an equal share of worldly resources, or compensation to others for what one takes. Between these sit sufficiency readings (A. John Simmons) on which one must leave others enough for a decent opportunity set but not an equal one. Underlying the whole dispute is a further exegetical split between those who read Locke's original common as a merely negative community — the earth simply unowned — and those who read it as a positive community in which mankind jointly owns the earth, so that appropriation would seem to require everyone's consent. Every move in this debate has been in print for decades; the positions have not converged, and as far as I can tell each side now grants the other most of its textual evidence and digs in anyway.

What interests me is that Locke himself seems to anticipate and dismiss the strongest readings of his own clause. In §28 he argues against requiring universal consent for appropriation: "If such a consent as that was necessary, man had starved, notwithstanding the plenty God had given him." And in §26 he writes that, the earth being given to men for their support, "there must of necessity be a means to appropriate them some way or other, before they can be of any use, or at all beneficial to any particular man." The necessity argument here — appropriation must be possible on pain of starvation, so no condition on appropriation can be one that human beings could not in practice meet — looks as though it rules out both the consent requirement and, perhaps, any demanding egalitarian reading of the proviso. Yet defenders of strong readings are perfectly familiar with these passages and are unmoved, which suggests to me that the debate is not really about what Locke's text says.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is a normative disagreement wearing exegetical clothes: the parties actually disagree about which baseline the proviso implicitly invokes — welfare compared to continued common ownership, opportunity for welfare, equal per-capita shares, or the consent of the affected — and since Locke never states a baseline, the text underdetermines the question, so each side projects its own independently held theory of distributive justice onto the clause and then argues about Locke. An alternative diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions under one heading: Nozick is asking what the proviso forbids, his egalitarian critics are asking what positive duty of justice acquisition triggers, and these can both have answers that never meet.

What I want is a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, not another verdict on which reading is correct. Specifically: Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions sharing one label? Is it really about Locke exegesis at all, or is an independent dispute about baselines and the point of a property-constraining principle doing all the work? Does the necessity argument in the passages quoted above genuinely constrain the range of tenable readings — and if it does, what does the persistence of strong readings in the face of it show about what the debaters take themselves to be doing? Finally, given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress — a sharper distinction (for instance, between the proviso as a condition on initial acquisition and as a standing constraint on holdings), a decision procedure for choosing baselines, a demonstration that the text settles the matter after all — or should we conclude that no progress is available and say why?
