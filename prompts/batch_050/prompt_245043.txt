I'm an undergraduate currently taking a course on phenomenology and existentialism, and we've just worked through the equipment analysis in Division One of Being and Time: the hammer example, the distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand, the way tools recede into their assignment-contexts when they work, and the three modes of breakdown (conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, and obstinacy) through which malfunctioning equipment and, with it, the "world" itself announce themselves. For the course's lighter assessment, I'd like you to write a piece of satire grounded in this material.

Write a training manual for newly hired technicians at a fictional IT support firm whose entire business model rests on Heidegger's account of breakdown. The firm's founder has concluded that properly functioning equipment is phenomenologically invisible — transparent, absorbed into the tasks it serves — and therefore that clients never genuinely encounter their own technology until it fails. The company accordingly sells "disclosure services": scheduled conspicuousness events, on-demand obtrusiveness (the strategic removal of essential equipment), and a premium obstinacy package (a device that neither works nor can be set aside, blocking an urgent task). Aim for roughly 600–700 words.

The humor must come from getting the phenomenology right rather than from generic absurdity. Please include: instructions that a functioning mouse, server, or printer must never be "noticed," and that any technician caught admiring working equipment has lapsed into the theoretical attitude the firm exists to cure; an explanation that staring at an isolated broken device is the least revealing stance available, since a device is disclosed only within the whole nexus of cables, software, deadlines, and the toward-which of the client's work; the firm's billing doctrine that every support ticket is a minor ontological event, because the client's worldhood lights up precisely when the assignment-structure is interrupted; and a glossary translating the three grades of breakdown into company jargon. I'd also like a short closing section in which the ethics department objects that the firm is merely breaking people's things, followed by the founder's rebuttal that breakdown only ever reveals a dependency-structure that was, as he puts it, "already in operation."

Keep the tone deadpan-corporate throughout: procedure codes, bullet points, a compliance note or two. The piece should remain readable to someone who has never read Heidegger while landing hardest for someone who has — exaggerate real features of the account (the transparency of the ready-to-hand, the priority of circumspect concern over detached staring, world announcing itself in malfunction) rather than inventing traits the account doesn't have. Please don't use any direct quotations from Heidegger's text; render everything in the firm's own managerial idiom, German terms permitted in parentheses where the glossary requires them.
