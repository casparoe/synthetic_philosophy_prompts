I'm prepping the final for my upper-level undergrad seminar on social ontology and I want the long essay question to be on generations -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, the whole menagerie. Here's the frame I gave the class mid-semester, which the question should lean on. We distinguished three things the word "generation" might be picking out:

1. Calendar cohorts -- sets of people defined by nothing but falling inside some birth-year interval (say 1981-1996). No claim that anything unifies them beyond the interval itself.
2. Imprint cohorts -- sets of people unified by living through the same formative events (a war, a financial crash, the arrival of the internet) at roughly the same age, so the event marks them in a way it doesn't mark people born a bit earlier or later.
3. Echo cohorts -- sets of people whose unity comes mostly from the label itself: once journalists and marketers start telling you you're a Millennial, you start acting like one, employers treat you like one, policy gets written about you, and the category starts producing the very traits it was only supposed to describe.

(The names are mine, they're not standard terms, so dont go hunting for them in the literature or complain that some author uses them differently.)

Write me ONE exam question for this course, aimed at students who've done the reading but are prone to reciting it, together with (a) a model answer and (b) a marking scheme out of 40 marks.

The question needs to be built around what I take to be the real crux of the topic: the echo-cohort mechanism cuts both ways. On one side, a kind whose members' traits are caused and held in place by stable feedback loops looks MORE like a real kind, not less -- arguably that's how plenty of respectable social kinds work (money, nations, maybe race). On the other side, if the differences between Millennials and Gen Z are manufactured by the labels, then the boundaries get set by journalism rather than by anything in the world, the edges drift every few years as usage shifts, and someone born in December 1980 ends up essentially different from someone born in February 1981 -- which starts to look like a reductio of the whole enterprise. I want a question that forces students to take a stand on which horn wins, using the three-way distinction above, instead of letting them write "generations are socially constructed, and here's what social construction means" for five pages.

Constraints:

- 90-minute in-class exam, closed book, one essay worth 40 of the exam's 100 marks. Students will have seen the three-way distinction before (it's on the syllabus) but won't have seen the question.
- The model answer should be a sketch, maybe 600-800 words, showing the arc of a top answer: what moves it makes, which horn it defends (pick one -- don't model a fence-sitter, though the scheme should say how to mark a good fence-sitter), and how it handles at least two of these hard cases: cuspers (people born right on a boundary), cross-national bleed (the events that supposedly define the cohorts are mostly American), and the drifting-boundary problem.
- The marking scheme should be in bands (first / 2:1 / 2:2 / third, or your local equivalent), and for each band name the specific moves that get you there and the specific mistakes that keep you out. It especially needs to penalize two failure modes I see every year: (i) treating "socially constructed" as a synonym for "not real" without argument, and (ii) sliding into the psychology of generational stereotypes (are Millennials actually lazy? etc.) instead of doing the ontology.
- Also give me 3-4 "trap" answers: short descriptions of plausible-looking responses that should score poorly, with a sentence each on why they look good but aren't.

Don't write the question so that it requires citing specific authors by name -- it's closed book and I don't grade on name-dropping. Students can gesture at positions without attributing them.
