I'm a journalist who covers science and technology for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about deepfakes and what philosophers have started calling the epistemic backstop -- the idea that audiovisual recording used to function as a kind of floor under public trust, because even when testimony was disputed you could in principle fall back on the tape. The worry, obviously, is that cheap, convincing synthetic media dissolves that floor: not only can fake video circulate, but real video loses its force because anyone caught on camera can just shrug and say it's a deepfake (what people call the liar's dividend). For the piece I've been collecting the standard proposed responses to this problem, and my editor says my list reads like every other article on the topic. Here's what I have so far: (1) technical provenance schemes, where cameras cryptographically sign footage at capture and platforms check the chain of custody, like the C2PA/content-credentials approach; (2) detection -- training classifiers to spot synthetic artifacts and hoping to stay ahead of the generators; (3) shifting trust from the artifact to the institution, i.e., we believe footage because a reputable outlet or a court vouches for it rather than because video is inherently hard to fake; (4) legal and platform-level remedies, like labeling mandates, liability for malicious synthetic media, and takedown regimes; and (5) a kind of adaptationist shrug -- the claim that publics adjusted to photoshop and to forged documents before, and will simply recalibrate to treat video the way we already treat text, as evidence only as good as its source.

What I'd like from you is one more response to the erosion of the backstop that isn't on this list and isn't a repackaging of one of these -- so not "detection but better," not "provenance plus regulation combined," and not a special case of trusting institutions. It can be a technical idea, a social or epistemic practice, or something more conceptual, like a reframing of what the backstop ever was. Ideally it should be something a clever grad student could plausibly propose, not pure science fiction, but I'm okay with speculative if it's clearly flagged as such.

One thing I want to be careful about, since my fact-checkers are thorough: when you explain your new proposal and how it differs from the five above, please be explicit about which underlying claims are pretty settled among people who study this stuff (say, that the liar's dividend is a real phenomenon, or that detection arms races favor the generator) and which are genuinely contested or empirically open (say, how much the public will actually distrust video, or wether provenance schemes can ever get the adoption they'd need). Don't present a contested empirical guess as established consensus just because it makes the piece tidier -- I'd rather have a messier but accurate picture of where the disagreement actually sits.
