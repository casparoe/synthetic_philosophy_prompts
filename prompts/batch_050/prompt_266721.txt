I have no background in history or philosophy beyond podcasts and falling down youtube rabbit holes, but I've spent the last year obsessing over how the Roman republic fell apart and whether it tells us anything about now, and I've noticed I have strong gut feelings about which comparisons are illuminating and which are lazy, and I can't figure out what rule my gut is following. So here are my verdicts, you tell me the principle.

Comparing Tiberius Gracchus getting rid of his fellow tribune Octavius (which nobody had done before but technically maybe wasn't illegal) to the US Senate refusing to even hold a vote on a supreme court nominee: illuminating, because both are about unwritten rules everyone assumed didn't need to be written. Comparing Trump to Caesar: lazy. Caesar spent a decade conquering Gaul with an army personally loyal to him, the situations have nothing in common mechanically. But then, comparing the big slave-worked estates swallowing up small farms in Italy to Amazon swallowing up small businesses: illuminating, at least to me. Even though the mechanisms there are just as different as in the Caesar one, so good luck squaring those two. Comparing the grain dole to modern welfare: lazy, it's basically always used as an insult rather than an analysis. Comparing the senate's emergency decree against Gaius Gracchus, the senatus consultum ultimum thing, to modern emergency powers that never quite get switched off: illuminating. Comparing the populares to cable news hosts: lazy, pure vibes. Comparing Pompey getting handed huge commands by popular vote over the senate's objections to a modern president expanding executive power because the voters like him: illuminating.

Part of what got me going on this was a passage in Appian's Civil Wars (book 1, I read it in the old Horace White translation on some free classics website) where he says: "The sword was never carried into the assembly, and there was no civil butchery until Tiberius Gracchus, while serving as a tribune and bringing forward new laws, was the first to fall a victim to internal commotion; and with him many others, who were crowded together at the Capitol round the temple, were also slain." And then right after he adds that after this "unseemly violence prevailed almost constantly, together with shameful contempt for law and justice."

So Appian's whole move is: find the first time an unwritten rule got broken in a way you couldn't take back, and everything after is downstream of that. What I want to know is whether my principle, whatever it turns out to be, blesses that move or condemns it. Is "locate the first norm breach that changed what everyone thought was possible" itself one of the illuminating analogy templates, or is Appian doing the lazy thing -- picking one dramatic moment because it makes a tidy story? Apply whatever principle you extract from my verdicts to his passage specifically, tell me which of my verdicts you'd have to throw out to make the principle clean (I suspect the Amazon one, definately tell me if so), and tell me what the principle predicts for a comparison I haven't listed: political violence at rallies and assassinations now versus the decades after 133 BC.
