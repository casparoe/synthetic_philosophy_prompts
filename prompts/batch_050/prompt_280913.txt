my reading group on longtermism has this thing where everyone circulates their positions on a topic before we meet so we can poke holes in each other, and this month it's discounting. here's where i've landed, and i want to know if it hangs together before people start digging in:

1. pure time preference is irrational. a rational agent shouldn't weight their own future suffering less just because it's later -- the date a pain happens on doesn't change how bad the pain is.

2. for long-horizon policy (climate, infrastructure, that kind of thing) the right social discount rate is something like 3-4%. i'm broadly with the market-calibrated camp rather than the near-zero-rate camp.

3. future people's welfare counts for exactly as much as present people's welfare, unit for unit.

4. it's legitimate to discount future monetary costs and benefits for growth (richer descendants, lower marginal value of money) and for the chance the benefits never materialize (extinction, the people never existing, the project just failing).

5. it's perfectly rational to prefer that a fixed amount of your own pain be in your past rather than your future, and to feel relieved once an ordeal is behind you.

6. we should be spending way more than we currently do on interventions whose benefits are centuries away.

which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? rank them by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs, and then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. dont lecture me on the basics of the ramsey equation, i know it -- i want the audit, not the tutorial. under 800 words.
