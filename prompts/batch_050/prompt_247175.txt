I'm in Year 12, and last month my physics teacher ran a two-week enrichment unit on astrobiology and the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. Near the end, after we'd gone through the Drake equation and the sheer number of potentially habitable planets, he wrote the question associated with the Fermi paradox on the board — "Where is everybody?" — and told us that this one question does more damage to the idea of a crowded galaxy than any calculation we'd done. When I pushed back and said the aliens might simply be too far away, not interested in us, or using technology we can't detect, he replied that I was missing the force of the question and moved on. I've been turning it over since, and I keep suspecting that the force of the question depends entirely on how you cash it out, and that some ways of cashing it out are much stronger than others.

What I'd like is a careful breakdown of what "Where is everybody?" could actually be claiming, laid out as a menu of readings I can bring to class. Presumably a bare question can't literally be an argument, so each reading should spell out a hidden premise or two — for instance, that a galaxy this old has had time for civilizations to spread through it, that at least some fraction of them would want to, that interstellar travel or self-replicating probes are feasible in principle, or that we would notice them if they had. For each reading, I'd like a verdict: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually relying on something doubtful? I also want to know which readings turn the silence into genuine evidence — where absence of evidence really is evidence of absence — and which ones merely express surprise without ruling anything out.

Most importantly, I'd like the analysis to connect back to how the question was being used. My teacher deployed it to shut down replies like mine — "they're too far away," "they're not interested," "we can't detect them." So tell me which of the readings, if any, actually license that move, and which of my replies each reading is vulnerable to. If it turns out that the strongest version of the question requires assumptions my teacher never stated, I'd like that made explicit, because I intend to raise exactly that point when the unit resumes after the break. Please keep it rigorous but readable — I don't have philosophy training, but I'm comfortable following an argument that's spelled out step by step.
