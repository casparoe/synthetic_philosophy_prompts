In Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics (W. D. Ross translation), Aristotle catalogues the common opinions about self-control and reports this one: "the incontinent man, knowing that what he does is bad, does it as a result of passion, while the continent man, knowing that his appetites are bad, refuses on account of his rational principle to follow them." I want to know whether this characterization — knowledge intact, passion the culprit — captures my own considered verdicts about weakness of will, and if not, what principle should replace it.

Here are my verdicts. First, a colleague on a strict diet sees the cake at a party, says aloud that eating it would be a mistake, and eats it anyway: paradigm weakness of will, and blameworthy. Second, a friend chooses, in March, the larger of two delayed rewards — £110 in 401 days over £100 in 400 days — yet when day 400 arrives takes the £100 immediately. Nothing passionate happens; she is calm, even slightly bored, both times. I still want to call this the same phenomenon as the cake, even though no appetite mastered her. Third, an acquaintance abandons his diet after deciding, sincerely, that health outcomes are mostly genetic; he eats the cake without inner conflict. Identical behavior to my colleague, but I say this is not weakness of will at all — he changed his judgment rather than acted against it. Fourth, a reformed smoker who feels no trace of craving walks past a tobacconist without a flicker: admirable, but I deny this is continence, because there is no bad appetite to refuse; Aristotle seems to reserve a different category for him, and I think Aristotle is right. Fifth, a man who knows he will be tempted signs a contract forfeiting £500 to a political cause he despises if he smokes, and consequently never smokes: I call him practically wise rather than continent, though I concede something in me wants to say he is merely outsourcing continence to his past self. Sixth, a woman firmly resolved to confront her selfish brother at dinner finds, in the moment, that sympathy dissolves her resolve and she stays kind. The outcome is good and I am glad she failed, yet I insist she displayed weakness of will — which sits awkwardly with my first verdict, where weakness of will was blameworthy.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that delivers all six verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that I can see which case motivates which clause, and say explicitly whether Aristotle's formulation in the passage above can serve as that principle — in particular, whether "passion" must be replaced by something like time-discounted valuation to accommodate my second verdict, and what that substitution does to the knowledge condition Aristotle builds in. If the best principle overturns one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why it should go. Then apply the resulting account to two cases I have not listed: someone who procrastinates on their most important task while productively completing every lesser task, and someone who yields to temptation only after drinking. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which argument, case, or empirical finding would lead you to a different principle — and where you take your reasoning to be most likely to fail.
