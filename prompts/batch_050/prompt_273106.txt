I'm TAing an undergrad ethics course (the unit is on animal ethics and moral uncertainty) and I'm working through responses to this exam question:

**Question:** Set out the "scale argument" for giving substantial weight to insect welfare in our moral deliberation. State precisely which quantities the argument depends on, and what would count as evidence bearing on each. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to the argument and say whether it succeeds.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

---

The scale argument says that the moral weight a group of animals should get depends not only on how likely they are to be sentient and how much they can suffer, but on how many of them there are. Insects outnumber vertebrates by an almost incomprehensible margin — there are something like 10^18 insects alive at any given time, hundreds of thousands for every vertebrate on Earth. So even if the probability of insect sentience is low, and even if insects can suffer less intensely than vertebrates can, the sheer number of insects means insect suffering could dominate our moral calculations.

More precisely, the argument multiplies three quantities: (1) the number of insects affected by some practice — insect farming already involves trillions of crickets and mealworms annually, and pesticide use affects far more; (2) the probability that insects of the relevant kind are sentient; and (3) their "welfare range," i.e. how much they can suffer relative to a familiar vertebrate. The product yields the expected moral weight of insect suffering, which can then be compared against the same figure for vertebrates.

On evidence: for quantity (2), the relevant evidence includes behavioral markers such as motivational trade-offs (whether insects will pay a cost, like accepting less food, to avoid a noxious stimulus), self-directed behaviors like wound tending, and flexible rather than purely reflexive responses to harm. Importantly, nociception alone — detecting harmful stimuli — is not evidence of pain, since it occurs in plainly non-sentient systems; the stronger evidence is costly, flexible avoidance. For quantity (3), the evidence is thinner, but one natural proxy is neural complexity: bees have on the order of a million neurons versus hundreds of millions in a chicken, so even granting bee sentience, bee suffering is plausibly at most a percent or so as intense as a chicken's. The number of affected insects (1) is an empirical question about population sizes and farming practices.

The strongest objection is the fanaticism or "Pascal's mugging" worry: multiplying tiny probabilities by enormous numbers threatens to swamp everything, and accepting that style of argument commits us to absurd conclusions elsewhere. But the objection misfires here. The probabilities at issue are not tiny — a credence of, say, 10–40% in insect sentience is nowhere near mugging-level — and 10^18 is not an arbitrarily large number that can be raised without limit to force any conclusion. Fanaticism objections only bite against arguments resting on genuinely minuscule probabilities, and this one doesn't.

Where does this leave us? The argument shows that insect suffering almost certainly constitutes the great majority of suffering on Earth. The expected number of sentient insects (population multiplied by probability of sentience) runs into the quadrillions, dwarfing the vertebrate population. Whether this conclusion should move us to act depends on one's views about expected value reasoning under moral uncertainty, but as a descriptive matter about where suffering is concentrated, the scale argument succeeds.
