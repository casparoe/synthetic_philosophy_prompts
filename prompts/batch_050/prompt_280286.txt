Write a parody regulatory document: the application guidelines and FAQ of a fictional Bureau of Certified Randomness, a government agency that examines finite sequences submitted by the public and issues Certificates of Randomness. About 600–800 words, in a deadpan bureaucratic voice throughout. The humor should come from taking algorithmic information theory completely literally: applicants whose sequence is all zeros are rejected as compressible no matter how honestly the coin was flipped, and the bureau's form letters lecture them on the difference between a random process and a random product; the digits of π are denied certification despite passing every statistical test; one applicant is told his string is inadmissible because his own cover letter describes it in fewer bits than it contains; and the bureau's flagship certificate, reserved for the first sequence too complex for anyone to describe, has been pending since the bureau's founding, for reasons the FAQ conspicuously declines to state. The bureau should take a definite side — certifying product-randomness, not process-randomness — and let the internal logic of that choice carry the satire, so that someone who knows Kolmogorov complexity and Martin-Löf tests finds the parody fair rather than merely silly. After the document, add a short note flagging where the piece leans on a contested reading of the algorithmic-randomness framework or exaggerates for effect.
