My position is that this shows exact qualitative sameness doesn't force numerical identity, and the thought experiment is doing honest work. His position is that it's a fantasy and proves nothing, because in the actual world there is never a case where counting two perfectly alike things as two rather than one makes a difference anyone could in principle detect. I suspect he's wrong about that last bit, but I can't quite produce the receipts.

So, what I'd like: give me an example, drawn from real physics or real mathematics rather than an invented possible world, of a situation where the question 'are these two indistinguishable things genuinely two, or really one?' is not idle metaphysics -- where treating them as two versus treating them as one changes a prediction, a count, or a calculation that actually gets used. I'm fairly confident something like this exists in statistical mechanics and again in quantum mechanics, because I've heard physicists say that identical particles are indistinguishable in some stronger sense than merely looking alike, and that this alters how you count the states available to a system. But I can't tell whether those cases really are instances of the two-spheres setup or something subtly seperate -- maybe the particles do differ in some hidden property after all, or maybe what changes is only our bookkeeping conventions rather than the world itself, which would be a win for my housemate.

For whatever example you go with: spell out the two ways of counting and concretely what difference each makes, say whether you think it is a genuine case of indiscernible-yet-two, and if so whether it vindicates or instead embarrasses the principle that indiscernibles must be identical (I can see it cutting either way, which is partly why I'm asking). Also flag anywhere the interpretation of the physics is itself contested, so I don't overclaim next time the argument comes round. Please answer in British English.
