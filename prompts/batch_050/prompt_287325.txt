W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art," delivered in 1926 and published in The Crisis, is usually remembered for a single blunt sentence: his insistence that all art is propaganda and always must be. But earlier in the essay he makes a stranger and, I think, more load-bearing claim. Describing what Black Americans glimpse about their country, he writes:

"Once in a while through all of us there flashes some clairvoyance, some clear idea, of what America really is. We who are dark can see America in a way that white Americans cannot. And seeing our country thus, are we satisfied with its present goals and ideals?"

I'm programming a film series on art and propaganda and want a reading of Jordan Peele's Get Out through Du Bois's essay, with this passage rather than the famous propaganda line as the pivot. Specifically: what kind of claim is "can see America in a way that white Americans cannot" — an epistemic-privilege thesis, a phenomenological one, a rhetorical one — and does the Sunken Place dramatize that claim, complicate it, or merely decorate it? The Armitages' Coagula auction, in which white bidders purchase Black bodies as vehicles for their own continued lives, reads like an obscene answer to Du Bois's question about whether we are satisfied with America's present goals and ideals; work out whether that scene is doing Du Boisian propaganda or something more ambiguous. Then press the places where the film resists the essay: Du Bois demanded that Negro art build a beautiful world and be judged finally by Black audiences, not only expose white failure — is a horror-comedy built for a crossover multiplex audience a fulfillment of that demand or a departure from it, and would Du Bois have recognized the film's genre machinery (hypnosis, jump scares, Rod's comic relief) as art in his sense at all? Finally: does the propaganda doctrine actually depend on the clairvoyance claim, and does Get Out supply an argument for that dependence that the essay itself never quite makes? Keep the reading honest in both directions — no reducing the film to an illustration, and no stretching Du Bois to fit a screenplay he never saw.
