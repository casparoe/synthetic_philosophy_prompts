I’m an eleventh-grade student helping design a 60-minute meeting of our school’s ethics club for 18 students with no prior philosophy background. Create a classroom activity on John Taurek’s challenge to the idea that we should always save the greater number. The activity should make participants feel the pull of both sides: saving five people instead of one seems obviously right, yet giving each person an equal chance can also seem like the only way to treat them as separate individuals rather than as parts of an aggregate.

The activity should be original rather than another trolley-problem discussion. Give it concrete roles, rules, resources, and small stakes, such as limited rescue tokens or points. Structure it so students encounter the problem through their decisions before hearing terms like “aggregation,” “equal chances,” or “separateness of persons.” Include at least one round in which participants choose under uncertainty about whom they will help, and a later round in which identities or outcomes become visible, so that the difference between fairness before the result and fairness after the result becomes vivid. If possible, arrange for students to occupy more than one perspective across rounds, such as rescuer, member of the larger group, and person who might be left alone.

Please provide:

1. A brief setup that the facilitator can present without first explaining Taurek’s view.
2. Complete rules and any materials needed.
3. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 60 minutes.
4. The choices or outcomes the facilitator should expect each round to produce.
5. A debrief connecting particular moments in the activity to Taurek’s challenge, while also presenting the strongest worry that an equal-chance procedure treats a chance of rescue as if it were morally equivalent to a person actually being rescued.
6. Advice about what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to respond.
7. An honest explanation of one important feature of the philosophical dispute that the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator will therefore have to supply directly.

The goal is not to make Taurek’s position look obviously correct or obviously absurd. Students should leave understanding why counting the numbers is morally compelling, while also seeing why simply adding up lives might fail to take each person’s separate claim seriously.
