I am trying to work out when believing a conspiracy theory is epistemically reasonable, and I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind my case-by-case verdicts. An American in 1973 who believed in the Watergate cover-up on the strength of the early press reports: reasonable. A Black American in the 1960s who distrusted public health authorities because of rumours about the Tuskegee study: reasonable, even before the story was confirmed. Someone today who believes the Moon landings were faked: unreasonable. A Soviet citizen in 1975 who reflexively disbelieved every official announcement, including the true ones: reasonable, though often wrong. Someone who treats every official denial as further evidence for the theory: unreasonable. And here is the pair I cannot reconcile: I want to say that the Tuskegee-informed sceptic was reasonable, but I do not want to say that someone who cites MKUltra as grounds for believing essentially any current allegation against an intelligence agency is reasonable, and I cannot see what distinguishes the two, since both reason from a documented precedent of institutional deception. Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts, stated in terms of the evidence available, the track record of the institutions involved, or the structure of the theory itself, and which of my verdicts does the best such principle force me to abandon? Please also say what it implies for a case I have not listed: an employee who comes to believe, purely from statistical anomalies in internal reports, that her own company is concealing a safety defect. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a letter, and in British English.
