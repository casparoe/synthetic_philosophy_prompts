Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about moral foundations theory — the research program in moral psychology according to which human moral judgment is built on a small set of innate, modular foundations (care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and sometimes liberty/oppression), and according to which, descriptively, political liberals tend to rely heavily on the first two while political conservatives distribute their moral concern more evenly across all of them.

My friend's position: this descriptive asymmetry is itself evidence about who is right. A morality that engages the full suite of foundations is richer, more complete, and better matched to the actual structure of human moral life; liberal moral monism (care and fairness only) is a kind of partial blindness, and conservatives are, on average, perceiving more moral reality. My position: the inference is illegitimate. Nothing in descriptive psychology tells us which foundations track genuine moral truths and which are vestiges of coalitional psychology — sanctity judgments, for instance, look like plausible candidates for debunking, in which case the asymmetry would show that liberals are discerning rather than deficient. I also pressed that the "evenly distributed" finding may be partly an artifact of how the standard questionnaire items are worded, and I proposed a test: if a new foundation were discovered on which liberals scored higher, would he update toward liberalism being richer, or would he find a reason to discount that foundation? He accused me of motivated reasoning in reverse.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Cato and Lin, who take up our two positions and do a better job with them than we did. Requirements:

- Cato defends my friend's inference from descriptive asymmetry to normative superiority; Lin defends my debunking response. Neither is a strawman: Cato should give the strongest available version of the "more foundations, more moral perception" argument (an analogy with perceptual or aesthetic sensitivity may help), and Lin should give the strongest available version of the genealogical debunking worry, including the point that the debunking strategy must itself avoid begging the question about which judgments need debunking.
- Real back-and-forth. Each character must respond to the other's actual previous point, concede where concession is due, and name the concessions as such.
- At some point, Lin must run the reversal test I proposed (a hypothetical new foundation on which liberals score higher), and Cato must answer it honestly rather than deflecting.
- At some point, the conversation must engage the questionnaire-artifact worry, and the two should disagree intelligently about how much weight a measurement concern can bear in a normative dispute.
- Each character should at least once state the other's position in a form the other accepts as accurate.
- The dialogue should end not with agreement but with the two of them jointly identifying the single premise — empirical, conceptual, or normative — whose truth would settle their disagreement, and each should say why they believe it or doubt it.

Roughly 900 to 1,300 words of dialogue, formal register, no narration beyond minimal stage directions.
