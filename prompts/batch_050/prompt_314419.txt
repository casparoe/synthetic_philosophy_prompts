I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind drafting the second chapter of my dissertation, and before I evaluate any particular account I want the specification first. The question: what must a theory of conceivability as a guide to possibility deliver in order to be adequate for use in modal metaphysics — where "adequate" means, specifically, good enough to serve as the evidential premise in arguments of the form "P is conceivable, therefore P is metaphysically possible," as in the familiar zombie argument against physicalism? Adequacy is relative to that purpose, not to conceivability's role in, say, fiction or everyday counterfactual reasoning.

Write the desiderata list: the requirements such a theory must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark each as non-negotiable for any adequate account or as a commitment of a particular camp (modal rationalists, dualists, modal skeptics). The list should cover at least the following, though I expect you to add what I have missed:

- how the theory handles a posteriori necessities (water's being H2O, Hesperus' being Phosphorus), where the negation seems conceivable but is impossible, and whether the machinery it introduces to explain those failures leaves the zombie inference standing;
- the mathematical stress test: the negation of an open conjecture such as Goldbach's seems conceivable, yet if the conjecture is true the negation is impossible. The theory must say whether this conceiving is evidence, and if it is not, explain why the conceivability of phenomenal consciousness without physical realization fares better — without simply defining the difference into existence;
- the conditions on the conceiver: whose conceivings count, under what idealization of attention, information, and coherence, and how the theory avoids making metaphysical possibility hostage to contingent facts about human cognitive capacity;
- an error theory: what the account says when conceivability and possibility come apart, and whether that explanation generalizes in a way that undermines the very conceivability evidence the account wants to preserve elsewhere.

In addition, any adequate theory must pronounce on a case I have constructed for the chapter, which I will call the latency case. A subject conceives of a scenario P in a sustained, detailed, apparently coherent way, and by every first-personally accessible measure the conceiving is ideal. Unbeknownst to the subject, however, an undetectable cognitive impairment — say a lesion that subtly degrades her grasp of one of the concepts involved — is doing the work: were the impairment repaired, the scenario would strike her as subtly incoherent. The impairment is in principle discoverable only by empirical means she lacks. Does her conceiving count as ideal? If the theory says no, it owes a criterion of ideality that is not circular — "ideal" cannot mean "conceiving in whatever way tracks possibility," on pain of making the conceivability-possibility link trivially true and evidentially empty. If it says yes, conceivability evidence becomes hostage to undetectable malfunction, and the theory owes an account of why such evidence should move anyone in the zombie case. State which horn each of your desiderata forces, and whether the case shows that some requirement on the list is misformulated.

Two closing tasks. First, identify any pair or subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, the demand that ideal conceivability be a priori accessible to the conceiver together with the demand that it reliably track a possibility that outruns the a priori. If the list itself contains an inconsistency, say so plainly; that would be the chapter's main result. Second, name the single requirement that excludes the most current contenders, and then score briefly — a paragraph each — the three main families against the list: a Chalmers-style modal rationalism built on positive, ideal conceivability; a Yablo-style account on which one conceives a world at which P; and a modal-skeptical or pure error-theoretic account that denies conceivability any evidential force outside the actual. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard. No bibliography or citations; I will supply those myself.
