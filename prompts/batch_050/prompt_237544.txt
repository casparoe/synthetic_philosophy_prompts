Something I keep running into, both in everyday arguments and in my own thinking. When a moral judgment is inconvenient or contested, one side tends to describe it as a bias to be corrected, while the other side describes it as a preference to be respected — and I cannot find any test that settles which description is right.

Three cases. First: a friend of mine gave up religion years ago but still finds, on reflection, that certain sexual arrangements strike him as wrong. He insists this is now a settled personal value, not a leftover from indoctrination; his secular friends insist it is a leftover. Second: nearly everyone I know grants special weight to their own children's welfare and treats this as a legitimate preference; yet when someone favors people of their own ethnic group, many of these same people call that a bias, even though the two attachments seem structurally alike — unchosen, deep, stable, and warmly endorsed. Third: a colleague who finds factory farming revolting treats her disgust as evidence, while dismissing the meat-eater's appetitive pull as a bias; the meat-eater, of course, reverses the labels.

What strikes me is that every criterion people propose — would you still endorse it under full information, would you accept it from behind a veil of ignorance, would you incur costs for it, has it survived careful reflection — can apparently be satisfied sincerely by the very judgments that others want to dismiss as biased. The lapsed believer, the parent, and the vegan each pass every test as easily as anyone does. Meanwhile, the ascriptions seem to track the ascriber's own commitments: what I share with you is a preference, and what I do not share is a bias.

Is there an established name or literature for this deadlock — specifically for the apparent symmetry of every proposed criterion, and for the way the preference-versus-bias label tracks agreement rather than anything independently observable? I know there are nearby notions, such as rationalization, motivated reasoning, and adaptive preferences, but none of them seems to capture the symmetry problem itself. If there is no established concept, please say so, propose a name and a precise definition, and mark the boundary: which cases fall under the concept, and which are ordinary disagreements about bias in which a genuine asymmetry between the parties can be shown. Please write your answer in American English.
