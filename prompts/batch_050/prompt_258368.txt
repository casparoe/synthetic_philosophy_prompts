Six months ago I closed the specialty coffee roastery into which I had put five years and most of my savings, and ever since, well-meaning friends have congratulated me on finally seeing that sunk costs are sunk. I accepted the advice at the time, but the more I turn it over, the less sure I am that the maxim deserves its reputation as exceptionless. I can dimly see that there might be situations where the amount one has already invested is itself evidence bearing on whether to continue -- since a rational person invests more, at each stage, precisely when their private information is encouraging -- but I cannot make the idea precise, and I want it stress-tested rather than half-believed.

Construct for me a thought experiment meeting these specifications. The setting must be ordinary and contemporary: no demons, no perfect predictors, no stipulated utility monsters. It must feature two agents facing the same venture in the same physical state, with the same forward-looking costs, where continuing is rational for the one who has sunk more and quitting is rational for the one who has sunk less -- with the asymmetry arising solely from the evidential content of the investment history. Both agents must be orthodox Bayesian expected-utility maximizers; you may not help yourself to signaling to third parties, reputational concerns, contractual lock-in, or preferences stipulated ad hoc. The scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph, and the differential verdicts must be generated from the information structure, not asserted.

Then demonstrate that the case works: lay out the priors, the signals, and the updating; compute each agent's expected value of continuing; and identify the single load-bearing feature. Show that the asymmetry collapses if investments are made uncorrelated with private information. Finally, give the strongest rejoinder available to a defender of the orthodox maxim -- presumably that real people honor sunk costs from loss aversion rather than from evidential reasoning -- and say whether that rejoinder defeats the case or merely confines it.
