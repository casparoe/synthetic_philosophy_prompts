During a roundtable on AI regulation last month, I argued that a certain class of recommender system is objectionable not merely because of what some users do with it, but because of the thing it is: its engagement-maximizing objective, applied at scale, predictably reshapes public discourse in ways no individual user chose. A colleague cut me off with a line I have heard many times before: 'Technology is neutral — it's how you use it that matters.' The implication was that my objection was a category error: blame the users, or the company, or the regulators, but not the artifact.

I suspect that slogan conflates several different claims, only some of which are true, and only some of which would actually defeat my objection. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing — for example, claims about whether artifacts can instantiate or encode values, about whether moral responsibility can attach to objects rather than agents, about whether a tool's effects depend wholly on context of use, about whether design choices are political choices, or about the symmetry between beneficial and harmful applications — and for each thesis state: (a) what exactly it asserts, stated as a proposition with a definite truth value; (b) whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, contentious, or simply false, with a sentence of justification; and (c) whether, if true, it would actually support my colleague's conversational move — that is, whether it blocks the claim that an artifact can be objectionable in itself, independent of particular acts of use.

Please present the readings as an enumerated menu rather than selecting and defending a single interpretation, and conclude by identifying which reading someone who deploys the slogan in a regulatory debate most likely intends, whether that intended reading is one of the defensible ones, and what the strongest version of my original objection would look like once the slogan is properly disambiguated.
