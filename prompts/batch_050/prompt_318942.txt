I am a novelist at work on a piece of historical fiction set in an invented country, and the voice of my narrator is that of a sober, retrospective historian. I need the novel's central event — the fall of a monarchy — to be causally engineered in a very particular way, so that the narrator's reflections on why it happened are philosophically exact rather than merely atmospheric. Please construct the scenario to the following specifications, then demonstrate that it meets them.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. A plausible invented setting — a fictional kingdom at some period between roughly 1700 and 1950, with no anachronistic technology and no supernatural elements — in which a single, determinate event occurs: the monarchy's collapse.
2. Two causal chains converge on that event. One must be structural and slow-moving (economic strain, institutional decay, demographic pressure, or the like); the other must be contingent and agential (a particular person's deliberate act). Each chain, had the other been entirely absent, would by itself have sufficed to bring about the collapse.
3. Both chains must actually be operative in the story — neither may be merely hypothetical — so the collapse counterfactually depends on neither: remove either one and the event still occurs via the other.
4. Crucially, the two chains must not be symmetric. One must preempt the other: the second chain must be poised to produce the collapse only if the first fails, the way a reserve assassin fires only if the primary one misses — rather than both striking simultaneously like two bullets. I want the reader's intuitions about which chain "really" explains the fall to diverge from what the standard but-for test delivers.
5. The whole case, stated as narrative, should fit in a single paragraph, and the characters' motives should be ones a reader of serious fiction would find credible.

The deliverable:

First, the scenario itself. Then show the reasoning: what verdict the simple counterfactual ("but for") test gives about each chain and why; what an interventionist or difference-making account of causal explanation says about the same case; and how the historian's ordinary practice of selecting one cause as "the" cause of an event would treat it. Identify the structural feature that separates preemption from genuine simultaneous overdetermination in your case, state whether removing that feature collapses the scenario back into the symmetric two-bullets situation, and explain how someone who insists that counterfactual dependence is the whole of causal explanation would attack the case's setup. Finally, advise me how the narrator should apportion explanatory weight between the two chains in the telling — given that the case is built so that this is genuinely contestable, I want the narration to be honest about it rather than to smuggle in a verdict.

Please say how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and single out the single claim in your response you are least sure of.
