So I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working on philosophy of history and historical explanation, and for a grad seminar on counterfactuals I'm supposed to give a presentation on contingency in history. My advisor suggested I anchor the whole thing in a piece of fiction instead of slogging through the historiography first, and I've landed on Ray Bradbury's "A Sound of Thunder" -- the time safari story where Eckels panics, steps off the levitating path, crushes a butterfly, and comes back to 2055 to find the spelling of English mangled and the fascist candidate Deutscher having won the election instead of Keith. It seemed like a gimmicky choice at first but the more I sit with it the more I think the story is actually two incompatible theories of history stapled together, and I want help pulling that apart.

Here's what I'd like. Read the story through the contingency-versus-convergence debate. On its face it dramatizes the strongest possible "for want of a nail" picture: one butterfly, one election, one ruined language. But notice that Time Safari Inc. runs on exactly the opposite principle -- they only ever target animals that were about to die anyway (the T. rex with the red paint mark, seconds before the tree falls on it), on the theory that changes which "don't matter" don't propagate forward. So the story's own machinery says history is fully convergent for the dinosaur and hyper-sensitive for the butterfly, and it never once explains what distinguishes the two cases. Is there a coherent theory of which counterfactual rewrites propagate and which get absorbed -- something in the neighborhood of the minimal-rewrite constraint historians put on counterfactuals, or a distinction between causal bottlenecks and causal redundancy -- that would actually vindicate the safari company's policy? Or is Bradbury's world just the conjunction of a convergentist and a hypercontingentist picture of history, glued together by plot necessity, and nobody should try to extract a consistent metaphysics from it? I want the reading to take the story seriously as an argument, not just as an illustration, and to say plainly where it cheats.

Two more things I keep tripping over. First, everyone who discusses this story name-drops chaos theory, but as far as I can tell sensitive dependence on initial conditions is a claim about deterministic dynamical systems and says nothing by itself about whether big historical outcomes ride on tiny events in the way the story needs -- plenty of chaotic systems are macroscopically stable. Is the story (and the way people cite it) trading on a confusion between mathematical chaos and historical contingency? Second, the fact that a butterfly flips the election from Keith to Deutscher treats the identity of the president as the kind of thing a small perturbation can reach -- which smells like a smuggled-in great-man picture of history. Would a structuralist say the real counterfactual the story should worry about is that 2055 America was the sort of place where the election was knife-edge enough for a butterfly to decide it, i.e. that the contingency was already baked into the background conditions? Please answer in American English.
