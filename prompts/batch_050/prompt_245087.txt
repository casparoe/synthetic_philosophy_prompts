I'm on the collections committee of a small regional museum, and we've landed in a proper mess over a terracotta statue. It's our star object, an eighteenth-century piece, and during the last refit a contractor knocked it off its plinth. It shattered into a few hundred fragments. The conservators reckon they can reassemble it with about 70% original material, filling the gaps with new clay that's chemically near-identical, and once it's inpainted you'd never know. That bit isn't the controversy.

The controversy is what we do next. We have a touring exhibition slot we've already committed to, and the loan contract specifies the 'original work'. Our insurer's valuation likewise assumes the original. Meanwhile a wealthy patron has offered to fund a full restoration on condition we label the result 'the statue, restored' rather than 'a reconstruction incorporating original material' -- she has Views, and her late husband apparently wrote a thesis on the ship of Theseus, which she keeps mentioning at meetings. One of our trustees thinks the honest answer is that the statue ceased to exist when it hit the floor and what's left is a lump of clay with a distinguished past; he wants us to sell the fragments to fund acquisitions. Another thinks that's mad and that a thing survives disassembly and repair all the time, as her hip replacement proves.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision (restore-and-exhibit-as-original, restore-and-label-as-reconstruction, or something else, and what we tell the insurer and the patron), but what I really want is the reasoning set out so the committee can vote on it: what, on your account, makes the reassembled thing the same statue or a different one, and does the answer change if the restoration happens in one go versus piecemeal over a decade? If the trustee's lump-of-clay line is wrong, say why in terms a room of non-philosophers can follow; if it's right, say what we're actually exhibiting. Either way the memo needs to take the strongest version of the losing side seriously, because half the commitee already holds it, and it should end with a couple of principles we could reuse when this comes up again -- the relined painting in storage and the museum's own Victorian roof suggest it will.
