Next month I am giving my first proper philosophy talk — a thirty-minute paper in the formal epistemology stream of a workshop, with a question period I fully expect to be longer than the talk. I come to this sideways: a decade of reading Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and the associated diaspora has left me fluent in Bayesian vernacular but aware that the room will contain people who have actually proved things. Prepare me for the question period.

The paper defends a specific update rule. The set-up I use is a variant of the lost-soldier puzzle: an agent is certain to be in exactly one of four equally likely regions, arranged as two zones (North and South), each split into a headquarters sector and an outer sector. Her prior is uniform over the four. She then receives a message whose entire content is a constraint on a conditional probability — if she is in the North zone, the probability that she is in the headquarters sector is 3/4 — and nothing else. No raw proposition is learned; the message makes no claim about the actual world, only about this conditional. My thesis is that the uniquely rational response is to take the posterior closest to the prior, in the sense of minimising relative entropy, subject to the new conditional constraint; I then argue this rule should govern all cases where an agent's evidence arrives as a shift in a conditional probability, and that rival rules (simply leaving the marginal over zones untouched, or Jeffrey-style updating on a reified "the message was sent" proposition) are either ad hoc or smuggle in content the agent never received.

The audience: mostly formal epistemologists and philosophers of probability, plus two or three Bayesian statisticians who attend out of habit. Assume they know the puzzle's literature cold and have a standing suspicion of grand claims built on a single toy case.

I want the objections they will actually raise, in the order I should fear them: how likely each is to come up, how much damage it does if I handle it badly, and phrased the way this audience would phrase it — their vocabulary, not the blogosphere's. For each, give me the reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and mark honestly where that reply is weakest rather than overselling it. Tell me what I should concede pre-emptively in the talk itself to defuse the predictable lines of attack, and which commitments I should refuse to trade away. Flag in particular: the objection that my chosen divergence measure is merely one of a family and the choice is underdetermined; the objection that a conditional-probability message has no propositional content and so nothing to update on; and the question of whether my rule survives when the learned conditional concerns zones whose prior marginals were unequal. Finally, tell me the objection you suspect I am secretly hoping nobody asks, and give me the least-bad answer to it anyway.

Please write the whole response in British English.
