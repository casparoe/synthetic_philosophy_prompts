I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about nudging and soft paternalism in public policy — calorie labels, default organ donation, auto-enrolled pensions, graphic warnings on cigarettes, that whole territory. The standard debate (autonomy vs welfare, Sunstein-style libertarian paternalism vs old-school hard paternalism) feels completly played out, and I don't want to reheat it. What I want is a piece on what's genuinely still unsettled and worth arguing about. So: which open questions in this area matter most — whether nudges lose thier effect once people know they're being nudged, whether there's a morally real difference between choice-preserving nudges and outright mandates, who gets to define the chooser's "true" interests in the first place, whether nudges mostly benefit the already-savvy, transparency requirements, or something I'm not even listing? Give me a defended prioritization, not a survey: which questions are upstream of which, which are actually answerable with evidence we have or could plausibly get, and which you'd drop because settling them wouldn't change anything about how policy gets made. Anchor it to my purpose — I want the reader (and any policymaker who picks the piece up) to come away with the two or three live fights that would actually change how they'd design or judge a nudge.
