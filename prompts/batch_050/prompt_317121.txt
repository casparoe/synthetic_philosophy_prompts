A friend and I have been arguing about rescue dilemmas, and I assembled an argument that now unsettles me, because it seems to prove something absurd and I cannot locate the weak joint. (1) If you can save either one stranger or five strangers from drowning, and know nothing else about any of them, you ought to save the five. (2) The only possible justification for (1) is that each person's claim on you counts for something positive and the claims add up, so the five together outweigh the one. (3) If claims add up across people in this way, then they must also add up across different kinds of harm, since nothing about addition restricts it to harms of the same size. (4) So if you can save one stranger from death or a sufficiently large number of strangers from a painful headache, you ought to relieve the headaches, because enough small claims added together will exceed any single large one. (5) But that conclusion is absurd; no number of headaches outweighs a life. (6) So either (1) is wrong too -- and my friend is right that you may as well flip a coin between the one and the five -- or the mistake lies somewhere in (2) or (3). I would much rather keep (1) than accept (4), and I suspect something slipped in the move from counting lives to aggregating harms, but I cannot say precisely what. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost the case for saving the greater number?
