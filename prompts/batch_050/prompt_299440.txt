I teach a course in the philosophy of language, and there is a phenomenon I keep encountering in ordinary life for which I lack a term, and I would like to know whether the literature has one. Consider two cases. First: in a shared household, one member announces, in an even tone and without any recognised standing to set policy, that from now on dishes are to be washed the day they are used. Nobody assents; nobody objects. Over the following weeks people largely comply, and months later the announcement is cited as having established the rule — as if it had been binding all along. Second: a junior employee writes in a group thread that the team does not reply to emails after 7pm. She has no managerial authority. The practice takes hold, and new hires are later told that she "made it a rule." What strikes me is that the utterance seems to acquire its normative force retroactively: at the time of utterance it neither misfires (as a performative uttered without authority standardly does) nor succeeds (since nothing yet binds anyone), and its later status as rule-establishing depends entirely on subsequent compliance, which it also somehow causes. This does not look like a standard exercitive, since the speaker lacks the standing; nor like a mere perlocutionary effect, since the outcome is not just behavioural but normative — people later judge violations as wrong; nor quite like Lewisian accommodation, since there is no conversational scoreboard being adjusted on the spot. Is there an established concept for this kind of deferred or ratification-dependent norm-creation, and how does it differ from the nearest notions in the speech-act literature? If there is none, I would ask you to say so, coin a term, define it with boundary cases — what distinguishes it from mere habituation, from successful exercitives, and from rules adopted by tacit consent — rather than simply assimilating it to the closest familiar category. And if my description rests on a confusion — say, if I have smuggled in a false assumption about when performatives take effect — I would rather you say that plainly than hand me a label for something ill-conceived.
