"The strongest version of the view comes from Peter French, who argues that corporations are full-fledged moral persons. His key idea is the Corporate Internal Decision structure: the corporation has an organizational flowchart plus 'corporate decision recognition rules,' and these work by aggregating the individual votes and intentions of employees into a single corporate intention, which is why we can say the corporation intended something even if no individual did. Because corporations have this decision-making machinery, French says they can intend, and intending is sufficient for moral agency, so a company can be blamed in its own right, not just the CEO or board.

"The best objection is from Manuel Velasquez, who holds that corporations can be morally responsible but only derivatively — the real moral agents are always the human beings whose acts the corporate act reduces to. Anything we call a corporate action is really just shorthand for a lot of individual actions, and attributing intention to the firm is a category mistake, like saying the crowd 'decided' to riot. Also, punishing the corporation usually lands on innocent shareholders and workers, which suggests corporate blame is misguided.

"My own assessment: I think the objection partly succeeds. Legal personhood is what makes corporations able to act and be blamed, and since the law grants them personhood, they qualify as moral agents too. But I agree that punishment falling on innocent parties is a serious cost, so corporate blame should be used sparingly."
