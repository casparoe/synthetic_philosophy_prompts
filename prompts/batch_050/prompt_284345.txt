I'm preparing discussion material for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on medieval philosophy, and this year I want to push my students past the standard inventory of "moves" in Book V of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, since in past years the best students could reconstruct those moves perfectly but had never once asked whether the list is exhaustive. Please act as a specialist in late antique and medieval philosophy with particular expertise in the Consolation. Before settling on your final answer, briefly brainstorm a few candidate ideas and then explain why you discarded all but one.

Here is the inventory I already have and do not need repeated: (1) the distinction between simple necessity and conditional necessity -- what God knows is conditionally necessary given that he knows it, but not simply necessary, so the fatalist conclusion doesn't follow; (2) the eternal-present solution -- God does not literally foreknow anything, since his eternity is the complete and simultaneous possession of unending life, and so his knowing a future event is like our seeing a present one, and observation does not cause what it observes; (3) the principle that knowledge conforms to the mode of the knower rather than the mode of the thing known, so a higher cognitive power can know in a unified, timeless way what lower powers (sense, imagination, reason) grasp only successively; (4) the reversal of the direction of dependence -- events do not happen because God knows them, rather God knows them because they will happen, so the necessity threat is defused at its source; and (5) the imagery of the elevated observer, as of someone on a height watching events unfold below, which is meant to make (2) intuitive rather than merely asserted. Note that several of these are variants or deepenings of one another, and I've flagged that in my own notes -- so for your answer to count, the new move must not be a rewording, special case, or recombination of any of these five.

What I want is one further interpretive or philosophical move -- either an additional Boethian (or plausibly Boethian) solution to the foreknowledge dilemma, or a fresh objection to Boethius's own resolution that the tradition's standard objections (e.g., that timeless eternity makes no sense, or that infallible past beliefs still constrain the future) do not already cover. State it precisely enough that a student could defend it in a term paper, and then be honest with me about whether it is actually any good: does it survive contact with the text of Book V, does it depend on anachronistically importing later scholastic machinery, and does it change how one should read Boethius's concluding claim that human freedom of the will remains intact? I would rather receive a genuinely interesting move with acknowledged weaknesses than a safe one presented as airtight.
