A few years ago my father received a cochlear implant, and what struck me throughout the process was the language everyone used without a moment's hesitation. The audiologists, the surgeons, and even the insurance paperwork all said the device would "restore his hearing" — not simulate it, not compensate for its absence, but restore the very capacity itself. Once I noticed it there, I saw the same assumption everywhere in modern medicine. Artificial heart valves are said to do the heart's work; dialysis is literally called renal replacement therapy; retinal implants are described as giving back a measure of sight; spinal cord stimulators are credited with genuinely relieving chronic pain rather than merely silencing its behavioral expression. In every case, a synthetic device is credited with really doing the job of the biological original, provided it reproduces the right causal and functional organization. No clinician suggests that my father merely behaves as if he hears, or that a patient on dialysis merely behaves as if her blood is being filtered.

But here is what puzzles me, and where I would like your philosophical help. This practice seems to presuppose that what makes something a case of hearing, sight, or pain relief is what the part does, not what it is made of — in other words, that mental and bodily functions are multiply realizable, and that silicon or titanium can instantiate them as legitimately as neurons and nephrons. Yet the very same medical establishment seems to hesitate exactly where that commitment should bite hardest. No clinical program I know of proposes to replace a damaged region of cortex, neuron for neuron, with a functionally equivalent prosthesis and then to credit the patient with the mental life that region used to support. If functional equivalence is enough at the auditory nerve, why not in the visual cortex? Is medicine's prosthetics practice committed to full-blown functionalism, or only to the weaker claim that devices can feed an intact biological system that is still the real seat of experience — a reading a defender of biological naturalism could accept, since on that view the implant restores hearing while experiencing nothing itself?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind behind medicine's prosthetics practice, tell me whether the practice strictly presupposes functionalism or is merely comfortable with it, identify where it is genuinely underdetermined between rival views, and say what would have to change in the practice and its self-description if biological naturalism turned out to be correct. Structure your response with these sections, in this order: The Practice, Stated Precisely (about 150 words — pin down exactly which everyday clinical claims are at stake); The Implicit Theory (about 250 words — the strongest reconstruction of the presupposed view); Rival Readings and Underdetermination (about 250 words — which other views license the same practice, and what would distinguish them observationally); The Cortical Prosthesis Test Case (about 200 words — use a gradual, neuron-by-neuron replacement of visual cortex as the probe); and Verdict (about 100 words — is the practice coherent as it stands, and what, if anything, it must give up).
