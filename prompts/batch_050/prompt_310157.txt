I'm in a reading group on late antique / medieval philosophy and this week we got to Book V of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, specifically prose 4, and I'm honestly stuck. The bit I'm wrestling with, in my own loose rendering (our translation is at home and I'm writing this on the train): Philosophy argues that whatever is known is known not according to the known thing's own nature but according to the capacity of the knower, and then she runs through a kind of ladder -- sense, imagination, reason, and what she calls intelligence -- using the example that the same thing, a human being, is grasped differently by each: sense gets the bodily shape, imagination gets a kind of figure without the matter, reason gets the universal form, and intelligence somehow sees the simple form itself in one pure act, without any of the rungs below. The higher faculty supposedly comprehends everything the lower ones grasp, but not the other way around.

Here's what's tripping me up. My friend Dan thinks this whole ladder thing is smuggled in to make the later move (that God's knowledge of our future actions doesn't force them to happen, because his knowing is of a different order than ours) seem earned rather than asserted. He says the man-example cheats, because it just assumes reason's take on the universal is the correct one and that imagination's version is defective -- but if you actually asked imagination, so to speak, it would report no deficiency at all, so the principle that knowledge follows the knower's capacity seems to presuppose a ranking it never argues for. I feel like he's being unfair but I can't articulate why. Can you (1) explain what the knower-capacity principle is actually claiming in this passage, as precisely as possible; (2) reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that a higher faculty can contain the lower one's object without the lower one being simply wrong; and (3) tell me whether Dan's objection lands -- i.e., is the hierarchy argued for here, or is it borrowed from elsewhere (and if borrowed, from where, roughly)? Also, if you can, connect it forward: does prose 4's epistemology actually do any real work in the eternity solution of prose 6, or are they two seperate arguments stapled together?
