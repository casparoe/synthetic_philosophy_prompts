I'm teaching a first-year module on philosophy of history next term and I want to end it on counterfactuals, and my plan is to screen It's a Wonderful Life and make the students actually work with it rather than just cry at the ending. So: read the film through the counterfactual history debate -- the one about whether events are genuinely contingent (Cleopatra's nose and all that) or whether the big currents of history more or less reassert themselves whatever individuals do.

Here's what I want the reading to grapple with. Clarence shows George a world in which he was never born, and Bedford Falls becomes Pottersville. On the surface that's the strongest possible brief for contingency, almost a great-man theory transposed to an ordinary man: remove one building-and-loan manager and the whole town goes to hell. But look closer and the counterfactual seems rigged. So much stays fixed -- same townspeople, same storefronts, same Potter with the same ambitions -- that the film quietly assumes the deep structure of the town is just given and George is the only variable. Isn't that exactly the methodological sin historians accuse bad counterfactuals of, the arbitrary minimal rewrite that freezes everything you like and changes only the one thing you're interested in? In a world without George Bailey, wouldn't there just be someone else running a building and loan, or not, and wouldn't the Depression and Potter's grip on the town have ground along regardless? On the other hand, maybe the film is smarter than the historians here: maybe it isnt making a claim about what would have happened at all, but using the counterfactual as an evaluative device, a way of measuring what a life was worth, and that's governed by different standards. Whether a counterfactual is legitimate as explanation and whether its legitimate as eulogy might be two genuinely different questions, and the film might be the best illustration of that difference anyone's made.

So: (a) work out which side of the contingency/inevitability debate the film is actually on, given both what changes in Pottersville and what conspicuously doesn't; (b) say whether Clarence's counterfactual would survive the standards that the counterfactual-method literature proposes, and whether that even matters if the film is doing eulogy rather than explanation; (c) pick two or three specific scenes -- the graveyard, Martini's bar, Mary at the library, whatever you like -- and show what the philosophical reading illuminates about them and where it starts to distort them; and (d) end by telling me what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the journal articles dont. I'm going to assign whatever you write as the seminar reading, so make it something undergrads can push back on.
