I'm a senior in high school, and our philosophy club is assembling a small printed anthology to close out the year. I've volunteered to contribute a poem about Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness, and I'd like you to write it for me.

The form should enact the theory. Present the poem as several competing drafts of itself on the page: an early stanza interrupted by a revised version, lines crossed out but still legible, a later stanza that quietly overwrites an image introduced earlier, perhaps a fragment in the margin that never gets incorporated. Crucially, there should be no final, authoritative version anywhere in the piece. The point of the model, as I understand it, is that consciousness isn't a finished performance shown in some inner theater where everything arrives to be judged by a central observer; instead, content gets fixed at different times by distributed processes, earlier and later versions coexist, and asking which draft is the real one is already the mistake. The poem's structure should make a reader feel why that question can't be asked, not just state that it can't.

Around 30 to 45 lines, free verse, no rhyming couplets. It should be accurate enough that our faculty advisor, who studied philosophy of mind, would nod rather than wince.
