I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and in ten days I'm presenting a paper at a small comparative philosophy workshop attended by two kinds of specialists who rarely read each other: two sinologists who work on classical Chinese texts and care about philological accuracy above all, and three analytic philosophers who work on the badness of death in the Epicurean and deprivationist traditions. My thesis is this: the Zhuangzi's equanimity in the face of death is best understood not as Stoic-style indifference, nor as the Epicurean conclusion that death is nothing to the one who dies, but as a re-identification of the self with the process of transformation itself — so that what dies is never quite the thing the mourner took themselves to be attached to. I lean on three episodes: Zhuangzi drumming and singing after his wife's death and defending it to Huizi, the cluster of passages where dying is figured as one turn in an ongoing process of change, and the conversations among the dying masters who treat their own deformity and dissolution with amusement.

Act as an expert in classical Chinese philosophy who also has a working command of analytic philosophy of death. Think carefully and, before drafting anything, brainstorm the full space of objections both camps could raise, then organize the result into a prep sheet I can study.

The prep sheet should give me, in roughly the order the objections will arrive in the question period: each objection phrased the way that audience member would actually phrase it (the sinologists will object on textual and translational grounds; the analytic philosophers will object on grounds of coherence and argumentative force); an assessment of how damaging each one is to the thesis as stated; what I should concede immediately and gracefully; and my best reply, with an honest note about where that reply is weakest. Anticipate in particular the charge that I am building a unified position out of passages from heterogeneous layers of the text, the worry that my reading renders the grief in the text unintelligible rather than transcended, and the objection that re-identification with process either collapses into the Epicurean move it claims to surpass or proves too much by dissolving every prudential concern. Include the single objection I am hoping nobody raises, and tell me why it is the most dangerous.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who know this material — about the text's composite character, the content of the relevant passages, and the standard interpretive options — from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading, mine or anyone else's, as the scholarly consensus. Where my thesis depends on a disputable interpretive step, flag it explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
