I'm a joiner — I make fitted furniture, been at it about fifteen years now. A few months back I fell down a rabbit hole and watched a couple of lectures about Heidegger, the bit in Being and Time about the hammer, tools being ready-to-hand versus present-at-hand, breakdown and all that. It properly rattled me, because it's the closest any philosopher has come to describing what my job actually feels like from the inside, at least as far as I can tell from a Youtube video.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Take the tool analysis — ready-to-hand, present-at-hand, circumspection (I think that's the word for the kind of looking you do while you're absorbed in the work), the way a tool only shows up as a thing when it breaks or goes missing, and the bit about how a whole kit of gear refers to other gear and to the job it's for — and restate each piece in the vocabulary I actually use at the bench: the tool dissapearing into the work, flow, muscle memory, the difference between using a chisel and stopping to inspect a chisel, that moment when a plane blade catches a knot and suddenly you're thinking about the plane instead of the cut. Be honest about the mapping rather than flattering it. If some piece of his only roughly matches workshop talk, say so and say where it strains.

Then the bit I really want: a residue report. Which parts of the analysis translate cleanly into how tradespeople talk, which parts you had to bend to fit, and is there anything he claims about hammers and workshops that has no expression at all in my vocabulary? And when something doesn't survive the translation — is that because my way of talking is missing something his has, or because he's dressed up something ordinary in very long words? I genuinely can't tell which, and it seems like the interesting question.

One thing to bear in mind: my mate Dave, who's a plumber and has no time for this stuff, reckons the whole thing boils down to "you notice your tools when they break" with extra syllables. I suspect that's unfair but I can't put my finger on where it goes wrong. If your residue report gives me something to come back at Dave with, all the better.
