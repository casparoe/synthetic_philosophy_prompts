I'm a cog sci PhD student moonlighting in a phil mind reading group, and last week I put together an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't find the weak joint. Here it is.

(1) For a state to be phenomenally conscious, there has to be something it's like for the subject to be in it.
(2) What it's like for the subject must be fixed by what the subject themselves is in a position to know and report about their experience -- otherwise the "for the subject" part is doing no work, and the experience belongs to nobody.
(3) A content is one a subject is in a position to know and report only if it's poised for free use in reasoning, report, and rational control of action -- i.e. access conscious, broadcast in a global workspace.
(4) So phenomenal consciousness requires access consciousness.
(5) Access consciousness is a purely functional profile, the kind of thing a broadcast architecture implements, with no leftover residue once the broadcasting is fully described.
(6) So either phenomenal consciousness just is access consciousness (and the supposed extra "hard" ingredient vanishes), or phenomenal consciousness floats free of anything the subject can ever access, in which case it's unknowable even to its owner and the concept is empty.
(7) Either way, there is no distinct phenomenal consciousness: the access/phenomenal distinction is an illusion.

The conclusion has to be wrong -- the distinction seems obviously coherent, and overflow cases (subjects seeming to see more than they can report) look like direct counterexamples to whatever premise they attack. But every premise looked fine when I wrote it. I suspect something slides between (2) and (3), but my officemate thinks (6) is a false dichotomy and I'd rather not concede that. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? Keep it under 400 words.
