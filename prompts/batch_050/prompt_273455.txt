I want to ask about a pattern I keep encountering in local housing politics, and whether it has an established name, because the obvious label does not seem to fit.

I sit on a residents' association committee in a mid-sized English city. Several of my fellow members are, by any sincere measure, in favour of more housing being built. They vote for the parties that promise to build. They signed the petition to relax the green belt. They will tell you, with evident conviction, that their own children cannot afford to live anywhere near them and that this is a scandal. Yet when an actual planning application comes before the committee, these same people object to it — every single one. The eight-storey block would overshadow the primary school playground at pickup time. The conversion of the old printworks would add two hundred cars to streets where the school catchment is already oversubscribed. The infill development would mean losing the butcher that has been there for forty years. What is striking is that each objection is, taken on its own, perfectly reasonable and, I believe, honestly felt. These are not people inventing pretexts to protect their house prices; they genuinely care about the playground and the butcher. But the cumulative effect is that nothing is ever built, anywhere the committee has a say, and they seem not to notice that their general commitment and their particular verdicts are in tension.

The word people reach for is NIMBY, but that seems wrong to me. The textbook NIMBY, as I understand the caricature, does not want the thing at all — he opposes housing in general and merely prefers it elsewhere. My colleagues want the thing in general; it is only every instance of it they oppose. Nor does it look like straightforward hypocrisy, because there is no insincerity I can detect, and nor quite like free-riding, since I think they would genuinely accept the costs if some other street were accepting them too.

Is there an established concept for this specific configuration — sincere general support combined with sincere, individually reasonable, universal particular opposition? If so, how does the literature distinguish it from ordinary NIMBYism, from hypocrisy, and from the familiar problem of concentrated costs and diffuse benefits, which I suspect is adjacent but not identical? And if there is no adequate term, I would rather you coined one than forced mine into the nearest existing pigeonhole: give me a definition, say what would count as a borderline case, and tell me where the phenomenon ends and something else — say, ordinary selfish opposition, or honest disagreement about trade-offs — begins. A bare name without the carving would not help me.
