After a recent referendum in my country — decided 52 to 48 on roughly two-thirds turnout — the head of government resigned, the incoming administration declared that the people had spoken, and legislators who had campaigned against the result announced that they would now vote to implement it, since their duty was to respect the will of the people. Yet nobody seems able to say what the people willed. Surveys suggested that voters on the winning side were motivated by quite different concerns that point toward incompatible policies; some admitted to voting as a protest, never expecting to win; and several of the positions bundled into the victorious camp would probably each have been rejected if put to the electorate one by one. At the same time, the very same political culture treats ordinary parliamentary majorities as perfectly reversible, demands supermajorities for constitutional change, and maintains entrenched rights and courts empowered to strike down whatever a majority enacts — as if the people's will, so understood, were emphatically not the last word.

I want to understand what this practice presupposes. When officials treat a bare majority in a single binary vote as the binding will of a collective agent — something that exists, is disclosed by the aggregation of ballots, and overrides a representative's own judgment about what to do — what theory of the will of the people are they thereby committed to? That the people constitutes a kind of agent with a unified will that votes reveal? That authority attaches to majorities as such, independently of what they decide? Or is the practice in fact compatible with a much thinner view, on which there is no collective will at all and the result is merely the output of a decision procedure we have agreed, for the sake of stability and peace, to treat as final? Please reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories), and be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Which rival views does it quietly rule out — for example, the view that aggregation over conflicting individual preferences can fail to yield any coherent collective preference to be discovered, or the trustee view on which representatives owe voters their considered judgment rather than their obedience? And can the two halves of the practice — referendum outcomes treated as sacred, ordinary majorities treated as revisable and constitutionally constrained — rest on consistent commitments, or must one half be given up? Finally, if we abandoned the idea of a unitary popular will altogether, what would concretely have to change in how governments speak and act after such votes, and would anything of value be lost?
