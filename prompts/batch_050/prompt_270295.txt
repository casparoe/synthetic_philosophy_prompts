I serve on the grants committee of a mid-sized public health foundation, and at our March meeting we must decide whether to commit roughly $25 million to one of two programs — a malaria chemoprevention campaign or a water-infrastructure expansion in the same region — or instead to spend $1.8 million and eighteen months commissioning an epidemiological study that would substantially sharpen our impact estimates for both, deferring the final choice until the study reports. At our last meeting the discussion got philosophical. One trustee argued that commissioning the study is close to a decision-theoretic free lunch: she invoked a result attributed to the statistician I. J. Good which, as she presented it, shows that an ideal Bayesian agent facing a fixed menu of options can never be worse off, in expectation, for having acquired costless information before choosing. Two colleagues pushed back. One noted that we are manifestly not that ideal agent: this committee has twice commissioned studies and then deadlocked over their interpretation, and the delay itself has costs the theorem does not count — a matching donor's pledge expires next year, the regional government may proceed without us and foreclose the most effective sites, and the transmission seasons that pass meanwhile are unrecoverable. The third raised a different worry: the study's findings would be public, and he suspects there is a real chance that no plausible result would actually change what we do, since both programs may clear our funding bar under any reasonable readings of the data, in which case we would have paid $1.8 million and eighteen months for information that could not have altered our choice.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — commission the study, commit to a program now, or some hybrid such as funding one program immediately while the other is studied — framed as a motion the committee could actually vote on, with a timeline. I want the reasoning, not just the bottom line. Identify which assumptions of the value-of-information result genuinely hold in our situation and which fail, and be precise about what work each failure does. In particular, I have a suspicion that the theorem, properly understood, tells us the study's findings cannot hurt a perfectly rational decision-maker but is silent on whether they are worth the fee, the delay, a changed menu of options, and our own demonstrated unreliability as interpreters of evidence; I would like that suspicion confirmed or corrected, with care given to the separate point that information has decision value only if some possible result would change the decision. Do not let the recommendation rest on a bare appeal to the theorem in either direction. Close with an honest statement of the strongest consideration favoring the course you recommend against, and explain why it does not prevail.
