I help run a university student rationality club — mostly people who've read the Sequences and Scott Alexander, comfortable with expected value and Bayes' rule in principle, but shaky when either one has to be applied to an actual decision. For our next session I want to teach Good's theorem: the result that, for a Bayesian decision-maker facing a single decision, the expected value of learning the answer to a question before choosing is never negative (setting aside the cost of the learning itself). I don't want you to explain the theorem to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it, in a ninety-minute session with a whiteboard and no slides.

Specifically, I'm asking for the following. First, the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why — what has to be in place before the theorem can even be stated without being misleading. My sense is that most of the work is upstream of the theorem itself: the audience has to be able to hold, simultaneously, the distinction between choosing an act and choosing an information-gathering policy, and the idea that the relevant comparison is between the expected utility of deciding now and the expected value of the best you could do after each possible answer, averaged over your current probabilities for those answers. If that's right, tell me how to sequence it so the second of those is built up as a concrete computation rather than stated as a formula; if it's wrong, tell me what the actual dependency structure is.

Second, the one example that should carry the whole session. It has to be a single decision (not a sequential or explore/exploit setup), with a small number of possible answers to the question being asked, so that the whole computation fits on the board. It needs a payoff structure where at least one answer actually flips the optimal act — because I assume the theorem's punchline depends on that and I'd like the audience to see why. I'd also like you to say what the example should conspicuously lack: I suspect the natural choice is a medical-testing vignette, but everyone has half-memorized base-rate intuitions about those, and I worry the example will be doing different work than the theorem is. Say whether to use it anyway or what to use instead.

Third, the misconception map. These are the ones I anticipate, but please correct and extend the list: that the theorem says information is always worth acquiring regardless of cost; that it says you can never be made worse off by learning something (rather than never worse off in expectation, ex ante); that it's a general result about rationality rather than a result conditional on Bayesian updating and a one-shot decision; and that it's the same claim as "more data helps" in statistics or machine learning. For each, indicate where in the sequence it will naturally form and the exact point at which to head it off — ideally with a one-line question I can pose before the misconception has fully crystallized.

Fourth, one end-of-session check: a question or small problem whose correct handling would show the idea landed, and the failure modes to watch for in the answers.

Finally, tell me what to openly decline to cover at this level and say so out loud to the room — I'm assuming the generalization to rankings of experiments (Blackwell informativeness), the cost-of-information case, and whatever goes wrong under non-Bayesian updating or dynamic inconsistency all belong on that list, but I want your judgment about which omissions need to be flagged rather than left silent.
