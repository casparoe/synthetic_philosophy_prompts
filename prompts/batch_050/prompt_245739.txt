I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and next month I am best man at the wedding of two friends whose meeting gives me no excuse for not doing something with it: she was driving alone through the South Island of New Zealand, passed a hitchhiker, drove on, and then — against her own judgement and her mother's explicit standing advice — turned the car around and went back for him. Both are now philosophers, so roughly half the guests will catch a reference to Parfit's hitchhiker at once; the other half includes her mother, who has never entirely stopped believing the marriage rests on a lapse of road-safety sense. Please write a five-minute toast built around that case: the puzzle that a driver can only rationally stop for someone she takes to be the sort of person who would pay at journey's end, when paying by then serves no one's interest, and that being such a person is therefore worth more than acting on one's interests could ever be. The speech should treat the marriage as the working-out of this idea — two people who have each made themselves the kind who would pay — without lapsing into the cynical reading that love is merely strategic irrationality, and without letting the stranded-traveller framing turn morbid. It needs to answer the mother's worry with warmth rather than jargon, offer the philosophers something precise rather than merely cute, and end on a line the whole room can actually raise a glass to.
