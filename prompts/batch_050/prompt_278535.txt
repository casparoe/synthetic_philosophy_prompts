I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a British magazine, and I am preparing a long feature on how behavioural genetics is reported in the popular press. My editor has asked me to include a sidebar fact-checking the sort of sentences about heritability that routinely appear in newspapers, including, I regret to say, our own pages.

You are an expert in behavioural genetics and the philosophy of biology, with substantial experience explaining statistical concepts to non-specialists. Please think carefully before answering; it may help to begin by briefly brainstorming the distinct ways a sentence about heritability can mislead a lay reader, and only then turn to the ranking itself.

Below are seven draft sentences of the kind my feature will examine. Each is the sort of thing a competent but hurried science writer might actually publish. None contains an outright fabrication; the question is how badly each one misleads.

1. Intelligence is around 50 per cent heritable, which means that about half of any given person's intelligence is due to their genes and half to their environment.
2. Because height is highly heritable, the dramatic increase in average height across the twentieth century must have a genetic explanation.
3. The high heritability of reading ability suggests that differences in the quality of schooling make little difference to how well children read.
4. If a trait turns out to have low heritability, we can conclude that genes are largely irrelevant to it.
5. Heritability estimates for a trait can vary substantially from one population or era to another, so a figure estimated in one country may not apply elsewhere.
6. The heritability of earnings is higher in some countries than others, which may tell us something about how uniformly those societies allocate opportunity.
7. Since the heritability of cognitive ability within wealthy countries is substantial, genetic differences are the most plausible explanation for average differences in cognitive test scores between countries.

Please do the following:

First, rank the seven sentences from most misleading to least misleading for a typical educated reader. Second, give each sentence a score out of 10, where 10 is maximally misleading and 0 is not misleading at all. Third, in two to four sentences per item, explain your placement: identify the specific confusion the sentence invites, and note any reading on which it might be defensible, so that your ranking is contestable rather than dogmatic. Where two sentences are close, say what tipped the balance. Finally, in a short concluding paragraph, state the single most important distinction a science writer should master before using the word heritability in print.

Please write your answer in British English.
