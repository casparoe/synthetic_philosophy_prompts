I've read the Sequences and followed the moderation wars on various community blogs for over a decade, and I now help run the curation pipeline for a rationalist-adjacent group blog: roughly thirty posts a week come through, and my team of four has to decide which empirical claims in a post can be published standing on the author's say-so, which must be independently verified before publication, and which must be cut. Each of us spends maybe twenty minutes per post, none of us is a domain expert in most of what we review, and the editor-in-chief signs off on appeals.

The two live philosophies on my team map onto the testimony debate in epistemology. The reductionists hold that accepting something on testimony is only ever justified when the hearer has positive, non-testimonial grounds for trusting the speaker in this instance: observed track record, internal coherence, consistency with what the hearer already knows, absence of distorting incentives. On this view testimony is not a basic source of knowledge; it bottoms out in perception, memory, and induction. The anti-reductionists hold that hearers have a default entitlement to accept testimony absent specific defeaters, the way we treat perception and memory; demanding positive reasons in every case is a skepticism we would never apply elsewhere.

Write me a decision procedure my team can actually run, claim by claim: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "verify or cut," and where it hands the decision upward because the question is one of values rather than facts. Say explicitly which commitments from each camp you are keeping and which you are dropping, and flag where the procedure will misfire. Handle at least these hard cases: anonymous sources, second-hand claims (the author is relaying someone else's testimony), claims where the author has a financial or reputational stake, self-reports of unusual personal experience, and claims of expert consensus.

Then run the procedure on three cases: an anonymous author's detailed account of dysfunction inside a well-known AI lab; a respected longtime community member's anecdote about a meditation technique curing her chronic pain; and a post asserting that a large literature in social psychology has failed to replicate. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and say what it cannot do.
