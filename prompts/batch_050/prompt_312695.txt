A colleague and I disagree about whether there is any principled obstacle to assigning probabilities to one's own deliberate choices. Her view: when I am deliberating about whether to ask my boss for a raise tomorrow, any credence I state -- say, 0.6 that I will -- is either self-undermining (learning my own number feeds back into the deliberation and changes it) or trivial (a report of which way I'm already leaning, not a genuine prediction). My view: the difficulty is merely practical, like predicting a chaotic system, and in principle my future choice is an event like any other, so a well-calibrated credence is possible even mid-deliberation. Please give me your own credence that my colleague is right -- that is, that there exists a genuine in-principle barrier, not just a practical one, to an agent assigning probabilities to her own not-yet-made decisions -- and decompose it: the probability that self-reference destabilizes any such credence, the probability that deliberation requires treating one's options as open in a way incompatible with assigning them chances, the probability that a decision theory can even function without credences over one's own acts, and any other sub-question you think the issue turns on, each with a number and a brief justification, then the combination. Finally, identify the sub-question your total is most sensitive to and what argument would move it.
