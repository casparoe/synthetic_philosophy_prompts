I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and over several years of reporting — on gain-of-function virology, open-source releases of capable AI systems, small-scale geoengineering trials, and gene-drive proposals — I keep returning to the unilateralist's curse: the argument, associated with Nick Bostrom and colleagues, that when an undertaking can be launched by any one of many independent actors, and each actor forms a private estimate of whether it is worth doing, the undertaking will tend to go ahead so long as even one actor's estimate is favorable. The more actors there are with the power to act alone, the more likely the most optimistic estimate sets the outcome — even if the majority correctly judge the initiative to be a bad idea. I am drafting a long essay on what this argument does and does not show, and before I commit to a position I want my own working views audited for consistency.

Please respond as a philosopher of science and social epistemology who is broadly sympathetic to the curse argument but alert to its misuses and overextensions. Think carefully rather than reaching for the nearest stock objection. Before producing your final answer, spend a few paragraphs of explicit brainstorming in which you test my commitments against one another pairwise and against the structure of the argument itself; then present the audit proper. Here is where I have landed:

(1) The curse is real and general: absent some coordination mechanism, initiatives that any of many actors can undertake unilaterally will systematically be undertaken too often, and the problem worsens as the number of capable actors grows.

(2) Scientific openness — publish rather than classify, release rather than restrict — should remain the default even for dual-use research; the burden of proof lies on those who would restrict.

(3) The curse draws its force from dispersed private information; where actors pool their information honestly and deliberate together, the curse largely dissolves. Transparency, understood as shared information, is therefore the principal remedy.

(4) Liberal democracies are structurally less exposed to the curse than autocracies, because consequential projects there must run a gauntlet of public deliberation before proceeding.

(5) Coordinated disclosure in cybersecurity — notify the affected party, observe an embargo, then publish — is the right institutional template for managing dual-use hazards in other fields.

My question is whether this package hangs together. For each genuine tension you identify, state which commitments are involved, how severe the conflict is, and what the cheapest revision would be. Order the tensions by severity, and explicitly distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones that dissolve on inspection — I do not want every pair flagged. Close by telling me which single revision would do the most to stabilize the set and what it would cost me rhetorically in the essay. I have no prior view about which commitment, if any, I would surrender, so do not pull your punches on any of them.
