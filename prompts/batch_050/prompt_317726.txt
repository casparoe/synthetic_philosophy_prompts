I'm in an upper-year ethics seminar and this week we got onto standing to blame -- the idea that a hypocrite somehow loses the right to call out the very fault they're guilty of themselves. After class my friend Sam and I tried to nail down why exactly hypocrisy disqualifies blame, and we ended up with an argument that seems to prove way too much, namely that basically no one ever has standing to blame anyone for anything. I'll lay it out as best I remember:

(1) If you are guilty of the same fault you're blaming someone for, you lack standing to blame them for it. (This is just the standard thought about hypocrites -- the guy who cheats on his taxes can't properly get on his high horse about his neighbor doing it.)

(2) Whether two faults count as "the same" can't turn on morally irrelevant surface details -- who the victim happened to be, what day it was, whether it was cash or favors. Same-ness has to be individuated by whatever makes the act *wrong*.

(3) But wrongs are made wrong by pretty general features -- betraying a trust, deceiving someone who relied on you, prioritizing your own convenience over a duty, exploiting someone weaker. The size of the betrayal or the identity of the victim isn't itself a wrong-*making* feature, it just changes how bad the instance is.

(4) Realistically, every adult has at some point culpably done something from each of those general categories -- broken a confidence, lied to someone who trusted them, shirked a duty because it was inconvenient.

(5) So for virtually any wrong anyone commits, the would-be blamer has themselves committed a wrong of the same kind, in the only sense of "same kind" that (2) and (3) leave standing.

(6) Therefore almost nobody ever has standing to blame anyone for anything. Moral blame is essentially dead as a practice.

Now, (6) can't be right -- blame clearly functions, and we distinguish constantly between people who can and can't properly deliver it. But I keep poking at the premises and each one looks defensible in isolation, and the steps feel tight. Sam wants to just bite (6) and say standing is an illusion, which seems like a dodge. If I had to protect something I'd protect (1), since it's the whole motivation for the topic, and I'm most suspicious that something funny happens between (2)/(3) and (5) -- but I can't articulate what.
