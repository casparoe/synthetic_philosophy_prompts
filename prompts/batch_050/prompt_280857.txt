I co-run a monthly discussion group for graduate students in clinical psychology and philosophy of science — about 14 people, and I have a 75-minute slot coming up. Many of them have read about Ian Hacking's idea of the looping effect of human kinds — the thought that classifying people changes the people classified, in a way that classifying, say, chemical elements does not — and they all nod along in seminar discussions, but I'm convinced almost none of them have really felt what makes human kinds different from natural kinds. It's too easy to treat the looping effect as a slogan. Design a structured activity that makes participants experience the mechanism firsthand: they should find their own behavior shifting in response to a label applied to them, notice themselves either conforming to or resisting the classification, and only afterward have the concept named and mapped onto what just happened. No lecture or framing up front — the less they know about the point of the exercise, the better.

Ideally the design would include a classification scheme invented for the occasion (so nobody brings in baggage from real psychiatric categories), some task or interaction in which the classifications actually matter, and at least one round in which classifications are revised or participants can contest them, so the group experiences the feedback loop running in both directions — people changing under a label, and the label changing under pressure from the people. Please give me concrete rules, materials, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and instructions for what I as facilitator should and shouldn't say at each stage. Then write the debrief: how to connect specific moments from the session to the looping effect, to the difference between human kinds and natural kinds, and to the question of what this means for psychiatric diagnostic categories specifically.

Also tell me candidly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, participants suspecting the trick and performing skepticism rather than genuinely reacting, or the invented categories feeling too arbitrary to bite — and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be honest about which features of real psychiatric looping the activity cannot reproduce: real diagnoses carry institutional power, treatment access, stigma, and stakes that a 75-minute game with fake labels cannot, and I'd like the debrief to acknowledge that gap explicitly rather than paper over it.
