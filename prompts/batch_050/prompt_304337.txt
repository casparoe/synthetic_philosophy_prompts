I serve on the ethics advisory panel of a regional civil-protection authority, and we are currently revising our standing emergency protocols. Three of them strike me as philosophically loaded, and my colleagues don't see why. Our flood protocol permits engineers to open sluice gates diverting floodwater away from a city and onto farmland, even where it is foreseen with near certainty that a few residents who refused to evacuate will drown, provided the expected number saved is considerably larger. Our wildfire protocol flatly forbids demolishing an occupied hamlet to cut a firebreak, even where the demolition would certainly halt the fire and save a town, and even though the residents' deaths would be no more wanted by anyone involved than the deaths in the flood case. Our triage protocol, meanwhile, directs medics to allocate scarce ventilators by expected survival, knowingly letting some patients die so that others may live, and nobody at the table batted an eyelid at that one.

When I pressed a colleague on the difference between the first two protocols, he said that in the flood case the deaths are a side effect of redirecting an existing threat, whereas in the firebreak case the destruction of the hamlet is itself the means by which the town is saved. I find this slippery: in the firebreak case it is the absence of fuel, not anyone's death, that stops the fire, so the deaths seem as incidental to the saving there as they are in the flood case. What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit moral theory running through this pattern of protocols. Does the set of them presuppose something like the doctrine of double effect, with its distinction between intended means and foreseen side effects, and can that distinction actually be drawn between my two cases without collapsing? Does the triage protocol rest on the same commitments or on a quietly different, aggregative one, and is the combination coherent? Be honest, too, about where the practice is underdetermined: could a rule-consequentialist endorse precisely these three protocols for reputation-and-coordination reasons, so that nothing in the practice strictly presupposes a deontological theory at all? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in each protocol if the authority instead adopted a straightforward act-consequentialist review in which only expected outcomes matter.

Begin with a single sentence stating the theory you take the protocols, taken together, to presuppose most strongly. Your whole answer should run to no more than 450 words.
