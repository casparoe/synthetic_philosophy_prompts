Having spent my working life as an experimental chemist and only in retirement turned to philosophy, I find myself stuck on the dispute between psi-ontic and psi-epistemic readings of the quantum state: is the wavefunction itself part of physical reality, or does it merely encode what an observer knows about a reality whose true state lies underneath? Rather than another summary of the debate, I would like a thought experiment built to order. Construct a toy universe whose underlying physics is entirely classical — definite ontic states, with each quantum state amounting to a probability distribution over them — in which two distinct, non-orthogonal states, say |0⟩ and |+⟩, are both compatible with at least one shared ontic state, so that preparing the system sometimes leaves the underlying reality identical regardless of which quantum state was prepared. Then, using two independently prepared copies of the system and a suitably chosen joint measurement, show that this overlap forces a prediction quantum mechanics contradicts, and identify precisely which assumption carries the load, since I suspect it is the independence of the two preparations rather than anything about measurement. State the verdict each side of the dispute delivers and why, explain what the psi-epistemic theorist must give up in order to escape the contradiction, and tell me honestly what becomes of the argument if the two distributions are forbidden from overlapping at all. Please keep the reasoning at the level of simple probability rather than Hilbert space formalism, and write your answer in American English, in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
