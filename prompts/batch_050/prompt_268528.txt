In November 2018, at a genome-editing summit in Hong Kong, the Chinese researcher He Jiankui announced that twin girls had been born from embryos he had edited with CRISPR to disable the CCR5 gene, with the stated aim of making them resistant to HIV. The work had been carried out covertly, on the strength of an ethics approval that proved to be falsified, and in defiance of a broad consensus among gene-editing researchers that clinical germline editing was premature. The reaction was near-universal condemnation, and He was later tried and sentenced to prison. I would like this episode analyzed through the lens of the unilateralist's curse.

The curse, briefly: where any member of a group can unilaterally undertake an initiative that affects everyone, and each member privately estimates the initiative's value, the initiative will be undertaken whenever even one member's estimate is positive. Because estimates are noisy, the highest estimate will on average overshoot the true value, so a group of sincere, unbiased estimators will collectively undertake initiatives of negative true value more often than any member intends — unless members discount their own judgment on the grounds that their willingness to act is itself evidence that their estimate is an outlier.

Please structure the analysis as follows. First, the mapping: who counts as the relevant group (the global community of researchers with the technical capacity to do this?), what exactly the initiative was, and whether the background conditions — a well-publicized moratorium consensus, widely distributed technical capacity, and large reputational prizes for being first — make this a clean or a strained instance of the curse. Second, what the theory explains: does it account for why the initiative came from this kind of actor (a peripheral figure with much to gain rather than a leading laboratory), why repeated summits and consensus statements failed to prevent it, and why the community's response took the form it did — not merely condemnation but efforts to raise the cost and difficulty of unilateral action, through registries, governance proposals, and criminal penalties? Third, where the model breaks: the curse assumes agents who share the group's ends and differ only in their estimates. What happens to the analysis if He's judgment was not sincere — if the payoff he pursued was fame rather than the outcome he claimed — or if he calculated that facts on the ground, in the form of living children, would soften opinion after the fact? And does the curse still bite when acting requires violating explicit norms, so that the initiative is better modeled as defection under penalty? Finally, run the argument in the other direction: does the episode confirm the theory, or does it instead show that the curse is a poor description of cases where the barrier to unilateral action is normative rather than technical — and would a rival model (a race-to-be-first dynamic, a principal–agent failure, or plain misconduct) explain the episode better? Where the theory is silent about a feature of the case, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
