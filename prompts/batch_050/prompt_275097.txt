After 40 years in condensed matter physics I've started reading philosophy of mind, and I keep bumping into panpsychism -- specifically the ethical versions of it. If even electrons or fields have some flicker of experience, people seem to think this has consequences for what we owe the physical world, but every argument I've seen produces absurdity the moment it touches actual physics. Before I let anyone sell me on the ethics, I want the spec first: what would a theory of sentience-in-fundamental-physics have to satisfy before its moral implications deserve to be taken seriously at all?

Give me the requirements list, with the consideration or case behind each. Some candidates from my own fumbling, correct or reorder as you see fit: (a) it must say which physical systems bear experience in a way that doesn't depend on how you coarse-grain the description -- otherwise "does this system suffer?" changes with the resolution of your model, and ethics can't ride on a choice of grain; (b) it must be compatible with relativity, meaning it can't need a privileged frame in which experiences exist, or else it owes an account of which slicing of spacetime the moral facts track; (c) it must come with an aggregation rule turning micro-experiences into something action-guiding, otherwise "everything suffers" makes ethics silent rather than demanding; (d) it must not contradict anything physics actually says, though maybe that's just my disciplinary chauvinism talking.

Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are the taste of one camp (constitutive panpsychists, IIT people, cosmopsychists), and say whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable -- that's the part I most want to see. Also identify the single requirement that knocks out the most existing contenders. A brief scorecard against the list is fine but keep the weight on the criteria. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming that killer requirement.
