I was at a barbecue last weekend and two of my friends, Lena and Marco, got into a surprisingly heated argument about something that happened to a third friend of ours, Jess. Jess asked her neighbor Priya to watch her dog while she was away for a long weekend. Priya agreed. On the second day the dog got sick (some kind of internal infection, the vet later said), Priya noticed the dog was lethargic and not eating but figured it was just sad its owner was gone, and did nothing. When Jess came back the dog was in bad shape and died at the emergency vet. The vet said it likely would have survived if it had been brought in roughly a day earlier.

Here's roughly how the argument went. (1) Lena said Priya caused the dog's death, flat out. The vet's testimony settles it: but for Priya's inaction, the dog lives. (2) Marco said causation requires doing something. Priya didn't do anything to the dog, the infection did. You can be negligent, sure, but "doing nothing" can't be a cause, otherwise the number of causes of any event explodes -- the dog would also have lived if Jess had stayed home, if a vet had walked by, if Priya's aunt had called that morning and happened to mention dog symptoms. So on Lena's account Jess also "killed" her own dog by going on the trip. (3) Lena said the difference is that Priya took on the responsibility. Random bystanders and Jess-on-vacation aren't the same as the person who agreed to watch the animal. Once you accept a duty, your omissions are on the causal ledger in a way other people's aren't. (4) Marco replied that duty-talk is moral talk, not causal talk -- Lena is smuggling in blame and calling it causation. The infection is the cause of death; Priya is the one we hold responsible. Conflating those two things is exactly the kind of sloppy thinking that makes people say nonsense like "everything causes everything." (5) Lena came back with: when a safety inspector signs off on a bridge they never actually inspected and it collapses, everyone -- journalists, prosecutors, the engineers -- says the failed inspection was one of the causes of the collapse. Marco says that's just loose everyday speech, and Lena says maybe everyday speech is tracking something real that his tidy action-only theory misses.

It never got resolved because someone put the game on. Can you go through the five moves and tell me who comes out ahead at each step, and what both of them are getting wrong or missing? Give me your answer as a table with three columns: "Move" (1 through 5), "Winner" (Lena, Marco, or Tie), and "Why" (two or three sentences max per cell). Then one last row labeled "Overall" where Winner is whoever you think has the better total position and Why summarizes the main thing the loser fails to see.
