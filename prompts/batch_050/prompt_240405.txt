I recently took a seat on the allocation committee of a regional hospital network, and one of our standing problems is how to distribute a limited stock of a scarce treatment when eligible patients outnumber doses. Our current policy is simple aggregation: allocate so as to save the greatest number. Reading around the ethics of this, I keep running into Taurek's challenge to that policy -- his argument that when you must choose between saving one stranger and saving several, morality does not require you to save the many, and that giving each person an equal chance (say, by a fair coin flip) is permissible because there is no aggregate subject who suffers the combined loss; the losses fall on separate individuals, one each. I want to stress-test my own reaction to this before our next meeting, and for that I need a case built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which Taurek's equal-chance procedure and the save-the-greater-number rule deliver different verdicts, with the pull toward counting numbers made as strong as it can possibly be while every premise of Taurek's argument remains intact. Constraints on the scenario: (i) it must be realistic -- a clinical or emergency-resource setting, no trolleys, no transplant-surgeon fantasies, no science fiction; (ii) the decision-maker must be a complete stranger to every person at risk, since Taurek's concessive remarks about saving one's friends or family are a separate issue I want bracketed; (iii) the stakes must be symmetric person-for-person -- same condition, same prognosis if treated, roughly the same life at stake -- so that no quality-adjustment can smuggle in a reason to favor one side; (iv) there must be no uncertainty about outcomes or identities; and (v) the scenario itself should be a single paragraph, with the numbers on the two sides made as lopsided as realism allows.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. State the verdict each procedure reaches and reconstruct the reasoning behind each: for Taurek, why the absence of a collective sufferer is supposed to license the coin flip even at extreme ratios; for the numbers side, the strongest version of the argument that equal concern for each person requires saving the greater number (for instance, the idea that each person on the larger side has a claim that is left wholly unbalanced if the coin is flipped, whereas flipping balances claims pairwise). Then tell me which structural feature of your constructed case is doing the work in generating the divergence, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed. In particular, address one variation directly: if the decision-maker is herself among the people at risk on the smaller side, does Taurek's reasoning survive, or does the case collapse into something where even his sympathizer should count numbers? Close by stating, honestly, what someone on the committee who finds your scenario decisive for the numbers would have to be assuming, and what a committed Taurekian would say the scenario leaves untouched.
