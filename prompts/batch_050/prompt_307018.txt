In sixteenth-century Korea, two of the most rigorous neo-Confucian thinkers of the Joseon dynasty, Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Ki Dae-seung (Gobong), spent years exchanging letters about what looks at first like a narrow classificatory question, and a generation later Yi I (Yulgok) and Song Hon (Ugye) resumed the exchange without settling it. The question concerns two lists of feelings inherited from the classics. The Four Beginnings are the sprouts of virtue Mencius locates in every heart: commiseration, shame and dislike, deference, and approval and disapproval. The Seven Emotions are the ordinary feelings catalogued in the ritual classics: joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire. Both sides accept the shared neo-Confucian framework in which every psychological event involves both principle (li) and material force (qi), and in which the two are never found apart. The dispute is over how to describe the genesis of the two groups. Toegye, after considerable revision, arrived at the view that in the case of the Four it is principle that issues them, with material force following along, whereas in the case of the Seven it is material force that issues them, with principle riding along — principle and qi are each said to issue something, though never separately. His critics objected that this divides what the shared framework declares indivisible: if principle and material force are inseparable, there cannot be two distinct issuances, and the honest thing to say is that all feelings arise through material force, with the Four distinguished only by the fact that their principled dimension is salient and reliably aligned with the good, and the Seven by their liability to excess and deficiency. Toegye's defenders reply that a merely perspectival difference in salience cannot explain why the Four are trustworthy guides to virtue while the Seven are not, and that issuance must mark a real asymmetry — something like causal or metaphysical priority — rather than a difference in what strikes the observer.

I have gone back and forth over this debate for years and I genuinely cannot tell what the two parties are disagreeing about, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. My working hypotheses, none of which I am confident in: first, that this is a substantive disagreement about whether principle has any generative activity of its own, with Toegye implicitly granting li a power theorthodox framework officially denies it; second, that it is a verbal dispute sustained by the ambiguity of the term for issuance, which might mean origination, triggering, predominance, or normative grounding, so that the sides never deny what the other asserts; third, that the two parties are answering different questions under one heading — Toegye giving an aetiology of moral knowledge, his critics a taxonomy of feelings — and the impasse is produced by conflating them; fourth, that the real fault line is over which phenomena count as data: the critic treats explanatory economy within the shared metaphysics as the constraint, Toegye treats the phenomenological reliability of the moral sprouts as non-negotiable and bends the metaphysics to fit.

Please diagnose the disagreement using the positions as I have laid them out, correcting my summary where it is wrong or incomplete. Tell me which, if any, of my hypotheses survives scrutiny, or give me a better one. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: would a decisive reading of Zhu Xi on whether principle can act settle it, or is that merely the exegetical shadow of the philosophical question? Is there a distinction — between kinds of priority, or between explanation and justification — that would dissolve the dispute, and would dissolving it show that nothing was ever at stake, or that something was? And if you think no resolution should be expected because both parties are constrained by shared premises that underdetermine the answer, argue for that directly rather than offering a survey of who said what.
