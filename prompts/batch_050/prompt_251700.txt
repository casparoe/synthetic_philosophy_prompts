# Is There a Grammar Before Language?

During my scientific career, I often encountered talk of mental representations, but the expression seemed to do more work than its users explained. A representation was said to be in the mind, encoded by the brain, or manipulated during reasoning, yet it was frequently unclear what sort of thing was supposed to be represented, encoded, or manipulated.

The language-of-thought hypothesis interests me because it offers a relatively definite answer. On its strongest interpretation, at least some thoughts have a constituent structure analogous to the structure of sentences. A complex thought is built from reusable parts, and its meaning depends both on those parts and on how they are arranged. Reasoning is possible because the mind can operate over these constituents without having to learn every complete thought independently.

The hypothesis is not that the skull contains tiny sentences in English, German, or any other natural language. Nor is it the claim that conscious thinking always feels verbal. It is better understood as a claim about the functional organization of cognition. If I can think that the dog chased the cat, I can ordinarily also think that the cat chased the dog. The two thoughts use the same conceptual materials, but their different structures give them different contents. The ability to move from one to the other suggests that the constituents are reusable rather than welded into isolated wholes.

Three commitments seem central. First, at least some representations have meaningful parts. Second, a part retains a reasonably stable contribution across different combinations. Third, cognitive processes can identify, replace, and recombine parts because of their structural positions. I will call this functional grammar. It need not consist of rules that a person consciously knows, and it need not resemble the grammar taught in school. It is grammar only in the weaker sense of systematic constraints on combination.

This formulation avoids a common confusion. Inner speech is obviously linguistic: I can silently rehearse what I intend to say, translate an idea from one language into another, or notice that a sentence sounds awkward. But these facts concern the conscious presentation of thought, not necessarily the medium in which the underlying thinking occurs. A person may struggle to express an idea precisely because the idea and its verbal formulation are distinct. Conversely, fluent inner speech might be a late product of a process whose basic operations are not themselves in a natural language.

The hypothesis also need not imply that all cognition is language-like. Mental imagery, spatial navigation, motor control, emotional appraisal, and perceptual pattern recognition may use formats very different from propositional structures. A more moderate claim is that certain forms of inference and deliberation require recombinable representations. The question is not whether the entire mind is a language, but whether some central part of it must have language-like organization.

Evidence from infants and non-human animals is relevant, though difficult to interpret. Creatures without natural language can track objects, distinguish agents from inanimate things, anticipate the results of actions, and adjust their behavior when the relations among objects change. If an animal can respond differently depending on whether one object supports, blocks, or merely touches another, its cognition may involve reusable representations of objects and relations. Such evidence does not establish a language of thought. It does, however, make it implausible to define the hypothesis so narrowly that only fluent speakers of a natural language could possess structured thoughts.

The most obvious objection comes from neuroscience. Brains appear to contain networks of neurons with continuously varying activity, not discrete symbols arranged into sentences. Why should we expect to find anything resembling grammar in such a system?

This objection is weaker if the hypothesis concerns the wrong level of description. A digital computer can manipulate symbols even though its physical substrate consists of voltages and switching elements. Similarly, a neural constituent might be realized by a distributed pattern rather than by one neuron or an anatomically separate unit. The relevant question is not whether brain tissue visibly resembles written language. It is whether stable functional roles can be found across transformations of neural activity.

Yet the appeal to levels of description creates a danger. If any information present in a brain counts as a symbol, the hypothesis becomes unfalsifiable. I therefore propose a distinction between statistical recoverability and syntactic availability.

Information is statistically recoverable when an external decoder can extract it from a system’s state. A thermometer’s reading may allow a scientist to recover the temperature of a liquid, but the thermometer does not thereby reason about the liquid. Likewise, an experimentalist might train a decoder to discover whether a neural activity pattern was produced by seeing a large or small animal. That result would show that information about size was present in the pattern. It would not by itself show that the brain treats size as an independently usable constituent of thought.

A constituent is syntactically available, by contrast, when the system itself can use it in a stable role across combinations. The system need not contain a discrete token shaped like a word. The constituent may be distributed, graded, or context-dependent. What matters is that substituting one constituent for another has predictable effects because the downstream processes are sensitive to the constituent’s functional role. On this view, the language-of-thought hypothesis is not committed to beads-on-a-string symbols. It is committed to causal organization that supports recombination.

This distinction bears on recent artificial intelligence. Systems built from large networks can produce highly systematic language while representing information in vectors rather than explicit sentences. It would be premature to conclude either that their success vindicates the language-of-thought hypothesis or that their distributed representations refute it. Systematic output does not prove that the internal mechanism uses recombinable constituents. But the absence of easily visible symbols does not prove that no functional constituents exist.

The more informative test would be interventional rather than merely observational. Suppose we could alter the part of an internal state associated with one conceptual component while leaving the rest intact. Would the altered component carry its inferential role into new contexts, or would its apparent identity disappear outside the narrow conditions used to detect it? The first result would support syntactic availability. The second would suggest that an external analysis had imposed more structure on the system than the system itself exploited.

Human cognition adds another complication: composition appears to be approximate rather than perfect. The contribution of a concept can shift with context; analogies can cut across established categories; and some thoughts seem to organize information around prototypes, images, or narrative patterns. These facts may show that a strict, exceptionless grammar is psychologically unrealistic. They do not show that no recombinable structure exists. A mind might employ several representational formats and move information among them, reserving language-like operations for particular kinds of abstraction and inference.

My provisional conclusion is therefore neither that the mind contains sentences nor that the metaphor of an inner language should be discarded. The valuable part of the hypothesis is an architectural constraint: where cognition displays reliable recombination, we should ask whether the relevant components are causally available to the system itself. The hypothesis will earn its keep if it explains learning, transfer, and characteristic errors better than theories that posit only unstructured associations or holistic patterns.

The real issue is not whether there is a private language hidden beneath public language. It is whether thought has a functional grammar: a set of reusable roles through which old components can generate new contents. If so, the study of thought should focus less on whether neural structures look like inscriptions and more on which patterns of activity can be detached, substituted, and carried across contexts.
