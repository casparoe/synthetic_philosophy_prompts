I am preparing to defend, at a workshop on long-run economic and demographic forecasting, the claim that Malthus was right but early: that his core mechanism — population pressing against subsistence once institutional and technological slack is exhausted — is fundamentally sound, and that the Industrial Revolution postponed its vindication rather than refuted it. Anticipating the obvious counter, my paper adds a criterion: a deferred prediction is legitimate only if the theory specifies in advance the threshold conditions (here, returns to innovation falling below a critical level relative to population growth) under which the mechanism reasserts itself. The audience consists of two historians of economic thought who regard the nineteenth century as a settled refutation, a demographer who holds that the fertility transition eliminated the mechanism rather than suspending it, and several philosophers of science with Popperian sympathies who will treat any 'right but early' defense as textbook immunization. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as this audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede about the nineteenth century up front; whether my threshold criterion survives contact with the demographer; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
