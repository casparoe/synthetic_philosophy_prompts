In the ninth and final objection considered in his 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence, Turing concedes that the statistical evidence for telepathy strikes him as overwhelming, and observes that if telepathy is genuine the imitation game is compromised, since an interrogator might identify the human participant by non-sensory means. His remedy is to run the test under conditions shielded against telepathy. The passage is usually treated as a biographical curiosity — a strange aside in an otherwise austere paper — and philosophers of mind and AI almost never ask what follows from taking it seriously rather than explaining it away.

Suppose a small research group — three philosophers of mind and two logicians, funded for two years — proposes to do exactly that. Their brief is not to investigate parapsychology but to work out what Turing's concession implies for the imitation game, for the criteria by which thought should be attributed to machines, and for the computational picture of the mind that the paper helped found. Their working premise is that Turing, an empirically minded scientist, accepted what he took to be the best statistical evidence of his day and treated the result as a problem of experimental design rather than of metaphysics.

Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order? Candidate questions include, but need not be limited to:

1. Does genuine telepathy undermine the imitation game as a test of thinking, or only as a test of machine thinking? If a telepathic interrogator could pick out the human, does that show the test conflates two things — the capacity to think and the capacity to imitate a human — that Turing intended the setup to separate?
2. Is the shielding remedy available to Turing on his own terms? Does demanding behavioral evidence for intelligence while accepting statistical evidence for an invisible faculty commit him to two different standards of evidence, and if so, which standard should govern the attribution of thought?
3. Could telepathy be mechanized? If a machine were built possessing whatever faculty telepathy consists in, would the objection dissolve — and does the answer bear on whether Turing's criterion is best read as functionalist, behaviorist, or neither?
4. What is the general lesson for how operational tests of mentality should be administered? On one reading, Turing's response is that any channel by which the control and the candidate could be distinguished, however exotic, must be closed off; on another, the passage shows that no purely behavioral test can ever be decisive. Which reading is better supported by the rest of the paper, and what follows from each for contemporary tests of machine intelligence?

For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and logicians rather than for parapsychologists, and what argument or result over the two years would move it up or down the list. Also say which commonly drawn implications you consider overrated — for instance, the readings on which Turing was plainly joking, or on which the passage exposes the test as crude behaviorism best abandoned — and briefly why. The deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey of the secondary literature.
