I chair the ethics committee of a mid-sized civil engineering firm. We have been invited to bid on a government contract to design and build a detention facility that everyone on the committee agrees will involve serious rights violations: it is specified to hold inmates in conditions of permanent overcrowding, with no outdoor access. The difficulty is this. A dozen firms are qualified to do the work, and if we decline, the contract will go to a competitor on essentially identical terms — the facility will be built to the same specifications, on the same timeline, whether we participate or not. Our involvement would make no causal difference whatsoever to the wrong in question. One faction on the committee therefore argues that declining is an empty gesture: we would sacrifice revenue, the harm would occur unchanged, and morality does not require pointless sacrifices. The other faction argues that knowingly executing this design would make us complicit in the wrongdoing regardless of our causal impotence — that complicity is a matter of willing participation in a wrongful project, not of difference-making, and that a person who joins a firing squad whose victim would die anyway has still done something wrong. A third voice suggests a middle course: bid, and use the design process to marginally improve conditions within the contract's constraints, on the grounds that participating in a wrong in order to mitigate it beats abstaining while the full wrong proceeds. Draft the committee's decision memo. It should state a bottom-line recommendation the board can vote on, take a position on whether the makes-no-difference consideration dissolves the moral problem or merely reframes it, and give the strongest available statement of the losing side before answering it.
