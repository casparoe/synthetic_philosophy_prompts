So last night I got into this long, kind of heated argument with my friend Dan about Bitcoin (I know, I know, everyone has this argument). He's a true believer, I'm a skeptic, and somehow — we'd both had a couple beers — we ended up arguing about what George Berkeley would have made of it all. Yes, the 18th century idealist bishop. Dan insisted Berkeley would've loved crypto; I said he'd have hated Bitcoin but been totally fine with ordinary fiat money. Neither of us could land a knockout blow, and it's still bugging me today.

Some context we dug up on my phone mid-argument, in case it's not familiar: Berkeley actually wrote a whole book about economics, The Querist, which is literally just a long list of numbered questions about how to fix Ireland's economy, and in it he pushes hard for an Irish national bank that would issue and oversee paper currency. The big idea running through it is that money isn't wealth in itself — it's more like a ticket or a counter whose entire point is to give the holder a claim on other people's labor, and gold and silver only count as money by convention anyway, so well-managed paper would do the job just as well or better. He was also obsessed with the idea that money should circulate and stir people to industry, and he had it in for idle hoards sitting around doing nothing.

So, please adjudicate: if Berkeley were alive today and someone patiently explained to him (a) modern fiat currencies like the dollar, (b) Bitcoin, and (c) a central bank digital currency, what would he likely think of each? Don't just say "he'd back fiat because it's state-issued" — actually reason from the ticket/counter picture and the money-exists-to-promote-industry bit, because here's the tension I can't resolve: Bitcoin seems like the purest realisation of his "money is whatever we conventionally treat as a ticket" idea, since it has zero metal backing and its value is entirely a matter of shared acceptance, and yet it's mostly hoarded as an investment rather than circulating, and no public authority steers it toward any social purpose. Which consideration wins out for him? Also, if there's a reading where Dan's side has something going for it, tell me, because I grudginly suspect it might.
