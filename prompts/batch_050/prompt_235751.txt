I TA for a phil of science seminar (about 14 undergrads, 75-minute sessions) and next week is on mechanisms. I keep getting stuck on a corner of the topic that I think is genuinely underrated: whenever anyone -- a doctor, an engineer, an ML anomaly-detection system -- flags something as anomalous, they've implicitly already decided what counts as "the same mechanism working normally" versus "a broken or different mechanism," and that decision doesn't seem to be readable off the behavior itself. Like, a watch that loses a minute a day: is that the watch-mechanism malfunctioning, or a perfectly fine instance of a slightly different watch-mechanism? My students nod along when I say this in lecture but I don't think they feel it. I want an activity, not a lecture, where the problem bites them before anyone names it.

So: design a game or simulation I can run in class where the students have to act as anomaly detectors on some stream of cases (cards, a hidden-rule device, a live demo, whatever actually works), and where the setup is rigged so that two or more equally reasonable ways of drawing the boundary of "the mechanism" deliver different verdicts about the same cases -- and the disagreement has to surface on its own through their calls, not because I prompt it. Give me the concrete setup and rules, any roles, a rough timeline that fits the 75 minutes with the debrief included, and then a debrief guide that maps specific moments in the game onto the conceptual options for mechanism individuation (by component parts? by organization? by function or role? by input-output profile?) and explains which option was quietly doing the work at each point. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I actually run this (students gaming the rules, someone settling the disagreement too fast, etc.) and how to handle it, and one feature of the real problem that the game structurally cannot reproduce so I know what I have to supply in discussion myself.

One more thing: please be explicit about which claims here are fairly settled among people who work on mechanisms (e.g., I gather there's broad agreement that individuation is interest-relative in some sense) versus which are live disputes, so I don't accidentally teach a contested stance as if it were the consensus.
