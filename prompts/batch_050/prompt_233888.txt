I write literary fiction, and at a recent workshop my antagonist was criticised with a sentence I have heard often in the trade: 'everyone is the hero of their own story.' It was offered as a cure for flat villains, it plainly did its work in the room, and yet the longer I sit with it the less I know what it actually claims. Is it a psychological report — that people in fact experience themselves as protagonists, with memory and attention arranged accordingly? A moral conjecture — that nobody ever acts in the belief that what they do is wrong, so that every wrongdoer has a justifying tale? A theory of selfhood — that a self is nothing over and above a narrative construction, something like what I gather Daniel Dennett meant by a centre of narrative gravity? Or is it strictly professional advice, a rule about giving even minor characters an interior life, which commits the novelist to no claim about real persons whatsoever?

Please set out, as a list I can take back to the workshop, the distinct precise theses this single sentence could be expressing. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false — and indicate which readings, if any, would actually support the use to which the sentence was put in my case. I would also like to know whether the strongest readings are mutually compatible, and whether the slogan, taken seriously, casts doubt on the god's-eye narration I habitually rely on: if every consciousness is a centre of its own story, is the omniscient narrator a lie?

Please answer in British English, in no more than 500 words.
