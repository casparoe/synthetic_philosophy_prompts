I am preparing a lecture on applied animal ethics for a third-year course, and rather than trot out the standard taxonomy of positions I would like to pressure-test my own intuitions about aquatic animals, which I have never forced into any kind of order. So here are my working verdicts, gathered over years of teaching and a stint on our university's animal welfare committee. Boiling a live lobster: impermissible. Percussive stunning of farmed salmon followed immediately by gill-cutting: permissible, indeed exemplary. Immersing sea bass in an ice slurry, where death comes slowly through chilling: impermissible. Fasting salmon for five or so days before slaughter, as is standard practice to empty the gut: permissible, barely worth remarking on. Eyestalk ablation of prawn broodstock in hatcheries: impermissible. Catch-and-release fly-fishing for trout, carefully done with barbless hooks: permissible. Dropping a live goldfish into a tank as food for a pet turtle that would take pellets just as readily: impermissible. And then the case I keep circling: threading live prawns onto a hook as bait for flatfish — I genuinely cannot settle on a verdict, and part of what I want is to be told what my other verdicts commit me to there.

What troubles me is that I cannot locate the principle, if there is one. The feeder goldfish dies for a mere convenience and I condemn it; the angled trout is hooked purely for my sport and I let it pass, though the trout's suffering seems harder to justify, not easier, since the turtle at least needs to eat something. The fasted salmon is plausibly made worse off for far longer than the boiled lobster, yet I shrug at the one and condemn the other. So: please propose a principle — or two or three candidate principles if no single one will serve — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. State it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, tell me what it implies about the live-bait prawns and about one or two cases I have not mentioned (say, the routine slaughter and disposal of the cleaner fish used to control sea lice on salmon farms, or the sale of live carp kept in tubs for customers to kill at home), and be candid about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If you judge that no principle short of a gerrymander gets all of them, say so plainly, and tell me which verdict you think has to go and why, rather than rescuing my consistency at any cost.

One further request about how the answer should be calibrated. I would like you to separate what is reasonably settled — for instance, the broad shape of the current evidence on pain in fish and in decapod crustaceans — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and not to present a minority or contested position as though it were the consensus view. Where your proposed principle rests on an empirical or philosophical premise that specialists dispute, flag it as such. I would much rather receive an honest map of the disagreements than a tidy answer that papers over them.
