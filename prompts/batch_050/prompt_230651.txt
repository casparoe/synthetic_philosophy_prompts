I'm preparing the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate course on philosophy of medicine, and I need one essay question on the naturalism–normativism debate about the concept of disease. The students have read the standard material (Boorse's biostatistical theory, Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction hybrid, and normativist critiques), so a question they can answer by summarizing those positions is worthless. I want a question that forces them to apply the theories to a case the readings never discuss and to diagnose where each theory's machinery actually does its work.

Here is the setup I have in mind, which you should incorporate into the question. Suppose a high-altitude population has, over many generations, evolved enlarged lung capacity and elevated red-blood-cell counts as adaptations to low oxygen. Members of this population who migrate to sea level as adults frequently develop chronic mountain sickness -- headaches, fatigue, and eventual heart strain -- because their bodies keep producing red blood cells at altitude-appropriate rates, thickening their blood. Within the migrant community the condition is common; within the sea-level population at large it is statistically rare. Now suppose the national health insurer of the sea-level country proposes to define "disease," for coverage purposes, as any condition that is both statistically abnormal in the insured population and a cause of significant suffering, and on that basis covers treatment for the migrants' condition -- while denying coverage for the same blood-thickening when it occurs in a patient whose identical physiology results from testosterone doping rather than ancestry, on the grounds that the doping case is "self-inflicted."

The exam question should ask students to do three things: (1) determine whether the migrants' condition counts as a disease on Boorse's biostatistical account, taking the reference-class problem seriously -- statistically typical for the migrant subgroup, atypical for the national population, and functioning exactly as designed by natural selection; (2) assess whether Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction hybrid does better here, given that the condition is harmful but arguably involves no dysfunction at all; and (3) evaluate the insurer's definition as a normativist proposal -- in particular whether its "self-inflicted" exclusion reveals that the definition is covertly doing evaluative work it does not acknowledge, and whether that is an objection or just honesty about what disease concepts are for.

The exam allows 75 minutes for this essay. Please provide: the final wording of the question as it will appear on the exam; a model answer of roughly 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points, stating what each is worth, and -- crucially for my graders -- a one-line note for each point describing the characteristic way a weaker student gets it wrong (for instance, treating "statistically normal in the home population" as if it settled the Boorsean question, or assuming the doping exclusion is obviously inconsistent with the definition rather than asking what theory of disease would justify it). The scheme should make it possible to distinguish a student who sees that the case puts pressure on the distinction between "abnormal" and "dysfunctional" from one who merely reports that Boorse is a naturalist and Wakefield a hybrid theorist.
