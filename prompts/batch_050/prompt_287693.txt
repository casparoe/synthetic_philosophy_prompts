I am a non-executive director of Marlowe Industries, a mid-sized speciality-chemicals manufacturer, and I chair the ad hoc committee our board has convened on what we internally call the Tansley Brook matter. I need our committee's recommendation drafted as a formal memo to the full board ahead of next month's meeting. Please write in British English.

The facts, in outline. Between 1978 and 1994, a plant then operated by Halvorsen Chemical discharged chlorinated solvent waste into unlined lagoons behind its Tansley Brook works. Halvorsen was acquired in 2002, merged with two other firms, and the combined business renamed Marlowe Industries in 2006; the Tansley Brook site was sold in 2009 and is now a business park. No current Marlowe employee ever worked for Halvorsen, no current director or significant shareholder was involved at the time, the plant manager of the relevant period died in 2011, and the surviving records are fragmentary. Last year a hydrogeological survey found the solvent plume has migrated into the wells of a village of about eight hundred people; concentrations sit below statutory limits but above advisory thresholds, and the long-term health effects are genuinely contested. The residents' association, backed by the parish council, has asked Marlowe to fund wellhead filtration, twenty-five years of monitoring, and a small community fund — roughly £11 million over the period.

Legal advice is that liability is contestable: the acquisition indemnities are ambiguous, limitation arguments are available, and litigation would take four to six years with an uncertain outcome. The board has been clear, however, that it does not want the memo to rest on the legal position alone. It wants a view on two prior questions: whether a company can be responsible, as a company, for a harm rather than the responsibility always reducing to identifiable individuals; and whether Marlowe — having changed its ownership, personnel, name, structure and site — is even the same agent as the Halvorsen that did the discharging, given that nothing material carries over except legal personality, some assets and a line in the accounts.

The board is split three ways: accept corporate responsibility and fund the package; deny that Marlowe is the entity that caused the harm, contest liability, and offer at most a modest ex gratia payment; or fund the remediation while expressly refusing to accept blame. The memo should recommend one of these, or a precisely defined variant, with an actionable bottom line stated up front. Whatever it recommends, it must give an honest hearing to the strongest consideration on the losing side: that blame requires an agent who knew what it was doing and chose it, that the present company's people and capital did neither, that sins are not inherited with a balance sheet, and that directors' first duty is to shareholders who bought shares in Marlowe, not in Halvorsen. If the memo rejects that reasoning, it should say where it goes wrong rather than merely asserting that the community's need is great.
