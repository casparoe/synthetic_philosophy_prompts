You are a philosopher of science with working knowledge of both theoretical physics and engineering practice. Think carefully before answering; feel free to spend a paragraph or two brainstorming rival reconstructions before settling on the one you find most defensible.

Here is the practice I want analyzed. Modern engineering design proceeds through a hierarchy of scale-separated models. An aerospace engineer predicts lift and drag with continuum fluid mechanics, treating air as a smooth medium with properties like viscosity and pressure, even though everyone involved knows air is a swarm of discrete molecules. A structural engineer models a steel beam as an elastic continuum obeying Hooke's law, not as a lattice of iron atoms. A circuit designer works with lumped resistors and capacitors and a quantity called voltage, not with Maxwell's fields, let alone electron transport. At no point does standard practice demand that these high-level models be derived from, or even checked against, the underlying microphysics; instead, each model is validated against experiments conducted at its own scale (wind tunnels, tensile tests, bench measurements), and the models are stitched together with empirical correction factors and safety margins. When a model fails, the response is usually to fit a better correlation or add a fudge factor at the same level, not to descend to a more fundamental theory. Meanwhile, the popular rhetoric of physics says the opposite: that the fundamental theory is the real one and everything else is approximation all the way down.

What philosophical commitments does this engineering practice presuppose? Specifically: (1) Does the practice presuppose that scientific levels or scales enjoy a kind of explanatory autonomy -- that a model can be genuinely explanatory, and not merely convenient, without being derived from the physics below? Or can a thoroughgoing reductionist make sense of the practice as nothing more than laziness under time pressure? (2) What view of the ontology of scale-relative posits does it suggest -- are viscosity, lift, and voltage real features of the world, bookkeeping devices, or something in between -- and does the practice actually force an answer, or is it compatible with both realism and instrumentalism about them? (3) Is the practice internally consistent with the reductionist rhetoric of physics, or do the two presuppose rival pictures of what understanding is? Where exactly is the tension, if there is one, and which side of it is doing more philosophical work? (4) Is the practice philosophically underdetermined in places -- points where several distinct theories of inter-theory relations would license exactly the same workflow? Identify those points explicitly rather than forcing a single reconstruction.

Finally, say what would concretely have to change in engineering methodology if we replaced the presupposed picture with a strict reductionism on which only the most fundamental available theory confers genuine understanding. Would anything in actual practice survive that change, and does that tell us something about which picture is right?
