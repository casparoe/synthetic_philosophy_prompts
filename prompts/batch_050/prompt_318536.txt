I spent four decades as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and in the past two years I have been working my way through contemporary metaphysics. I keep returning to the dispute about composite objects: the nihilists who deny that there are any tables, only simples arranged tablewise; the universalists who hold that any two things whatsoever compose a further object; those, like van Inwagen, who occupy some restrictive middle ground and take the Special Composition Question seriously as a question with a correct answer; and the deflationists, following Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions and more recent work on quantifier variance, who contend that the parties are speaking different languages in each of which their own claims come out true. The debate has been running for decades, the arguments on every side appear to be common knowledge, and nobody seems to have moved.

Here is the thought experiment that crystallised my puzzlement. Imagine two physics communities who agree on every measurement but disagree over whether a Bose–Einstein condensate is one object or many atoms behaving coherently. We physicists would settle this, or dissolve it, by asking what difference either answer makes to prediction, to manipulation, to the form of the theory. My working conjecture — which I offer as a conjecture, not a verdict — is that the metaphysical dispute has stalled because there is no analogue of that question: each side weighs theoretical cost on its own scale, the nihilist counting entities as a physicist might count free parameters, the universalist instead counting ideological primitives and gerrymandered restrictions, so that both can honestly declare their own theory the simpler. I would also like you to consider a second possibility I find harder to dismiss: that the dispute survives because intuitions about cases (the ship of Theseus, the statue and the clay) function as data, and data cannot be dismissed as mere talk.

So my request is diagnostic, not adjudicative. What kind of disagreement is this, really: a verbal dispute resting on an equivocation over the existential quantifier; a substantive dispute about the fundamental structure of reality; a dispute about which questions are well-posed; or two communities answering different questions under one label? Support the diagnosis from what each side actually argues, assess my conjecture about the missing difference-making question, and state what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Conclude with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the disagreement (for example: verbal, substantive, ill-posed, or a brief hybrid phrase).
