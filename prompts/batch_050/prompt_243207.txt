I'm making a handout for my decision theory seminar: a flowchart called "which response to the Ellsberg paradox is actually yours?" that students walk through in about ten minutes. Setup is the usual two-urn case — one urn with 50 red and 50 black, one with 100 balls in unknown proportions, plus the standard finding that most people prefer betting on the known urn for *both* colors, which no single probability assignment can rationalize. Each branch point has to be a question answerable from the case itself, not by knowing any labels — e.g. "if the unknown urn's mix had been fixed by an independent coin flip per ball, would you still rather bet on the known urn?" or "is someone with these preferences making a mistake, or perfectly rational?". Leaves must include at least: the strict-Bayesian "your preferences are just irrational" response, multiple priors with worst-case evaluation, non-additive probabilities, a model with a probability distribution over probabilities where ambiguity itself carries disutility, the competence/source account (it's about how much you know, not the event), and the "you're allowed to have no determinate credence at all" option. Flag any leaves your tree can't separate, and for at least one empty combination of answers, argue whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just undefended — my hunch is "these preferences are rational AND some precise single probability must fit them" is incoherent, but don't take my word for it.
