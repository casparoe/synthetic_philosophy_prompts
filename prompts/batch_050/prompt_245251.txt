I'm a final-year PhD student in philosophy preparing a two-year postdoctoral fellowship application, and I'd like you to help me draft the one-page project description. The project sits at the intersection of rational choice theory and what is sometimes called ergodicity economics: the observation that for decisions whose payoffs compound multiplicatively over time, maximizing the time-average growth rate of an individual's trajectory can diverge sharply from maximizing the ensemble average across possible worlds, and that standard expected-utility reasoning conflates the two. My planned angle is one I think is genuinely understudied: the ergodicity critique seems to derive its force from repetition -- from the fact that an agent faces the gamble again and again so that a time average exists at all. But many of the decisions where the critique is invoked most dramatically are precisely the ones that cannot be repeated: irreversible, one-shot choices such as those involving existential risk, where a single catastrophic draw ends the trajectory rather than merely lowering its growth rate. My project would ask whether the time-average framework says anything determinate about genuinely one-shot irreversible decisions, and if it does not, what rationality should demand of an agent facing them -- whether the right move is to fall back on ensemble thinking, to treat irreversibility itself as the relevant asymmetry, or to reframe the agent as a member of a sequence (a civilization, a lineage) for which a time average does exist.

In the proposal I need the question stated precisely, a clear explanation of why neither the standard expected-utility picture nor the existing ergodicity-economics framing settles it (here you should articulate the strongest version of each side rather than the weakest), and an account of my proposed method: conceptual analysis of what a time average could even mean without repetition, combined with a small number of constructed decision problems designed to test where each framework's intuitions pull apart. Please also say what a positive result would look like and -- since fellowship committees ask -- what would count as the project failing, and what I'd have learned anyway. Keep the scope realistic for a single researcher over two years with no experimental budget. You may refer to positions and arguments by description; I'll supply the citations myself, so please don't include a bibliography or attribute specific claims to named papers.
