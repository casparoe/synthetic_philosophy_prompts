ok so my roommate and i were arguing about this last night and we ended up constructing an argument that seems to prove way too much, and neither of us can find the exit. Help?

Background: we're both happy with the idea that moral status comes in degrees — dogs plausibly matter more than oysters, oysters more than rocks, etc. Here's the argument:

(1) A being's moral status depends entirely on facts about the being itself — its psychology, organization, whatever it can feel or do — not on its species membership, what it's made of, or whether we happen to like it.

(2) Take any two beings that are identical except for one tiny physical difference: one neuron more or less, one synapse rewired. The facts that ground moral status are nearly identical in both.

(3) Two beings that are nearly identical in all the morally relevant facts can't differ much in moral status. Otherwise a single neuron would make some huge moral difference, and that's absurd — moral status shouldn't be hostage to one neuron.

(4) There's a chain of beings, some actual and some merely possible, running from me down to a thermostat — through apes, dogs, mice, fish, insects, ever-simpler nervous systems, then simple artificial networks, ending in a bimetallic strip that just switches a heater on and off — where each link differs from its neighbor by only one tiny change of the kind in (2).

(5) From (3) and (4): every adjacent pair in the chain has nearly equal moral status, so chaining along, I have nearly the same moral status as a thermostat.

(6) A thermostat has basically zero moral status. So I have basically zero moral status. (Or run it the other direction: swatting a fly is nearly as bad as swatting me, which is just as unacceptable.)

I refuse to accept (6), but each premise honestly seems fine when I look at it on its own. I really want to keep (1) — I don't want moral status to turn on species loyalty or on carbon vs. silicon, because that seems like the worst kind of favoritism.

So: which premise or step should I give up? Explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs. And does your fix do any work on the real-world version of the problem (how much should I actually care about insects?), or does it only handle the fantasy chain in (4)?
