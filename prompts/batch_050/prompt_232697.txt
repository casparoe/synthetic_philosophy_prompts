In six weeks I am speaking at a colloquium that mixes analytic philosophers of art with practicing musicologists, and I need a question-period prep sheet. My paper defends a thesis about the ontology of recorded music: a commercially released studio track is not a performance of a musical work but a distinct kind of artifact, a work in its own right, and most confusion about authenticity, covers, remasters, and lip-syncing scandals comes from evaluating tracks against norms that properly apply to performances. My positive picture holds that the type/token framework developed for classical scores transfers badly to studio production, because in the studio case the constitutive decisions are fixed at the mixing desk rather than on the page, and what listeners are faithful to when they object to a remaster is the track, not any underlying performed work.

Prepare me for the objections this particular audience will raise. The philosophers in the room will know the standard literature on musical works as types, on performances as tokens, and on authenticity of instrumentation; the musicologists will care less about ontological parsimony and more about whether the view can handle how musicians, producers, and listeners actually talk and make judgments. Give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper if I answer them badly, each phrased the way someone from the relevant side of the room would actually put it. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, flag which premise of my view the reply depends on, and mark honestly where the reply is soft or merely deflecting. Distinguish objections I should concede gracefully, adjusting the thesis at the margins, from objections where conceding would collapse the whole position. End with the single question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the most dangerous one. Keep the whole sheet under 1,200 words; no citations needed, I will supply them.
