Design a thought experiment in which a person escapes an echo chamber through impeccable epistemic conduct and ends up *less* accurate about the very topic the chamber concerned — and where this outcome is not luck. The chamber's members must be genuinely wrong on the substance, so the result cannot be that she abandoned a community that happened to be right; the loss has to fall out of the structure of the trust network she moves into. Constraints: a realistic contemporary setting (a hobby community, a profession, or an online forum — no hypothetical drugs, simulators, or perfectly reliable sources); every step she takes, from first doubting her community to deciding whom to trust on the outside, must be defensible by ordinary Bayesian or virtue-epistemic standards rather than by hindsight; and the scenario should distinguish mere selective exposure (an epistemic bubble, which contact with outsiders can pop) from a true echo chamber (where the network has pre-installed reasons to discredit outsiders), with the escape route exploiting the former and being defeated by something like the latter on the other side. After the scenario: walk through each of her steps and why it is rational at the time; identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work, and show that removing it collapses the case into the boring version where the chamber was accidentally right; then state what the case implies for the standard advice that the cure for echo chambers is exposure to outside views, and how a defender of that advice would argue your protagonist made an identifiable mistake.
