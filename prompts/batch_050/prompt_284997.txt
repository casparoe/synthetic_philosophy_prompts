I'm a second-year math PhD student who started auditing a philosophy seminar this semester, mostly because my algebraist officemate keeps insisting that the objects we study "are really out there" while the logician down the hall insists they are nothing but marks on paper governed by convention. I went in expecting the platonism–nominalism debate to be like a math problem: old, but with actual traction. Instead I've found that the same handful of moves — the indispensability of mathematics to physics, Benacerraf-style worries about how we could ever know about causally inert objects, Field's program of rewriting science without numbers, fictionalism, various structuralisms — get recycled endlessly, and nobody in the seminar, including the professor, has changed their mind about anything in months. Participants seem to agree on all the relevant facts about mathematical practice, mathematical language, and the role of math in science, and yet the disagreement persists at full intensity.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the debate is secretly three different debates wearing one hat: a semantic question about what mathematical sentences mean, an ontological question about what exists, and an epistemological question about how mathematical knowledge is possible — and that each side wins the debate it thinks it's having while losing the other two. Alternatively, I sometimes suspect the disagreement bottoms out in a brute difference about what the word "exists" should be allowed to range over, in which case it might be purely verbal. But I could also imagine that it's a genuinely substantive standoff resting on unarguable starting intuitions, like the feeling that mathematical truth needs a truthmaker versus the feeling that it plainly doesn't.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, or several questions conflated — using what each camp characteristically says and concedes, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about mathematical cognition, a change of question, or nothing at all. Give your answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
