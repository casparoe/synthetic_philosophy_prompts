So my roommate and I were reading Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) for a reading group and she hit me with an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but neither of us could find the loose screw. The passage she's leaning on is this one, from the shipowner bit, where Clifford has just asked whether the owner would be any less guilty if the ship had happened to make it across safely:

"When an action is once done, it is right or wrong for ever; no accidental failure of its good or evil fruits can possibly alter that. The man would not have been innocent, he would only have been not found out."

And right before that he says the guy "had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts."

Her argument, roughly:

(1) Whether a belief is morally OK depends entirely on how you came by it, not on how things turn out. That's exactly what the passage says: right or wrong for ever, and the lucky shipowner is "not found out," not innocent.

(2) So you can't defend a way of believing by pointing to its good consequences. Clifford forecloses that move explicitly.

(3) Believing is enough like acting that Clifford holds us responsible for it in the same currency (he calls the shipowner guilty of the deaths, full stop, not just careless).

(4) Trusting someone is believing stuff about them -- that your friend will pay back the loan, that your partner isn't lying to you -- and the evidence for that is never complete. People do betray each other, everyone knows this, and you know it too while you're trusting them.

(5) And here's the kicker: trusting someone *works by* stifling doubts. Part of what it is to trust your friend is deliberately not running the background check you could run, not reading their texts, not dwelling on the ways they might screw you over. That's available investigation you're declining on purpose, same as the shipowner who didn't get the ship overhauled.

(6) So trust has the exact structure Clifford condemns: a sincere, comfortable conviction bought by refusing patient inquiry, and (by (1) and (2)) its usually working out fine doesn't launder it.

(7) So trusting anyone is wrong. Every time. And honestly by the same lights so is believing the sun will rise tomorrow, since induction never gives you complete evidence either.

So Clifford, taken at his word, turns out to forbid trust and ordinary inductive belief, which is absurd -- the man cannot have meant we should all be suspicious paranoids who verify everything. But when I tried to say "come on, trusting people obviously works out well," she just pointed at the passage and said that's the one move Clifford explicitly blocks. Which premise do I reject? I'd rather keep Clifford's basic point than give up on trusting people, but tell me what each option costs. Also, am I even reading that "right or wrong for ever" passage correctly, or is it doing less work than she thinks?
