I run a two-hour ethics module for a professional society of civil and structural engineers — about 24 participants, mid-career, split into tables of six. The thing I want them to feel, rather than be told, is that a person can be genuinely implicated in a wrong outcome without having caused it in any counterfactual sense: the project would have gone ahead without their signature, their bolt, their sign-off, and yet "it made no difference either way" doesn't feel like it gets them off the hook. Design a staged activity, in two parts, that produces this discomfort mechanically. Part one should create overdetermination: a threshold mechanism where the mildly bad collective outcome (no real stakes — points, snack priority, something the facilitator mildly sacrifices) is triggered only if at least N people opt in, but where the group predictably overshoots N, so that each individual opt-in was causally idle. Part two should then strip out even the possibility of causal relevance: participants are invited to publicly join, by name, an already-guaranteed venture — their joining changes nothing at all — and the room should feel that this is somehow worse or at least different from part one's idle clicks, even though it does less. Give me concrete rules, the payoff structure, what the facilitator must conceal and reveal about the threshold N and about other participants' choices, and a timeline. Then give me the debrief: map specific moments of the activity onto the distinction between making a difference to an outcome and intentionally participating in it, and onto the reasoning by which an entire group can be collectively responsible for something no member caused. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this — I'm especially worried about participants treating it as a game-theory exercise to be gamed rather than a moral situation to be inhabited — and how to handle each failure. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: my guess is that in the game the pull participants feel is reputational rather than genuinely moral, since the "wrong" is trivial and there's no real victim, so tell me where the analogy to actual complicity in engineering projects is most likely to break down and what I'd have to observe during a run to conclude the design needs to be rebuilt rather than patched.
