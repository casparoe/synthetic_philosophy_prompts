I have been reading discussions of split-brain patients and am struck by how little the debate over the unity of consciousness seems to advance. One side argues that the divided streams of perception and action in commissurotomy patients establish two subjects of experience sharing a body: each hemisphere can acquire information, guide behavior, and display preferences independently of the other. The other side emphasizes that outside artificial laboratory conditions such patients remain coordinated, claim to feel unified, and often act as a single integrated agent; on this view, consciousness may be unusually partitioned without there being two persons or two centers of awareness. A third position treats the patient as one person whose consciousness is only partially unified, with some experiential contents jointly accessible and others not. Yet all three camps appeal to much the same evidence: divided visual fields, independent hand movements, cross-cueing, preserved everyday competence, and patients’ own reports.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is rather than simply choosing a winner. Is the impasse mainly a hidden empirical question about how much information is actually integrated, a conceptual dispute about what would count as a separate subject of experience, a conflict between behavioral and first-person evidence, or a case in which the parties are answering different questions under the label of unity? Test your diagnosis against the kinds of evidence each side treats as decisive, and consider whether an intermediate notion such as degrees of unity merely relabels the problem or genuinely improves it. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a new experimental paradigm, a formal criterion for experiential integration, or an admission that ordinary language contains no determinate answer. Finally, state how confident you are in the diagnosis and identify the specific point on which your answer is least secure.
