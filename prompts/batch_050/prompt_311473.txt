My roommate is doing a stats PhD and last week, in the middle of a stupid argument about whether our friend group should actually move to Lisbon, she wheeled out an argument that's been eating at me, because it seems to prove way too much. Cleaned up, it goes like this. (1) Take any two people with regular priors — priors that assign probability 0 only to what's logically impossible, since anything else is just dogmatism (Cromwell's rule and all that). (2) Suppose both update by conditionalizing on the same growing stream of evidence. (3) Then by the standard merging-of-opinions results, their credences converge: with probability 1 they end up agreeing on everything in the limit. (4) And 'the limit' isn't some distant horizon, because likelihoods swamp priors roughly exponentially in the amount of shared data, so after a modest pile of common evidence any leftover disagreement is negligible. (5) Rational people conditionalize, and rational people hold regular priors, since ruling out a genuine possibility in advance is prejudice with a sigma algebra. (6) Evidence is effectively common now anyway — anything one of us can read, both of us can read. (7) So persistent disagreement between rational people is impossible: if two people keep disagreeing after years of shared evidence, at least one of them is irrational. (8) But economists disagree about inflation for decades while staring at basically the same numbers, my parents have disagreed about my career for a decade while meeting the same me, and it seems nuts that every long-running disagreement like that must contain an irrational party. (9) So either the merging theorems are technically true but epistemically useless, or Bayesianism as a norm of rationality is just false.

I really don't want (9). If forced to sacrifice something, I'd rather give up (6) than give up on my parents being reasonable, but I suspect the argument is wierdly broken somewhere earlier and (6) is a distraction. Which step should I actually be attacking, and what does rejecting it cost? If the rot is in (3) or (4), don't just say she misstated the math — tell me what the convergence theorems do legitimately imply about real disagreements between real people, if anything, since that's the part I actually care about. And because this is half math, half epistemology: please say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and flag the single point in your own answer where you're least sure you're right.
