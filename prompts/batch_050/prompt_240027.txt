When I argue with friends about the history of science, I keep leaning on an intuition that turns out to be shakier than I thought, and I want to know whether the shakiness is an established topic or just my own confusion. Two examples of the intuition first. Ptolemaic astronomy and some early rival were both wrong about the heavens, but nearly everyone agrees one of them was nearer to how things actually are. And when two weather forecasts both fail -- the day turns out hot, wet, and windy; one forecaster predicted hot, dry, and calm, the other predicted cold, dry, and calm -- most people say the first forecaster was closer, even though both were wrong and both got two out of three features right or wrong by simple count. So there is some notion of one false claim being nearer the truth than another, and it does real work in how we talk about scientific progress and about which errors are forgivable.

Here is what disturbed me. Take the forecast that got everything wrong -- cold, dry, calm. Now describe the day's weather using two invented categories instead of the usual three: call a day "balanced" if it is hot exactly when it is wet, and "settled" if it is hot exactly when it is windy. The actual day is both balanced and settled. But the all-wrong forecast, cold-dry-calm, also describes a balanced and settled day, so in the new vocabulary it comes out perfect, while the forecast that seemed closer (hot, dry, calm) now gets both categories wrong. Nothing about the facts changed; only the scheme for describing them did, and the ranking of which falsehood is nearer the truth flipped. I have not been able to convince myself that the invented categories are illegitimate in any principled way -- they are definable from the original ones, after all.

So my questions. First, does this flip phenomenon have an established name, and is there a recognized literature on whether the notion of "nearer to the truth" can be made independent of the descriptive scheme? I don't want just a label: I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is taken to be, and where its edges are. Second, is it the same phenomenon as the more mundane fact that closeness rankings shift depending on how much weight you give different respects of comparison -- say, caring more about temperature than about wind -- or are these two distinct problems that merely look alike? Third, does the flip survive if we restrict attention to "natural" vocabularies? Switching from Celsius to Fahrenheit never reverses which of two temperature guesses is closer, as far as I can tell, so maybe the whole problem is an artifact of gerrymandered categories; but I have never seen anyone prove that natural vocabularies are safe, or even say clearly what makes a vocabulary natural enough. If there is no established treatment of that restricted question, say so plainly, and tell me what you would call the restricted problem and how you would state it precisely.

Please answer in American English.
