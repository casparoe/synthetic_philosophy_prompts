I'm a working evolutionary ecologist and I do a lot of peer review for mid-tier journals — maybe two or three manuscripts a month. Something that keeps bugging me: authors lean on "fitness" constantly. You get lines like "individuals with trait X achieved higher fitness" or "the trait was favoured because it increased fitness," and roughly half the time I get this nagging feeling that the claim is doing no explanatory work at all — that fitness is just being read off whoever happened to survive or reproduce, so "the fittest survived" comes out true by definition. That's the old tautology objection, obviously. But I also know the standard reply, the propensity interpretation: fitness is a dispositional property of an organism or type, its probabilistic propensity to leave descendants in a given environment, not a redescription of actual outcomes. I've read bits of the philosophy of biology literature on this (Mills and Beatty, Brandon, that sort of territory) and I find it convincing in the abstract, but I have no idea how to actually *use* it when I'm staring at a methods section at 11pm.

So here's what I want. You're a philosopher of biology who's spent years refereeing for both philosophy and science journals, and who thinks carefully before answering rather than pattern-matching to a lecture. Take the propensity interpretation of fitness, with the tautology objection as the thing it's meant to answer, and turn it into a reviewer's decision procedure: an ordered sequence of tests I can run on any fitness-invoking claim in a manuscript, in maybe twenty minutes per paper, that tells me whether the claim is doing genuine explanatory work or is circular. It needs to handle the hard cases I actually see — fitness estimated from lifetime reproductive success (where the measurement *is* the outcome), stochastic environments where the propensity and the realised outcome come apart, claims at the level of types versus token organisms, and inclusive-fitness talk, which honestly confuses me at the best of times. Tell me what to do when the paper gives me no numbers at all, and mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls that I've just dressed up as procedure. I'd rather you be honest about the latter than pretend they're mechanical.

Then apply the procedure to three short hypothetical manuscript claims I'll make up on the spot (just invent plausible ones yourself — e.g., a mark-recapture study, a theoretical life-history model, and a verbal argument about "the fittest" genotypes in a fluctuating climate), and make sure at least one of them is a case where the procedure tells me to reject something I'd instinctively want to accept, and explain whether that means the procedure is too strict or my instinct is wrong. Before you write the final checklist, do a quick brainstorm of the ways fitness talk goes circular in practice so I can see you've thought about the failure modes, not just the theory. Also say, at the end, what the procedure can't catch — where a claim can pass every test and still be lousy evolutionary reasoning. British English throughout, please.
