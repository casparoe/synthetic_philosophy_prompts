So here's my situation. I work on complicity, and I've got a paper I've been shopping around that I'm now presenting to the ethics/political philosophy colloquium at a decent research department. The paper defends a non-causal account of complicity: roughly, you can be complicit in a wrong even when your contribution makes no difference whatsoever to whether the wrong happens or how bad it is. My line is that complicity isn't about causing harm, it's about knowingly participating in a wrongful scheme -- where participation means doing your assigned bit of a plan you understand, intending your bit to be a bit of that plan. So the guy who votes for the unjust policy, the consumer who buys the sweatshop shirt, the mid-level bureaucrat who processes one file in a giant machine of persecution -- all complicit on my account, even though in each case the outcome is fully overdetermined and nothing would have gone differently had they stayed home. I take this to be a feature, since I think the causal accounts (difference-making, NESS-style contributory stuff, etc.) all collapse on these cases or get gerrymandered into oblivion.

The audience: this department has two well-known consequentialists (one works on collective action problems and expected utility arguments for voting, so she will have Views), a Kantian who works on coercion and using-others-as-means, a guy who does philosophy of action and gets very precise about intention and shared agency (he's read all the Bratman/Tuomela stuff and thinks most ethicists are sloppy about it), plus the usual smattering of grad students. Nobody there works on complicity specifically, which cuts both ways.

Prep me for Q&A. I want:

- The objections I'll actually get from THIS crowd, in rough order of likelihood, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it (the action theorist is not going to say "but did you really cause it though")
- For each objection: is it a genuine threat or can I absorb it, what should I concede gracefully up front vs. hold the line on, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes
- Where my account is genuinely soft -- the case or distinction I'm least confident about, that I should have a considered position on rather than improvise
- The question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what my best available answer to it is anyway

One thing to flag: I'm not willing to just retreat to "well, it's wrong for OTHER reasons" on the hard cases -- the whole point of the paper is that complicity itself is doing the moral work, so replies that quietly offload onto deception, convention, or rule-worship are giving away the store even if they'd get me out of the room alive. Also I have a bad habit of over-conceding under pressure, so tell me which concessions are safe and which ones unravel the view.

Feel free to poke real holes -- I'd rather find out now than in front of the search commitee members who'll be there.
