I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of migration, and I've been reading Dummett's stuff on immigration and refugees (mainly his little book from the early 2000s where he develops the idea that what matters morally is where someone's life is actually centred, not their legal status or paperwork, and that once a state has let someone build a life there it's forfeited the right to expel them). I want to pressure-test his framework against the Windrush scandal in the UK. Here's the setup as I understand it: people who came from the Caribbean as kids in the 50s and 60s, legally at the time, lived and worked in Britain for decades, and then under the hostile environment policies of the 2010s got flagged as illegal immigrants because they couldn't produce documents nobody ever told them to keep -- some lost jobs, housing, healthcare, some were detained and a few were actually deported to countries they hadn't seen since childhood. It seems like an almost perfect vindication of Dummett's view, since the wrong was basically done through documentation, exactly the thing he says is morally irrelevant. But I want a genuine analysis, not a victory lap. So: walk me through what Dummett's principles say about each stage of the scandal (the original admissions, the decades of unremarked residence, the retrospective documentation demands, the deportations), then tell me where his framework is actually silent or unhelpful -- for instance, does he have anything to say about compensation, or about the fact that the state created the documentation gap itself by not issuing papers in the first place, or about what the state should do when it was its own administrative failure? And finally: does the episode strengthen his position overall, or does it expose a weakness, namely that his view gives strong protection to settled residents but the scandal shows states can effectively un-settle people by reclassifying them, so the real battleground is over who counts as settled in the first place?
