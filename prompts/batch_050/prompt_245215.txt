My town is renaming our elementary school and next week there's a public meeting where anybody gets three minutes at the mic, and I signed up to speak, which I am now regretting. The school is named after a guy from the 1800s who did some genuinely admirable stuff (he basically bankrolled the town's first free library and pushed for public schooling for girls when that was not a popular position) and also made a good chunk of his fortune in ways that were legal and totally normal back then and that we would now consider monstrous. The worst part is I keep flip-flopping on what I actually think, which is why I need help.

Trying to sort my own head out I fell down an internet rabbit hole and ended up reading this lecture by Lord Acton, the 19th century historian, called A Lecture on the Study of History. There's a stretch toward the end where he goes off on historians who excuse the bad things people in the past did on the grounds that everybody thought it was fine at the time. I'm paraphrasing here, but his line is basically that the moral law doesn't change with fashion -- opinions and manners and creeds come and go but right and wrong stay put -- and that a historian who softens the verdict on a great man because he was a product of his era is debasing the whole enterprise, letting people off a hook that history exists precisely to keep them on.

At first I read that and thought, yes, finally, someone said it. But the longer I sat with it the more it wobbled. Because how does Acton know the unchanging moral law he's so confident about isn't just the morality of his own time wearing a fancy hat? He couldn't step outside 1895 anymore than I can step outside 2025. And if nobody can actually climb out of their own moment to check, then when I say the school guy was a man of his time and my neighbor says that's no excuse, maybe we're not even disagreeing about him -- maybe we're disagreeing about whether anyone can see past the water they swim in, and the whole argument is really about that.

So write me the speech. About three minutes read aloud. It has to genuinely engage with Acton's claim -- not just mention it for decoration, but test it: does his position even make sense if every generation thinks IT'S the one that finally sees clearly, and where does that leave us when we're the ones holding the chisel on the school sign? I want it to land somewhere honest, even if honest turns out to be "this is harder than both sides at the meeting are pretending." Mixed audience: there's a retired history teacher who will definitely know the Acton lecture and will notice if I butcher it, and then a couple hundred neighbors who've never heard of him and are honestly half there because of the construction traffic. It needs to work for both groups at once.

One more thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables -- just continuous prose I can print out and read off the page, like a letter. And don't make me sound like a professor. I install kitchen cabinets for a living and this whole thing has been keeping me up at night.
