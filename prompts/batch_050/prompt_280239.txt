Last week at our local ACX-adjacent meetup we got into an argument I've been chewing on ever since. The setup: suppose I find out my neighbor is having an affair. There are two things I could do that are both perfectly legal — I could tell his wife (rude, sure, but it's true information, and nobody owes anyone silence about it), and I could ask him for money for various things. But the moment I combine them — pay me $2,000 or I tell your wife — I've committed a serious crime in basically every jurisdiction. One friend who's in law school just shrugged and said "yeah, everyone knows that's a paradox, there isn't a clean theory," which is fine as bar-exam prep but deeply unsatisfying as an actual answer. What makes it weirder is that neighboring conditional threats are totally fine: "settle with me for $50k or I'll file this lawsuit" is standard practice, "match this counteroffer or I take the other job" is fine, even "compensate me for what you did or I go to the press" is at least arguably fine. And in a lot of blackmail cases the victim would clearly prefer the threat never be carried out, so the usual move you see in coercion debates — the threatened party prefers having the option on the table — cuts weirdly here, since the "option" is just paying to suppress true information the blackmailer had no duty to keep quiet about anyway.

So what I want to know is: what does blackmail law actually presuppose about what's wrong with conditional threats? Is it committed to some view where you can hold a right to do X while having no right to sell your forbearance from X? Does it presuppose that rights are "for" particular interests — like, my freedom to tell the wife exists for her sake or for information-flow reasons, and pocketing money to stay silent is a misuse of it? Or is it really just an incentives argument — we don't want a market that pays people to dig up each other's secrets — which is policy, not a theory of the wrong itself? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed, but also an honest account of where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories would license the exact same doctrine, versus where the doctrine genuinely rules some views out. And the part I actually care most about: is the same legal system even consistent with itself elsewhere? Plea bargaining, NDA settlements, non-disparagement clauses, and "resign quietly and we won't say why" exits all look a lot like paying people not to exercise rights they definately have. If we rebuilt the law on top of whichever theory of wrongful threats turned out to be the coherent one, which currently-legal practices would turn out to be blackmail, and which currently-criminal ones would stop being crimes?
