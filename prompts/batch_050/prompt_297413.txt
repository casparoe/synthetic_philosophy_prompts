So I'm writing a political novel -- mid-level state politics, nothing glamorous -- and my protagonist is a deputy mayor who has signed off on something genuinely rotten (burying a report about a contaminated housing development because exposing it would tank a flood-relief bill that thousands of people need). Classic dirty hands setup. I've been collecting the stock justifications characters like her give for doing wrong in office, because I want her to NOT use any of the obvious ones. The ones I have:

(1) The arithmetic one -- yes it was wrong, but more people are better off, so on balance it was right.
(2) The replaceability one -- if she'd refused, someone else would have signed it anyway, and at least this way she kept her seat at the table to do good later.
(3) The role-morality one -- private scruples are a luxury; the job has its own obligations and she swore an oath to the whole town, not to her own conscience.
(4) The emergency one -- normal ethics is for normal times, and a crisis suspends the usual rules.
(5) The guilt-burden one -- the honest politician knows she's doing wrong and carries the stain herself so the public doesn't have to.

Give me a sixth justification she could offer, with a straight face, to her estranged daughter who's confronting her about it. It has to be genuinely different from the five above -- not a blend, not one of them with the emphasis moved around. It also has to be something a thoughtful, self-aware person could actually believe, not a strawman I'm setting up to knock down, because I've read too many novels where the corrupt official is obviously full of it.

Format your answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or outro: 1) the new justification stated plainly, 2) why it isn't just one of my five in a trench coat, 3) the strongest objection to it, 4) whether it survives that objection (be honest -- if it collapses, tell me), and 5) one line of actual dialogue where she says it to her daughter.
