Some background: I'm a PhD student in political theory and this semester I'm TAing an undergrad seminar on the Republic. For the week on Glaucon's challenge (Book II, the ring of Gyges bit) I want to replace my usual discussion handout with a one-page flowchart titled 'which answer to Glaucon do you actually accept?' and have students walk themselves down it before class, so we can spend the session on where they landed instead of on definitions.

Please build me that decision tree. Concretely:

- Everyone grants the same setup before the tree starts: a just man and an unjust man both get rings, reputation and punishment are off the table, and the question is whether any reason to stay just survives. The first node after that should split on whether 'getting away with it' is even coherent — i.e. whether undetected injustice could still cost you something.
- 7 to 9 branch nodes total, each a yes/no or pick-one-of-three question an undergrad can answer from the challenge alone. Nothing phrased as 'do you think moral reasons are categorical?' — that's just asking them to name their label, and the whole point is they shouldn't need to know the labels.
- At least one node has to be the cheerful immoralist case: suppose Gyges sleeps great, feels no guilt, flourishes by his own lights and would take the ring again knowing everything — has the 'injustice wrecks your soul' answer just lost?
- These leaves have to be reachable: (a) the answer Plato actually gives, that injustice is a disorder of the soul so it harms you even when undetected; (b) the contract or mutual-restraint reading where morality is a deal among people who'd each rather defect, which means the ring-holder may rationally break it and Glaucon sort of wins; (c) the Prichard-ish move that the question 'why be moral?' rests on a mistake and needs no answer; (d) a constitutivist answer where you can't deliberate about anything at all without already being bound; (e) the error-theorist dissolution, no reason to be moral because there's nothing there to be right about; (f) an internal-reasons answer where there may just be nothing to say to a contented amoralist — blame him anyway. If the tree needs more leaves to cover the space, add them.
- Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. On (b) in particular, say out loud that it vindicates Gyges rather than refuting him.
- At the end: point out at least one combination of branch answers that nobody seems to occupy and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just unloved. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually seperate, because I'd rather show students the blurry borders than pretend the map is cleaner than it is.

Format: an indented text tree I can paste into a doc, questions numbered Q1, Q2... and leaves lettered, with leaf names kept to a few words each so students can find themselves on it without any secondary lit.
