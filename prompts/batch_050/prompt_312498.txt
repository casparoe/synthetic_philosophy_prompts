I work as a distributed systems engineer, and my working model of the world is compositional: a system's behavior is whatever falls out of its components, their internal state, their interfaces, and their wiring. In my vocabulary, a whole is never anything over and above its parts and their interactions -- that is not a metaphysical commitment so much as a design constraint, since it is what makes systems buildable, debuggable, and testable at all.

I have been reading about panpsychism and the combination problem, and I want to understand it in terms I can actually reason with. Please restate the problem in the vocabulary I use -- modules, encapsulation, state, message passing, aggregation, composition, clusters, and the distinction between properties of a node and properties of a system -- as faithfully as you can. Cover at least the following sub-worries, which I take to be distinct: (1) the thought that there is no operation that takes many separate private points of view and yields a single unified point of view, the way William James put the original worry; (2) the thought that whatever micro-experiences the parts enjoy would not even be about the same things as the macro-experience of the whole, a mismatch sometimes discussed under the heading of a palette problem; and (3) the thought that the internal structure of a unified experience, say a visual field, does not seem to mirror the structure of the underlying parts and their relations, as structural-mismatch arguments press.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the combination problem translate cleanly into my vocabulary? Note that weak emergence is no surprise to me: a cluster has throughput, fault tolerance, and latency profiles that no individual node has, and nobody is puzzled by that. Which parts translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets distorted? And which parts cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all?

The question I most want answered is this. In my framework, the whole is nothing beyond the parts and their interactions by construction. So when the panpsychist claims that a unified subject of experience cannot be recovered from any collection of smaller subjects, is that claim inexpressible in my vocabulary because my vocabulary is missing something real -- something a genuinely complete description of the world would need -- or is it inexpressible because the panpsychist is covertly assuming precisely the kind of non-compositional, non-functional property that my framework was built to eliminate? Please do not smuggle a conclusion into the translation itself: if some step of the restatement only goes through on the assumption that experience is not a functional or relational property, flag that assumption explicitly rather than baking it in. And if the panpsychist has a fair comeback available from within her own framework -- for instance, that the compositional picture simply presupposes what the combination problem denies -- state that comeback in my terms too, if it can be stated in my terms at all.
