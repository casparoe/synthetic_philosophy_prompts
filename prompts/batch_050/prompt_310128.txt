I've spent the past year in a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and the one topic we keep circling without convergence is whether probability makes sense in the Everett interpretation. My own position is that the decision-theoretic program associated with Deutsch and Wallace, supplemented by envariance-style symmetry arguments, essentially shows that a rational agent who accepts the Everettian picture is compelled to weight branches according to the Born rule, and I'd put my credence in that claim at about 0.65. A colleague in the group thinks the whole program is circular -- that the axioms smuggle in probabilistic assumptions under the guise of rationality constraints, and that the alleged derivation therefore proves nothing a skeptic of many-worlds should accept. She puts her credence in the claim at roughly 0.1.

Here is what I'd like from you. State your own credence in the following proposition: a rational agent who is fully convinced that unitary quantum mechanics is complete and that measurement outcomes correspond to decoherence-defined branches can be shown, by argument rather than by postulate, that the uniquely rational policy is to assign credences to branches in proportion to their Born weights. Then decompose the question. Candidate sub-questions include: whether decoherence actually supplies a branching structure definite enough to bear a measure at all; whether the notion of self-locating uncertainty between branches is coherent, given that pre-measurement there is no fact of the matter about which branch "I" will occupy; whether the decision-theoretic axioms really are probabilistically neutral or secretly presuppose what they derive; whether envariance arguments stand independently or collapse into the decision-theoretic ones; and whether, even if the derivation succeeds, it answers the deeper complaint about why I should care about my descendants in proportion to their measure rather than merely note that I do. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how you combine them into your final number, and flag which component the overall credence is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me where my disagreement with my colleague most likely lives. If you had to locate the 0.65 versus 0.1 gap primarily in one of your sub-questions, which would it be, and what piece of analysis or argument -- from either side -- would move your own number most? I'm not interested in a bibliography; I want the reasoning in the open so the weak point is visible.
