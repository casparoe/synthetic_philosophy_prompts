I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on moral revolutions -- the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade, the disappearance of dueling and footbinding, and other cases where a practice went from respectable to unthinkable within a few generations. The seminar keeps circling around the same dispute: did these norms end because of moral arguments, because of shifting economic interests, because of honor and status dynamics, or because of some combination, and our readings disagree sharply about abolition in particular. For my final essay I don't want to re-litigate one historical case; I want to write about the agenda of the field itself. So: which open questions in the study of moral revolutions are most worth settling first, and in what order?

To give you a sense of what I mean by "open question," here are candidates I've been collecting, but please push back on the list or add to it: (1) whether moral arguments function as causes of norm collapse or mainly as after-the-fact consolidations that stabilize a change already underway for other reasons -- call these "trigger arguments" versus "consolidation arguments"; (2) whether there's any defensible way to distinguish a genuine moral revolution from a mere change in incentives or fashion, given that the agents themselves usually redescribe what happened in moral terms afterward; (3) what makes a norm "ripe" for collapse in the first place -- why some widely condemned practices persist for centuries while others fall fast once criticized; (4) whether the honor-and-status mechanism that seems central in the dueling and footbinding cases generalizes, or whether it was specific to aristocratic and face-oriented cultures; and (5) whether we can identify, in real time, which currently respectable practices are candidates for future revolutions, or whether that kind of prediction is hopeless because we can't step outside our own moral framework.

What I'm asking for is a defended ordering, not a survey. For each question you prioritize, tell me: which other questions are downstream of it (e.g., does answering the trigger/consolidation question change what we even need from the ripeness question?); how tractable it is for a philosopher using conceptual and historical tools rather than, say, a sociologist with datasets; and what evidence or distinctions would move it up or down the ranking. I'd also like you to state the criteria you're using -- upstream-ness, tractability, neglectedness, stakes for how activists and reformers should act now -- and to flag where the criteria conflict. Feel free to argue that one of my five is ill-posed or overrated; I care more about the reasoning behind the agenda than about protecting my list.
