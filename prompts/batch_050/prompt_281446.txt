hey, so i'm a phd student in philosophy (i work on moral responsibility, but this semester i'm TAing an upper-level undergrad course on personal identity) and i've been asked to write one of the questions for the final exam. students get about 75 minutes for it within a 2.5 hour exam. the prof wants a scenario-based question on parfit and teletransportation, and she's specifically banned the standard case because they all just regurgitate the lecture notes on it.

so here's a variant i came up with, and i want you to build the question around it: the teletransporter works by scanning you, destroying your body in the process, and building a perfect copy on mars out of new matter. standard so far. but in my version, it turns out the destruction of the original is not physically required -- the scanner could run in a non-destructive mode and leave the earth original intact. the only reason it destroys you is a company policy, decided by the operators, and in principle they could flip a switch after scanning and let both versions walk away. the twist i want the question to press on: whether "you survive the trip" seems to depend on a policy decision made by a technician after your scan is complete, which seems like a crazy thing for your survival to hinge on.

i want the question to force students to actually engage with why parfit separates survival from identity (i.e. relation R, psychological connectedness/continuity with the right kind of cause, doing the work instead of numerical identity), and to confront what the fission-style pressure does to that move once both continuers exist. a good answer shouldn't be able to just say "identity is not what matters" and stop -- it has to grapple with whether what matters can rationally depend on extrinsic contingent stuff like a switch position, and if not, what parfit-style view you're actually left with.

deliverables:
1. the exam question itself, with the case stated crisply and a prompt that makes students take a position rather than survey the literature
2. a model answer of roughly 600 words
3. a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks for each totaling 20, plus a one-line note per point on how students typically get it wrong (e.g. treating the non-destructive mode as obviously death for the original, or conflating "what matters" with "what we'd have reason to care about prudentially" without argument)

and please don't make the marking scheme just a checklist of vocabulary ("mentions fission = 4 marks") -- i want it to name the actual argumentative moves an A answer makes versus a B answer, so i can defend the grades when students inevitably complain.
