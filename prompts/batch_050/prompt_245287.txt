I'm teaching a first-year seminar on time in philosophy and film, and we're pairing Confessions Book XI with Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one). Early in Book XI Augustine prays, "At Your nod the moments flee by. Grant thereof space for our meditations among the hidden things of Your law, nor close it against us who knock." I want to build the class around this: for Augustine the moments flee for creatures but not for God, whose present doesn't pass away, and later in the book he argues the future and past don't really exist at all -- only a present of things past (memory), a present of things present (attention), and a present of things future (expectation), with time itself being a kind of distention of the mind.

So read Arrival through that. Louise, learning the heptapod language, comes to "remember" her daughter before Hannah is even born. Is that what Augustine thinks is impossible -- the future existing, genuinely, to be visited -- or is it better described as his "present of things future" blown up to fill a whole life, expectation become as vivid as memory? Does the fleeing of the moments just stop for her, and if so, is she approaching something like the divine eternity Augustine thinks we can't have, or is she still distended, just in a weirder shape? And be honest about where the film resists the reading: Augustine's future isn't there yet, but Louise's seems fixed -- she knows the loss coming and walks into it anyway, which looks more like fatalism than anything in Confessions XI. Does the film vindicate Augustine, refute him, or dramatize something his account simply has no room for -- like grieving someone who hasn't died yet?
