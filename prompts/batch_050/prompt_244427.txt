I sit on my county's pretrial services oversight board (volunteer gig, I'm a stats grad student and honestly this is above my pay grade, but here we are). Judges here use a commercial risk-assessment tool that scores defendants 1-10 on likelihood of rearrest when setting bail. Last month the vendor sent us an audit certifying the tool is fair: among defendants who get, say, a 7, the fraction who are actually rearrested is basically identical for Black and white defendants. So a 7 "means the same thing" no matter who you are, end of story. Meanwhile the public defender's office filed a complaint showing that if you look only at defendants who were NOT rearrested, Black defendants were much more likely to have been scored high-risk than white defendants — way more false alarms, meaning more people sitting in jail pretrial who would have been fine. So the vendor says the tool is fair and the PD says it's discriminatory, and both have spreadsheets.

Here's the thing though: both sides seem to be right about the numbers. The vendor's statisticians and the PD's statisticians have checked each others math and nobody disputes any of it. And the genuinely annoying part is that rearrest rates really do differ between the groups in our historical data (I know, that's a whole separate fight about what "rearrest" even measures, but set that aside) — and apparently, once base rates differ, you mathematically cannot have the tool be calibrated like the vendor wants AND have equal false-positive rates like the PD wants, except in degenerate cases. So it's not that one side is lying; it's that the tool cannot satisfy both definitions at once, and the vendor quietly picked one without ever saying there was a choice to be made.

So what I want to understand before our next meeting: what does each side's metric actually presuppose about what makes a decision fair? Like, what theory of fairness is the vendor committed to by treating calibration as the whole question, and what theory is the PD committed to by centering error rates? Does the vendor's practice strictly require their theory, or would several different views also license what they're doing (and vice versa)? And is our county's own policy even internally consistent — our equity ordinance says city tools must "treat like cases alike" AND must "not impose disproportionate burdens on protected groups," which now looks to me like it might be demanding the mathematically impossible. If we rewrote the ordinance to presuppose the PD's theory of fairness instead, what would concretely have to change about how the tool gets built, audited, and used by judges?
