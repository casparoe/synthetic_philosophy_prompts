So here's my situation. I write novels for a living, but once a semester I do a guest stint at the MFA program where my partner teaches -- mostly I run sessions on moral psychology for fiction writers, how to make characters who do bad things believable without making them cartoon villans. This spring I'm doing one 90-minute session on complicity, specifically the kind where nobody actually causes the bad thing. Twelve students, all fiction writers, no philosophy background, and I refuse to lecture.

Here's what I want them to feel in their bodies before we name anything: the weird tension in cases where your individual act makes zero causal difference (the harm happens anyway, or the harm is past, or there's some threshold you personally didn't tip) and yet you'd be mortified to have your participation known. Fiction is full of this -- the bystander who bought the ticket, the clerk who processed the form -- and my students always write these characters as either secretly pivotal (they weren't, that's the point) or as innocent (they aren't quite that either).

Design me an in-class game or staged activity that produces that tension. Some constraints and requests:

- Real stakes in the room, small ones. Maybe chocolate, maybe the good pens I bring, maybe control over the playlist. I want actual reluctance, not hypothetical reluctance.
- Ideally the mechanics use thresholds or aggregation so that at least some students' choices are genuinely causally inefficacious, and they know it, and they participate anyway (or abstain and then have to explain why, which is its own tell).
- Give me the full rules, materials list, a timeline for 90 minutes, and a script for what I say at each transition, because if I improvise I will accidentally moralize and ruin it.
- The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto the conceptual distinctions: causing vs. participating, individual vs. collective wrongdoing, and that whole family of cases where a million people each do something harmless and together it's a catastrophe. I'll bring up Parfit's harmless-torturers-style cases myself in the last ten minutes, so the activity shouldn't.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it -- writers are extremely good at finding loopholes and one of them will definitely try to organize the others.
- And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce. I suspect the real thing involves actually not knowing whether you mattered, and a game might collapse that uncertainty too neatly. If so, tell me how to paper over it or what to say in the debrief instead.
