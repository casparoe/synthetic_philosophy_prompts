I'm drafting a grant proposal for a project on interpretability methods for large language models, and one reviewer of an earlier version pressed me on a question I realized I couldn't answer rigorously: when my field says things like "the model represents the Golden Gate Bridge in this direction" or "this feature tracks deception," what would actually have to be true for those claims to be correct, as opposed to being a convenient shorthand for a correlation some tool surfaced? Before I stake out a position, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata that an account of representation in artificial neural networks would have to meet in order to underwrite claims of that form — claims specific enough to do work in a safety case, not just in a blog post. For each desideratum, give the case or finding that motivates it. In particular, I want the list to cover at least the following territory:

- The distinctions the account cannot collapse: between a concept being *encoded* somewhere in the activations and the model actually *using* it in producing its output; between a probe's ability to decode a property and the network representing that property in any sense that matters; between a feature being in the model and a feature being in the analyst's decomposition of the model.
- The empirical results it must be consistent with: that individual neurons and directions are often polysemantic; that decompositions into "features" change with the width of the decomposition and the method used, so that what counts as one feature can split into several under a finer-grained analysis; that small rotations of a basis can scramble interpretability while leaving function intact; that interventions on putative feature directions sometimes steer behavior as predicted and sometimes don't.
- The verdicts it must deliver: it should say something determinate about contested cases, such as whether a network whose internal geometry mirrors some structure of a task domain thereby *represents* that domain, even if the mirroring was never selected for and plays no causal role.

Then do the two things that matter most to me. First, identify which desiderata are genuine constraints on any adequate account and which are parochial demands of one camp — I suspect, for instance, that "the account must make representation fully independent of the analyst's choice of tools" is stronger than anyone actually needs, but I want to see that argued rather than assumed. Second, tell me whether any subset of the plausible desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable — for example, whether demanding both that feature content be fixed by causal role and that it survive arbitrary invertible transformations of the representation space creates real trouble, and if so, where exactly it bites. If the list itself turns out to be a no-go result, that's a finding, not a failure.

Finally, briefly run the main contender frameworks against the list: a deflationary or purely instrumentalist line on which "the model represents X" means nothing over and above "treating it as representing X is predictively useful"; a causal-interventionist account on which representation requires that interventions on the posited vehicle produce the right downstream effects; structural or isomorphism-based accounts; and adapted versions of informational or teleological theories from philosophy of mind. Keep this scoring short — the criteria and their motivating cases are the deliverable. A table for the final scorecard is fine.
