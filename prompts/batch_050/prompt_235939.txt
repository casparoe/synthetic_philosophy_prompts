My friend who took an Islamic studies course last year tried to explain the whole Mu'tazila vs. Ash'arite thing to me, and honestly it sounded a bit too neat. Her version went something like this:

"Basically in the 9th and 10th centuries Muslim theologians were fighting over the same question as Plato's Euthyphro. The Mu'tazilites said good and evil are objective features of actions that human reason can figure out all by itself, without revelation. So God's commands just track what's already good, which means God is bound by the moral law the same way we are. The Ash'arites thought that puts something above God, so they went the other way: an act is good purely because God commands it, full stop. If God commanded lying or murder, those things would be good, and since God creates literally everything including our actions, there's no sense in which He owes us anything or can do wrong. They even had this doctrine called 'acquisition' for human acts which is really just determinism with extra steps. So the upshot is the Mu'tazilites were basically rationalists a thousand years ahead of their time, the Ash'arites were pure authoritarians, and once Ash'arism became the standard Sunni creed, rational ethics in Islam more or less froze in place."

Now, I get the feeling she's flattening things, because whenever someone tells me a centuries-long debate reduces to "rationalists vs authoritarians" I get suspicious, and I've also heard that Shia theology kept a lot of the Mu'tazilite side alive which would mess with her ending. But I can't pinpoint where she's gone wrong vs. where she's just compressing.

Can you audit this for me? Go through the main claims one at a time and tell me what a scholar of kalam would accept as fair, what's a defensible simplification, and what's an actual misrepresentation. Some specific things I'm unsure about: (a) is "God is bound by the moral law the same way we are" something the Mu'tazilites would accept, or is that already a slanted way of putting their view? (b) did the Ash'arites really bite the bullet that God could command murder and it would be good, or is there more structure to their position that she's skipping? (c) is the 'acquisition' = determinism jab fair? And finally: even once the summary is corrected, does her overall takeaway about one side being proto-rationalists and ethics freezing under Ash'arism survive, or does it depend on the distortions?
