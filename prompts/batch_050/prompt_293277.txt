I'm a PhD student in philosophy with a focus on normative ethics and the foundations of welfare economics, and I'm writing a seminar paper on whether formal results in social choice can do genuine normative work. The immediate occasion is a running disagreement with a classmate. After working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem -- the result that if individual preferences and social preference over lotteries each satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and social preference respects a Pareto condition, then social preference must be representable by a weighted sum of the individual utility functions -- I came away thinking it is one of the strongest arguments we have for something in the neighborhood of utilitarianism. My classmate insists the theorem is essentially a bookkeeping device: it shows that certain consistency constraints force a certain functional form, but the weighted sum it delivers need not track anyone's actual good, so it licenses no substantive ethical conclusion at all. My credence that the theorem, conjoined with premises defensible on independent grounds, constitutes a sound argument for weighted utilitarianism as the correct criterion for evaluating social alternatives under risk is around 0.65; hers is around 0.1.

I would like your own credence in that proposition, stated as a number, but I am much more interested in the decomposition than the number itself. Please break the question into the sub-questions you take to be load-bearing, assign each a rough probability with a line or two of justification, and show how they combine. The terms I would expect to see include, at minimum: the probability that the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms are normatively compelling when imposed on social preference specifically (as opposed to individual choice under risk, where their grip is strongest); the probability that the relevant Pareto condition survives scrutiny in cases where individuals hold preferences about things other than their own good; the probability that the theorem's conclusion is substantive rather than merely formal -- that is, that the individual utility functions entering the sum can be interpreted as representations of individual well-being rather than as arbitrary vNM representations whose units and zero-points smuggle in the interpersonal comparisons the theorem is often advertised as avoiding; and the probability that fairness or distributive objections (the charge that the result is indifferent to how expected utility is distributed across persons, since it weighs ex ante prospects rather than ex post outcomes) defeat the normative reading even if the formal result stands. And given the decomposition, where do you think my disagreement with my classmate most likely lives: is she denying a premise I accept, reading the formalism differently, or simply weighting the sub-questions differently? I want to know whether our dispute is genuinely about the theorem or about something further upstream.
