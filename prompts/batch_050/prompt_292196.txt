Next month I'm presenting a paper at a formal epistemology workshop defending the thesis that Jeffrey conditionalization is not a genuinely distinct updating rule but only a redescription of ordinary conditionalization on a finer-grained space: whenever an agent seems to receive "uncertain evidence" that merely shifts a proposition's probability to some value short of one, the very same change can be redescribed as plain conditionalization on a richer event (for instance, that things appeared that way to the agent), so a second rule is unnecessary and the cases Jeffrey's rule was designed for never actually occur. The audience will be working formal epistemologists, most of them sympathetic to Jeffrey's rule, plus two people who work on reflection principles and diachronic rationality and one decision theorist. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, roughly ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased the way a formal epistemologist would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
