Conciliationists about peer disagreement hold that learning that a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with me about p requires a substantial reduction in my confidence in p; steadfasters maintain that, at least in many cases, I may rationally retain my original view. The familiar moves are all on the table: the self-defeat objection (conciliationism seems to undermine itself once I learn that peers disagree about conciliationism), the independence question (whether I may legitimately re-use the very first-order reasoning my peer and I dispute), the spinelessness objection to conciliationism, and the asymmetry between clean toy cases like splitting a restaurant bill and messy cases like philosophy itself. After roughly two decades of increasingly sophisticated work, the literature shows little convergence, and each side tends to answer the other's cases rather than engage its arguments. My suspicion is that the two camps are answering different questions under one label — one asking how a rational agent should respond to evidence of her own fallibility, the other asking what epistemic status her actual reasoning retains — but I am not confident of this. Diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it: is this a disagreement about the evidential force of disagreement, about which cases count as data, about the aims of belief, or a bedrock clash of intuitions beyond the reach of argument? And given your diagnosis, say what — a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a reformulation — would count as real progress, or argue that none should be expected.
