So I'm running an intro political philosophy seminar and I want to make one of those flowchart quizzes ("which position on inherited wealth are YOU?") that I can hand out before we start the unit, so students figure out where they land before they've learned what the positions are called.

The question the quiz sorts on: what, if anything, may the state do about wealth passed on at death? Build me the decision tree. Some ground rules:

- About 7-9 questions max, each phrased as a concrete scenario or claim the student reacts to, not as jargon. So not "do you endorse luck egalitarianism" but more like "your grandma leaves you $2 million while your roommate inherits nothing but a couch — did anything unfair happen, and if so, to whom?" Questions should be answerable by someone who's never taken philosophy.
- The leaves I definitely want: (1) pure entitlement view — it's the giver's money, taxing a gift at death is no more justified than taxing a birthday present; (2) inheritance-as-income — the heir got a windfall, tax it like lottery winnings, no special moral status; (3) equal-start egalitarian — heavy tax to fund genuinely equal opportunity for kids; (4) a cap view — giving is fine but no single person may recieve more than some ceiling; (5) abolish bequest rights entirely — dead people don't get a say over property, though lifetime gifts are a seperate question; (6) family-integrity view — transfers inside a family are expressions of love and the state should keep its hands off; (7) a dispersal/anti-concentration view where the point isn't equality per se but stopping dynastic wealth from warping politics.
- The hard part, and the reason I'm asking you instead of copying a textbook: the diagnostic questions need to actually seperate these. In particular I want questions that pry apart the "whose claim is being overridden" issue (the dead giver's intentions vs the living heir's expectations vs the would-be competitors' opportunity), and the "why this advantage and not the others" issue (if the state may tax bequests, what about tutoring, connections, accents, good genes?). If two of my leaves answer every question identically, say so explicitly and tell me what further question would split them, even if it's weird.
- Also: sketch the space of possible answers before slotting positions in, and tell me which combinations of answers nobody on my leaf list occupies, and your best guess why — is that combo incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely a live view I forgot?
- One sentence per leaf telling the student what their result means, written in the obnoxiously cheerful voice these quizzes always have.
