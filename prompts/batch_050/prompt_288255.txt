Analyze the fight over Minneapolis's 2040 comprehensive plan (the 2018 one that ended single-family-only zoning citywide, later challenged in court by neighborhood and environmental groups) through the lens of Lefebvre's "right to the city." I'm most interested in a specific tension: Lefebvre's framing is built around current inhabitants and their use value against exchange value, but the Minneapolis fight was arguably between two groups' use values -- people who already live there and want to keep their neighborhoods as they are, and people who'd like to live there but are priced or zoned out. So: who is the bearer of the right on each side of the episode, what does the theory say each actor (city council, homeowner groups, renters, the would-be residents who never got a voice) should have done, and where does the right-to-the-city lens illuminate the episode vs. strain against it? In particular, does the theory have anything coherent to say about people who don't live in the city yet, or is that a genuine hole it never anticipated? End with a verdict: is Minneapolis a case that confirms the framework, or one that shows it can't handle intra-city distributional conflicts?
