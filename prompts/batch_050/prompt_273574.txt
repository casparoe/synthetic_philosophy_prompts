US federal agencies evaluate major regulations by cost-benefit analysis: they monetize reductions in mortality risk using a value of statistical life estimated from wage premiums and stated-preference surveys, convert other impacts into dollars, sum everything across all affected persons, and treat a rule as justified if the net sum is positive, with no weighting by who gains or who loses. Suppose we ask what theory of social welfare this practice presupposes. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is often invoked nearby: if individual preferences and social preferences each satisfy the expected utility axioms, and social preferences respect unanimous individual preferences (a Pareto condition), then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. Does the theorem actually underwrite the practice? Please address at least the following. First, the theorem aggregates utilities, while the practice aggregates willingness to pay, which varies with wealth; does cost-benefit analysis therefore presuppose that money is an acceptable proxy for utility, or is it not even attempting to maximize the Harsanyi sum? Second, the theorem's conclusion is forced by its axioms, including the requirement that social preferences themselves obey the independence axiom under risk; does the practice thereby inherit that axiom's contested implications -- for instance, that only ex ante expected utility matters, ruling out fairness concerns about how risks or chances of life are distributed across identifiable people? Third, if one rejected one of the theorem's premises, what would a principled revision of the practice look like? Throughout, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and note where it is philosophically underdetermined.
