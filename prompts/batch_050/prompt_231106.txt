I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I am writing a long piece on the quiet revival of utilitarian reasoning in policy circles: cost–benefit analysis, effective philanthropy, pandemic triage, existential-risk work. In the background of nearly all of it sits a claim I have now heard from three separate interviewees in almost identical words: that in the 1950s John Harsanyi gave something close to a mathematical proof that the rational way to weigh everyone's interests is to add up individual wellbeing with equal weights, and that anyone who accepts some mild-looking axioms about rationality and benevolence is thereby committed to utilitarianism whether they like it or not. I want to stress-test this before I put it in print.

So take the position at full strength: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that, given plausible assumptions about individual and social rationality under uncertainty plus a Pareto-style respect for everyone's good, social evaluation must take the form of a weighted sum of individual utilities, and a further impartiality step delivers equal weights, and hence utilitarianism. Play it straight and give me a map of its exposure. Which premises and which inferential steps do the load-bearing work? For each, what exactly would have to be true for it to fail — not just that it could be doubted, but what the world, or our concept of welfare, or our standards of rationality would have to look like? In particular I want to know: whether the theorem really delivers utilitarianism or merely a skeleton that other views (prioritarian, sufficientist, egalitarian ones) can also be dressed in; whether there is a contested move between utility in the decision-theoretic sense and welfare in the ethical sense, and what would settle it; whether the impartial-observer or equal-chance reasoning that supplies the equal weights can be resisted without abandoning impartiality itself; and whether there are cases where the axioms are all satisfied and the conclusion still seems wrong.

Please distinguish defeaters that would be fatal — a step whose failure collapses the argument for everyone — from ones that would merely be awkward, forcing qualifications or narrowing the theorem's scope. I am more interested in what a fair-minded defender of the result would concede counts against it than in what hostile critics say, and I will find it most persuasive if you can show me the single step where the argument is thinnest and say honestly whether the thinness is a problem of mathematics, of interpretation, or of morality. If the conclusion is that the position is well insulated, say so, and say whether that insulation is a virtue or a sign that the theorem has quietly been drained of ethical content.

Give me the analysis in prose first, and then summarise it as a table with the following columns: (1) Premise or step — the axiom or inferential move, named plainly; (2) Role — the work it does in the argument; (3) What its failure would require — a concrete description of what would have to be true for it to give way; (4) Severity — decisive, serious, or merely awkward; (5) Defender's view — whether a reasonable proponent of the theorem would themselves concede this as a live exposure, and why. End with a one-sentence verdict on how much of the popular claim, as I have stated it, the theorem genuinely underwrites.
