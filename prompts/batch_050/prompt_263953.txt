You are an accomplished formal poet with a genuine working knowledge of the philosophy of mathematics — someone who can hold a sestina together technically and also understands, from the inside, why it might matter whether the number 2 is an element of the number 4. I would like to commission a poem from you, and I ask that you treat this as a serious commission rather than an exercise in cleverness.

A little about where I'm coming from. I have no academic training — I left school at eighteen and have worked in trades and then in bookkeeping ever since — but a few years ago I fell down a rabbit hole of popular books and lectures about the foundations of mathematics, and one puzzle got its hooks into me and won't let go. It goes like this. If you build the natural numbers out of sets one way — each number being the set of all the numbers before it — then 2 turns out to be a member of 4. If you build them another equally respectable way — each number being just the singleton of its predecessor — then 2 is not a member of 4. Both constructions deliver the very same arithmetic; every sum comes out identical. So the question "is 2 an element of 4?" seems to have no answer, or two answers, which feels like the same thing. The conclusion some people draw is that numbers aren't objects at all but positions in a structure: that 2 is nothing over and above "what comes after 1 and before 3," the way a shortstop is whoever stands between second and third base. I find this thrilling and disturbing in equal measure. If it's right about numbers, maybe it's right about more than numbers. And if it's wrong, I want to feel where it breaks.

Here is the commission. I want a sestina, and I chose the form because I think it *is* the puzzle: a sestina takes six end-words and permutes them through six stanzas in a fixed spiral, so each word keeps returning but never in the same position — and then the envoi tries to hold all six words in just three lines at once. Is the word its position, or does something in it carry across every rearrangement? I want the form to do the philosophical work, not merely decorate it. A reader who knows the debate should watch the end-words migrate and feel the argument happening; a reader who knows nothing of it should still get a real poem about recurrence and identity — about whether anything in a person, or a thing, survives all the roles it occupies.

Specific requirements:

- A strict sestina: six stanzas of six lines each, using the standard end-word permutation (6-1-5-2-4-3 and so on), followed by a three-line envoi that deploys all six end-words.
- Choose the six end-words deliberately. Ideally some should be able to function as "objects" and some as "relations" or "positions," so that the permutations themselves stage the dispute rather than just illustrating it.
- Commit. I don't want a poem that suspends judgment between "the word is its position" and "something in the word outlasts every position." Pick one verdict and let the poem argue toward it, with the envoi as the place the verdict lands. I'll respect a strong poem for the wrong answer far more than a timid poem for the right one.
- No name-dropping of philosophers or mathematical jargon for its own sake. The rival set constructions and the indifference of arithmetic to them should be present in the poem's logic, not recited in it.

Before composing, please think it through on the page in a few sentences: your six end-words and why, the arc of the argument across the stanzas — where the rival construction enters, where the poem turns — and what exactly the envoi commits to. Then give the poem. After the poem, add a short note telling me plainly where it takes liberties with the philosophical position or leans on one contested reading of structuralism. Since I have no way to check you on this, I'd rather you tell me than leave me trusting a beautiful distortion.
