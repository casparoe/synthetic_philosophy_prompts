Since retiring after forty years in materials science — much of it spent subjecting specimens to stresses they were never designed to survive — I have joined a small philosophy reading group, and we have lately been working through Plato's Laws. In Book I the Athenian stranger, addressing a Spartan and a Cretan whose cities forbid wine, defends the supervised symposium on grounds I found startling. This passage (Laws 649d–650a, in R. G. Bury's translation) provoked a genuine quarrel at our last meeting:

"And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight? And when a man is a slave to the pleasures of sex, is it not a more dangerous test to entrust to him one's own daughters and sons and wife, and thus imperil one's own nearest and dearest, in order to discover the disposition of his soul? In fact, one might quote innumerable instances in a vain endeavor to show the full superiority of this playful method of inspection which is without either serious consequence or costly damage."

The dispute, between two members named Marcus and Elena, ran roughly as follows.

(1) Marcus took the passage to state a neglected truth about how we vet people for power. Interviews, references, and a spotless record all observe a man precisely when he knows he is being observed; wine suspends the performance. A man who is cruel when drunk has the cruelty in him — alcohol lowers the guard, it does not build the cruelty. So a supervised drinking session is, as the Athenian says, a cheap and safe instrument for learning what every sober interaction conceals, and a polity that forbids it is throwing away information.

(2) Elena replied that this conflates removing an inhibition with revealing a trait. A test is only as good as the correlation between conduct under test conditions and conduct in office, and alcohol does not scale up the pressures of office; it substitutes different ones — blunted judgment, flattened affect, impaired impulse control. What the symposium displays is how a man behaves on wine, not how he governs sober. Worse, the test misleads in both directions: a charming drunk may be a cold calculator in committee, since ambition and vindictiveness are sober vices that wine is as likely to dissolve as to expose.

(3) Marcus answered that this objection proves too much. The Athenian never claims the symposium reproduces the conditions of office; he claims it is cheaper and safer than the realistic alternatives, which are precisely Elena's sort of test — do business with the man to learn whether he is savage, trust him with your family to learn whether he is dissolute. Those trials are dangerous, expensive, and no less correlational. If a test condition must replicate the deployment condition, then every proxy test in every field fails the standard, including the ones her own argument tacitly prefers.

(4) Elena conceded the general point about proxies but drew a distinction she said the passage itself supports: tests that magnify the load on a mechanism versus tests that alter the mechanism. Exertion makes the same heart work harder; alcohol makes a different subject altogether. And she accused Marcus of misreading Plato besides. The passage names two purposes, testing and practice, and its emphasis on the cheapness, harmlessness, and playfulness of the inspection fits a training ground — wine gives citizens a supervised arena in which to rehearse self-command amid temptation — rather than a diagnostic instrument for selectors. On her reading, the symposium is moral education, and Marcus has mistaken a pedagogy for a psychometry.

I was asked to chair the discussion rather than to rule on it, and I find I cannot rule even in private. My trade taught me to trust instruments that reveal latent flaws under controlled stress, which is exactly why I distrust my own instinct here.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say who has the better of it and why. In particular, I should like to know: whose reading of the passage is more faithful to what the Athenian actually argues; whether the disagreement between "wine reveals character" and "wine produces a temporary character" is an empirical question, a conceptual one, or both, and what would settle it; and whether the comparative claim about cheapness and safety survives even if Elena's point about mechanisms stands. Finally, say who is more right overall — and if both parties are missing something, say what.
