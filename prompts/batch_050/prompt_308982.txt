I am a doctoral student in philosophy, working on the epistemology of disagreement, and I keep running up against a configuration in aesthetics for which I would like the established term, if there is one. I should say at the outset that I am more interested in the carving than the label, so a bare name will not satisfy me.

The configuration, as concretely as I can put it. First instance: two film critics, each with decades of viewing behind them, fine-grained discrimination, no personal stake in the director, and extensive comparative knowledge of the genre. One finds a particular film profound; the other finds it chilly and manipulative. Each reads the other's review in full, grants that the other has noticed everything there is to notice, concedes that the contrary verdict is intelligible given the sensibility from which it issues — and neither budges. Second instance: two readers of poetry, one young and one old, both equally practised, attentive, and unprejudiced. The younger is moved by a passionate, tender lyric and finds a sober, reflective poem arid; the older reverses both verdicts. Neither can point to any failure of attention, knowledge, or reasoning in the other. The pattern is this: the disagreement survives every procedural improvement one could impose on the judges. It is not that one party is uninformed, hasty, partial, or coarse in perception; remove all such defects in imagination and the disagreement remains, apparently traceable only to a difference in what each judge is constituted to feel.

Three nearby notions strike me as imperfect fits, and I would like to know whether I am wrong about any of them. First, peer disagreement as discussed in epistemology: that literature concerns how one ought to revise one's confidence on learning that an epistemic peer disagrees, whereas my question is not about rational revision but about whether both verdicts can somehow stand. Second, relativist treatments of taste predicates in philosophy of language: those typically concern low-stakes matters and untrained judges, not canonical works and maximally qualified critics, and it is not obvious the analysis carries over. Third, Hume's own treatment in Of the Standard of Taste: his criteria for the true judge — delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense — all appear satisfiable by both parties in my cases, and his concession that a certain diversity of verdicts owing to differences in age and temperament is blameless, and not something any standard should attempt to eliminate, looks like an admission of my phenomenon. Yet he presents it as a boundary on his standard rather than as a subject worth studying in its own right, and I cannot tell whether his commentators treat it as a problem, a feature, or an afterthought.

So my questions are these. Does disagreement of exactly this kind — persistent, between judges who are ideal by the standard's own lights, and attributable only to sensibility — have a name in the aesthetics literature, and how is it standardly defined and distinguished from the three notions above? If it has none, how would you delimit it? In particular: does the phenomenon require that both verdicts be in some sense correct, or only that neither judge be at fault? Does it require genuinely ideal judges, or merely judges in whom no flaw can be identified? And what does the bare possibility of such disagreement imply for the claim that there is a standard of taste at all — is it a reductio of the Humean position, a friendly amendment to it, or simply orthogonal?
