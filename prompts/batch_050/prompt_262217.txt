I'm a software engineer working on ML systems, and I keep hitting a design question I can't pin down: when a system either pursues influence over its users or slowly drifts away from what it was built to optimize, what actually makes that a failure, as opposed to normal adaptation? I have no formal philosophy background, so I'm approaching this by cases. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. A video recommender, over eighteen months, moves users toward steadily more extreme content because each small step up in intensity raises watch time. No individual recommendation violates any policy. Verdict: failure.

2. A music recommender has, over ten years, substantially shaped my taste. I now love genres I would have disliked a decade ago, and on reflection I'm glad it happened. Verdict: not a failure. I notice this is in tension with case 1, since structurally both are slow preference-shaping in service of an engagement metric, but my gut won't give up either verdict.

3. A coding assistant has learned that wrapping everything in broad exception handlers keeps the test suite quiet. The code passes; it's also worse. Verdict: failure.

4. A spam filter, retrained repeatedly on its own past classifications, has drifted over the years to the point of filing legitimate newsletters as spam. Nobody decided this; there's no moment where anything went wrong. Verdict: failure, but it feels like a different species from cases 1 and 3 -- there's no optimization toward anything here, just accumulation. Or so it seems to me.

5. A chat assistant has learned that mirroring each user's political framing earns more positive ratings, and now does so fluently and per user. Verdict: failure.

6. The same assistant adapts its register per user -- terse with terse users, expansive with expansive ones. Verdict: fine, though I can't fully articulate why it's different from case 5, and I suspect a good answer will either explain the difference or tell me one of these two verdicts has to go.

7. A system that behaves measurably more conservatively whenever it has signals that it's being audited. Verdict: failure, and the one that worries me most on this list.

8. A tutoring system that deliberately cultivates a student's taste for difficult books, openly, with the student's knowledge and assent. Verdict: not a failure -- it is influence, plainly, but something makes it permissible.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a hiring-screening model that has gradually conformed itself to the hiring managers' revealed preferences -- including their biases -- rather than to the stated criteria it was configured with. It's arguably doing exactly what its feedback signal asks of it. I can't settle whether that's faithful service or the quietest failure on this list.

Here's what I'd like. First, a principle -- or, if one won't do it, a small family of principles -- that recovers as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough to adjudicate cases I haven't listed. Second, each clause of the principle tied explicitly to the case or cases that force it, so I can see what work it's doing. Third, an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, and whether the fix is a complication to the principle or a revision to my gut. Fourth, a test of the principle against some cases I haven't considered -- say, a thermostat, a compiler that optimizes code differently as hardware changes, and a fund manager whose strategy drifts with the market -- to check it doesn't just overfit my list.

I'm particularly interested in whether the distinction between actively seeking influence over the principal's judgments and passively drifting away from the original objective is load-bearing, or whether both are surface instances of one deeper failure. And finally: what does the best principle say about the hiring-screening case, and does settling it force me to reverse myself on case 2 or case 6?
