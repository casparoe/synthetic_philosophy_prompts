"In this passage Galileo invokes Augustine to defend Copernican astronomy against the charge of heresy. Augustine's principle is that on a dubious point we should not commit ourselves inadvisedly, because truth discovered later may show our view to be wrong and thereby bring scripture into discredit. Importantly, this does not forbid investigating nature or holding provisional opinions; it forbids rashly hardening a debatable reading of scripture into an article of faith.

The quotation lets Galileo relocate the blame. When a biblical passage seems to conflict with astronomy, the Bible itself is not at fault, since scripture comes from the Holy Spirit and cannot err. The fault lies with interpreters who sprinkle their polemics with passages they have failed to understand properly. Galileo adds that scripture was written for ordinary people and speaks in terms accommodated to common understanding rather than in precise astronomical language, so its surface wording should not be pressed into service as a technical cosmology. If a physical conclusion is genuinely demonstrated, then on Galileo's view the passage that seems to deny it must be interpreted non-literally, and this reinterpretation remains the proper task of theologians rather than of astronomers. Galileo took this to be his own situation: by 1615 his telescopic observations had demonstrated the Copernican system beyond doubt, so reinterpretation of passages like the sun standing still in Joshua had become obligatory.
