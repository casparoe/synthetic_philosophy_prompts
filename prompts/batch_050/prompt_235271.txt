Last night I had a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether soldiers fighting on opposite sides of a war stand on the same moral footing. Her view -- which she described as the traditional one -- is that combatants on both sides are moral equals: the justice of the cause is a question for statesmen, and a soldier who fights honorably in an unjust war does nothing morally worse than one who fights honorably in a just war. I kept pressing the revisionist objection that liability to be killed tracks what you are doing and how responsible you are for it, so that an unjust aggressor forfeits rights the defender retains. We went in circles, largely, I suspect, because we never identified where exactly our views diverged. We agreed about many individual cases and still disagreed about the general principle, which suggests our dispute was really about which questions are doing the work.

I would like you to build me a decision tree that settles where a person actually lands in this debate, designed for readers who have never heard the names of the positions. The tree should begin from a shared starting commitment -- that fighting in war can sometimes be permissible -- and branch through questions framed as concrete cases rather than doctrinal statements. Cases I want included: the reluctant conscript who poses a lethal threat but bears little or no responsibility for being there; the volunteer who sincerely and reasonably believes his war is just when it is not; the just combatant who kills an enemy soldier when capturing or retreating was a live option; and the officer who knows the war is unjust but would be executed for refusing to fight. Each branch should be labeled by the claim or judgment that divides it (for instance, whether liability turns on posing a threat or on being responsible for it; whether reasonable ignorance of injustice restores moral equality; whether excuse and justification must be kept apart here; whether permissions to kill come in degrees of necessity). The leaves should include at least: the traditional moral-equality view, the responsibility-based liability view, a view on which sincerely mistaken unjust combatants are excused and thereby regain equal status, and contingent pacifism -- the position that real wars never or almost never satisfy the relevant constraints, so the equality question is moot because nobody may fight.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions within that space. For any coherent-looking combination that no position in the debate actually occupies, note it as an empty leaf and diagnose briefly whether it is vacant because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot genuinely separate (I suspect the excuse-restoring view and the traditional view may be indistinguishable by cases alone, but tell me if I am wrong). End with a one-paragraph statement of the single branch node where you judge the deepest disagreement in the literature to live, and why the cases at that node are the ones my friend and I should have argued about instead.
