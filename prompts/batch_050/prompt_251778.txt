I teach an upper-level seminar that sits between epistemology and evidence law, and every year the same thing happens: the students all agree that it would be wrong to find for the plaintiff in the classic gatecrasher case (a rodeo with a thousand attendees, only 499 tickets sold, the organizer suing a randomly picked attendee for admission on no evidence but the numbers), but they splinter completely on why, and the textbook just lists rival theories by name. What I want from you is a diagnostic decision tree I can hand out in week one: an instrument that takes a student from the shared starting intuition that bare statistical proof is somehow deficient, through a series of branching questions, to the theory of legal proof they actually hold. The leaves should include at minimum: outright probabilism (if the statistics clear the operative standard of proof, they suffice), relative plausibility or explanationist theories, normic support theories (proof must make the conclusion normally true rather than merely probably true), modal epistemological accounts (the inference from evidence to verdict must be sensitive or safe), weight-of-evidence or second-order probability views, and the family of views on which legal proof requires individualized evidence for dignitarian or rule-of-law reasons. If you think a further leaf belongs there, add it; and if two of these genuinely cannot be separated by verdicts about cases, say so explicitly rather than inventing a spurious branch.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the name list: each branch node should be a concrete case or claim on which the positions demonstrably come apart, phrased so a student can answer it without knowing any labels. Among the diagnostic materials, please use at least the following: the matched-pair comparison in which a plaintiff injured by a bus offers (i) market-share statistics showing that one company runs 80% of the buses on that route versus (ii) an eyewitness of stipulated 80% reliability; a cold-hit DNA case in which a database trawl over a hundred thousand profiles yields a single match with a one-in-a-million random match probability; the question whether whatever defect afflicts statistical proof in criminal trials equally afflicts it in civil ones; and the counterfactual probe of whether the factfinder would have reached the same verdict regardless of what the particular defendant had actually done. Where the branching questions admit a combination of answers that no published position occupies, note the empty branch and give a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is vacant because incoherent, because unattractive, or simply unexplored.

A few constraints. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes and six to eight leaves; deeper trees stop working as a handout. Each leaf should carry the position's name, one sentence stating its core claim, and one sentence on the verdict it is most embarrassed to deliver. Do not include citations or a reading list — I will add references myself, and I would rather have your reconstruction of the logical geography than guesses at page numbers. The audience is students who have read one introductory survey of the statistical-proof debate but could not yet attach names to views, so the questions must sort them by what they believe about the cases, never by which label sounds respectable.
