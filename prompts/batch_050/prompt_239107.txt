Okay so I'm prepping my moral luck seminar and I'm tired of the usual essay prompt, so here's what I want instead. Imagine two of my (fictional but very commited) colleagues who've been going at this for years: Professor Hale, who takes the Bernard Williams / Thomas Nagel line that moral luck is real and that the "control principle" — the idea that we can only be morally assessed for what's under our control — is simply false as a general claim; and Professor Okafor, who thinks the control principle is non-negotiable and that everything the luck-accepters point to is better explained away (epistemic effects, revised practices, whatever). They've finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration.

Draft the joint statement they'd produce. Scope it narrowly: ONLY resultant luck (the drunk driver who hits a pedestrian vs. the equally drunk driver who gets home fine, that kind of thing). They agree to set aside circumstantial and constitutive luck for a later document, and the statement should say so up front.

What I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both will actually sign. Think: the case descriptions are symmetric up to the outcome; ordinary practice really does blame the unlucky driver more; the control principle really does have strong intuitive pull; neither side is just confused about what the cases involve; etc. Don't water these down into mush — find the strongest things they can genuinely share.

2. A disagreements table where each row states one precise point of disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that side would accept the wording as their own. I'm especially interested in whether the disagreement is over (a) what our actual judgments in these cases even are, (b) whether differential blame in the symmetric cases is moral assessment proper or something else, and (c) whether the control principle is a conceptual truth about morality or a defeasible norm.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one or both of them. For each crux, name a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both agree bears on it, and write down, in advance, what each professor predicts about it (e.g., what Hale predicts people will say about a variant where the two drivers are in different legal jurisdictions, and what Okafor predicts).

4. Finally: if any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, the statement should flag it and explain why.

Format it as the agreed-claims section first, then the table, then cruxes, then the verbal-dispute flags. Sign it with both their names at the bottom.
