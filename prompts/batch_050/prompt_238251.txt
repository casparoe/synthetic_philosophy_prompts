So I'm a third-year PhD student (history of medieval philosophy, technically, but I drift into ethics a lot) and in three weeks I'm giving a talk at our department's medieval workshop defending a paper that's... honestly maybe a bit ambitious. The thesis is basically that Abelard's ethics -- the stuff from his Ethics where the whole moral weight sits in consent, not in the deed and not even in the desire -- isn't just a historical curiosity but is actually a better answer to the modern moral luck debate than anything on offer now. My pitch is: Abelard already took the hard line that the thwarted sinner and the successful sinner are equally guilty, because the deed adds nothing to the sin, and that this is a feature not a bug -- it's the only view that fully dissolves moral luck without retreating to the wishy-washy position that what we control is just trying. I also lean on his examples, like the servant who kills his master only to save his own life and (by Abelard's lights) doesn't sin, and the really dicey one where the persecutors who act from conscience would sin by doing otherwise. I want to argue the conscience case is the price of admission, not a reductio.
