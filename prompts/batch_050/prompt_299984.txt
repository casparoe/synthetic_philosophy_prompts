I am presenting a colloquium paper next month defending a norm of assertion I call the defensibility norm: one may assert that p only if one could, on the spot and in response to a challenge, produce an adequate defense of p. The view is meant to compete with the standard menu -- the knowledge norm (assert only what you know), the truth norm (assert only what is true), and the belief and justified-belief norms. Its two distinctive consequences are that a true or even known proposition can be impermissibly asserted when the speaker could not stand behind it under questioning, and that a false proposition can be permissibly asserted when the speaker's grounds were scrupulous. My argument sketch: (i) ordinary challenges to assertion -- "how do you know?" "what makes you say that?" -- are requests for a defense, not requests for the speaker to certify knowledge, so the practice is built around defensibility; (ii) we systematically excuse false-but-well-grounded assertions in a way the truth norm cannot explain; (iii) the norm explains the felt impropriety of asserting lottery propositions on purely statistical grounds, since there is nothing to say in their defense that the ticket-holder doesn't already grant; and (iv) testimony is handled by requiring asserters to vouch for the reliability of their source chain rather than to possess first-hand knowledge.

The audience is a general philosophy colloquium, but the composition matters: roughly half philosophers of language, several of whom work in a Stalnaker-style framework where assertion is modeled as a proposal to update the common ground; a bloc of formal epistemologists who will immediately test the view against lotteries, prefaces, and higher-order evidence; a couple of epistemologists of testimony; one or two people sympathetic to the knowledge norm who think every rival collapses back into it or into justified belief; and almost nobody whose own project is norms of assertion, so they will not grant me the standard dialectical moves.
