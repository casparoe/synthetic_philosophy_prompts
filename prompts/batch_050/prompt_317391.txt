You are a philosopher serving as the external member of a university's Animal Research Ethics Panel. The panel can fund only one of two proposals this cycle, both involving aversive-conditioning studies that use repeated mild electric shocks. Proposal A concerns forty common octopuses; each animal has roughly 500 million neurons, but about two-thirds of these are located in the arms rather than in any central brain, and it is disputed whether octopus experience is unified or somehow distributed across semi-autonomous limbs. Proposal B concerns two hundred mice; each has roughly 70 million neurons concentrated in a single brain, and mammalian pain mechanisms are comparatively well understood. The panel's draft welfare policy ranks species by 'neural complexity' without defining the term. The proposer of A argues that approval should track the capacity to suffer, not raw neuron totals; the proposer of B argues that on any reading of the policy that takes neural complexity seriously, each octopus puts more neurons at risk than each mouse, so B should go ahead.

Think carefully through the strongest arguments on each side before drafting; then write the short opinion you would circulate to the panel. It should recommend one proposal with reasons, take a position on whether brain size or neuron count is itself morally relevant or merely a defeasible proxy for sentience, and address the distributed-nervous-system complication head-on. Give the best case for the proposal you reject, and state what evidence or policy amendment would change your recommendation.
