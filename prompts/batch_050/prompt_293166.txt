I recently moved into a quality-assurance position at a large organization, and the job has forced me to notice a pattern that I keep seeing in different institutions, and I want to know whether it has an established name — or whether it should. The pattern is familiar in outline: a quantitative measure is introduced as a stand-in for some harder-to-observe aim, and behavior bends toward the measure. But the specific configuration that interests me is what happens at the end of that process, which I think is stranger than the usual story. A friend who works in hospital administration describes how an emergency-department waiting-time target led, predictably, to patients being kept in ambulances outside so the clock would not start. What interests me is not that initial gaming but what followed: after a few years, staff began discussing waiting times as if they simply were the quality of emergency care, and new hires were trained with the target presented as the definition of good triage rather than as one imperfect indicator of it. A relative who lectures at a university describes the same arc with student evaluations: at first, easing the workload to improve scores was a cynical accommodation everyone privately understood as such; a decade later, the department's younger faculty genuinely cannot say what "rigorous teaching" would mean apart from strong evaluations, and a colleague who suggests the scores have diverged from real learning is treated as attacking good teaching itself.

What strikes me is that the standard accounts I know — Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, the general idea that a measure ceases to be a good measure once it becomes a target — all seem to presuppose that the divergence between proxy and goal remains visible in principle: the cynic games the number while privately tracking the true aim, or the analyst can still point at the gap and say the correlation has collapsed. In the cases that interest me, that gap has been absorbed. The proxy has stopped being a proxy and become constitutive: the original goal survives, if at all, only as ceremonial language in mission statements, and the accusation of "gaming" stops being coherent because there is no longer any institutionally shared access to what the gaming would be a deviation from. It also seems to me that this endpoint condition might be self-sealing in a way ordinary Goodhart dynamics are not — a gamed metric can be reformed or abandoned, but it is not clear how you reform a metric that has become the very meaning of the thing it once measured.

So my question: is there an established term for this specific configuration — the point at which measure-to-target corruption completes itself by reconstituting the goal — and a literature that treats it? I suspect it sits near goal displacement in the older sociology of organizations, and near various ideas about reification and performativity, but I am not sure any of those names picks out exactly the phenomenon above rather than its neighbors. I would not be satisfied with a bare label; what I want is the carving. What exactly distinguishes this from ordinary specification gaming, from goal displacement, and from simple hypocrisy about metrics? Where are its edges — what would count as a case that almost fits but does not? And does the analysis, once made precise, suggest anything about whether such institutions can recover the lost goal, or whether the condition is effectively irreversible short of collapse?
