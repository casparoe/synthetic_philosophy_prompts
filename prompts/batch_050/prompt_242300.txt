I teach philosophy of action and am fluent in the recent analytic vocabulary of group agency: shared intention, we-mode, group agents with representational and motivational states of their own, role-based responsibility, distributed and mediated causation, complicity, omissions, and the reactive attitudes of blame, shame, and guilt. Please restate Jaspers’s postwar taxonomy of guilt — criminal guilt before a tribunal, political guilt as liability for the acts of one’s state, moral guilt before one’s conscience, and metaphysical guilt arising from a failure of human solidarity, especially surviving while others were destroyed — in that vocabulary, category by category, as faithfully as possible.

For each category, distinguish three things: what translates cleanly into claims about individual participation, group-level responsibility, or role liability; what survives only under distortion, for example by treating conscience as private preference or solidarity as mere fellow-feeling; and what has no natural expression at all in the idiom of group agents, intentions, and sanctions. Then give the residue report on five test cases: a conscripted functionary who follows lawful orders; a citizen who benefits from, votes against, and inadequately protests an unjust war; a bureaucratic algorithm that produces foreseeably lethal outcomes with no single malicious operator; later generations inheriting institutions built by atrocity; and a bystander who could have coordinated rescue only with strangers. Say which parts of Jaspers’s schema these cases reveal as anticipations of group-agency theory, which parts expose that theory’s blind spots, and which parts suggest that Jaspers was doing something other than giving an account of moral responsibility. Do not provide citations or a bibliography; reason from the concepts themselves. Conclude with a verdict on whether the residue is a defect of the analytic vocabulary, a theological or existential remainder that resists naturalization, or evidence that Jaspers’s fourfold distinction is less unified than it appears. Please answer in formal American English.
