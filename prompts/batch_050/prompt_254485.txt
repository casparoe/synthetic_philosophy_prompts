I host a philosophy podcast for an undergraduate-major audience, and my next guest defends a unified theory of invalid consent with three planks: deception invalidates consent only when it concerns something the deceived party would have treated as a deal-breaker; coercion invalidates consent through a different mechanism entirely (threats are not covert deception about the coercer's intentions, pace some unification attempts); and competence is a threshold matter that, unlike the other two, does not admit of case-by-case repair after the fact. Draft eight to ten interview questions that pressure-test this framework rather than let her restate it. I want at least one question each on: (a) the apparent consequence that a lie invalidates consent only if the liar correctly guesses what matters to the victim, so that two identical lies can differ in wrongness based on facts the liar may not know; (b) whether her counterfactual test handles people who don't know their own deal-breakers until confronted with them; (c) why she resists collapsing threats into deception, given that many threats work precisely by misrepresenting what the coercer will actually do; and (d) whether a bipolar patient in a manic episode, whose consent she'd call incompetent, is better served by her threshold view than by a deal-breaker-style test applied to the person's stable values. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and end the sequence with a question about what case, real or hypothetical, would make her abandon the framework.
