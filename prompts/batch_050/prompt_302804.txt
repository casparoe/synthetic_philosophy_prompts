In discussions of constitutional monarchy, I often hear the monarch compared to a referee in a football match: during ordinary play the referee stays out of the game, but when the players foul one another, dispute the score, or refuse to restart, an impartial official must intervene so that the contest can continue. Defenders of monarchy use this comparison to argue that an unelected head of state can be compatible with democracy, since the monarch does not make policy but merely safeguards the constitutional rules. Critics reply that the comparison flatters the institution: unlike a referee, a hereditary monarch is neither selected for competence nor accountable to the players, and in countries such as the United Kingdom the relevant rules are partly conventional and therefore especially unsuited to discretionary enforcement.

Which disanalogies are merely incidental, and which matter to the democratic argument—for example, the fact that a referee applies publicly stated laws of the game, whereas constitutional conventions may be contested, or that a referee is appointed and removable while a monarch succeeds by birth?

Finally, assess whether the analogy supports the stronger conclusion that constitutional monarchy is a useful democratic safeguard, the weaker conclusion that it need not be undemocratic, or neither.
