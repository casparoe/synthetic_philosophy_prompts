I'm in a political philosophy seminar that's just finished the unit on Rawls, and I keep noticing something in my public economics readings that I can't sort out. In regulatory cost-benefit analysis, agencies routinely treat a dollar of cost or benefit as a dollar no matter whose pocket it lands in: a regulation that imposes costs on high-income households and delivers slightly larger benefits to wealthy consumers counts as an improvement, and the standard defense of this is that the winners could in principle compensate the losers, so the analyst needn't worry about the actual distribution. Yet the same governments that use this criterion run steeply progressive tax-and-transfer systems, and when finance ministries publish distributional analyses of budgets -- tables showing who gains and loses by income decile -- the political legitimacy of a budget is widely taken to depend on those tables, as though it matters a great deal that the worst-off groups not be made worse off even when aggregate gains are positive. So within one policy apparatus we seem to have two incompatible criteria: distribution-blind aggregation in the regulatory analysis, distribution-sensitive evaluation in the fiscal analysis.

What I'd like to understand is what these practices presuppose, philosophically. Does the distributional-analysis side presuppose something like the difference principle -- that policy is to be evaluated by how the worst-off group fares -- or merely the weaker claim that the marginal social value of a dollar declines with the recipient's income, which a sufficiently sophisticated utilitarianism also delivers? Does the cost-benefit side presuppose that the compensation test tracks a real moral improvement, or is it better reconstructed as a division-of-labor view on which efficiency and distribution are handled by different institutions and needn't be jointly consistent? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of each practice, distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- that is, where several rival theories of distributive justice would license exactly the same procedure. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in regulatory analysis if the government actually committed to a maximin criterion instead (does it even make sense to apply maximin regulation-by-regulation rather than to the basic structure as a whole?), and whether the apparent inconsistency between the two practices is a genuine incoherence or an artifact of evaluating different objects.

One request about how to calibrate the answer: please be explicit about what is settled among philosophers and economists who work on this -- for instance, what Rawls's own view actually commits one to, and the well-known objections to treating cost-benefit analysis as value-neutral -- versus what remains genuinely contested, such as whether maximin-style reasoning is defensible outside the original position, or whether welfarist frameworks can fully capture what the distributional tables are doing. I'd rather be told a question is open than be handed one camp's answer as the consensus.
