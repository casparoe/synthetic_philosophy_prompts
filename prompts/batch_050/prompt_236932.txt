I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading about decision-theory puzzles involving perfect predictors. Two problems keep coming up in proximity to each other — counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail — and I want a careful comparison. Since the exact framing matters, let me state both as I understand them; correct me where my setup is off.

Counterfactual mugging: A perfect predictor flips a fair coin. On heads, it gives me $10,000 if and only if it predicted that I would hand over $100 in the tails branch. On tails, it asks me for $100. The coin comes up tails, and I'm standing there facing the request.

XOR blackmail: A perfect predictor sends me a letter if and only if exactly one of the following is true: (a) my house has termites, or (b) I will pay $1,000 upon receiving such a letter. The letter explains this arrangement and requests the money. I receive the letter. Whether I actually have termites is causally independent of the letter and of my payment, and the cost of untreated termites dwarfs $1,000.

Here is what I'd like:

1. A structural comparison. Both problems involve a predictor conditioning something on my counterfactual payment behavior, yet the verdicts I've seen go in opposite directions: the style of reasoning that says pay the mugger seems, at first glance, to say pay the blackmailer too, but many people who endorse paying in the mugging case refuse in the blackmail case. Where exactly does the asymmetry live? Is it about when the policy gets fixed (before versus after learning which branch I'm in), about what the payment buys, or about the direction of the counterfactual dependence?

2. The ex ante perspective. I think of an agent's behavior as code written in advance. If I specify a policy once, before knowing which branch I'll find myself in, which policy wins in expected-utility terms in each problem? Can a single coherent decision procedure output pay in one problem and refuse in the other without special pleading, or do the two problems genuinely demand different treatment?

3. The apparent retrocausation. In XOR blackmail, refusing to pay upon receiving the letter seems to leave me with termites in a way that feels almost backwards in time, and this is the part that most confuses my programmer intuitions. Spell out what is actually going on: what does the letter's existence depend on, and why isn't refusing a way of causing termites?

Finally, I don't want false confidence. Please give your verdict on whether the two problems reduce to a single principle or must be treated separately, and then say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it held up, would flip your answer, and where your reasoning is most likely to be going wrong.
