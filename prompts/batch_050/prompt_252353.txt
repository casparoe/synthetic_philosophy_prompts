For my upper-level undergraduate seminar on experimental philosophy, our instructor has asked each student to draft one practice exam question for the unit on cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions, together with a model answer and a marking scheme; the strongest submissions will be used on the real exam. Please write my submission.

The exam question should concern the restrictionist challenge arising from studies such as those by Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich and by Machery, Mallon, Nichols, and Stich, which report that American and East Asian participants respond differently to Gettier-style cases. However, it must not be answerable merely by summarizing those studies. Instead, the question should present candidates with the following fictional follow-up result and ask them to work out its philosophical consequences: in a large preregistered replication across six countries, the between-culture differences in Gettier-case verdicts shrink to statistical insignificance once participants' scores on a measure of cognitive reflection (their tendency to override an initial intuitive answer in favor of a deliberated one) are entered as a covariate; the strongest predictor of Gettier-style verdicts within every country is a participant's score on that reflection measure; and formal philosophical training predicts verdicts only in the Western samples. Candidates should have 75 minutes to assess what this result would mean for (a) the restrictionist argument that the method of cases must be confined to a calibrated expert class whose intuitions diverge from the folk's, (b) the rival diagnosis that cross-cultural findings track performance errors or pragmatic interference with a shared underlying competence rather than genuinely divergent epistemic intuitions, and (c) the question of whose verdicts the new result vindicates — whether it is better news for defenders of the method of cases or for a more thoroughgoing skeptic who denies that anyone's case judgments, expert or folk, track epistemic facts.

The model answer should sketch the strongest version of a complete response in about 600 words. The marking scheme should be out of 30 marks, organized around exactly five must-hit points, stating how many marks each point carries and ending with a one-line note on the mistake candidates most commonly make on that point. The five points should include at least the following: (1) recognizing that the fictional result does not simply refute restrictionism but relocates the calibration problem, since the question of which group counts as the epistemically privileged reference class reappears as the question of whether high scorers on the reflection measure are the relevant elite; (2) distinguishing the claim that verdicts vary across groups from the claim that the underlying epistemic competence varies, and applying that distinction to the covariate result rather than merely asserting it; (3) noticing that explaining away cultural variation via cognitive reflection cuts both ways, since it vindicates the method of cases only given independent reason to think reflective responders track the truth rather than merely converge with each other; (4) addressing whether the cross-cultural agreement among less reflective participants constitutes evidence for a universal folk epistemology or instead a shared source of noise; and (5) arriving at a defended overall verdict about what the hypothetical study would show regarding the epistemic standing of case intuitions, with marks reserved for answers that commit to a position rather than surveying possibilities and ending on a hedge.
