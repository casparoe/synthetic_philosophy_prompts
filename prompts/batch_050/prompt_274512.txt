I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and while reporting a piece on why fiction grips us I sat in on a philosophy workshop where a graduate student defended an argument I keep turning over in my notebook. It seems to prove far too much: by the final line, Conan Doyle's detective ends up sharing an ontology with unicorns, golden mountains, and worse. I'd like a careful diagnosis for a sidebar I'm drafting. The argument ran as follows. (1) Some sentences containing the name "Sherlock Holmes" are plainly true: Holmes is a detective, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street, and Holmes is more widely discussed than any flesh-and-blood detective who ever lived. (2) A sentence with a name in subject position can be true only if that name refers to something; an empty name would leave such sentences false or truth-valueless. (3) So "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something. (4) Referring to something just is that thing's being there to be referred to — one cannot so much as talk about what is not, in any sense, there. (5) Therefore Sherlock Holmes has being, even if not flesh and blood. (6) Nothing in steps (1) through (5) turns on any special fact about Holmes, so the reasoning applies equally to every fictional character, every mythical creature, and indeed every object anyone has ever managed to describe, including contradictory ones like the round square. (7) Hence the realm of being is populated by all of these.

The student conceded that (7) is hard to swallow but insisted the only exits are denying (1) — which she dismissed as a willingness to call the obvious false — or denying (4), which she said conflates what exists with what we can talk about. My instincts say something slipped earlier, around the move from (1) to (3), since the three example truths in (1) don't all seem to be true in the same sense, but I can't articulate the objection cleanly enough for print. Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? Which step should I reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? If the right answer is instead to accept the argument's validity and surrender a premise, please price the standard alternatives for me — the fictionalist paraphrase strategy, the view that fictional characters are abstract artifacts we genuinely refer to, the Meinongian embrace of non-existent objects, and the free-logic option of letting names fail to refer — so I can explain to readers what each buys and what each gives up. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than deny (7) outright, but I want to know what I'm paying. Please write your answer in American English.
