You are a philosopher of language with a specialty in the ethics of assertion and a working knowledge of how large language models are built and deployed. I would like you to analyze a real episode through the lens of Harry Frankfurt's account of bullshit, and then run the analysis back against the account itself. Work through the classification carefully, considering rival verdicts before settling on one, rather than jumping to the label that fits the headlines.

The episode is the widely reported 2023 affair in which two New York lawyers, representing a plaintiff in a personal-injury suit against an airline, filed a brief in federal court that cited several judicial decisions that do not exist. The citations had been produced by ChatGPT; one of the lawyers had asked the chatbot to verify that the cases were real, received reassuring answers, and did not check any of them against a legal database. When opposing counsel and the judge could not locate the cases, the lawyers filed further material that included excerpts from the fictitious opinions, also generated by the chatbot. The court sanctioned the lawyers, holding that they had acted in bad faith by persisting in a sham even after the doubts were raised.

Frankfurt's taxonomy, as I take it: the liar tracks the truth in order to conceal or distort it, and so is constrained by it; the bullshitter is indifferent to whether what he says is true or false, caring only about the effect on his audience; and plain error is falsehood without any such attitude, the result of ordinary epistemic failure. Please use that taxonomy as the instrument and do the following, leading with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence and then giving the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of this request.

First, the mapping. Classify, with argument, each of the following as lying, bullshit, error, some combination, or none of the three: (a) ChatGPT's generation of the fabricated citations, and its confident affirmations when asked to check them; (b) the lawyer's initial submission of the brief; (c) the lawyers' conduct after the court questioned the citations; and (d) the chatbot developer's public presentation of the system as a source of reliable information. Be precise about who or what is the bearer of the relevant attitude in each case, and about what the equivalent of an audience and an intended effect would be.

Second, the strain points. Frankfurt's bullshitter has an attitude of indifference toward the truth; a language model plausibly has no attitudes at all, only a training objective and a disposition to produce fluent text. Say whether applying his category to the model extends it, distorts it, or reveals that it was always implicitly about the stance of a speaker toward an audience rather than about inner states — and consider whether the honest verdict is that the model does something the taxonomy has no slot for, neither lying nor bullshitting nor merely erring, but confabulating in a way that only superficially resembles all three.

Third, the reverse direction. What does the episode show about the adequacy of the taxonomy? In particular: the court's bad-faith finding rested on the lawyers' failure to check, not on any intent to deceive, which suggests the live moral category in practice is neither lying nor bullshitting but something like culpable epistemic negligence; and the episode's real damage may lie in the mismatch between fluent assertion and any grounding in fact, which is arguably Frankfurt's insight generalized beyond cases where anyone is indifferent. Say whether the episode confirms Frankfurt's framework, exposes a gap in it, or shows that it was never designed for cases in which the most consequential speaker in the chain is not an agent at all.

Where the classification depends on facts about how language models work that are genuinely unsettled, say so rather than forcing a verdict. At the end, state how confident you are in your overall analysis, and single out the one classification or claim in it that you are least sure of, with a sentence on why.
