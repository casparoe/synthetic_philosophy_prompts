The essay tries to import some ideas from my old field into the debate about societal "collapse" -- the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, Easter Island, that whole literature. Please tell me honestly whether the analogy does any real work or whether I'm just a retired guy redescribing things in vocabulary I happen to know. Anyway, here's the draft:

---

**Is collapse a thing, or just a point of view?**

When ecologists talk about ecosystems, we have a concept called a regime shift. A lake can flip from clear water to turbid, algae-dominated water when phosphorus crosses a threshold, and the flip is fast, hard to reverse, and dramatically changes what lives there. But note: nothing "died." The lake is still a lake. The total biomass sometimes even goes up. What changed is the *structure* -- which species dominate, how energy flows, what the system can do.

Historians and popular writers talk about societal collapse constantly: the Bronze Age palace systems around 1200 BC, the Classic Maya cities, Rapa Nui. The framing is usually tragic -- failure, breakdown, a warning for us. But reading this literature as an ecologist, I keep noticing that the actual events look less like a lake drying up and more like a regime shift. The palaces at Mycenae stopped being built, yes, but the people mostly didn't vanish; they lived differently, in smaller units, with less hierarchy and less writing. The Maya walked away from the great cities of the southern lowlands, but Maya people and Maya languages are still here today, in the millions. Something ended, but what ended was a *particular configuration* -- a ruling class, a monumental style, an administrative apparatus -- not "a society" in any simple sense.

This suggests to me that "collapse" is less a property of events than a property of descriptions. Whether the Maya "collapsed" depends on which features you take to be constitutive of the thing you're tracking. If the thing is "the Classic Maya political order," then yes, collapse. If the thing is "the Maya people," then no collapse, just change. There's no fact of the matter sitting underneath, waiting to adjudicate. It's a choice of individuation, the same way whether my old lake "collapsed" depends on whether you individuate lakes by clarity or by continued existence.

There's a second point, which I think is sharper. Ecologists know that regime shifts are often *adaptive at some level*. A system under stress that can't sustain its current structure does better, in the long run, by flipping to a cheaper structure than by clinging to the expensive one until it physically cannot feed anyone. If small-scale, low-overhead village life is the "cheap structure" for post-palace Greece, then what we call collapse might sometimes be closer to a successful adaptation -- the society doing the smart thing -- and the real failures are the cases where elites successfully *prevented* the shift and everyone starved inside an intact palace. By that logic, the Easter Island story, if it really was a population crash, might be the unusual case: not collapse but the failure to collapse early enough.

So my tentative conclusion: the therapy here, if I can put it that way, is to stop asking "why did this society collapse?" and start asking "why did it persist in an unsustainable configuration for as long as it did, and what made the eventual transition as gentle or as brutal as it was?" Persistence of a bad structure is the thing that needs explaining, not its abandonment.

---
