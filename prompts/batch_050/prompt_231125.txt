I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in political philosophy (about 15 students, one 90-minute session), and we are about to begin a unit on social choice and individual rights. I want an in-class activity that makes Sen's liberal paradox — the impossibility of a Paretian liberal — land as a live problem before the students ever see the theorem. Design the activity for me, observing the following constraints.

First, the setup. The students should be put in a position where they themselves propose, and feel proud of, two principles that look obviously defensible: (a) certain choices about a person's own conduct — what they read, how they dress, what they do in private — are that person's to make, and the group should treat that person's preference over the relevant pair of alternatives as decisive; (b) if every member of the group prefers one alternative to another, the group should not settle on the dispreferred one. The activity must then arrange the players' preferences and decisions so that these two principles, operating together through the group's own agreed procedure, generate either a cycle in the collective ranking or an outcome every participant ranks below an available alternative. The students must discover this in the mechanics of play, not be told it is coming.

Second, the materials and rules. Specify exactly what goes on the cards or information sheets each player receives: their private-sphere choice, and their meddlesome preferences about others' choices. Specify the aggregation procedure by which the group decides, so that the paradox shows up as a property of the procedure rather than of anyone's bad faith. Give a round-by-round timeline and a scoring scheme with small stakes that incentivizes players to keep both principles alive as long as possible instead of abandoning one at the first sign of trouble.

Third, scaling. Sen's original illustration involves two people and a single controversial book; explain how to scale the structure to 15 participants without diluting the logic — whether through pairs, a chain of overlapping private spheres, or sequential rounds — and say which option you recommend and why.

Fourth, no naming. The students should not hear the name of the theorem, the names of the axioms, or Sen's name until the debrief.

Fifth, the debrief. Map each moment of the activity onto the formal result: which moments instantiate minimal liberalism, which instantiate weak Pareto, and where the demand for a consistent collective ordering breaks. Then walk through the exits and what each one costs: excluding or discounting meddlesome preferences, weakening the Pareto requirement, reconceiving rights as actions to be exercised rather than as constraints on which social states may be selected, or restricting the domain of admissible preference profiles. For each exit, indicate how a player who had adopted it mid-game would have had to play differently, so students see that each rescue amputates something they wanted.

Sixth, failure modes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, students resolving the toy case by majority vote and concluding the paradox is an artifact of the rules, or students quietly revising their stated preferences once the cycle appears — and how to handle each failure without breaking the game.

Seventh, be honest about the gap. State plainly which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it is the generality of the impossibility, the fact that no aggregation rule whatsoever can jointly satisfy the conditions, rather than merely the failure of the one procedure we used in class — and how I should supply that missing piece in the lecture that follows.
