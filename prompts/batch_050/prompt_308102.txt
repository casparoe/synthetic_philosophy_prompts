In Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, the central question is disarmingly simple to state: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Consider how much an 'a' can vary — the double-story 'a' of a roman book face, the single-story 'a' of italics and everyday handwriting, the blackletter 'a' of a Fraktur text, the contorted 'a' of a gridfont (a typeface designed under the constraint that every letter must be built on the same tiny grid of squares, so that no glyph may use a feature unavailable to any other). Readers sort all of these into one category without effort. The project posed a second question alongside the first: what makes the letters of a single typeface cohere — what shared stylistic essence, the spirit in the project's name, runs through every glyph of one font, such that its 'a' and its 'g' visibly belong to the same family? Call the first the category question and the second the style question.

I have been collecting candidate answers to the category question, and I believe my list covers the standard ones:

1. Template or prototype matching. The mind stores an ideal or average 'a'; a mark is an 'a' if it resembles the prototype closely enough. (Strained by the fact that the allographs seem to share no shape at all that could serve as the prototype.)
2. Structural or topological invariants. An 'a' is defined by an abstract part-structure — a closed bowl joined to a stem — preserved under stylistic deformation. (Strained by the single-story 'a', which has no stem, and by blackletter.)
3. Functional or contrastive role. An 'a' is whatever occupies the 'a'-slot in a writing system — the grapheme that contrasts with the other twenty-five in the relevant positions. Letters as chess pieces: identity from role, not material.
4. Recognitional dispositionalism. An 'a' is whatever competent readers reliably and non-inferentially read as 'a'. (Threatened by circularity: read it as what?)
5. Family resemblance. There is no common core; 'a's are held together only by overlapping strands of similarity, as games are. (Arguably less an answer than a principled refusal of one.)
6. Historical lineage. An 'a' is whatever descends by an unbroken chain of copying and gradual deformation from the ancestral letterform that ran from Phoenician aleph through Greek alpha to Latin a. Genealogy, not geometry.

I want a seventh answer that is genuinely different from these six — not a rewording, not a special case, and not a hybrid ("a template constrained by role" does not count). Three conditions apply. First, the proposal must engage the style question as well as the category question: it should say how being an 'a' relates to being an 'a' in this font, and which of the two, if either, is explanatorily prior. Second, it must be tested against at least these cases: (i) the single-story/double-story allograph pair, which share no obvious shape invariant; (ii) a pixel-identical shape that is one letter in the Latin alphabet and a different letter in Cyrillic (the shape of Latin 'p' is Cyrillic's er, pronounced /r/); (iii) a glyph that is unambiguous within a word but ambiguous in isolation, as routinely happens with gridfonts; and (iv) a wholly novel typeface encountered for the first time, where no template, precedent, or reading history is yet in place. Third, state explicitly how the proposal differs from at least the two listed answers it most resembles, and close with an honest verdict: does it survive its confrontation with these cases, or does it quietly collapse into one of the six?
