I work in formal verification for a living (proof checkers, trusted computing bases, reproducible builds, the whole mess) and I've been trying to wrap my head around the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. As I understand it the idea is roughly: an agent that only wants to take actions it can prove safe, and that builds a successor running the same decision procedure, has to prove that the successor's proofs are sound — but Löb's theorem says a consistent system can never prove its own soundness, so it can't vouch for a copy of itself, and the whole line of self-improving agents gets stuck at the first generation. In his 1931 paper On Formally Undecidable Propositions of Principia Mathematica and Related Systems, Gödel drops this remark in a footnote: "Every epistemological antinomy can likewise be used for a similar undecidability proof." So here's my question. Restate the Löbian obstacle as precisely as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — TCBs, verified kernels, sandboxing, signing off on a build, trusting a compiler bootstrap — and tell me which parts of it translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart in engineering practice at all. And take a stand on the footnote: is the Löbian obstacle really just one of Gödel's antinomies dressed up (the liar wearing a hard hat), such that the residue is a quirk of self-reference that real systems route around anyway, or is something genuinely lost in the translation that engineers like me are missing? I want your committed verdict with reasons, not a menu of options. Also please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, like an email from a colleague.
