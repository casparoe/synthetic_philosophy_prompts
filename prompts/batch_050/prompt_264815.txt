I teach an upper-division undergraduate seminar on democratic theory -- 18 students, one two-hour session -- and I want a structured simulation that makes students feel why democratic backsliding is so hard to identify as it happens: a democracy can be eroded through a sequence of moves, each of which is legal, procedurally proper, and defensible on its own terms, so that even attentive, well-intentioned observers disagree about whether backsliding has occurred at all, let alone when it began. The students should run into this problem themselves before it is named.

Here is what I need designed. Split the class into roles: a governing party with a sincere policy mandate (they should genuinely want to pass their agenda, not play villains), an opposition, and an assessment panel acting as an international democracy watchdog that must vote at checkpoints on whether backsliding has begun, working only from a deliberately thin definition (something like a substantial weakening of democratic checks and accountability). The heart of the design is a menu of reforms the governing party can enact, one per round. Each reform must be genuinely double-faced in the design itself -- carrying both a plausible public-interest rationale (modernizing an overstaffed court, equalizing districts after population shifts, making public broadcasting accountable) and a real power-consolidating effect -- so that the two readings are entangled rather than one being a fig leaf over the other. Give me the rules, the turn structure, the checkpoint mechanics, and a timeline that fits two hours, including how to keep the governing party's incentives sincere so the room doesn't treat them as cartoon authoritarians.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the concept's fault lines: incrementalism, the gap between legality and democratic legitimacy, intent versus outcome, the question of who gets to decide (voters, courts, scholars, foreign monitors), and the suspicion that the label functions as a partisan weapon -- students may well reach for Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or recent US episodes here, and the debrief should be able to absorb that. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure mode, and be frank about which features of real backsliding (timescale, real stakes, a media environment, genuine public opinion) the simulation cannot reproduce.

Finally, throughout the design and debrief materials, keep separate what is broadly agreed among scholars of democratization -- that contemporary backsliding is typically incremental and law-following, and that certain cases are widely treated as paradigm instances -- from what is genuinely contested, such as where exactly the boundary lies, whether particular contested cases qualify, and whether the concept is applied asymmetrically. Do not present any particular boundary criterion or case classification as settled consensus.
