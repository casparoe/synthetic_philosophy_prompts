Is there a name for this? I keep running into a specific kind of argument, and I'm not sure it has a label, or whether it's just a special case of something better known. The basic shape is: someone asks you to do something based on reasoning where a tiny chance gets multiplied by an enormous payoff. But here's the wrinkle that bugs me — in these cases, the size of the payoff isn't fixed independently of how likely the story is. The bigger the promised payoff gets, the more that very bigness should count as evidence *against* the story. Like, a guy on the street says give him your wallet or he'll press a button that tortures a trillion simulated minds — my credence in his story should drop *because* of the trillion, not in spite of it. Same thing when someone says a particular god will punish you infinitely for not performing some ritual: the infinity isn't an extra feature of the threat, it's a reason to trust the claim less. And I saw it in a non-philosophical context too, a startup pitch where the projected market kept growing every slide, and each bump made me believe the projections less.

So the pattern is: payoff size and probability aren't independent, and the argument relies on treating them as if they were. Is that a named thing? It's not just Pascal's mugging in general, because the mugging case is usually stated with the probability fixed, and it's not just risk aversion, and I don't think it's exactly "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" either, because that's about claims in general, not about the payoff magnitude feeding back into the probability. If there's no established term, coin one for me and define it carefully enough that I can tell what falls under it and what doesn't — like, does it apply to long-shot charities where the payoff is big but the mechanism is mundane, and if not, why not.
