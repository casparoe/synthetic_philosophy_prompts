Next month I'm giving our joint philosophy/cog-sci colloquium talk, and I need to survive the Q&A. The paper defends disjunctivism about perceptual experience: roughly, the view that a veridical perception of the coffee mug and a perfectly matching hallucination of it are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, not two instances of one common experiential kind with different causes. My main line is the usual relational one -- in the good case the mug itself is a constituent of the experience, and the matching hallucination shares no such constitution, it only seems introspectively the same.

Here's the thing: the audience will be maybe two philosophers of mind (friendly-ish, one a known type-identity sympathizer) and the rest are vision scientists and a couple of neuroimaging people. They are not going to raise the argument from hallucination in its textbook form. They're going to come at me with stuff like proximal stimulation, common neural signatures, and what their experiments supposedly show.

So draft me a prep sheet: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are to get asked and how much damage each does. Phrase each one the way a vision scientist would phrase it -- in their vocabulary, with whatever experimental result or methodological commitment they'd appeal to. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a candid note about where that reply is soft or hand-wavy. Tell me what I should just concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging. And give me the objection I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one where my view is genuinely most exposed -- along with the best I've got if someone does. One thing I care about: I want to know which of their objections actually engage the metaphysical claim and which ones just assume the common-factor picture without arguing for it, and how to say that without sounding condescending to a room full of scientists who outnumber me.
