In Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren (1930), John Maynard Keynes diagnoses the mass unemployment of his day as follows: "We are suffering, not from the rheumatics of old age, but from the growing-pains of over-rapid changes, from the painfulness of readjustment between one economic period and another." First, explain what Keynes means by this — in particular, what distinguishes his "growing-pains" diagnosis from both the revolutionary and reactionary pessimisms he rejects, and how it supports his century-scale optimism about the eventual arrival of abundance and leisure. Then, describe the most important implications of this diagnosis for how we should think about today's concerns that automation and AI will displace human labor faster than economies can adjust. If Keynes is right that such disruption is a transitional maladjustment rather than a terminal condition, what follows — for individual attitudes toward work, for policymakers, and for the long-run question of what a good life looks like once the economic problem recedes? Finally, say whether you think the analogy between his moment and ours holds, and where it breaks down.
