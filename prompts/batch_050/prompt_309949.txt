I teach an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of fiction, and for our unit on empty names and truth in fiction I want to close with Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead rather than the usual Holmes-and-unicorns examples. Read the play through that lens and help me build the session. The standard puzzle asks whether a name like Sherlock Holmes refers to anything and how a sentence about him can nonetheless be true in the story; Stoppard's play seems to raise a harder version, because Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are imported from an existing fiction, Hamlet, in which their deaths are already fixed. Are the men on stage the same characters Shakespeare killed off, or new fictional objects wearing old names, and does the answer change what is true of them? Consider the opening coin-toss sequence, where the coin comes up heads over and over and Guildenstern begins to suspect that the ordinary rules no longer apply to them: is this best understood as the characters noticing that they live inside a fiction, or only as creatures of one story feeling the gravitational pull of another? Then take the ending, where the pair sail toward an execution they dimly know is coming and the Player suggests that death on stage is the only kind audiences believe: what does the play's treatment of their foreknown, twice-told death do to the claim that what is true in a fiction is fixed by what the work makes fictional? Where does the play resist the standard theoretical apparatus -- accounts of truth in fiction in terms of what the story says or what readers are to imagine -- and where would deploying that apparatus flatten the play into a mere illustration? Please point me to two or three scenes or exchanges my students can discuss without having the text in front of them, flag any reading I should avoid because it distorts either the philosophy or the play, and end with what you think the play contributes to the debate about fictional names that a straight philosophical argument cannot.
