Colleagues in my policy shop have taken to invoking Chesterton's fence — in its usual one-line form, "never tear down a fence until you know why it was put up" — to veto reforms, most recently a proposal to abolish a grant-review procedure whose original rationale is a matter of record and, to my mind, straightforwardly bad. Take the slogan apart into the distinct claims it could be making: the near-trivial (understand a thing before changing it; change has unintended costs), the substantive Burkean readings (the functions of long-standing practices are often tacit and distributed, so visible uselessness is evidence of a hidden function rather than of none; practices that endured were selected for something), the dialectical (the burden of proof always falls on the reformer), and the strong and dubious (age itself confers authority; survival vindicates). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible thesis, or false; which of them the people who reach for the slogan are probably relying on; and — crucially — whether it can justify a veto in the case where the fence's purpose is documented and discredited, since the slogan's literal condition is then satisfied and the veto must be borrowing a stronger premise. I suspect no defensible reading can do the work my colleagues need. If you agree, say what would change that verdict — what evidence about tacit institutional functions or failed reforms would show the principle still bites even against a known-bad original rationale. If you disagree, name the reading that carries the weight and defend it against the charge that it begs the question against reform as such.
