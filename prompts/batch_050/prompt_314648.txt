Student's answer:

"Singer's argument rests on the principle that if we can prevent something very bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we morally ought to do it. The drowning child case establishes this principle through an easy rescue: nobody would refuse to wade in and save a drowning child just to avoid ruining their clothes. Singer then argues there is no morally relevant difference between that case and donating to famine relief, so people in affluent countries are obligated to give a large portion of their income rather than spend it on luxuries.

Essentially, Singer is making a utilitarian argument: suffering is bad wherever it happens, so we should promote welfare impartially, which means giving until giving more would leave us worse off than the people we're trying to help. He admits this is demanding but holds that demandingness is no objection to a moral truth.

The strongest objection, in my view, is the collective action problem. When many bystanders could rescue the child, no single person is obligated, because responsibility is shared among them. Applied to famine, this means that unless everyone else gives their share, my obligation lapses. Singer concedes this point, so his conclusion only holds under ideal conditions where everyone does their part, which limits how much it demands of us in the real world."
