Having retired from experimental physics, I have been working through Hofstadter's account of the self as a "strange loop": the 'I' is not a substance but a high-level, self-referential pattern in the brain, and so a coarse-grained, lower-resolution copy of a person's pattern can persist in the brains of those who knew them well, leaving the dead distributed, partially, across other heads. My reading group keeps reaching for a particular analogy to make this palatable: the self is like a melody, which exists in no single score or performance, survives the loss of any one instantiation, and can be replayed by other instruments, however imperfectly. The analogy is being made to do consoling work: if selves are pattern rather than stuff, death is less final than we suppose. I would like an audit of how far it holds. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the score, the performance, the instrument, the listeners — then the points where the mapping breaks (my instincts point to resolution loss, the absence of ongoing causal coupling between the copies, and the first-person perspective), and finally a judgment on whether the consoling conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the melody picture was smuggling it in all along. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
