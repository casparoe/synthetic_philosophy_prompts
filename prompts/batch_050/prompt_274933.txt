**Two ways to put a proposition on the table**

The debate over the norm of assertion asks what warrant you need to properly assert p. The knowledge account says you must know p; its rivals say you need only truth, or only justified belief. The usual evidence is by now familiar: asserting "p but I don't know p" is defective; flat-out asserting that someone's lottery ticket lost, on purely statistical grounds, seems wrong even though you're almost certainly right; and "how do you know that?" is a standing, legitimate challenge to any assertion.

I want to argue that this debate rests on a false presupposition: that there is one speech act, assertion, governed by one norm. Look instead at what the speaker invites the hearer to *do* with p. Sometimes an assertion asks the hearer to rely -- to act on p, spend money on p, repeat p onward. When my mechanic says "it's the alternator," I am being invited to authorize a repair. Sometimes an assertion asks only that the hearer consider p -- turn it over, test it, see what follows. When someone in a seminar says "maybe the regress just stops at seemings," nobody is being asked to rely on anything; p is on the table for inspection, not use.

My proposal: there is one norm, but it is indexed to the invitation. Call it the uptake norm. You must not assert p in a way that invites reliance unless you know p. You may assert p in a way that invites only consideration if you have reasonable belief in p and can make your epistemic position legible on request -- that is, if someone asks how you know or why you believe, you owe them an honest answer about the actual state of your evidence, including its weaknesses. Call that the legibility condition. Reliance-invitations without knowledge are defective even when reasonable; consideration-invitations with legible reasonable belief are fine even when the proposition turns out false.

This explains the standard evidence better than the monolithic knowledge norm. "How do you know that?" is a fair challenge to my mechanic and obnoxious pedantry in the seminar, and the knowledge norm alone can't mark that difference -- on that view both assertions stand or fall by the same standard, so the challenge should be equally apt in both. Lottery assertions feel wrong because they invite reliance ("so I can throw the ticket away?") while being backed by something visibly weaker than knowledge. And Moore-paradoxical assertions are defective on either reading, since asserting p while disavowing knowledge violates both the reliance condition and legibility.

The obvious objection: my "consideration-invitation" is just hedging, and the whole proposal collapses into the knowledge norm plus a remark that "maybe p" and "I think p" aren't flat-out assertions. I think this is wrong for two reasons. First, hedges are sentence-level markers, while what I'm describing is a standing feature of the exchange -- the mechanic who says "hmm, it could be the alternator" in a repair context is still inviting reliance, hedge or no hedge. Second, hedges are systematically ambiguous between genuinely limiting the invitation and mere politeness ("I think we should get started" limits nothing). If the norm really only cared about knowledge of unhedged content, the ubiquity of hedges as politeness would be a mystery.
