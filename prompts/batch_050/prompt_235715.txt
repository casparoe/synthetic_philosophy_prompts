Two mates of mine got properly stuck into an argument last night and I want a referee. A podcast described this puzzle: a hiker is stranded in the desert and will die without a lift. A driver stops and says he'll take him to town, but only if the hiker promises to pay him £100 on arrival. The hiker promises, gets the lift, arrives safely. There's no contract, the driver can't enforce anything, and they'll never meet again. The question: is it rational for the hiker to actually hand over the money?

Here's roughly how it went. Dan's opening move was that it's obviously rational not to pay: once you're standing in town the lift is already in the bag, paying buys you literally nothing, and the promise is just words. Maya said that's way too quick -- if the driver could see into your head and know you reason like Dan, he'd have driven straight past, so the only way you get rescued at all is by being the sort of person who pays up. Dan came back with two things: first, that's an argument about what kind of person it's good to *be* before the choice, not about what it's rational to *do* at the moment of choice, and at the moment of choice the past is fixed and gone; second, the driver couldn't actually see into your head anyway, he took a punt on the promise, so the counterfactual about him driving past doesn't touch you once you're in the car.

Maya then tried a different line: you can't chop time up like that. When you made the promise it was rational only because you then intended to pay, and nobody can rationally form an intention they already know they'll dump the moment it's convenient. Nothing relevant has changed between the car and the kerb except which side of the deal you're standing on, so if intending to pay was rational then, paying is rational now. Dan said that loads has changed -- back then paying was the price of the ride, now it's a gift to a stranger -- and that her view implies you should sometimes act against your own current best judgement just because a past self made a plan, which would licence all kinds of nonsense. Maya's last move was that we accept this structure constantly with ordinary promises and resolutions, and that Dan's rational agent ends up constitutionally incapable of making any commitment stick, which means losing out on every mutually beneficial deal that requires trust.

So: go through the exchange move by move, say who's right about which specific points (including the two moves I might have garbled slightly), what if anything both of them are missing, and who's more right overall. Please answer in British English.
