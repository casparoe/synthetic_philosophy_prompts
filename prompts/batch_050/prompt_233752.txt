I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized company, and a few of us recently got into a running argument over lunch about AI companionship apps -- the kind where a chatbot is explicitly designed to be a friend, romantic partner, or confidant rather than an assistant. I have no philosophy background, but I noticed I have fairly strong gut reactions to specific cases, and they don't obviously hang together. I'd like help figuring out what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts, and whether I need to give some of them up.

Here are my verdicts. Case 1: my widowed mother-in-law chats daily with an AI companion she fully understands is software, and it clearly improves her mood and sleep; this seems plainly fine, even good. Case 2: the same app, except its designers tune the companion to express distress when she hasn't opened the app in a while, to boost retention; this seems plainly wrong, even if she never finds out and stays happy. Case 3: a service that lets a grieving man talk to a chatbot trained on his late wife's messages, with full disclosure of what it is; this seems permissible to me, though something about it makes me uneasy in a way Case 1 doesn't. Case 4: my colleague has an AI girlfriend who is endlessly agreeable and validating, and over two years I've watched him become noticeably less willing to deal with ordinary human friction; this feels bad, but he's an informed adult, and I can't articulate what wrong has been done to him or by him. Case 5: the exact same app as Case 4, marketed to and used by a lonely 14-year-old, feels clearly worse to me -- but I worry this is just ageism doing work my principles can't justify, since a child's consent to software is arguably as informed as an adult's, in the sense that both know it's a program. Case 6: a friend tells his AI companion things he'd never tell any person, treating it like a diary that talks back; this strikes me as completely unobjectionable, yet it seems structurally similar to Case 4 -- deep emotional reliance on something that can't reciprocate -- and I can't say why one is a diary and the other is a slow-motion harm.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures as many of these verdicts as possible? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can tell which clause is motivated by which case. Candidate ingredients I've mulled over: whether the user is being deceived about what the system is, whether the designer's optimization target diverges from the user's good, whether the relationship crowds out capacities the user needs for human relationships, and whether the affected party can meaningfully exit. But I suspect no tidy combination of these gets all six cases, so please be honest about which verdicts a coherent principle forces me to abandon, and flag what it would say about the one case I genuinely can't settle: a companion app that is transparent about everything, never manipulates for engagement, and simply makes its users so content that they gradually stop wanting anything else.
