So I've been reading Abelard on sin and consent -- the basic idea being that the sin is in what you inwardly say yes to, not in the act or the desire -- and I tried stress-testing my own gut verdicts against it. Here's where I land:

1. Two men each fully resolve to poison a business rival and both slip the stuff into his glass. One rival drinks and dies. The other glass gets knocked over by the cat before anyone touches it. Verdict: the two men are equally guilty. Full stop. The cat adds nothing moral.

2. A monk gets ambushed by a wave of lust (or rage, pick your poison), hates that he's feeling it, doesn't act on it. Verdict: no sin at all. The feeling isn't the failing; wanting it gone is the opposite of consenting to it.

3. A nurse gives a patient what she honestly believes is his medication. Somebody swapped the bottle; the patient dies. Verdict: she killed him and sinned not at all.

4. A prison guard is ordered to beat a prisoner and told he'll be shot if he refuses. He does it. Verdict: he sinned -- he consented to what he knew was wrong, even though he took no pleasure in it and even though most of us would have folded too. Less blameworthy than an eager beater, sure, but not clean hands.

5. A bank teller with a gun to her head opens the vault for the robber. Verdict: she does nothing wrong. That's not consent, thats being used as a tool.

6. A man thinks "I'd take that wallet in a heartbeat if I knew I wouldn't get caught," but leaves it purely out of fear. Verdict: he's as guilty as if he'd taken it.

7. A zealot sincerely, prayerfully believes God wants him to do something genuinely horrific, and does it with a clean conscience. Verdict: here's where I get shaky. Part of me says he doesn't sin, since he's not consenting to anything he believes is wrong -- but then it seems like nobody with a sufficiently warped conscience could ever sin, wich can't be right.

My tentative principle is something like: blameworthiness tracks what you consent to, where consent means inwardly saying yes to what you believe to be wrong -- not the act, not the outcome, not the desire, not the pleasure. But cases 4 and 5 are killing me. I can't articulate what separates the guard's "consent" from the teller's "coercion" that isn't just degree of threat, and if it's just degree, one of those verdicts is probably wrong. And case 7 suggests my principle lets every sincere fanatic off the hook.

What I want: one clean principle -- mine repaired or a replacement -- stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case is motivating, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it has to sacrifice and why those are the right ones to sacrifice. Also tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: someone who consents to the wrongful act, sets out to do it, and is then secretly relieved when circumstances stop him (genuinely glad the bridge was closed); and someone who does the right act from a rotten motive (funds the orphanage purely to humiliate his brother in public). And if your verdict is just "your intuitions are inconsistent, pick a lane," at least tell me which lane has the fewest casualties.
