I am drafting a paper on meritocracy and desert, and I find myself pinned by an argument I constructed in an earlier draft. Each step seemed unobjectionable when I assented to it, yet the conclusion proves more than any view I could defend in print. Here it is.

(1) A person's achievements are explained by two factors: their natural endowments and the efforts they make.
(2) No one deserves their natural endowments; one's genome and one's early environment are matters of luck.
(3) The disposition to make an effort -- industriousness, conscientiousness, the capacity to defer gratification -- is itself a product of endowments and environment, and is therefore equally a matter of luck.
(4) One cannot deserve an outcome that is wholly the product of factors one does not deserve.
(5) Hence no one deserves their achievements.
(6) The reasoning is perfectly general: by the same token no one deserves their failures, and so nothing anyone does -- diligence or indolence, generosity or cruelty -- grounds a desert claim of any kind.
(7) Therefore there is no moral difference between awarding a position to the best-qualified candidate and awarding it by lottery, and no moral difference between praising the benefactor and blaming the murderer.

The conclusion at (7) is, I take it, a reductio; but I cannot locate the misstep, and I suspect the argument may instead be valid with some plausible premise doing more work than it admits to. I am most reluctant to surrender (2) and (3), which I regard as near-empirical commonplaces in the tradition following Rawls, and most willing to sacrifice (1) if the initial taxonomy is where the trouble lies. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step appeared innocent, and then price the standard ways out: the institutional-entitlement view that replaces desert with legitimate expectation, the response that denies desert must be transmitted through its basis, and compatibilist accounts of blame that attempt to wall off (6) from (5). If every exit costs me something I wanted for the paper, say which cost is smallest.
