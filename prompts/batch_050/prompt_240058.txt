After engineers at a software firm began gaming the metrics used for promotion -- writing verbose code when lines-of-code counted, splitting trivial tickets when closure counts did -- the firm adopted a policy of deliberate measurement opacity: evaluation criteria are rotated every cycle, some data are collected but never disclosed, and final decisions rest on holistic managerial judgment rather than any published score. A peer firm, facing the same problem, went the opposite way: it multiplied metrics into a balanced scorecard, on the theory that no one can game twenty measures at once without doing the real work. What view of the relation between a metric and the good it proxies does each practice presuppose? Does the opacity practice assume that the underlying good is stable and knowable independently of measurement -- and if so, why should undisclosed managerial judgment track it better than a disclosed number? Does the scorecard practice assume the good is decomposable into parts, each separately measurable? Say where each practice is philosophically underdetermined -- compatible with several accounts of what measurement does to the measured -- and what each would have to change if the good in question were partly constituted, rather than merely distorted, by the way it is measured.
