Two colleagues of mine fell out over lunch about generics — statements like "ducks lay eggs" — and I said I'd put it to someone level-headed. Priya's position is that a generic is roughly a quantified claim about most of the *normal* members of a kind. On her view, "ducks lay eggs" is true because we read it as quantifying over normal adult female ducks; restricting the domain is just how such sentences work. Accordingly, she says "ticks carry Lyme disease" is false, since only a small percentage of ticks carry it, and normal ones don't. Marcus replied that frequency can't be the criterion at all: "mosquitoes carry malaria" is plainly true even though the vast majority of mosquitoes carry nothing of the sort, while "paperback books outsell hardbacks" being true of most books sold wouldn't make "books are paperbacks" an acceptable generic. Priya conceded the malaria point but argued it only shows that *striking* properties — dangerous, distinctive ones — lower the threshold, whereas for boring properties frequency still governs: "gardeners are left-handed" is false at twelve per cent even though Lyme disease is true at two. Marcus countered that nothing about laying eggs is striking, so her hybrid theory still can't handle the duck case; and her restriction to females is circular, since the same trick would license "ducks are female". Priya said restricting the domain isn't circular when the restriction is to biologically normal cases; Marcus said "normal" is doing all the work and explains nothing. They left it there. Who is right about which specific point — the status of the tick sentence, the mosquito–paperback asymmetry, whether domain restriction is legitimate or question-begging — and is there anything both are missing? Please adjudicate each point separately, and if you think the dispute rests on a confusion about what generics are doing, say so plainly rather than splitting the difference politely.
