I’ve read enough LessWrong and cognitive-science-adjacent blogs to be suspicious when two smart camps keep generating evidence without the disagreement visibly shrinking. The Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn mental imagery debate feels like that.

As I understand it, the imagery side points to things like mental scanning times, rotation results, interference between imagery and perception, and neural overlap between seeing and imagining. The propositional side replies that none of this shows the brain literally uses a picture-like representation: subjects might use tacit knowledge about vision, reconstruct the answer from descriptions, respond to task demands, or implement the relevant geometry in some non-pictorial neural code. Brain activation can show that similar machinery is involved without showing that the stored format is pictorial.

I’m less interested in who wins than in what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it:

- a factual dispute about the brain’s representational format;
- a dispute about what would count as evidence for a format, as opposed to evidence for content or processing;
- a level-of-analysis confusion between phenomenology, computation, algorithm, and neural implementation;
- a verbal dispute because terms like pictorial, analog, spatial, and propositional are doing too much work;
- or an unfalsifiable architecture debate where any result can be absorbed by adding tacit knowledge on one side or by weakening picture-like into quasi-pictorial on the other?

Please diagnose the impasse rather than giving me a standard textbook survey. Seperate the strongest claims each side needs from the stronger slogans they sometimes sound like they’re defending. In particular, distinguish at least these questions: whether imagery has a distinctive conscious character, whether it represents spatial relations directly, whether behavior is best explained by an analog process, whether the underlying data structure resembles an array, and whether the neural implementation has topographic organization. Which of those can the classic behavioral evidence actually bear on, and which require a different kind of argument?

I’d also like a gears-level account of what new evidence could move the debate under your diagnosis. Would better neural decoding settle it, or merely relocate the argument? Would an experiment showing isomorphic transformations settle format, or still be compatible with a sufficiently weird propositional system? Is there a formal way to distinguish a representation from a simulation of that representation? If part of the disagreement is basically impossible to resolve empirically, say so plainly rather than pretending one more neuroimaging result will do it.

If your diagnosis is that the camps are answering different questions, identify the question each is really answering and which question matters most for cognitive science. If it’s a genuine empirical dispute, state the most plausible crux as concretely as possible. No citations or bibliography needed.

End with a final line beginning Summary: followed by one sentence naming the real nature of the disagreement and the most plausible route to progress.
