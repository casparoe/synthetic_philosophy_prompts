After a career in experimental physics, I have been reading epistemology in retirement, and I have settled on a position about the Meno problem that I would like stress-tested. The position is this: knowledge is better than mere true belief only because it is more durable. A true belief acquired by luck is untethered — much as the Meno's analogy of the wandering statues suggests, it will be shaken loose by the next change of circumstance or the first piece of counter-evidence — whereas knowledge is fastened to the facts by justification and will survive inquiry. On my view, the entire value gap between knowledge and true belief reduces to this difference in stability; there is no further intrinsic or distinctly epistemic value in knowledge as such. What would count against this? Some candidate defeaters I can see myself: a true belief that happens to be perfectly stable despite being unjustified, say an unshakable conviction acquired by accident — if stability is the whole story, it should be as valuable as knowledge, and I am unsure whether that is a defeater or a bullet worth biting; our preference for knowledge even in questions we will never revisit, where durability seems irrelevant; and the thought that knowing involves an achievement attributable to the believer, whereas lucky truth does not, which feels like a value not captured by my account. Please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, state which of them a committed stability theorist would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as trading on intuition alone, and assess honestly whether the position has an insulating move available — reclassifying any stable-but-unjustified belief as not genuinely stable after all — and if so, whether the availability of that move is a defect in the position or a legitimate refinement of it.
