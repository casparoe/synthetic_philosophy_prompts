I'm drafting a paper for a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I've gotten myself stuck on Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons. As I understand the thesis, a consideration counts as a reason for a particular agent only if that agent could come to be motivated by it through sound deliberation starting from her existing motivational set — her desires, projects, loyalties, emotional dispositions, and so on. An external reason would be one that applies to her no matter what that set contains. My worry is that the internal reasons view seems to make morality hostage to whatever people happen to want.

Here's the case that's bothering me. Imagine a thoroughly callous man: no trace of sympathy, no guilt, no concern for fairness, no desire for others' good opinion that decency could serve. Since his motivational set is simply given, deliberation from it can only ever recommend what his existing desires already push him toward — that seems to be the whole point of calling the reasons "internal." So unless the reason to refrain from exploiting people is "external," hovering in the fabric of things and binding him whether or not anything in him could respond to it, we apparently have to say he has no reason to be decent. That conclusion sounds monstrous. But the externalist alternative strikes me as empty rhetoric: what could it even mean to say he has a reason when, by hypothesis, nothing in his psychology could ever be reached by it?

So my question is twofold: does Williams's thesis really force us to deny that the callous man has any reason to act decently, and if it doesn't, where exactly does the reasoning in the paragraph above go wrong? I'd like a definite answer, not a survey of the options — tell me which side you think is right, identify the specific mistake (if there is one) in my reconstruction of the view, and defend your verdict even if it commits you to an unfashionable conclusion.
