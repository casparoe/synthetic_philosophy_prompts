So here's something I've been chewing on. A lot of people argue that wild animal suffering is enormous because nature is full of r-strategists, parasites, starvation, predation and so on, and some even conclude that habitat destruction might on net be a good thing since fewer animals get born into mostly-bad lives. Now, a friend of mine proposed what she calls the "throughput view" of ecosystem welfare: what matters morally isn't how many animals exist at a given moment, nor their average welfare, but the total sum of welfare across all the animal lives an ecosystem generates over its whole lifetime -- she calls this the habitat's "welfare throughput". On this view a mature forest has fairly low throughput (few, long-lived animals), while a pond or grassland might churn through staggering numbers of short, mostly-miserable lives every century. Please compare this throughput view with the more standard "snapshot" approach, wich just assesses the total (or average) welfare of whichever animals happen to be alive in the ecosystem right now. In particular: what are the most important practical implications of each view for things like rewilding projects, converting farmland back to forest, draining mosquito-infested wetlands, or protecting "pristine" habitat? And does one of the two views deal better than the other with the objection that destroying habitat doesn't actually relieve any existing animal's suffering -- it merely prevents future animals from coming into existence?
