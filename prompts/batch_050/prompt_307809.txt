You are an experienced examiner in formal epistemology who has set comprehensive exams for a decade and has a well-earned reputation for questions that cannot be answered by reciting the literature. Think carefully before you draft: begin by brainstorming (internally, not in your output) where the real intellectual cruxes of the problem of old evidence lie -- the places where a candidate who has merely memorized Glymour, Garber, and Earman will fall apart, while a candidate who understands what the problem actually is will shine -- and then build the question around the crux you judge most discriminating.

I am on the committee setting this year's PhD comprehensive exam in epistemology and philosophy of science. Draft one essay question on the problem of old evidence, answerable in 75 minutes, aimed at testing whether candidates can do more than summarize the standard dialectic: old evidence seems to confirm (the perihelion of Mercury and general relativity being the usual exhibit), but if the evidence is already known then the rational agent's credence in it is 1, and conditionalizing on a proposition with credence 1 leaves credences unchanged, so standard Bayesianism seems to rule the confirmation out.

Specifically, I want a question built around the following tension, which I think separates real understanding from recitation: some writers treat the problem as showing that Bayesian confirmation theory needs a patch (a counterfactual credence function representing what the agent would have believed before learning the evidence, or a formalism for "learning" logical relations between theory and evidence rather than new empirical facts), while others argue the problem is an artifact of the idealizations themselves -- logical omniscience and the diachronic rule of conditionalization -- and that once you see which idealization is doing the work, there is nothing left to solve because the evidence already did its confirming work when it was first learned. The question should force candidates to take and defend a position on which diagnosis is right, and to spell out what each side costs: in particular, what the "the evidence already confirmed when learned" reply implies about cases where the *relevance* of old data to a theory is only discovered later, and what the counterfactual-credence patch requires an examiner to say about whose counterfactual credences are at issue and how they are constrained.

The question must also include one twist that prevents a prepared answer from being ported straight onto the page: ask candidates to consider a case in which a theorist constructs a theory specifically to accommodate data she already possesses, and to say whether, on their preferred resolution, that data confirms the theory more, less, or exactly as much as it would have had the theory come first -- and what that verdict implies for the venerable intuition that successful novel predictions confirm more strongly than mere accommodations.

Deliver, alongside the question itself:

1. A model answer of the kind a genuinely excellent candidate could produce in 75 minutes (outline form is fine, but the key argumentative moves must be explicit, not gestured at).
2. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20) and, for each point, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong.
3. A short paragraph explaining what distinguishes a first-class answer from a merely competent pass on this question -- the move or insight that the marking scheme cannot fully capture in checklist form.

Keep the question self-contained: a strong candidate should need no knowledge beyond the standard literature on the problem of old evidence and the prediction-versus-accommodation debate, and should not need to recall any specific passage or result by chapter and verse. Avoid questions that can be gamed by name-dropping; if your draft question can be answered adequately by listing who said what, revise it until it cannot.
