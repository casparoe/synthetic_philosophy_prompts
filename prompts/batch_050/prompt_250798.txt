Explain a worry I keep running into with mental causation, and tell me where it actually bites.

Setup: suppose physical closure is true enough for neuroscience, every token mental event m has a full physical base p in the brain/body/environment, and higher-level facts supervene on lower-level facts. Now add three extra assumptions people sometimes smuggle in separately: causes have to make a difference to their effects; difference-making is understood interventionistically; and a legitimate intervention on a variable has to change that variable without independently fixing the effect by some back-door route.

Here is the in-the-weeds part. Invent a distinction between two kinds of macro-variable. Call a variable screen-real if an ideal intervention on it can be specified in macro terms while leaving open which exact micro-realizer occurs, like turning a thermostat setpoint from 20 to 22 without caring which molecules do what. Call a variable shadow-real if the only way to wiggle it is to wiggle one particular microphysical realizer, in which case the macro intervention is really just the micro intervention wearing a coat. The claim is that folk-psychological variables like belief, desire, intention, regret, and deciding are screen-real in ordinary talk but shadow-real in the metaphysics room once closure plus supervenience are enforced.

Please explain, without giving me a bibliography, how this distinction changes the causal exclusion problem. In particular: does the usual response that mental and physical causes are not competitors because they belong to different levels actually help if interventions on mental variables are shadow-real? Does saying the mental cause is more proportional or more stable than the neural cause rescue it, or merely redescribe why the micro cause is good enough? Where exactly does overdetermination stop being benign and start becoming epiphenomenalism with better PR?

Use a concrete case if useful: Ana decides to text her ex; some corticothalamic pattern realizes the decision; the thumb moves. But dont just solve it for Ana. I want the general fault line: which assumption should a defender of robust mental causation reject, which assumption should a reductionist reject, and why is it harder than it looks to reject causal inheritance from realizer to realized without making realization itself spooky? Also say whether the problem gets better, worse, or merely more embarrassing if we move from type physicalism to functionalism, and from neurons to dynamical-systems variables over brain-body-environment.
