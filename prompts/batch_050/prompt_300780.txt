I am writing an essay on moral exemplarism and would like a reading of Miloš Forman's Amadeus (1984) as a test case for Plutarch's program of moral biography and its limits. That program, as sketched in the prologues to the Lives, rests on several claims: that Plutarch writes lives rather than histories; that character is disclosed as much in small gestures and passing remarks as in celebrated deeds; that paired lives allow the reader to weigh two characters against one another; that contemplating noble lives works on the beholder like a mirror, producing an impulse toward imitation; and that honest biography must record faults, since an airbrushed portrait is hagiography and instructs no one. Amadeus stresses nearly every one of these premises. It is structured as a synkrisis, yet the comparison corrupts the comparer: Salieri's lifelong contemplation of Mozart does not improve him but is the very engine of his ruin, suggesting that exposure to excellence may teach envy rather than virtue. It grants its subject flawless excellence in one domain coexisting with vulgarity and fecklessness in others, pressing the question whether character and achievement can come apart as cleanly as the film insists, and whether Plutarch's confidence that greatness of soul betrays itself in trifles survives that separation. It is narrated by the subject's enemy, a self-declared unreliable biographer who in the Requiem dictation scene becomes the amanuensis of the man he is destroying. And its closing gesture canonizes mediocrity itself as morally instructive, a category Plutarch's project excludes. Which scenes engage Plutarch's premises most directly, what does the film understand about moral learning from lives that philosophical treatments of exemplarism miss, and where does the film resist being turned into an argument about biography at all?
