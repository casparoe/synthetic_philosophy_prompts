The question on the paper was this. It was set by my friend, not me:

---
Question 4. In chapter 13 of Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy, Russell discusses the contradiction about the class of all classes that are not members of themselves, and argues that the lesson is not merely that some particular class fails to exist, but that no statement about ALL the members of a certain totality can be allowed to create new members of that same totality -- otherwise the totality was never really all of them. In your own words: (a) reconstruct how the contradiction arises from the naive principle that every condition determines a class; (b) explain how Russell's theory of types is supposed to block it; and (c) say whether, in your view, the theory of types diagnoses a genuine mistake in reasoning or simply legislates the paradox out of existence. Defend your answer.

And here is one of the answers, typed up verbatim (the student wrote it by hand but I've transcribed):

---
(a) Naive set theory says that for any property you can name, there is a class containing exactly the things with that property. Take the property of not being a member of yourself. Most classes have this property -- the class of cats is not itself a cat, so it isn't a member of itself. Now form the class R of all such classes. Ask whether R is a member of itself. If it is, then it has the defining property, which is not being a member of itself -- contradiction. If it isn't, then it does have the defining property, so it is a member of itself -- contradiction again. Either way we get a contradiction, so the naive principle has to go.

(b) Russell's answer is the theory of types. Classes are arranged in a hierarchy: individuals at the bottom, classes of individuals above that, classes of classes above that, and so on. A class can only draw its members from the level directly below it, which means that a statement like "R is a member of R" comes out false -- R sits at one level and can only contain things from the level beneath, so it doesn't contain itself, and neither does anything else. Self-membership is ruled out, so the paradoxical class never gets formed.

(c) I think the theory of types diagnoses a genuine mistake. The barber paradox has exactly the same structure -- the barber shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves -- and there the conclusion is simply that no such barber exists. Russell's paradox shows the same thing: no such class R exists, and type theory explains why, by showing that the description of R smuggles in an illegitimate self-reference. So the theory is not just legislation; it identifies the vicious circle that both paradoxes share and bans it.
---
