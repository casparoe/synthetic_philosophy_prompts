Please take on the role of a philosopher of law with a strong background in law and economics, and approach this the way you'd approach a genuinely hard classification problem: think carefully, brainstorm candidate concepts first, and only then settle on a verdict. I run a small business, I read legal and moral philosophy as a hobby, and last weekend I got into a long, unresolved argument with an old friend from law school. It started with three cases we both agree on individually but can't reconcile. Case one: a man discovers an embarrassing but true fact about his neighbor and offers, for ten thousand dollars, to keep it to himself. Everyone agrees this is a serious crime. Case two: a company files a trademark application and discovers that a stranger who monitors the filings registered the obvious matching domain name an hour later and now offers to sell it for fifty times what it cost him. My friend calls this sharp practice but plain commerce. Case three: an investor quietly accumulates a stake in a firm, drops loud hints about a hostile takeover, and accepts a premium from the firm's own management to sell the stake back and disappear. This happens in the open, is reported approvingly in the business pages, and nobody goes to jail.

Here's what struck me at two in the morning, when we'd stopped shouting and started drawing diagrams on napkins: the underlying shape of all three transactions seems identical. Someone with no independent interest in a position acquires it, or finds themselves in it, precisely because another party will pay to have it neutralized. The "product" being sold is nothing but the seller's own restraint — the seller walks away, and the buyer ends up exactly where they started, minus the payment. Nothing is built, delivered, or improved. Yet we call the first instance a felony, the second a nuisance, and the third a Tuesday. And here's the twist that broke my friend's best theory: in the two legal cases the leverage was manufactured deliberately, in cold blood, with the sale planned in advance, while in the criminal case the man merely stumbled into information he never sought. So "deliberately creating leverage for the purpose of selling it back" cannot be the feature that makes the practice wrong — if anything, it seems to make it more respectable.

So my question is twofold. First: is there an established name for this general category of transaction — the sale of one's own forbearance, where the thing refrained from is something one had a perfect right to do, and where the buyer's payment purchases only a return to the status quo? I know the individual species have names in some corners of the law, but I'm asking about the genus. Second, and more important: is there a named principle or feature that is supposed to sort the legitimate instances from the criminal ones, given that deliberate manufacture of leverage fails as a candidate? Please don't just hand me a label. Tell me how the general category, whatever it's called, relates to and differs from extortion, from ordinary bargaining, from rent-seeking, and from a standard hold-up; tell me which proposed criteria in the vicinity actually survive my three cases. And if the genus genuinely has no established name, say so plainly, coin a term, define it precisely, and give me the boundary cases — what minimal change to each of my three scenarios would push it across the line in either direction.
