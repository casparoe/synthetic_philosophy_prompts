I'm a TA for an intro philosophy of mind course and I also run a little reading group on the side, and last week one of my sharper undergrads pulled a move on the Chinese Room argument that I haven't been able to cleanly block. Everyone knows Searle's setup: the guy in the room shuffling Chinese symbols according to a rulebook, producing flawless answers without understanding a word of Chinese, and the moral that running a program — mere formal symbol manipulation — can never by itself amount to understanding. My student said fine, let's grant all of that, and then argued that the exact same reasoning goes through for brains. She wrote it up like this: (1) A system understands something only if what's going on inside it isn't merely formal symbol-pushing — the symbols have to actually mean something to the system, not just be marks shoved around by rules. (She says this is Searle's core insight, and I'm inclined to just give it to her.) (2) A digital computer, however fancy its program, only ever does formal symbol-pushing. (3) So no computer understands anything just in virtue of running a program. (4) But a brain, at the level where the causal work actually gets done, is in the same boat: neurons fire according to electrochemical laws, ions cross membranes, and no individual neuron means anything by firing. You could instantiate the same pattern of state transitions in water pipes, or in a swarm of people passing notes, and nothing at that level of description would change. (5) If purely formal operations can't add up to understanding in the computer case, they can't in the brain case either — the causal topology is the same kind of thing whether it's implemented in silicon or carbon, and the physical processes don't know or care what substrate they're running on. (6) So brains don't understand anything either. (7) But my brain obviously understands things — I'm understanding this sentence right now. (8) Therefore the Chinese Room argument proves too much: whatever kills machine understanding kills brain understanding, and the only escape hatch is claiming that biological tissue has some special causal power to produce meaning, which looks like special pleading for carbon.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I've read the standard replies to Searle — the systems reply, the robot reply, the brain simulator one — but those are all defenses *against* the argument, whereas she claims she's just taking Searle's own premises and walking them forward one more step, so hitting her with the systems reply feels like changing the subject. My instinct is that something slippery happens around (4)–(5), specifically the slide from "you can *describe* the brain's operations formally" to "the brain *only does* formal operations," but I also have a nagging feeling that Searle himself leans on exactly the biological-causal-powers move she mocks in (8), in which case maybe the argument is just sound and the Chinese Room really does stand or fall on a thesis about neurons that nobody has ever cashed out. So tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked fine on first pass, and what rejecting it costs. And if your verdict is that the argument is valid and the weak point is a premise I'd rather keep — for the record, I'm most attached to (1)–(3) and to (7), and I'd sacrifice (4) before any of those — then I'd like to hear what the causal-powers-of-brain-stuff thesis would actually have to say for itself to be more than magic carbon.
