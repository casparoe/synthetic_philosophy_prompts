I write the interpretive text for a mid-sized history museum, which means I constantly have to compress centuries into a few wall panels. I've noticed my team keeps telling every story as though it were aimed at the present: struggles culminating in today's rights, past science as a string of errors slowly corrected toward what we now know, dead ends mentioned only as things happily overcome. I've been using a rule of thumb I call the rewind test: before publishing, ask whether the people inside the story, if told where events were heading, would have recognized that endpoint as success. If most would, I let the forward-marching narrative stand; if not, I rewrite it so the past is presented on its own terms. I suspect the test is too crude, but I don't know exactly where. Please turn it into a genuine procedure my team can run in under an hour per exhibit: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, how to distinguish a narrative that is merely convenient from one that is genuinely justified because something really did improve, and the points where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a curator's judgment. Then run the finished procedure on three exhibits I have in development: one on the history of surgical anesthesia, one on the decline of witch trials in Europe, and one on the expansion of voting rights in my country. At least one of the three should come out with a verdict I won't enjoy defending at the next team meeting, and tell me what the procedure cannot settle no matter how well it's followed.
