I teach a combined history-and-philosophy capstone seminar (about 14 students, advanced undergraduates, one 75-minute session), and I've run into a recurring frustration: whenever we discuss why some event happened — the outbreak of the First World War is our running case — my students argue as though there were a single correct answer competing with rivals. Was it the assassination, the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, German strategic culture? They treat causal explanations like candidates in an election, and my attempts to explain overdetermination and multi-causal explanation on the whiteboard haven't broken the habit. I'd like you to design a structured activity that breaks it experientially: something where the students themselves are forced to apportion causal responsibility among several well-known factors, and then discover, through the rules of the exercise rather than through my lecturing, why the apportionment frame itself collapses when each factor is sufficient in the circumstances and none is individually necessary.

Please specify the full activity: the materials (e.g., cause cards or whatever you think works), the rules and any roles, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and the mechanism by which students are made to run into the problem — for instance, some structured procedure in which they must first commit to numerical weights and are then made to test those weights against counterfactuals (removing one cause and asking whether the war still happens), so that they feel the incoherence of their own accounting rather than having it pointed out. I'd also like a debrief plan that maps what happened in the room onto the relevant distinctions — overdetermination versus preemption, necessary versus sufficient conditions, difference-making versus contribution, and the historian's practice of "weighting" causes — so I can connect their frustration to the conceptual vocabulary afterward without lecturing for more than ten minutes.

Finally, two honest addenda. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where students will game the rules, where they'll conflate causal contribution with moral or legal blame, and how I should respond in the moment rather than after the fact. Second, tell me frankly which feature of genuine historical overdetermination the activity cannot reproduce — my suspicion is that any card-based game treats the causal factors as fixed and discrete, whereas real historiography involves contested evidence and causes that individuate differently depending on how finely you describe them — and what I need to supply myself, in discussion or in a follow-up session, to close that gap.
