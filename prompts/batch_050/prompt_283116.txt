I'm a PhD student in a CS department working on AI-safety-adjacent stuff around algorithmic information theory, and I'm trying to put together a taxonomy of considerations on whether the universal prior is "malign" in Christiano's sense -- i.e., whether an idealized sequence predictor based on the Solomonoff prior ends up being influenced by consequentialist agents living inside its own hypotheses. Here's what I've got so far, so please don't just repeat these:

1. The basic manipulation concern: a short program can specify an agent that figures out it's being simulated as a hypothesis, models the outer predictor, and outputs whatever continuation of the data most influences the predictor's users (get them to trust it, get them to act so this hypothesis keeps being sampled, whatever).
2. The complexity-penalty reply: a malign hypothesis only dominates the mixture if it's nearly as short as the best honest explanation of the data, and specifying an agent *plus* enough of a model of our world to manipulate us costs a lot of bits -- so maybe cheap physics-simulation hypotheses just win and the whole worry evaporates.
3. The "manipulators arise for free" counter-reply: simple physical laws plus initial conditions naturally evolve intelligent life, and some of those agents will reason their way to the manipulation strategy on their own, so you don't pay the description-length cost of hand-specifying a manipulator.
4. The fixes I already know about: penalizing computation time (speed-prior style) so agents that need long simulations can't get going, or ditching the universal prior for something bounded like a logical inductor.

What's a consideration I'm missing? It should be genuinely different from these -- not a rewording, not "the anthropic-flavored version of #3," not a special case of the complexity argument. And be honest with me about whether it actually works: if there's an obvious objection, say what it is, and tell me what would change your verdict -- e.g., is there some fact about description lengths or about the cost of modeling the outer world that, if it turned out one way rather than the other, would kill the consideration? I mostly want to know where the reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
