So I cover science and behavioral economics for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why people trust some predictions and shrug at others -- polls, weather models, their brother-in-law's stock tips. Of course I fell down the Ellsberg paradox rabbit hole, and every single explainer on earth uses the same two urns with the red and black balls. My readers will fall asleep. I want to illustrate the actual conceptual fault line with a scenario people might actually live through.

Here's the build I need. Construct a pair of decision scenarios with these specs: (1) realistic, everyday settings -- no urns, no balls, no lab gambles with experimenters, and stakes a normal person could plausibly face, like a few hundred dollars or a work decision; (2) the two scenarios must be structurally identical in their probability and ambiguity profile -- in both, the decision-maker's own best estimate of the uncertain event is the same (say around 60%), and the matched known-risk alternative is the same fixed-odds option (a genuine 60% chance, like a roulette-style device or a coin-flip chain they can verify); (3) the ONLY thing that differs between the two scenarios is the decision-maker's familiarity with the domain -- in scenario A they're judging something deep in their wheelhouse, in scenario B something they know absolutely nothing about, even though their raw evidence is equally thin in both. The target behavior: in one scenario the decision-maker picks the known-risk option over their own judgment (classic Ellsberg move), but in the other they actually prefer betting on their own judgment over the verified 60% chance -- ambiguity seeking, not aversion.

Then do the verification work. First, pick one formal model of ambiguity aversion (maxmin expected utility or the smooth ambiguity model, your call, but commit) and show it predicts the SAME choice in both scenarios, since the ambiguity structure is stipulated identical -- meaning the flip can't be explained by ambiguity attitude alone. Second, show how a competence- or confidence-based account (the Heath-and-Tversky style idea that people prefer to bet on their judgment where they feel knowledgeable) does generate the flip. Third, convince me spec (2) really holds -- that I haven't smuggled in extra information in the "competent" scenario that a Bayesian could legitimately use. Finally, give me the strongest counterattack from the ambiguity-aversion side: presumably something like "feeling competent just means perceiving less ambiguity, so the scenarios were never structurally identical to begin with." Tell me whether that objection defeats the construction or just redescribes it, and what it would take to settle the question empirically. A short paragraph per scenario is fine, and don't make the decision-maker a pro gambler or a stats professor -- I want someone my readers recognize.
