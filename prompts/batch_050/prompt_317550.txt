I had a long argument with a friend last night about classical Chinese ethics, and we ended up stuck. The disagreement was over Mozi's doctrine of impartial care versus the Confucian ideal of graded love — concern that is strongest toward one's family and diminishes as one moves outward toward strangers. My friend, who reads the primary texts, kept insisting that the two positions differ in ways that cannot be captured by how much weight each assigns to different people's interests. I work in public policy and am fluent in the vocabulary of social welfare functions, distributional weights, and discounting over social distance, and I suspect the difference really does come down to something like the shape of a weighting function.

Settle this. Restate both positions in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: Mozi's impartial care, including his claim that partiality is the root of social harm and his argument that caring for others' families is the most reliable way to secure care for one's own, as a social welfare function or decision rule; and the Confucian position, graded love rooted in familial affection and extended outward through ritual and role relationships, in the same terms. Then give me the residue report: which commitments of each position translate cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in welfare-function vocabulary at all. Conclude with a verdict on whether the residue shows my framework is missing something my friend can see, or whether it dissolves once you notice it concerns something else — motivation, institutions, or the formation of preferences rather than their aggregation.

After the analysis, summarize it in a table with these columns: Element (the specific claim or commitment); Position (Mozi, Confucius, or both); Translation (its rendering in welfare-economics terms, or "none"); Fidelity (clean, distorted, or lost); Notes (what was fudged or omitted).
