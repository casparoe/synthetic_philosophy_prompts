Write a villanelle about the Kelly criterion -- the staking rule that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth rather than its expected value. The two refrains should embody the two quantities at the heart of the idea: the first refrain should voice the arithmetic intuition (the average outcome across parallel bettors, the expected value of a single trial), the second the geometric intuition (the growth rate of a single bettor's wealth compounded through time, where a loss is never symmetric with a gain because what is lost must be won back from a smaller stake). Let the form do the argument's work: a villanelle's refrains return the way returns compound, so each recurrence of the second refrain should land differently -- carrying more weight, more accumulated consequence -- than it did the stanza before, while the first refrain should come to sound increasingly thin, a truth about ensembles that keeps failing the individual who repeats the bet. Use the tercets to develop the concrete mechanics: why a favorable wager can still be ruinous at full stake, why betting a fixed fraction rather than a fixed amount changes everything, and why the prudent gambler ends up wagering less than the odds invite. In the closing quatrain, where the refrains finally stand side by side, let the resolution emerge without a lecture: the two averages part company precisely where time enters, and the bettor who survives is the one who optimized for the path, not the average. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with ergodicity economics would recognize the argument, and good enough as verse that someone who isn't would still want to read it twice.
