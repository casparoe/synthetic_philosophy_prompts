Several jurisdictions have adopted what is often called the Nordic model of sex-work regulation: selling sexual services is decriminalized, but buying them is a criminal offense, and the state pairs this asymmetry with funding for exit services and with public framing of prostitution as something done to sellers rather than chosen by them. By contrast, New Zealand decriminalizes both sides and brings the industry under ordinary labor and health law, while Germany and the Netherlands license and tax brothels as businesses. I want to focus on the Nordic model's asymmetry itself -- punish the buyer, never the seller -- and ask what it presupposes philosophically.

What view of the transaction does that asymmetry only make sense under? Candidate readings include: that the seller's participation is presumed constrained enough by economic circumstance or prior victimization that attributing full criminal responsibility to her is unjust, while the buyer's is not; that purchasing sexual access is a distinctive wrong against the seller or against women as a class, independent of any particular seller's welfare; or that the state aims at abolishing the market altogether and the asymmetry is purely tactical, with no theory of the transaction behind it at all. Please reconstruct which of these -- or which combination -- the practice actually presupposes, as opposed to merely fitting well with, and identify which rival views it quietly rules out (for instance, a straightforward autonomy view on which competent adults may sell intimate services on the same footing as other stigmatized or burdensome labor).

Then test for internal consistency. The same legal systems that adopt the Nordic model generally permit other intimate or bodily markets -- commercial surrogacy is contested but paid egg and sperm donation, hazardous occupations, and deeply personal care work are legal -- and they do not elsewhere respond to exploitation in a market by criminalizing only the customer while formalizing the seller as a permanent victim-category. Does the asymmetry presuppose something about sex specifically that distinguishes it from these cases, and if so, what must that something be for the practice to be coherent rather than ad hoc? Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced: what follows for buyer-side criminalization if one adopts the autonomy view, and what follows for exit services and the victim framing if one adopts a pure harm-reduction view? Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each part of the reconstruction, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
