I'm writing a paper for a lit-and-social-science seminar and want to read Arthur Miller's The Crucible through the informational cascades / rational herding literature. Here's the reading I want pressure-tested: Salem looks like a textbook cascade. Once Tituba confesses and starts naming names, every new accusation is both privately rational and publicly informative -- each girl who falls down afflicted, each defendant who confesses adds to the pile of public evidence, and pretty soon even someone with genuine private doubts (Hale, eventually) is rationally discounting his own judgement against the accumulated testimony. And the cascade should be fragile in the way the models predict: one credible defector can unravel the whole thing, which is maybe why the court is so frantic to extract Proctor's signature at the end.

So: where does the cascade model actually illuminate the play, scene by scene, and where does Miller resist it? Some places I suspect it breaks: the girls aren't aggregating noisy signals, they're manufacturing them -- the fits are faked, so the public "evidence" was never evidence of anything, and the standard models assume the signals are honest even when misleading. Abigail and Putnam have motives (jealousy, land) that have nothing to do with wanting to be right, whereas cascade models assume agents are trying to match their action to the truth. And dissent in Salem gets you hanged, which looks less like herding and more like coerced preference falsification -- nobody's fooled, they're just scared. Are those the same mechanism or genuinely different ones, and do they make different predictions about how the thing ends (a scared public can snap back fast, a genuinely convinced one can't)?

Also do Proctor's refusal to sign the confession: in cascade terms, is he refusing to add one more durable false signal to the pile, and does the play treat the signed document as more dangerous than a spoken lie precisely because it circulates as public evidence? If my reading distorts either Miller or the models, say so plainly. Under 1000 words.
