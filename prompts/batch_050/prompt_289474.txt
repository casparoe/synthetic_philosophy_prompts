After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have recently become interested in philosophy and joined a reading group on chance and computation. Our latest discussion concerned two 128-bit sequences. Sequence A consists entirely of zeros but was, by stipulation, produced by a carefully calibrated physical generator giving independent fair bits. Sequence B looks statistically irregular but was produced by a short deterministic recurrence, so it has a compact description. One participant defended a process view: randomness belongs primarily to the source or generating procedure, so A is random despite its striking pattern and B is not, however disorderly it appears. Another defended a pattern view: randomness can also characterize a finite sequence itself, roughly insofar as it lacks an informative compressed description; thus A is highly nonrandom as a string, while B may count as random relative to observers unable to discover its rule, even though its generating process was deterministic. Each accused the other of confusing an object with evidence about its origin.

Act as a professional philosopher of probability who is also conversant with algorithmic information theory. Address the fact that algorithmic randomness for a finite string is relative to a description method and generally to a chosen complexity threshold, and explain whether this makes finite-string randomness merely conventional, usefully relative, or both.
