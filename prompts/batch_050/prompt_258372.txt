I'm putting together a unit for my upper-level philosophy of art seminar on the paradox of fiction, and this year I want to anchor it in a film instead of the usual literary examples. I've settled on the opening montage of Up -- the wordless sequence where Carl and Ellie meet as kids, marry, save for their trip to Paradise Falls, watch life keep emptying the jar, grow old, and then Ellie dies. Everytime I show it, half the room is sniffling by minute four, which is philosophically perfect and also kind of embarassing for everyone involved.

So here's what I'd like. Act as an expert philosopher of art who also knows the aesthetics-of-film literature well. Think carefully before committing -- brainstorm the candidate explanations first, then narrow down and argue. Give me a reading of the montage through the paradox of fiction: Radford's original puzzle about why we respond emotionally to characters we know perfectly well aren't real, plus the main moves since (Walton's make-believe and quasi-emotion line, the thought-theory reply that we just need to entertain thoughts rather than hold beliefs, and the older suspension-of-disbelief idea if you think it's still alive). But push on three complications I've been chewing on. First, the montage has no dialogue: if our emotion is meant to be a response to thoughts we entertain about characters, what exactly is doing the work in a scene where almost nothing is asserted and the score arguably carries the affect? Is the sadness really about Ellie, or is it a free-floating mood the music induces that the fiction merely decorates -- and if the latter, does the paradox even properly arise here? Second, when I ask students why they cried, most say something like: it made them think of their own grandparents, their own relationships, their own fear of running out of time. That's self-directed emotion, not fiction-directed. Does that dissolve the paradox (the real object was sitting in the theater all along) or merely relocate it? Third, a colleague of mine insists animation sidesteps the puzzle altogether, because stylized characters function as emotional shorthand rather than simulated persons, so nobody even pretends Ellie exists. I suspect she's wrong -- the stylization seems to me to sharpen the puzzle, since the less realistic the figure, the weirder the tears -- but please actually adjudicate rather than just agree with me.

End with a verdict: which theoretical line best handles this scene, and what, if anything, does the scene teach the debate that the usual armchair examples don't? The main deliverable should be prose, since it's going straight into my lecture notes, but after the essay give me a comparison table with exactly these columns: Theory | One-line core claim | How it explains the montage | Where it struggles with the montage | Verdict (survives / needs modification / fails). Include rows for Radford-style irrationalism, Waltonian make-believe, thought theory, and my colleague's shorthand proposal. No verbatim quotes from the film or the papers, please -- I don't have the texts in front of me either.
