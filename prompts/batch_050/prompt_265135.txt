I work in computer vision and have no training in philosophy of mind, but I keep running into references to the old debate between Stephen Kosslyn and Zenon Pylyshyn over mental imagery -- the so-called imagery debate about whether the brain stores images in a picture-like format or only in a propositional, description-like one. I would like this debate restated in the vocabulary I actually command: tensors, feature maps, topographic organization of activations, embeddings, attention windows, scene graphs, inductive biases, and the distinction between a data structure and the operations defined over it. Treat me as fully fluent in that vocabulary and as knowing nothing of the philosophical one beyond what you supply.

Concretely, I want three things. First, state each position as precisely as you can in my terms. As I understand it, Kosslyn's side rests on results like these: when subjects scan attention across a remembered map, response time grows with the depicted distance; when they mentally rotate a figure, time grows with the angle; when an imagined object is "small," its details take longer to report. Kosslyn infers that imagery lives in a quasi-pictorial medium that preserves metric spatial relations and is inspected by operations that traverse it. Pylyshyn replies that these results show only what subjects know about the tasks and the physical situations being imagined -- tacit knowledge, plus what the experimenter implicitly asks of them -- and that if beliefs and goals can alter "imagery" behavior (cognitive penetrability), the format inference fails; the underlying representation may be propositional all the way down. Translate both the theory and each of these signature results and objections into claims about architectures: what would count as a depictive representation in a network, what would count as a propositional one, and what experiments on a model would discriminate between them.

Second, give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into architecture-and-operation talk, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in how we describe learned representations? My suspicion is that the whole debate dissolves in my vocabulary: a convolutional feature map is just a big vector, spatial organization is a matter of connectivity and inductive bias rather than data type, and any network can emulate either format -- so "format" talk looks like loose talk about what is really a question of which operations are cheap. If that suspicion is right, say so and say what the two sides were actually disagreeing about once it is cashed out. If it is wrong, tell me what it misses.

Third, for whatever does not survive translation, give me your verdict on where the fault lies: is the residue a defect of my framework -- something about minds that tensor talk genuinely cannot express (I suspect phenomenology and the sense that one is inspecting an image may land here) -- or a sign that the original claims were less clear than they looked? Do not smuggle the assumptions of either side into the translation and call it neutral; where you must choose an interpretation to make the translation go through, flag the choice and say what hangs on it.
