I work at a small philanthropic foundation, and over the past year our grant evaluations have run into a strange family of problems that I suspect moral philosophers have already mapped, though I don't know the vocabulary. Two examples keep recurring. First, whenever we compare two interventions whose benefits are projected over an effectively unbounded future -- say, a program that slightly raises the quality of education in perpetuity versus one that prevents a temporary crisis -- the math seems to say both outcomes are "infinitely good," yet everyone in the room has a firm intuition that one is better, and it feels like the decision procedure, not the intuition, is broken. Second, in the other direction, whenever someone argues that an action has even a tiny probability of affecting an enormous, possibly unbounded number of future beings, that consideration seems to swallow every ordinary, near-certain, finite benefit on the table, so that the rational choice appears to be dictated entirely by the speculative long-shot and the mundane evidence stops mattering. In both cases our staff have a strong sense of what the right answer is and no way to make the arithmetic agree with it.

What I'm asking is whether these are one problem or two, and whether either has an established name. I realize the second one sounds adjacent to the old wager argument for believing in God, where an infinite payoff supposedly dominates any finite stake, and to the modern "mugging" version where a trivial probability of astronomical stakes is meant to extort you -- but the situations I describe aren't quite either, because here both options can carry infinite expected value, or one option's infinitude comes from duration rather than from a speculative magnitude, and the paralysis arises from comparing infinities rather than from a lone infinite prize. I also suspect the first problem is distinct from the well-known puzzle where adding more happy lives eventually makes a huge population of barely-worth-living lives preferable, since nothing in my examples depends on the trade-off between quality and quantity of lives. So: is there an established concept for the breakdown of comparison when both alternatives carry infinite value, and a separate one for decision-making being hijacked by any nonzero probability of unbounded stakes? Where exactly does each differ from the wager-style argument, from the population puzzle, and from simply applying a discount rate to future benefits? If one of the two genuinely has no name, propose a label, give a precise definition, and mark the boundary cases -- that is, which nearby situations the coined concept should and should not cover, and what test would tell.
