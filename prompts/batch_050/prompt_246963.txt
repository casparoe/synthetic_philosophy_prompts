I host a little philosophy night at my local library once a month. Nobody there has any training, we just pick a topic, read whatever we can find, and argue about it over bad coffee. Next month I somehow got volunteered to lead the session on illusionism about consciousness, which I'm now regretting because I picked it after an argument with my brother-in-law and I only half understand it myself. As far as I can tell the idea is something like: the "what it's like" quality of experience, the redness of red and the hurtiness of pain, isn't really there, and introspection misrepresents our brain states as having these qualitative feels when actually there's just information processing going on. The room will be about ten people: a retired nurse, two software guys, a high school art teacher, and one regular who will definately try to connect everything to quantum mechanics. I get maybe 40 minutes of talking before discussion.

Don't explain illusionism to me, plan how I should explain it to them. What's the order of ideas, and what depends on what? I suspect you can't even state the position until the room separates "being conscious" from "phenomenal consciousness" in some rough way, but maybe that's wrong, tell me. What's the one example that should carry the whole session? My instinct is a visual illusion like the two lines with arrows, but I worry that example backfires because visual illusions are themselves experiences, so it sort of assumes the thing illusionism denies. Pick the carrying example and tell me what it needs to show and what it needs to not accidentally smuggle in. Then the misconceptions: I'm expecting, roughly in this order, "so you're saying consciousness doesn't exist, which is insane," "an illusion needs someone to be fooled, so this refutes itself," "this is just behaviorism with a new hat," and "so my pain doesn't actually hurt?" Tell me which of these will actually come up and in what order, where in the sequence I should head each one off (some maybe need defusing before they even get said), and which one is actually a good objection that I should admit is a good objection rather than talk past. Finally: one question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether they understood the position as opposed to just absorbing a slogan, and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level (I'm guessing the meta-problem stuff and the specific theories of who says what, but you tell me).
