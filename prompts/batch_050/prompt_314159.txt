My younger brother is awaiting sentencing after causing a serious accident while driving drunk, and my family has split into two camps over it: my mother keeps saying that, given his childhood and everything that followed, he never really had a chance, while my uncle replies that nobody poured the drinks for him and nobody turned the key in the ignition. This argument has been running at every family gathering for months, and it is the reason I enrolled in a continuing-education seminar on free will at our local university this term. This week the instructor assigned William James's essay "The Dilemma of Determinism," and while the essay is over a century old and I find James's style difficult, I can tell he is circling exactly the question my family keeps colliding with. Three passages in particular have me stuck, and I would like help understanding them — not a summary of the essay, which I can get anywhere, but a careful unpacking of these specific moves.

The first passage comes early on, where James announces the premises of his whole approach: "The arguments I am about to urge all proceed on two suppositions: first, when we make theories about the world and discuss them with one another, we do so in order to attain a conception of things which shall give us subjective satisfaction; and, second, if there be two conceptions, and the one seems to us, on the whole, more rational than the other, we are entitled to suppose that the more rational one is the truer of the two." The second is a single sentence: "our first act of freedom, if we are free, ought in all inward propriety to be to affirm that we are free." The third is where James introduces his famous terminology: "The word 'chance' I wish to keep, but I wish to get rid of the word 'freedom.' ... Old-fashioned determinism was what we may call hard determinism. It did not shrink from such words as fatality, bondage of the will, necessitation, and the like. Nowadays, we have a soft determinism which abhors harsh words, and, repudiating fatality, necessity, and even predetermination, says that its real name is freedom; for freedom is only necessity understood…"

Here is what I cannot make sense of. (1) In the first passage, James seems to say that "rational" means something like "subjectively satisfying" rather than "supported by evidence," and that we are *entitled* to treat the more satisfying conception as the truer one. I honestly cannot tell whether this is a modest premise or an outrageous one — doesn't it license believing whatever we find comforting, including my mother's version of my brother's story? What work is this principle doing in the essay, and how much of his later case for indeterminism collapses if you refuse to grant it? (2) Is the "first act of freedom" sentence an argument, and if so, is it not blatantly circular — assuming we are free in order to affirm that we are free? Or is James making some other kind of point about what believing in freedom must be like from the inside, and about why we should not expect the question to be settled by proof? (3) Why would someone who defends free will want to *get rid of the word* freedom while keeping "chance," of all words — what does he think the word freedom has been corrupted into? And when he describes the soft determinist's view as freedom being "necessity understood," what position is he actually attributing to them? In the seminar we learned that today's compatibilists say something different — that you act freely when your action flows from your own desires and deliberation rather than from coercion, so that "could have done otherwise" means roughly "would have done otherwise had you chosen to." Is that the same target James is attacking, or a different one, and would his objection touch the modern version? (4) Finally, bring it down to my family's dinner table: on James's framework, what would have to be true for my brother to have freely driven that car — is James committed to saying that both futures, driving and not driving, were genuinely open that night? And does James leave any coherent middle ground between my uncle's "he chose it, full stop" and my mother's "he couldn't have done otherwise," or is his point precisely that there is no stable middle, only a choice about which of the two conceptions you can live with? If the answer requires explaining how James uses the notion of chance later in the essay to define what indeterminism even claims, please include that, because I suspect my confusion in (1) through (3) is really confusion about what James thinks the two sides are actually disagreeing over.
