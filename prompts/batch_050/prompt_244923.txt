So I've been in a running argument with my roommate for months, and last week it got weirdly personal. Her brother has early-onset dementia, and she was talking about how she can't stay angry at him for the cruel things he says anymore, the anger just... doesn't come, even when part of her thinks he's still in there somewhere. I'm writing a paper on Strawson (the Freedom and Resentment stuff) and this hit me right in the thesis, because the position I've been defending is basically a maximalist reading of Strawson: that there's no fact about whether someone deserves blame over and above our practices of holding each other responsible -- the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt) and when we find it appropriate to feel or suspend them are all the "moral responsibility" there is. Asking whether blame is *really* deserved, independently of what our participant stance does, is on my view like asking whether a joke is really funny apart from whether anyone finds it funny. So on my view my roommate's situation isn't a tragic epistemic problem where she can't tell whether blame is warranted; the fact that the objective attitude has replaced resentment in her just *settles* the question.

My problem is that my paper is due soon and my professor keeps writing "but what would show you you're wrong?" in the margins and I honestly can't answer her. So: what would count against this position? I don't want the generic free will preamble (determinism, gods, etc.), I want the actual exposure map. Which intuitions would have to give way -- e.g., the intuition that a slaveholder in 1700 who was fully "normalized" by his culture is still blameworthy even if no one around him, including him, felt any reactive attitudes about it? Is that a defeater for me, or do I just bite it and say our current attitudes are the tribunal? What about manipulation cases, where someone's reactive attitudes were engineered in them -- does the view have the resources to call those attitudes inappropriate without smuggling in an independent standard of desert? Does the view collapse into pure expressivism about blame, and is that a refutation or just a relabeling?

Also rank the defeaters for me, fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a committed Strawsonian would actually concede versus absorb by saying "our practices just evolved." Because that's the thing that worries me most: it feels like the view can absorb literally anything, since any counterexample can be redescribed as a case where our attitudes changed or should change. If the position is unfalsifiable in that way, is that a vice, or is it exactly what you'd expect if responsibility really is a practice rather than a fact? And what would my roommate's case actually show about the view -- does her slipping out of resentment show the view is right (the desert question evaporated with the attitude) or does the nagging feeling she still has, that maybe blame is still *owed* even unfelt, point at something the practice view can't capture?
