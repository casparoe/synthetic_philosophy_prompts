I'm putting together the materials for a graduate reading group in philosophy of biology, and we've just spent two weeks arguing past each other about whether a honeybee colony is an organism in the same sense as a bee is. Half the room kept saying "superorganism" as if that settled anything, and the other half kept pointing at the Portuguese man o' war and the lichen on the windowsill and asking where exactly the line is supposed to fall. What I realised we lack is not another definition but an instrument: something that would let each participant work out which position they actually hold, rather than which label they find appealing. Could you write me a decision tree that does this for the question of biological individuality — that is, whether entities such as holobionts (a host plus its symbiotic microbes), clonal colonies like aspen stands, siphonophores, and eusocial insect colonies count as organisms?

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. Each branch node should be a question phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — ideally a concrete case or a yes/no commitment on which the views genuinely come apart (for example: whether an entity needs a single bottlenecked reproductive origin, such as a one-cell stage, to count; whether what matters is that the parts' fitness interests are aligned and policed against cheating; whether physiological and metabolic integration is sufficient on its own; whether the immune system's self/non-self boundary settles the question; whether it matters that the entity could survive removal from its colony). The leaves should be named positions — use the standard labels where they exist (evolutionary-individual views, physiological or metabolic views, immunological views, the superorganism verdict, holobiont-as-organism views, and eliminativism about the whole question) and invent honest labels where a coherent combination of answers has no established defender, noting in a sentence why that cell is empty (incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored). Aim for between six and nine leaves, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over the gap.

After the tree itself, I'd like a compact structured summary as a table with exactly these columns: "Leaf" (the position's name), "Path" (the sequence of yes/no answers that reaches it), "Parade case" (the single entity it classifies most comfortably — e.g. honeybee colony, lichen, man o' war, human-plus-microbiome, aspen stand), "Hard case" (the entity it classifies most awkwardly), and "Most awkward commitment" (one sentence on the bullet its defender must bite). The tree is for people who have read a little philosophy of biology but have not committed to a view, so avoid jargon at the nodes where a plain question will do.
