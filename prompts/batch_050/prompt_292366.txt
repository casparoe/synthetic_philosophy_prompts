Yesterday evening I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend about money, prices, and interest, and I would like help reconstructing what was actually at stake. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in Aristotle's practical philosophy and the philosophy of economics. Work carefully and deliberately: before composing your final answer, brainstorm at least three distinct candidate principles that might underlie my verdicts below (including ones you expect to reject), and only then commit to your preferred analysis.

The background. My friend defends an uncompromising subjectivism: an exchange is just whenever both parties consent without fraud or literal force; prices are nothing over and above what buyers actually pay; money is sheer convention, a token that means whatever a community lets it mean; and the ancient and medieval condemnations of interest were superstition. At one point he quoted a passage against me — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, Chapter 5 (trans. W. D. Ross):

"And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money... it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

His reading: if demand is the standard, then whatever people happen to want and pay is the measure of value, full stop; and if money can be altered and rendered useless at will, there is no "natural" use of money that lending at interest could violate. I suspect he is misreading the passage — it says that demand is the standard, not that each person's momentary willingness to pay defines justice, and elsewhere (Politics, Book I) Aristotle distinguishes acquisition for the needs of the household from acquisition aimed at unlimited accumulation, treating the latter as a defective form of life. But I could not articulate my objection cleanly in the moment. Hence this request.

Here are my verdicts on the cases we fought over:

1. Famine bread. The only baker in a besieged town triples the price of bread; the townspeople pay because they must. My verdict: the baker wrongs them, consent notwithstanding.
2. The last umbrella. A street vendor raises umbrella prices sharply during a sudden downpour; a commuter pays gladly. My verdict: perfectly fine. (My friend triumphantly noted that 1 and 2 are structurally identical.)
3. The merchant loan. A lender charges 5% on a loan financing a merchant's trading voyage; both stand to profit if the voyage succeeds. My verdict: fine.
4. The subsistence loan. The same 5% charged to a destitute man borrowing for a week's food. My verdict: exploitative, although the rate and the consent are exactly as in case 3.
5. The barter ratio. Two farmers swap a plough for grain directly, no money involved. I feel there is a fact of the matter about whether the ratio was fair, independent of either party's subjective satisfaction: if one of them later learns that ploughs everywhere else fetch twice the grain, he was wronged, not merely unlucky.
6. Seashell money. An island community adopts shells as currency. A trader finds a beach of identical shells on another island, ships in a boatload, and quietly buys up half the island before anyone catches on. My verdict: he cheated — and the community is right afterward to repudiate the shells. Yet if money is sheer convention, as the passage's final clause seems to say, it is unclear what he did wrong before the repudiation. This is the case where my friend's reading bites hardest.
7. The career moneylender. A frugal man saving his wages in coin for twenty years: blameless. A man whose entire occupation is turning coin into more coin, even at uniformly "fair" rates: something seems to me defective in the life, though I cannot point to any individual transaction as unjust. My friend sees no difference.

What I want from you:

(a) Interpretation. Explain what Aristotle means in the quoted passage by making demand the standard that "holds everything together" while money merely represents demand by convention. Does the passage support my friend's subjectivism, or does it instead make the justice of an exchange answerable to something real — need, contribution, the maintenance of the association of exchange — that happens to be measured through a conventional unit? Take a position; do not merely survey options.

(b) The principle. State the principle — or, if one will not do, a small set of jointly applied principles — that best captures my seven verdicts. State it precisely, indicate which cases motivate each clause, and show how it handles the two pairs I find hardest to reconcile (case 1 with case 2, and case 3 with case 4).

(c) The cost. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, if any, and which single verdict you would advise me to surrender if I must surrender one. If you conclude that some of my verdicts are strictly irreconcilable and the honest upshot is that I must choose between them, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

(d) Application. Tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I did not list: surge pricing for transport out of a city under an evacuation order, and the sole employer in a one-factory town setting wages well below what comparable workers earn elsewhere.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,300 words; the Aristotle passage does not count toward this. Fidelity to Aristotle matters less to me than the precision of the principle and your candor about its costs.
