hey, so i'm a phd student in aesthetics and i'm TAing a seminar on the paradox of tragedy, and for our last meeting i want to hand out a poem as a kind of conversation piece. here's the angle i want it to take, because the standard solutions all bug me: catharsis, pleasure-in-the-craft, "it's only fiction" — every classic move explains the paradox by dissolving it, splitting the experience into a pain part and a pleasure part and reconciling them. but i think the interesting position is that there's nothing to reconcile: the very same feature of the experience is the grief and the pleasure, the way a strict form can make you want to return to the line that hurts most.

so write me a sestina where the six end-words are all grief words (loss, ache, keening — you pick, but they should stay painful throughout, no softening into "healing" or "peace"). the sestina's rotation should do the philosophical work: each stanza puts the same six wounds in new positions, and the reader should gradually start feeling the rotation itself as a satisfaction, so that by the envoi the pleasure of the pattern and the pain of the words are recognizably one event, not two that got traded. someone who knows the paradox literature should be able to tell which position the poem is staking out without it ever being named, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem about grief.
