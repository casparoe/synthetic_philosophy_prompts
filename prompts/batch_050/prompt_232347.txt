so i've been teaching a little intro unit on medieval philosophy and i keep coming back to Boethius's solution to the foreknowledge problem in the Consolation, and honestly i've half-converted to it. my position, roughly: the problem only looks fatal if you picture God as someone standing in time, looking ahead and seeing the future before it happens. but if God is eternal in Boethius's sense -- possessing the whole of illimitable life all at once, not just persisting through endless time -- then God doesn't *fore*know anything. he just knows, the way i know what i'm watching happen right now, and my watching the game doesn't cause the goals. so divine knowledge of what i'll do tomorrow no more constrains me than your watching me type this sentence constrains my typing. add in the modal point that follows from this: from "God knows i'll do X" you only get that X is necessary conditionally (given that God knows it), not necessary in itself, and conflating those two is the fallacy that generates the whole puzzle. free will survives.

so: what would count against this? i don't want the generic "well some people prefer open theism" stuff. i want to know where the position actually has exposure. a few things i'm specifically unsure about:

1. does the eternity move secretly give up something big elsewhere -- like, does a God outside time turn out unable to know tensed facts at all (what time it is *now*, what's happening *today*), and is that a cost the view can absorb or a refutation?
2. the "knowing is like watching" analogy: if God's relation to my future acts is really like my relation to present events, doesn't that make my future acts *already fixed* relative to God in exactly the way the timeless move was supposed to avoid? is there a real difference between "fixed because foreknown" and "fixed because timelessly known," or is that a distinction without a difference?
3. the simple/conditional necessity thing -- i've seen people argue Boethius (or Aquinas after him) just gets the modal logic wrong, or at least that the distinction doesn't do what's needed. how serious is that objection?

rank the defeaters from "this would sink it" to "awkward but survivable," and for each say whether a defender of the Boethian line would concede it counts against them or would wave it off. also -- and this matters to me since i'm teaching it -- be careful to separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested. like, if the "eternity dissolves the problem" reading is itself a contested interpretation of the text rather than the consensus, tell me that straight, and don't present any particular resolution as the settled view if the field is actually split. i'd rather tell my students "nobody agrees" than repeat a tidy story that's really one camp's story.
