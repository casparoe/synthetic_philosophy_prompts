I am designing a teaching instrument for a third-year philosophy of mind seminar, and I would like you to build it for me. The seminar works with the familiar hierarchy of increasingly self-improving cognitive designs: creatures whose competence is fixed by natural selection over generations, creatures that can learn within a lifetime through reward and punishment, creatures that can test candidate actions in an internal model before risking them in the world, and creatures that can import designed thinking-tools (language, notation, formal methods) from a surrounding culture. Everyone in the room can recite those four kinds; what they cannot do is say, for any actual organism or artefact, which kind it is and why.

Write me a decision tree that performs this sorting. The following constraints apply.

First, every branching question must be phrased as an empirically answerable test or observation, not as a label or a piece of jargon. 'Does it represent?' is banned; 'if its world were subtly rearranged, would its repertoire change within its own lifetime?' is the right register. Someone should be able to apply each question to a specimen without having read the underlying literature.

Second, the tree must be built from the logical space of answers, not from a list of famous names. Once you have fixed the branching questions, enumerate the combinations of answers they permit, and for every combination state whether it is occupied by a recognised kind, occupied by something the standard taxonomy has no name for, or empty — and if empty, diagnose whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely undiscovered, or ruled out by the structure of the earlier questions.

Third, run the following specimens through the finished tree and report where each lands, flagging any that the tree cannot place cleanly: a thermostat; the slime mould that solves mazes by reshaping its body; an octopus that learns to unscrew jars but cannot watch another octopus do it; a large language model trained once and then frozen; the same model equipped with a persistent scratchpad it can consult on later occasions; and a human chess novice who improves by reading annotated games. Where a specimen lands in an unexpected leaf, say which single branching question did the work.

Fourth, identify the point in the tree where the standard hierarchy is most often accused of smuggling in an ordering — the assumption that these kinds form a ladder rather than a scatter — and add one supplementary question at that point which would separate a creature that has a capacity from a creature that merely behaves as if it does. State explicitly whether you think that supplementary question is answerable in practice.

Keep the whole instrument to no more than seven branching questions, and write it so that a student could be handed the tree without the essay that generated it.
