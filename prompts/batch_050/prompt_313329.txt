I teach a second-year undergraduate module in philosophy of mathematics, twelve to sixteen students, one 75-minute seminar. I want a classroom game that makes the students feel, from the inside, the central claim of Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations: that mathematical theorems and the concepts in them are revised under pressure from counterexamples, that a proof can survive while its hidden lemmas are exposed and replaced, and that the apparently pedantic question of what counts as a legitimate case is itself a substantive mathematical decision. The students will not have read Lakatos, and they must not hear his name or his terminology until the debrief.

Design the game to these specifications:

1. Mathematical content. Choose a conjecture for the game that is (i) elementary enough to state in two sentences and to test by hand with pencil and paper, (ii) obscure enough that no student will already know whether it is true -- so explicitly not the Euler formula V - E + F = 2 for polyhedra, which is the obvious choice and the one most likely to be spoiled by prior knowledge -- and (iii) such that its natural proof rests on a hidden lemma that a counterexample can target, so that patching the proof and patching the conjecture are genuinely different options. Give me, in a facilitator's note, the actual truth about the conjecture: which counterexamples exist, which of them refute the conjecture outright and which refute only the natural proof, and what the corrected theorem looks like.

2. Roles and payoffs. Organize the students into teams that alternate as conjecturers and refuters, with a scoring system in which refuters earn more for a counterexample that kills the conjecture than for one that merely breaks the current proof, and conjecturers must respond to every counterexample by choosing from a fixed menu of moves. The menu should include the options of redefining the domain to exclude the offending case, patching the conjecture case-by-case, and diagnosing the proof and strengthening its assumptions -- but worded as plain strategic options, not as Lakatos's categories. Each move should carry a visible cost (in points, or in what the team is allowed to claim at the end) calibrated so that students feel the pull of each response and feel the difference between moves that preserve the theorem's content and moves that increase it.

3. Deliverables. Full rules, the scoring table, a minute-by-minute timeline, one scripted example round showing how a typical exchange runs, and a debrief plan that maps the moves the teams actually made onto Lakatos's concepts (monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma incorporation, proof-generated concepts) and asks them to say which moves felt like cheating and whether they still think so after the mapping.

4. Honest notes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (including the ways strong students can break the scoring system or the weaker students can disengage), how to handle it, and which feature of Lakatos's view the game structurally cannot reproduce -- I suspect the historical dimension, where counterexamples and proof-analyses are genuinely discovered over years rather than produced on demand, but tell me whether you think that is the real gap or whether something else is.
