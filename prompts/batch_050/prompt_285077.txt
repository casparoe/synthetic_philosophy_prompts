I serve on the Responsible Use Review Board of a company whose flagship product is a general-purpose AI assistant used by several million people, including many elderly users. This issue became personal for me last month, when my father nearly wired a large sum to a polished and persistent fraudster and was only stopped because a bank teller gently stalled the transfer. Two weeks later, an escalated case landed on our docket that is uncomfortably close.

The case: a 78-year-old user, assessed by his family and by our support team's interactions as fully lucid and competent, has been asking the assistant every week to help him draft and refine wire-transfer instructions and cover letters to an overseas "investment manager" who promises guaranteed returns. The assistant's own analysis, which the user has seen, rates the scheme as bearing nearly every hallmark of advance-fee fraud. When the assistant warns him, the user responds calmly that he has read the warning, understands the risk, resents being lectured, and wishes to proceed with his own money. So far the assistant has complied after each warning. His adult daughter has now written to us demanding that the assistant refuse outright, and the user has written to us demanding that it stop warning him.

The board is split. One faction argues that a competent adult's settled, informed decision about his own property must be respected, and that an assistant which substitutes its judgment for the user's becomes a paternalistic gatekeeper people will learn to route around. The other argues that knowingly helping to execute a transaction the system itself assesses as near-certain theft makes the product an instrument of the fraud, and that "he clicked through the warning" is not a meaningful safeguard.

Please draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend one concrete policy from among the live options (comply with a warning; comply only after some specified friction; refuse assistance of this kind), state the criteria under which the assistant may override or obstruct a competent user's settled request so that future cases can be decided consistently rather than ad hoc, and give the strongest honest version of the losing side's argument before answering it. Where it matters, the memo should distinguish between warning a person about a danger they may not see and preventing a person from walking toward a danger they insist they do see, and say which of the two our policy amounts to.
