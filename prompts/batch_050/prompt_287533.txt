Eight years ago I began mentoring new hires at my firm on my own initiative. It is not in my contract, I am not paid for it, and nobody asked me to start. Over time I added more: staying late to help other teams meet deadlines, organizing the annual charity drive, covering for colleagues with sick children. I did these things because they seemed good to do, not because I thought I had to.

Last month I told my manager I wanted to give up the mentoring and reclaim my evenings. She reacted as though I had broken a promise. Colleagues now introduce new staff to me as our mentor, and one teammate remarked, in all seriousness, that the department would be in trouble without me. Nobody is angry, exactly, but the air has changed, and I find myself in a strange position: it seems that by doing more than was required for years, I have somehow made the extra into the required.

My sister's advice is brisk: do your job and nothing more; your obligations end at your contract, and whatever lies beyond it is optional, which means you may stop whenever you like. I find this unsatisfying from both directions. If everything past the contract is genuinely optional, I do not see why anyone should be praised for doing it — we do not normally admire people for actions that carry no weight whatsoever. Yet if such actions do carry moral weight, I do not see why omitting them should be entirely blameless. The line my sister draws between the required and the optional-but-good feels less like a principled boundary than a convention we agree not to examine. And there is a practical puzzle beneath the theoretical one: if generous habits breed expectations, and expectations harden into obligations, then sustained generosity appears to be self-erasing — each act of going beyond one's duty quietly raising the floor of duty itself. That looks like an argument for doing less good in the first place, which cannot be right, though I cannot say why.

Please advise me. I am after more than a verdict on the mentoring. I want to understand the structure of my situation. Is there a principled line between what morality demands and what it merely welcomes, and if so, where does it come from? Can a history of freely chosen kindness generate genuine obligations toward the people who have come to rely on it — and if it can, does that give someone in my position a reason, early on, to be less kind? Finally, given your answers, what should I actually do?
