Analyze the ancient astronaut phenomenon as a test case for the demarcation problem in the philosophy of science. I mean the wave of popular alternative archaeology set off by Erich von Däniken's Chariots of the Gods (1968) and continued in its many sequels and later television series: the Nazca lines interpreted as landing infrastructure, the stonework at Tiwanaku and the Giza pyramids attributed to extraterrestrial engineering, the sarcophagus lid of Palenque read as a depiction of an astronaut at the controls, and scriptural passages such as Ezekiel's vision recast as garbled eyewitness reports of spacecraft. The movement sold tens of millions of books, drew sustained rebuttals from archaeologists and scientists, and has survived those rebuttals for over half a century.

I am not asking for a debunking; take it as given that the archaeological mainstream is correct about the sites. What I want is an analysis of the episode through the three major positions on demarcation, and then an assessment of what the episode does to those positions in return.

First, Popper. Von Däniken's characteristic rhetorical move is the interrogative hedge: the central claims are advanced as questions rather than assertions, and apparent counterevidence is absorbed by auxiliary hypotheses (the visitors concealed their traces, the decisive artifacts were destroyed or remain buried). Yet the programme also issued straightforwardly falsifiable claims, for instance that particular blocks could not have been cut, moved, or fitted with the technologies available to the cultures in question, and experimental archaeology duly falsified several of these. Falsificationism therefore seems to deliver an awkward split verdict: the movement's unfalsifiable core is unscientific, while its most egregious concrete claims count as scientific precisely because they stuck their necks out and were cut down. Work through whether this verdict is an artifact of applying the criterion at the wrong level of description, or a genuine embarrassment for the criterion itself.

Second, Lakatos. Ancient astronautics looks like a textbook degenerating research programme: it generates no novel predictions, its protective belt does all the work, and it survives by re-describing anomalies already catalogued by the research tradition it rejects. But Lakatos's criterion is explicitly diachronic and permissive, since young programmes are granted time to mature. On what grounds, and after how long, does it license the verdict we want here?

Third, the deflationary turn associated with Laudan's critique of the demarcation project in the early 1980s. Does the episode vindicate the pessimistic conclusion that no set of necessary and sufficient conditions will sort this case correctly, or does it instead show that looser family-resemblance criteria are sufficient for the judgments we actually need?

Finally, run the analysis in reverse and tell me what the episode reveals about the demarcation project. The classical criteria were developed against physics and its imitators, but von Däniken mimics historiography, not experimental science: the apparatus is citation, site description, and source reinterpretation, not prediction and controlled test. Historical inference is singular and evidential rather than law-testing, so it is not obvious that a criterion of testability applies to it at all. If the real vice of ancient-astronaut writing is not unfalsifiability but something else entirely, such as systematic argument from ignorance, anomaly-mongering, and refusal to weigh established background knowledge against a cherry-picked residue, then perhaps pseudohistory requires its own demarcation criterion, or perhaps demarcation was always about epistemic conduct rather than logical form. Take a position, and where a theory is genuinely silent about some feature of the episode, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
