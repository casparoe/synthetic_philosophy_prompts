Is there an established name for this decision pattern, or is it just a footnote somewhere? I'll describe it because I cant find it in the usual pop stuff about satisficing.

When I'm searching through options one at a time -- apartments on a rental site, freelancers bidding on a job, grad schools to apply to -- I start with what feels like a fixed "good enough" bar. But I've noticed the bar isn't fixed at all. It quietly resets itself to just above the best thing I've seen so far. If the third apartment I view is great, everything after gets compared to that one, and I end up holding out for something nearly as good as the best-seen option, even though my original bar was way lower and I would have called myself a satisficer going in. And the flip side is worse: if the first few options happen to be bad, the bar sinks too, and I'll happily sign something I would have rejected out of hand on a different week, because relative to what I've seen it looks fine. Net result: my choices depend a lot on the *order* I happened to encounter things in, which really bugs me when I think about it.

To save you some guesses: it's related to satisficing but not identical, because the satisficer in the textbook has a stable aspiration level and mine floats with my search history. It's related to anchoring, but anchoring is usually about some number another person hands you, not a standard you build yourself out of your own search. And it's not the moving-goalposts / hedonic treadmill thing where you raise your standards after achieving something -- this all happens mid-search, before I've chosen anything at all.

So: does this have a name in the bounded rationality or judgment-and-decision-making literature? I have a vague sense that Simon said something about aspiration levels adjusting with experience, and if this is really just that idea, tell me what that discussion is actually called and how the floating-bar version differs from the static satisficing story. If there's no clean established term, coin one and define it tightly enough that it's clearly distinct from satisficing, anchoring, and reference-dependence in general -- and give me the boundary cases, i.e. what a near-miss that doesn't count as this pattern would look like. No citations needed, just the concepts and the carving.
