At a panel discussion on corporate complicity last month, a discussant was asked about a mid-level logistics manager at a manufacturing firm who had, over several years, routinely signed off on shipping manifests he must have known concealed illegal waste dumping. The discussant waved the question away by saying it was a textbook case of the banality of evil: the man was no monster, just a functionary doing his job, and that is precisely how the worst things get done. The phrase clearly did real argumentative work in that exchange — it seemed simultaneously to explain the manager, to excuse him, and to issue a warning about the rest of us — but I have since noticed people using the same phrase to mean apparently incompatible things: that evildoers are intellectually shallow, that great wrongs require no wicked intentions, that anyone would have done the same inside the same bureaucracy, that modern organizations manufacture atrocity the way they manufacture everything else, and that the perpetrators of the Holocaust in particular were mostly unideological careerists.

Please disambiguate the slogan. Lay out, as a numbered list ordered roughly from the most platitudinous to the most radical, the distinct precise theses that the banality of evil could be expressing. For each reading, I would like four things: (a) state it as a claim with determinate truth conditions rather than as an atmospheric gesture; (b) classify it as trivially true, substantive and defensible, or probably false, with a sentence or two of justification; (c) say whether it is fundamentally a thesis about the psychology of perpetrators, about the causal structure of organizations, or about the metaphysics of evil itself, since I suspect conflating these three levels is the main source of the confusion; and (d) say whether that particular reading would actually license the discussant's use of the phrase to redescribe — and perhaps partially exculpate — the logistics manager, or whether the discussant needed a stronger reading than the one he could defend.

Two further requests. First, one of these readings is generally taken to be the view Hannah Arendt herself advanced about Adolf Eichmann in Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil (1963) — roughly, that his wrongdoing flowed not from fanatical hatred but from a kind of thoughtlessness, an inability to consider things from another's point of view. Please identify which item on your list corresponds to that view, state it in paraphrase (no quotations needed), and address the following worry: several historians have since argued that the historical Eichmann was in fact a committed ideological antisemite, not the drifting careerist Arendt described. If the motivating example fails, does the thesis fail with it, or can the reading be detached from Eichmann's actual psychology and defended on independent grounds — and if so, what would those grounds have to look like? Second, tell me which reading people most frequently intend in casual use, and whether casual use tends to rest on one of the defensible theses or instead to smuggle a strong, probably false claim in under the cover of a weak, banal one.
