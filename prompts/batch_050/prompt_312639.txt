I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to make sense of a dispute I keep running into in the ethics literature: whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. By this I mean situations where an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, cannot do both, and did nothing wrong in arriving at the conflict. One side argues that such situations cannot really occur: if you work out what morality actually demands, apparent conflicts dissolve, because reasons can be weighed against each other, or because you cannot be obligated to do the impossible, or because at least one of the two supposed obligations was never genuine. The other side points to the phenomenology of hard cases -- the guilt, regret, and sense of residue that persist even after someone did what was, all things considered, the right thing -- and argues that this residue shows a real obligation was left unfulfilled, not merely an unfortunate trade-off.

The debate seems to me to have run for decades without either side conceding ground, and my working hypothesis, as an outsider, is that it is not really about the cases at all.
