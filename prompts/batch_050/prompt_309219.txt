I am trying to determine when an advance directive has authority over a person’s present interests, and I would like you to infer the principle behind my verdicts on the following cases. Please treat this as a philosophical question about self-government, not as a request for legal doctrine or hospital policy.

1. A competent adult scheduled for surgery refuses a blood transfusion because he regards bodily boundaries as inviolable in this respect. His refusal is stable, informed, and unrelated to any misconception about the medical risks. Immediately before anesthesia, he confirms it again. During surgery, an unexpected hemorrhage makes transfusion the only way to save his life. The surgical team should not transfuse.

2. A different patient signed a blanket refusal of transfusion twenty years ago while still deeply embedded in a religious community she has since left. Two days before surgery, now plainly competent, she says that the old document no longer represents her and that she would accept a transfusion if one became necessary. Her family insists that her apostasy was the result of her spouse’s influence and asks the hospital to follow the older, “more authentic” refusal. The later revocation should control.

3. A person with bipolar disorder, while well, executes a carefully considered commitment directive authorizing involuntary hospitalization during a manic episode. She specifically provides that any attempted revocation made during mania should count as evidence that the directive has become applicable. Months later, while manic, she articulately revokes it and passes a standard bedside assessment of decision-making capacity, though her grandiose plans and lack of sleep are evident to those who know her. I think the earlier commitment should control despite the apparently competent revocation.

4. A woman diagnosed with early-stage dementia spends several months discussing the likely course of the disease with her physician and family. She writes that, if she becomes unable to recognize family members, she refuses artificial nutrition and hydration, antibiotics for pneumonia, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. She says that continued biological existence in that condition would not be a benefit to her. Years later, she is in exactly the condition described. She cannot explain her situation or remember the directive, but she appears generally content and enjoys music and meals. She develops pneumonia, and antibiotics would probably restore her to her prior condition. I think the refusal of antibiotics should be honored.

5. Suppose the same woman instead loses the ability to swallow safely. A feeding tube would sustain her for years, although inserting and maintaining it would involve some discomfort. She resists neither the procedure nor the tube once present and otherwise continues to appear content. I also think her earlier refusal of artificial nutrition should be honored, even though doing so will foreseeably shorten her life.

6. Now suppose that the directive instead says that, upon reaching the same stage of dementia, she wants a physician to administer a lethal drug. At that later point she is content, makes no current request to die, and cannot reaffirm the prior request. I do not think the earlier request should by itself authorize the physician to kill her. This is so even if the document is highly specific and was repeatedly confirmed while she was competent.

7. A man who has repeatedly said that he does not want to be kept alive in a permanent vegetative state suffers a traumatic brain injury. He recovers consciousness but acquires substantial impulsivity and a markedly different personality. Nevertheless, he can understand his prognosis, appreciate his options, and give reasons for his choices. He now says he wants all available treatment, including long-term artificial ventilation if necessary. His spouse argues that the person who made the prior directive would have regarded this new man as a stranger. I think the later preference should control, notwithstanding the personality change.

8. An elderly woman with mild dementia can no longer manage her finances or reliably recount her medical history, but she can state stable values: she dislikes hospitals, wants to remain at home, and cares more about comfort than longevity. She cannot remember signing an old directive requesting aggressive treatment, and when it is read to her she says that it sounds like something she wrote to please a now-deceased relative. Her family is divided. I think her presently expressed values should matter more than the old directive, even though she may not satisfy a stringent test of capacity.

There are at least two tensions here. In cases 2 and 3, I allow an earlier self to override an apparently competent later statement in the bipolar case but not in the religious-conversion case, even though both changes can be redescribed as departures from the person’s former values. In cases 5 and 6, I allow an earlier instruction to authorize withholding life-sustaining treatment from a contented person but not an active intervention causing that person’s death, even though the person’s earlier aversion to the future condition may be equally strong in both versions.

Is there a coherent principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that each clause is motivated by particular cases. In doing so, distinguish among several possible grounds of authority: precedent autonomy, the present self’s current choices, the present self’s non-articulated interests, best interests, narrative or psychological continuity, and protection against coercion or impaired judgment. Do not simply label every earlier choice “autonomous” and every later contrary choice “incapacitated.”

The principle should also answer these further questions:

- Does a directive become weaker merely because it is old, or only because age makes psychological discontinuity, changed information, or an intervening revocation more likely?
- Can a person bind a future self who will predictably wish to revoke the commitment only because of a disorder? If so, what prevents that rationale from licensing others to disregard any value change they regard as pathological?
- Does the distinction between refusing treatment and requesting assistance in dying do real work here, or is it merely a proxy for certainty, reversibility, and the need for contemporaneous confirmation?
- When the earlier instruction and present behavior conflict, under what conditions may a caregiver privilege the patient’s present comfort over the life plan the patient formerly endorsed?

Finally, apply the resulting account to two cases I have not decided. First, a patient with a valid refusal of resuscitation is brought to hospital during a brief episode of delirium and repeatedly demands that everything be done; the delirium is expected to resolve within hours. Second, a person in moderate dementia cannot remember making a directive refusing dialysis but can understand, in simple terms, that dialysis will prolong her life at the cost of regular discomfort; she consistently says she wants it. If your account cannot preserve all eight of my verdicts, identify the verdict you would abandon and explain why it is less defensible than the principle that displaces it.
