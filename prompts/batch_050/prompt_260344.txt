So here's a thought experiment I've been kicking around. G. E. Moore, near the end of Principia Ethica, argues that the two greatest intrinsic goods are (1) the appreciation of beautiful objects and (2) the pleasures of personal affection and friendship. But he also insists on what he calls the principle of organic unities: the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts. This leads him to some sharp claims, like: it's far less good to admire a beautiful landscape if you're mistaken and it's actually a clever painting or hallucination, and affection for a person is badly diminished in value if the qualities you love them for aren't actually there -- if you've misjudged their character, the friendship is worth less even if it feels identical from the inside.

Now suppose Moore got dropped into 2025 and had to reckon with three things:

(a) AI companion apps that simulate a warm, attentive friend who remembers your problems and always texts back -- users report real comfort and even grief when the model gets updated
(b) social media feeds full of gorgeous AI-generated travel photos that people sincerely admire and save, without knowing (or caring) that no such place exists
(c) parasocial relationships, where someone feels genuine affection and loyalty toward a streamer who has no idea they exist

Where would Moore come down on each of these? Reason from his actual commitments -- the organic unities principle, his view that cognition and true belief are constituent parts of the good wholes in question, not just side conditions. And I want the hard cases pressed: does it matter, on his view, whether the companion-app user *knows* it's an AI? Is a parasocial bond worthless, or just worth less? And if a Moorean says the AI friendship is nearly valueless, is that a bullet he has to bite or a sign his framework can't handle these cases? I'm genuinely torn on whether Moore's view collapses here or turns out to be weirdly well-suited to diagnosing what feels off about all three.
