I'm a novelist on a deadline (Tuesday, brain is soup) writing a near-future riff on Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor: after the plagues, a soft-spoken Regent gives everyone bread, a daily little miracle of prediction, and elections that are mostly weather. My villain, or maybe my hero, gives this argument and now it seems to prove way too much.

1. A choice is a burden for someone when, if they keep it, they reliably end up ashamed, panicked, or begging somebody to take it back.
2. Under scarcity, lots of ordinary adults are like that about the core liberal choices: what to believe, whom to trust, when to obey, whether to risk the child’s dinner on a principle. Call this the bakery fact; don't fight me on exact stats, it's the pattern.
3. If an institution can lift a burden without removing anything the person would later, lucidly, name as part of themselves, lifting it is care rather than domination.
4. The stuff freedom is made of looks exactly like those burdens: option-guilt, blame for outcomes, suspicion that the authorities are lying, and the vertigo of being unpicked by any story.
5. So liberal rights are not respect for persons; they are a billing system by which the strong make the weak pay for the strong's clean conscience.
6. If 5 is right, the only honest politics is a gentle inquisition: feed people, reassure them, stage consent like rain, and never let the flock see the shepherd's hand.
7. But my nurse heroine, who has watched people choose badly and still stand taller because the choice was theirs, says even the burned hand prefers its own fire.

So either she's sentimental, or nearly every defense of freedom deflates into an aesthetic taste for scars. Where does this crack, if it does? If I have to keep two things I'd keep 1 and 7, but tell me what I'm paying, and whether the move is an equivocation, a scope sneak, a modal slide, or a premise I just have to bite. Be specific enought that I can steal it for chapter nine.
