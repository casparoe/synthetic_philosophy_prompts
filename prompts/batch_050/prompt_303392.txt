I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long piece on what it means for anything -- a person, a court, a language model -- to genuinely follow a rule rather than merely behave as if it did. While drafting, I ran into the rule-following problem associated with Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, and I want to pressure-test my understanding by having a case built to order. The standard classroom example involves arithmetic and a deviant function that coincides with addition on all inputs tried so far. I've seen that example too many times, and my readers will have too, so I want something fresh.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. First, the scenario must involve no mathematics at all -- no plus-like function, no numerical extrapolation; instead, set it in a realistic domain where rule-following matters practically, such as a judge applying a legal standard, a doctor following a diagnostic protocol, or a content-moderation policy applied by a contractor. Second, the scenario must be engineered so that the protagonist's entire finite history of past applications is equally compatible with (at least) two distinct rules, where the two rules diverge on a case the protagonist is about to face -- and the divergence must arise naturally from the texture of the domain, not be stipulated by fiat. Third, the case must be one on which two responses to the skeptical challenge come apart: a dispositionalist account (the rule followed is fixed by what the protagonist is disposed to do) and a communitarian or skeptical-solution account (correctness is fixed by patterns of communal agreement and correction) should deliver different verdicts about whether the protagonist's upcoming application is right, or at least different diagnoses of where correctness could come from. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, do the check: state the two candidate rules explicitly, show that the past behavior underdetermines the choice between them, and explain precisely why each of the two accounts delivers the verdict it does. Then identify which feature of your scenario is doing the philosophical work, describe what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and sketch how a defender of the account that fares worse in your case would try to defuse it. Finally, say in a sentence or two whether you think the case generalizes to artificial systems that follow policies -- since that's the hook my article needs -- without pretending the answer is obvious.
