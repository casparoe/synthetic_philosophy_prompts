I'm a philosophy PhD student (decision theory adjacent) and somehow ended up as the student rep on my department's small grants committee. The situation: an eccentric alum, a retired actuary, has offered to fund our annual graduate travel budget in perpetuity, but strictly on his terms. At present we get a guaranteed £4,000 a year from the department. His offer replaces that with a yearly draw: an independent third party flips a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if the first heads lands on flip n, we receive £2^n. So half the time we get £2, a quarter of the time £4, and so on, with no upper cap. He'll commit to this forever, in writing, and he's cheerfully pointed out in his letter that the expected value of each draw is infinite. Half the commitee says we'd be mad to turn down infinite expected value; the other half points out that in any given year we almost certainly end up with under £100, and there goes the conference season.

I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo: accept, decline, or accept with conditions (say, negotiating a floor, or taking the average of several draws per year). It needs a clear actionable recommendation with reasons the donor could actually respond to, and it has to give the strongest argument on the losing side a fair run rather than waving it off. If the recommendation leans on some standard fix -- diminishing marginal utility, the donor's finite bankroll, risk aversion -- say how much weight that fix can really bear here rather than just citing it. I'd also like the memo to state what would change its verdict: which argument or consideration would flip the recommendation, and where the reasoning is most likely to break. British English please.
