Henry Sidgwick helped found the Society for Psychical Research in the 1880s, served as its first president, and spent decades personally investigating mediums, séances, and thought-transference experiments -- work he pursued with the same rigor he brought to ethics, and partly because he hoped that evidence of a spiritual dimension to human life might bear on what he saw as the deepest unresolved problem in his moral philosophy, the dualism of practical reason between rational egoism and utilitarianism. He was also repeatedly burned: celebrated subjects were caught in fraud, results he had provisionally accepted collapsed under scrutiny, and he ended his life far more skeptical than he began, while still insisting the evidential question had never been closed dishonestly on either side.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, where parapsychology comes up surprisingly often: as a test case for priors, as a cautionary tale about motivated reasoning on the skeptic side, and in the well-known argument that psi research functions as a control group for the scientific method itself -- if standard statistical practice can make precognition look real, the problem is with the practice, not the subject matter.

With that background: suppose Sidgwick were transported to the present and brought up to speed on the last fifteen years. He learns about Daryl Bem's 2011 paper in a leading social-psychology journal reporting nine experiments purporting to show retroactive influences on cognition; the subsequent replication crisis across psychology; the rise of preregistration, registered reports, and Bayesian reanalysis as proposed remedies; and the continued publication of pro-psi meta-analyses alongside null replications.

What do you think his considered position would be? In particular: (1) Would he regard Bem's publication in a mainstream journal as vindication of the SPR's original strategy of fighting for psi's scientific respectability, or as evidence that mainstream science simply imported the SPR's failure modes? (2) Given his history of extending credit to investigators and being defrauded anyway, where would he set the evidential bar now -- and would he treat preregistration as the methodological fix the nineteenth century lacked, or as beside the point? (3) Would the modern rationalist control-group argument strike him as the best available case that his life's empirical work was not wasted, or as a subtle insult -- a claim that psi research matters only as a methodological cautionary tale? (4) Sidgwick hoped psychical research might resolve the dualism of practical reason by revealing a moral order to the universe; do you think he would still see any live connection between the empirical psi question and ethics, or would he have given that up? Please ground your predictions in his actual views and temperament rather than producing a generic Victorian-skeptic pastiche, and flag where the evidence about what he would think runs out and you are extrapolating.
