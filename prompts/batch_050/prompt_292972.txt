I run a 75-minute philosophy of language seminar for 14 advanced undergrads, and next week we hit indicative vs. counterfactual conditionals. On the page they always nod along at the classic Kennedy-assassination pairs and then write essays that completely ignore the distinction, so I want an activity that makes them feel it in their gut before anyone names it.

Design me a game with these specs:

- Participants should end up treating two conditionals with the same antecedent very differently -- one indicative, one counterfactual -- in a way that costs them something, so the divergence shows up in their behavior rather than in their answers to discussion questions. I'm picturing bets or wagers with points instead of money, where a bet on the indicative works like a conditional bet that gets called off and refunded when the antecedent turns out false, while the counterfactual version can't be cashed out that way and has to stand or fall some other way -- but work out the actual mechanics yourself, I'm not wedded to that sketch.
- Materials: cards, dice, envelopes, slips of paper. Nothing electronic. The scenario generating the conditionals has to be rigged so that the indicative really is close to certain while the counterfactual is genuinely a coin flip or worse. That's the hard part of the design and where versions I've tried before fall apart (usually the counterfactual accidentally becomes near-certain too, or both become guesses), so spend real effort there and tell me exactly why your setup keeps them apart.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes: setup, the rounds, and the moment where they notice what they've been doing -- ideally they discover the asymmetry themselves when the payouts land, not because I point at it.
- A debrief that maps what happened onto the theory: the indicative's assertibility tracking the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, the counterfactual needing something like what goes on in nearby worlds where the antecedent holds -- and why the called-off-bet structure mimics the first but structurally can't mimic the second.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: a student who took linguistics blurts out the terminology in round one and ruins the discovery; someone refuses to bet on anything; a group figures out how to game the scoring), and how to handle each without deflating the activity.
- An honest note on which feature of the distinction the game can't reproduce. My worry is that betting behavior might collapse two different things -- the semantics of the conditional and the pragmatics of when it's okay to assert one -- and I'd like to know going in whether the activity actually separates them or just confates them in a way that's productive anyway.

Casual is fine, I mainly need it to actually work in a room with real students.
