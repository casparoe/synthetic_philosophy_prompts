Imagine David Hilbert were brought to the present day and briefed on what has become of his program: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem and the resulting impossibility of a finitary consistency proof for anything approaching the full axiomatic framework of mathematics; the fact that another century of intensive set-theoretic work has nonetheless turned up no contradiction; the results of reverse mathematics showing that the overwhelming bulk of ordinary mathematics can be formalized in subsystems vastly weaker than ZFC, some of them arguably amenable to near-finitary justification; and the practice of contemporary set theorists, who adopt ever stronger large cardinal axioms whose consistency is unprovable even within ZFC, defending them on grounds of fruitfulness and the non-appearance of contradictions. What do you think Hilbert's considered verdict would be? Would he see his program as refuted, merely amended, or vindicated in spirit? Would he treat the merely empirical confidence in the consistency of ZFC as an existential risk to mathematics or as a manageable one? And would he accept the set theorists' inductive defense of large cardinals, or condemn it as precisely the unsecured reliance on ideal elements that he had set out to eliminate?
