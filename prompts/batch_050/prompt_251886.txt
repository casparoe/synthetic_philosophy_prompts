Every Oscar season we get another batch of biopics, and the drill is always the same. The film opens with "based on a true story," then compresses three years into a week, merges four colleagues into one guy, invents every private conversation word for word, and stages a confrontation that never happened. Nobody blinks at any of that. But sometimes a film invents something bad -- a real person, now dead, gets shown taking a bribe he never took, or abandoning a friend he never abandoned -- and suddenly there's outrage, op-eds, the family issuing statements, maybe a defensive note tucked into the credits. Meanwhile the purely fictional movies carry that boilerplate about how any resemblance to actual persons is entirely coincidental, even when the resemblance is obviously the whole selling point. And everyones intuitions seem to track time: rewrite the private life of a medieval king however you want, but get very careful about anyone whose kids are still alive to watch the premiere.

So: what does this whole practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ethics for me. Does the pattern only make sense if a dramatization owes the dead fidelity to *character* (what they were like, what they would and wouldn't have done) but not fidelity to *facts* (dates, guest lists, who said what in the kitchen)? Is there a coherent rule buried in there, something like "invent freely what nobody could ever have known, but never contradict what people did know," and does that rule survive contact with the whitewashing cases, where the sin is deleting rather than inventing? And what's the best reading of the time-decay thing -- do we actually think obligations to the dead fade as the decades pass, or is that just squeamishness about the living relatives in the audience? Also take seriously the boring hypothesis: maybe there's no ethics here at all, and the whole pattern is just defamation law (you generally can't libel the dead) plus box office, with the "outrage" functioning as free marketing. Can that cynical story explain every feature of the practice, or are there bits it can't account for? Finish by telling me what the industry would concretely have to do differently if we committed to one clean theory or the other -- either strict accuracy about any real person portrayed, or no obligations to the dead whatsoever.
