I am drafting lecture notes on the doctrine of double effect for an undergraduate ethics unit on trolley cases, and I want to make sure I state the doctrine correctly. As I understand it, the doctrine holds that an action causing harm is permissible provided the harm is foreseen but not intended: that is why diverting the trolley onto the side track is acceptable while pushing the man off the footbridge is not, since in the first case the death is a mere side effect and in the second it is intended. On that reading, though, a commander who bombs a densely populated city to destroy a single weapons cache acts permissibly so long as he sincerely aims only at the cache and merely foresees the civilian deaths. That conclusion seems clearly wrong, so I must be misstating something. But if I fix the account by adding that disproportionate side effects are impermissible, doesn't the doctrine collapse into ordinary cost-benefit weighing, leaving the intention condition with nothing to do? What exactly does the doctrine of double effect claim, and what distinctive work does the intention condition do once proportionality is in place?
