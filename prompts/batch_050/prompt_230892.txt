I am a high school junior writing a term essay for a combined English and philosophy elective, and I want to build it around the ending of John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men. As a reminder of the situation: Lennie, who is cognitively disabled and does not understand his own strength, has accidentally killed Curley's wife. Curley is gathering a lynch mob and has said he will shoot Lennie in the gut so he dies slowly. George finds Lennie first, calms him by retelling their shared dream of the farm, and shoots him in the back of the head before the mob arrives.

Please read this ending through the philosophical debate about killing versus letting die and doing versus allowing harm. Specifically:

1. James Rachels famously argued, using his pair of bathtub cases (Smith drowns his cousin; Jones merely watches as his cousin slips and drowns), that the bare difference between killing and letting die is not, by itself, morally significant. Does George's case support or challenge that equivalence thesis? On one hand, George's alternative to killing Lennie really was letting him die horribly, so killing looks like the lesser evil here in a way the bathtub cases do not capture. On the other hand, George actively intends Lennie's death rather than merely foreseeing it, which the doctrine of double effect treats as morally weighty. Which framework best fits what the novel actually dramatizes?

2. The novel gives us other deaths to compare: Carlson shooting Candy's old dog, and Curley hunting Lennie. Does Steinbeck seem to treat George's act as morally continuous with either of these, or as something distinct? What does that implied ranking suggest about whether intention, motive, or relationship is doing the moral work?

3. Where does the novel resist a tidy philosophical reading? I do not want an essay that just uses George as an illustration of a thesis. If the equivalence thesis or the double effect doctrine strains against some detail of the scene (George's shaking hand, the retelling of the dream, his devastation afterward), say so honestly.

4. Finally, what does the novel show about this debate that the standard thought experiments cannot? I suspect the crucial difference is that the person who kills Lennie is the person who loves him most, and no bathtub case has that feature. Tell me whether you think that feature matters morally or only emotionally.

Please point me to specific scenes or moments I should discuss (describing them is fine; I do not need verbatim quotations), and write the whole response in American English.
