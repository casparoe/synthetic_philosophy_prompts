I am a novelist, and the central figure of the book I am writing is an ageing logician who has staked his intellectual life, and not a little of his inner life, on the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be a machine — the argument associated with Lucas and later revived by Penrose. The anti-mechanist holds that a mind can, so to speak, see the truth of its own Gödel sentence, which no machine confined to proving only what it can prove could ever do; the mechanist replies that this quietly assumes the consistency of the human reasoner, which is precisely what cannot be assumed of fallible, error-prone thinkers like ourselves. I have been reading this debate in order to write the logician and the young cognitive scientist who spars with him across the book, and what strikes me is that it has run for more than sixty years with almost no movement: everyone accepts the same theorems, everyone knows the same objections, and neither camp has budged an inch. Before I can decide how their confrontation in the novel should end — in refutation, in a genuine concession, or in two people permanently talking past one another — I need to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it mathematical, is it a hidden empirical question about whether human reasoning is in fact consistent, is it a dispute about what the idealization 'machine' is meant to capture, or is it largely verbal, two camps answering different questions under one name? Give me a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually argues rather than a survey of positions, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a theorem, an experiment, a conceptual distinction — or whether the question was ill-posed from the start. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
