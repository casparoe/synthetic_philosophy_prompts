Write a press release, in the flat, unimpeachably confident prose of a university communications office, announcing that the (fictional) Institute for Fundamental Culinary Theory has confirmed its flagship theory without conducting a single experiment, tasting, or controlled comparison of any kind. The theory should be some mundane, obviously decidable claim — for instance, that there is exactly one correct construction of the grilled cheese sandwich, or that one regional style of chili is objectively optimal — and the comedy should arise from the fact that the claim could be settled in an afternoon in any kitchen.

The release must justify the result using, in recognizable and faithful form, the three standard arguments of non-empirical theory confirmation, transposed without comment into the culinary setting:

1. The no-alternatives argument: the institute's researchers have spent years attempting to conceive of a rival theory meeting the same standards of coherence, simplicity, and scope, and have failed; the probability that an alternative exists is therefore low, and so the theory is probably true.
2. The unexpected-coherence argument: the theory has turned out to explain matters it was never designed to explain — acceptable condiments, the proper behavior of dinner guests, the failure of certain celebrity chefs — and this unlooked-for explanatory reach is itself evidence in its favor.
3. The meta-inductive argument: other programs in the institute's broader tradition (the Department of Salad Dynamics, the Center for Broth Studies) have produced theories that were eventually vindicated, and theories arrived at by these methods have a strong track record, so this one will presumably be vindicated too.

Include a headline, a dateline, two or three deadpan quotations from the institute's director and a spokesperson (invented names), a paragraph noting that empirical testing is planned for a later phase and has been for some years, a sentence of thanks to the institute's colleagues in fundamental physics, who showed the way, and a boilerplate About the Institute paragraph. End with the director's response to a critic, which is simply to observe that the critic has proposed no alternative theory of grilled cheese.

The satire should land the standard criticisms without ever stating them: that the no-alternatives argument may measure only the researchers' imagination, incentives, or funding rather than the theory's truth; that the track record depends on careful bookkeeping about which past programs count as belonging to the tradition; and that the whole apparatus can confer full scientific confidence on a claim a child could test. But the transposition must be fair: a reader acquainted with the actual debate over non-empirical confirmation should recognize the three arguments as accurately rendered and see that the target is the reasoning itself, not a distorted version of it. The director may be permitted one stretch of genuinely formidable-sounding methodology. No winking, no asides, roughly 600 words.
