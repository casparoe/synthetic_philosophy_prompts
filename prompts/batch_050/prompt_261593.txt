I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been reading about Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* and the "hinge epistemology" literature that grew out of it, and I've talked myself into a position I want stress-tested.

The position, roughly: some commitments — that there's an external world, that my senses aren't systematically deceiving me, that the past wasn't fabricated five minutes ago, that the rules of inference I'm using right now work — are not beliefs I hold on evidence and couldn't be, because anything I'd offer as evidence already presupposes them. They're the scaffolding inquiry stands on rather than part of the structure being inspected. Doubt only makes sense against a background of things not doubted, the way you can only file a bug report against a system you otherwise trust to be running. From this I conclude that Cartesian skepticism isn't refuted so much as shown to be misplaced: the skeptic's demand for justification of the hinges themselves is a category error, like demanding a proof that the proof-checker is correct using only the proof-checker. I take this to be a better response to skepticism than Moore's (which strikes me as table-pounding) or contextualism (which feels like changing the subject).

What I want is a map of what would count against this position. Some specific pressure points I've noticed, which you should engage with rather than just confirm:

1. If hinges aren't beliefs and aren't truth-apt, it's unclear how they can do epistemic work at all — how can something that can't be true or false justify anything? But if they *are* truth-apt, the view starts to look like ordinary fallibilism with extra steps.
2. Hinges apparently shift over time — the river-bed image suggests some propositions move between being scaffolding and being tested. Does the view have a principled account of when a hinge becomes examinable, or is that left embarrassingly vague? The history of science and of my own field (assumptions baked into systems that later had to be ripped out) suggests the boundary is real but messy.
3. The relativism worry: if different communities have different hinge systems, does hinge epistemology have anything to say about deep disagreement between them, or does it just declare such disagreement rationally undecidable and call that a result rather than a failure?
4. The worry that this "dissolves" skepticism only by redefining knowledge out from under the skeptic — that the skeptical question survives intact once you stop insisting that certainty is an epistemic status at all.

For each objection you consider, tell me whether a committed hinge epistemologist would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as a misunderstanding, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. If you think some of my pressure points above are actually weak or misstate the view, say so plainly. Be honest about whether the position has been qualified into near-unfalsifiability — if no possible argument could touch it, that's itself a finding I want flagged. Finally, say what would change your own verdict: which argument or consideration, if it succeeded, would lead you to conclude that hinge epistemology fails as a response to skepticism, and where your reasoning here is most likely to be wrong. Please write in American English.
