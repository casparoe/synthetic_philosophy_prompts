I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been working through William James's Pragmatism on my commute after a friend in a book club insisted I'd enjoy the lecture on truth. She was right, but it left me with a question nobody in the club could answer. In Lecture VI, "Pragmatism's Conception of Truth," James writes:

"It is hard to find any one phrase that characterizes these consequences better than the ordinary agreement-formula—just such consequences being what we have in mind whenever we say that our ideas 'agree' with reality. They lead us, namely, through the acts and other ideas which they instigate, into or up to, or towards, other parts of experience with which we feel all the while—such feeling being among our potentialities—that the original ideas remain in agreement. The connexions and transitions come to us from point to point as being progressive, harmonious, satisfactory. This function of agreeable leading is what we mean by an idea's verification."

Here's my situation. James died in 1910. A couple of decades later, Frank Ramsey sketched what's now called the redundancy theory of truth — roughly, the idea that appending "is true" to a claim adds nothing beyond the claim itself — and that line of thinking grew into the deflationary family of theories (disquotationalism, minimalism) that treat truth as a thin logical device rather than a substantive property. Reading James's passage as an engineer, I keep going back and forth. On one reading, deflationism looks like exactly the anti-metaphysical move James would cheer: no mysterious static correspondence relation, just the role the word plays in practice — and James even predicts in the same lecture that his theory will eventually be "admitted to be true, but obvious and insignificant," which is precisely how deflationists present their view. On another reading, James is saying truth is a real, substantive process — the "agreeable leading" is doing actual work in his account, not just annotating assertions the way a type annotation does nothing at runtime — so deflationism would strike him as the "inert static relation" view wearing new clothes.

So: reconstruct, as carefully as the textual evidence allows, what James would most plausibly have made of deflationary theories of truth had he lived to see them. Ground your answer in the passage above and in the broader argument of Pragmatism rather than in generalities about pragmatism, and say whether he'd count deflationism as a vindication, a rival, or a misunderstanding of his view — including how he'd likely respond to the objection that his "agreeable leading" account collapses into either deflationism or a crude "true means useful." Please indicate how confident you are in your overall reconstruction, and single out the specific point in it where you are least sure you're getting James right.
