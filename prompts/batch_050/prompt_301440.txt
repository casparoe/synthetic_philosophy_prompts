I am trying to work out whether my intuitions about karma, understood strictly as a theory of moral desert, hang together, and I would like you to find the principle behind them — or tell me which intuitions I have to give up.

By karma as a theory of desert I mean the following stipulative view, which you should take as the target rather than any particular religious doctrine: there is an impersonal moral order, requiring no judge, under which an agent's intentional good and bad acts generate fitting benefits and sufferings for that same agent, possibly deferred and possibly across rebirths. The question I care about is what has to be true of the agent, the act, and the connection between them for the fitting suffering or benefit to be genuinely deserved rather than merely occurring.

Here are my verdicts on cases:

1. A career criminal's life collapses through the natural consequences of his own choices — paranoia, betrayal by associates, imprisonment. His misery is deserved. This is my clearest case.

2. A man commits fraud at thirty, loses all memory of it at sixty after a head injury, and is punished at sixty-five. Still deserved, I think, though it unsettles me. He is the same person in the same life.

3. A woman suffers a debilitating illness, and the karmic explanation is cruelty she committed in a previous life, of which she of course has no memory. Not deserved. Whatever links her to that prior agent is too thin for desert, and I cannot say why without also threatening case 2, which is exactly my problem.

4. A man wrongs his business partner, later repents sincerely, makes full restitution, and is genuinely reformed. The karmically fitting misfortune arrives twenty years later anyway. Not deserved, or at least substantially less deserved. Moral transformation should count for something against desert.

5. A mother performs meritorious acts and dedicates the merit to her adult son, who then prospers. His good fortune is not deserved — he did nothing — and yet I find unearned benefit noticeably less objectionable than unearned suffering, and I am not sure I can justify the asymmetry.

6. A kingdom's subjects share in a famine explained by the king's injustice — collective karma. Not deserved by the subjects. Desert strikes me as essentially individual here.

7. A man donates generously but purely for reputation. Some merit is generated, but much less than if his motive had been compassionate. Intention modulates desert without eliminating it.

8. A child is born into severe deprivation, explained by misdeeds in a prior life. Not deserved — and I notice this is case 3 again with the stakes raised, which makes me suspect one of my verdicts has to go.

My tentative principle is this: an outcome is karmically deserved only if the subject of the outcome stands in whatever relation to the original agent that ordinary, single-life practices of punishment presuppose, and only if the agent has not undergone genuine moral transformation between act and outcome; and the fittingness of the outcome is proportioned to the intention behind the act.

Please do the following. First, test my tentative principle against all eight cases and say precisely where it fails — I suspect it cannot deliver my verdicts on both 2 and 3, and I want the failure located exactly. Second, state the best principle or pair of candidate principles you can construct that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, identifying which case motivates each clause. Third, tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, no reasonably simple principle can accommodate, and which one you would advise me to abandon and why. Fourth, say what your preferred principle predicts for three cases I have not considered: an agent who acts wrongly under severe but resistible coercion; an agent whose good fortune results from an ancestor's merit without any dedication of it; and an agent who intends grave harm, attempts it, and succeeds only in producing a minor inconvenience. Finally, I would like a verdict on the asymmetry in case 5: is there any defensible principle on which unearned benefit is less objectionable than unearned suffering, or should I treat that intuition as noise?
