I am putting together the final problem set for an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of physics, and I want one question on Boltzmann brains that cannot be answered by reciting the standard pop-science gloss (a fluctuation with your exact brain state is more likely than a whole low-entropy universe, so something is wrong with cosmologies that predict many such fluctuations). The students have seen that gloss; what I want to test is whether they understand *why* the problem is supposed to be a problem, and where candidate solutions actually do their work.

Here is the setup I want the question built around. A cosmologist proposes the following dismissal of the Boltzmann brain problem: "Observers with my exact memories and experiences, produced by thermal fluctuation, are vastly outnumbered by observers with *almost* my experiences whose next moment of experience is chaotic nonsense. But any observer whose experiences are about to become chaotic cannot trust their own reasoning about cosmology, including the reasoning that led them to the Boltzmann brain worry in the first place. So the only self-consistent stance is to assume I am a typical *ordered* observer, and the problem dissolves." Meanwhile, a second cosmologist proposes a different fix: modify the cosmological model so that the universe transitions into an empty de Sitter state only finitely often, so that fluctuation-produced observers are never actually produced in large numbers, and argues this is the *only* legitimate kind of solution because the first cosmologist's reasoning is circular.

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, framed so that students must (a) state precisely what makes a theory's predicting many Boltzmann brains objectionable -- distinguishing the "cognitive instability" version of the worry from the merely statistical "it's unlikely that I'm a brain" version, and explaining why the former is the philosophically serious one; (b) evaluate the first cosmologist's dismissal, in particular whether the appeal to typicality among ordered observers can be justified without already assuming that the student's evidence is reliable; and (c) compare the two cosmologists' strategies on the question of whether the Boltzmann brain problem is an *empirical constraint on cosmological models* or a *logical defect in a class of arguments*. Students should have 60 minutes.

2. A model answer of roughly 500-700 words that reaches a verdict. The verdict I have in mind, which the model answer should defend, is roughly: the first cosmologist is gesturing at the right idea (cognitive instability) but overstates it -- the instability argument shows that a theory predicting we are *probably* Boltzmann brains undermines its own evidential basis, not that we may simply stipulate typicality among ordered observers; and the second cosmologist is right that modifying the cosmology is a cleaner response, but wrong that it is the *only* legitimate one, since the instability argument functions as a theory-level selection criterion rather than as circular reasoning.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 points, organized as four or five must-hit components with the marks for each, and for each component a one- or two-sentence note on the characteristic way a weaker student gets it wrong -- for example, treating the problem as just "it would be weird if I were a brain in a fluctuation," or attributing to the cognitive instability argument the stronger claim that Boltzmann-brain-rich theories are logically inconsistent, or evaluating the first cosmologist's typicality assumption without noticing that it needs independent justification.
