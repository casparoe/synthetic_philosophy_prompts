I'm a junior on my university's student-faculty grade appeals committee, and I'm also in an epistemology seminar where we just covered the peer disagreement debate (conciliationism vs. steadfastness and all that). We've got a live case that is basically that debate in real life. A student appealed her final exam grade, so the department had two faculty members re-grade it blind and independently. Both are experienced, both used the same rubric, neither knew the other was grading it, and nobody thinks either of them was careless or biased. One came back with a 74, the other with a 91. The committee is deadlocked: half want to split the difference and give her an 82 or 83 since the graders are equally competent, and the other half thinks averaging is irrational -- if the graders really disagree, one of them saw something the other missed, and averaging just locks in whichever error happened. Getting a third read isn't possible before the appeals deadline, so don't recommend that.

Can you draft the commitee's decision memo to the dean? Act as if you're a professional epistemologist who consults for academic institutions on exactly this kind of thing. Think carefully before writing, maybe brainstorm the strongest version of each position first and then commit to one. I need a clear bottom-line recommendation the committee can actually vote on, the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the best argument on the losing side. One thing the memo has to grapple with: our chair insists this case isn't really about peer disagreement at all. She says a 17-point spread on the same rubric is evidence that the rubric itself is broken, not that either grader is unreliable -- so the right fix is procedural (rewrite the rubric, retrain graders) rather than epistemic (averaging or deferring). Say whether she's right, and what follows for this case if she is.
