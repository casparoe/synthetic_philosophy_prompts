I'm a philosophy prof (ethics of agency / personal identity stuff) who somehow ended up on my local hospital's clinical ethics committee, and I keep getting pulled into the same kind of consult: a patient with moderately advanced dementia has an advance directive from years ago, and what the directive says now conflicts with what the patient, by all appearances, currently wants or seems content with. Classic then-self vs now-self problem -- the committee members are smart clinicians and social workers but none of them have read any of this literature, and every consult we basically reinvent the wheel over the phone at 9pm.

What I want from you: help me design a written decision procedure that our ethics consult service could actually hand to a rotating group of clinicians, that translates the philosophical work on precedent autonomy and the authority of the earlier self into something runnable. For context, the field more or less splits between views where the earlier, competent self's considered values get precedence (since the directive expresses the person's life-shaping values) and views where a still-sentient patient's current experiential interests and apparent contentment should win, at least when they're clearly living a life that isn't miserable to them -- plus hybrid positions. I don't need you to rehash that debate at length; I need the operationalization.

So concretely, the procedure should specify:

- The questions the consultant asks, in order, before anyone argues about what to do (e.g., how to assess whether the directive actually applies to the current situation, what evidence counts for the patient having or lacking current preferences that deserve weight, how much the circumstances have to have changed from what the author envisioned before "this isn't the situation they wrote it for" becomes a live option).
- Which philosophical distinctions absolutely have to survive the translation into a checklist (I'm guessing: competence at time of writing, specificity of the directive, whether the current self still has the capacity for *any* values vs only experiences, the difference between the patient resisting a treatment and merely not endorsing one) and which ones you can safely drop without doing violence to the theory.
- Where the procedure terminates in an answer and where it has to hand off to a human judgment call -- be honest that some of this can't be proceduralized.
- Roughly where the burden of proof sits at each step, since in practice the default matters a lot when everyone's exhausted and the family is in the room.

Constraints: the consultant typically has under an hour of bedside time, may be doing this by phone, the people executing it are not philosophers and won't reliably apply terms like "precedent autonomy" correctly, and the decisions are high-stakes but not usually emergency-split-second (we can usually take a day or two). It gets used maybe 3-4 times a month.

Then run the procedure on three cases, including at least one where it gives an answer I won't like:

1. A 78-year-old with Alzheimer's, directive from 6 years ago refusing artificial nutrition and hydration, now apparently cheerful, eats by hand happily, but a swallowing study says she needs a feeding tube to avoid aspiration risk.
2. A 45-year-old with a psychiatric directive (a so-called Ulysses contract) directing that he be hospitalized and medicated during manic episodes even over his objection; he's now manic, articulate, and vehemently refusing, and arguably currently competent-seeming on some measures.
3. A directive that's vague -- something like "no heroic measures" -- for a patient who's now minimally conscious, where the family insists "this is exactly what she meant" and the nursing staff isn't so sure.

Also tell me where you think the procedure will systematically misfire, and what you'd tell the committee about its limits in one paragraph we could put on the cover page without scaring everyone off.
