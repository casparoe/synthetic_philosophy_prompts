"Locke held that personal identity over time consists in continuity of consciousness: you are the same person as some past individual just in case you can remember that individual's experiences as your own. It follows, on Locke's view, that if you have completely forgotten some episode of your life, then the person who lived through it was, strictly speaking, not you, and you therefore cannot justly be held responsible for it. Thomas Reid refuted this theory with his brave officer case: an aged general remembers being the young officer who captured an enemy standard, and that young officer remembered being the schoolboy who was flogged for stealing fruit, but the general no longer remembers the flogging. Since identity is transitive while memory is not, Locke's criterion implies the general both is and is not the schoolboy — a contradiction. Reid thereby showed that personal identity must instead be grounded in the persistence of an immaterial soul, which guarantees the unity of the self across a lifetime."
