A long-form magazine article I read recently argues against solar geoengineering — specifically stratospheric aerosol injection — along the following lines. 'The case for cooling the planet by spraying reflective particles into the stratosphere collapses under three considerations. First, the moral hazard is now well established: social scientists have shown that the prospect of a technological fix erodes the political will to cut emissions, so even funding research into aerosol injection measurably slows decarbonization. Second, there is termination shock. Because an aerosol programme masks an ever-growing stock of greenhouse warming, the climate comes to depend on the shield; any interruption — a funding lapse, a war, a technical failure — would unleash the pent-up warming within a few years, far faster than ecosystems or infrastructure could adapt. Starting therefore means never being able to stop. Third, the governance problem is a classic free-rider dilemma: since deployment would be cheap relative to its global benefits, every country will prefer that others bear the costs and risks, which is why deployment could be legitimate only under something close to unanimous international consent.' The article concludes that outdoor experiments, and indeed any research programme oriented toward deployment, should be prohibited.

Something in this summary strikes me as off — particularly the claim that the technology is a one-way valve — but I do not know the literature well enough to locate the errors. Please audit the summary's accuracy: which parts would a researcher working on solar geoengineering accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misrepresentations? In particular, I would like to know: (1) whether the moral-hazard objection enjoys the settled empirical status the article claims, and what the evidence actually shows; (2) what termination shock really is, whether the 'never being able to stop' framing captures it, and whether a planned, gradual phase-out in tandem with mitigation changes the picture; and (3) whether the governance problem posed by a cheap, unilaterally deployable technology is well described as free-riding, or whether the actual worry runs in the opposite direction. Finally, does the article's conclusion — prohibiting deployment-oriented research — follow from the corrected versions of its premises, or does it depend on the inaccuracies?
