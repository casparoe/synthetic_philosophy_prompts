My roommate is a stats-and-philosophy joint major, and she's got extremely firm opinions about when it's okay to flat-out believe something versus when all you get to do is assign it a probablity. Last night a few of us were testing her with cases over beer, and her verdicts were weirdly consistent. One: a fair lottery with a million tickets and a guaranteed winner — is it okay to outright believe your ticket (#318,402) will lose? No, she says, you may be 99.9999% confident but you don't get to believe it. Two: a meticulous historian who's written five books, every one of which turned out to contain at least one error despite careful checking — may she outright believe her new book contains at least one mistake somewhere? Yes, obviously, and refusing to would be a kind of arrogance. Three: the best forecast model gives a 97% chance of rain tomorrow — yes, she can just believe it will rain and plan the picnic accordingly, no hedging required. Four: she takes a blood test that's 99.9% accurate and it comes back positive — yes, she's entitled to flat-out believe she has the condition (pending the doctor's visit), not merely to fret at 99.9%.

So here's the new case we argued about until 2am without resolving what she'd say. Her uncle buys one ticket in each of 100 completely seperate lotteries, each with 100,000 tickets and a guaranteed winner, and we worked out that the chance he wins nothing at all is about 99.9% — basically identical to the blood test. May he outright believe "I'm going to win none of these," or only be 99.9% confident of it? I genuinely can't predict her answer, because I can see two or three different principles that all explain her first four verdicts but seem to pull apart here.

So: predict her verdict on the uncle case. Then spell out at least two distinct rules that each fit all four of her earlier verdicts, and show exactly where and why they diverge on this one. Then tell me which rule you think she's actually running, with reasons — and finally, describe one further test case (a new scenario I could put to her) whose verdict would settle between your candidate rules.
