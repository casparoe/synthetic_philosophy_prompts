Jeremy Bentham argued, unusually for his time, that consensual sexual conduct between adults in private — including conduct his contemporaries counted among the gravest crimes — was none of the legislator's business, on the grounds that it produced pleasure for the participants and harmed no one else. His reasoning rested on his general utilitarian apparatus: pleasures and pains are to be weighed by their intensity, duration, certainty, fecundity, purity and extent; law should intervene only where conduct worsens the overall balance; and the mere disgust or antipathy of third parties is not the sort of suffering a legislator may legitimately count. Yet Bentham was also an empiricist who expected his conclusions to track the facts about consequences, and some of his factual assumptions — about population, disease, marriage, and the conditions under which consent is reliable — belonged to the decades around 1800.

Reconstruct, with reasons, what Bentham would most plausibly have said about four features of contemporary sexual life he could not have anticipated:

1. The platform-mediated sexual economy: adults selling explicit images of themselves, or sexual services, through subscription platforms that take a cut of the proceeds. No third party is directly affected, but critics argue that such markets channel economically precarious people into choices they later regret, and that the regret is predictable in the aggregate even where each transaction is voluntary.

2. Realistic synthetic imagery: computer-generated sexual imagery depicting real, identifiable people without their consent, circulated among strangers. In some cases the depicted person never learns of it. The interesting question is where, in Bentham's scheme, the harm resides, and whether a harm the victim never experiences counts at all.

3. Relationships between adults where one party is economically or professionally dependent on the other — an employee and her employer, a doctoral candidate and her supervisor. Such relationships are legal and consensual in the thin sense, but critics argue that consent under dependence is compromised even absent any explicit threat.

4. Artificial companions: convincing robotic or virtual sexual partners, used in private, which defenders treat as a harmless private pleasure and critics argue corrode their users' capacity for reciprocal human relationships, with knock-on effects on people who never encounter the devices.

For each case, derive Bentham's position from his actual principles rather than from what a generic modern liberal or a generic modern utilitarian would say, and do not tidy him into a contemporary progressive: where his period assumptions would lead him somewhere a modern reader will find unpalatable, say so. Where his principles underdetermine the answer, identify precisely which premise is missing rather than papering over the gap. In at least one of the four cases, show two of his commitments pulling against each other — for instance, his refusal to count mere antipathy against his own insistence that the fecundity of a pleasure's consequences must be weighed — and say which you think he would sacrifice. State, for each case, what empirical evidence would have moved him, given his self-understanding as a follower of facts rather than of sentiment. Conclude with a general verdict: does his framework survive contact with these cases intact, or does some component — his treatment of disgust, his thin account of consent, his assumption that harms are localised to identifiable and affected persons — require repair, and is that repair available within utilitarianism or only from outside it?
