You are an experienced instructor of ancient philosophy and epistemology who has run many small seminars. I teach a combined course (about 14 undergraduates, one 80-minute session) on the Meno problem: why knowledge should be more valuable than mere true belief. My students reliably nod along when Socrates compares true opinions to the statues of Daedalus that run away unless tied down, but I don't think they feel it -- most of them privately suspect that if the belief is true, who cares how you got it?

Design a structured in-class activity, no lecture beforehand, that makes them feel the difference before it is named. I have in mind something along these lines, though you should improve on it: some participants guide others through a task (a route, a construction, a sequence of choices) where some guides genuinely understand the underlying layout and others hold only correct-but-ungrounded instructions, and the activity is rigged so that pressure, interruption, or a small change in conditions exposes the difference. Crucially, I want the activity to also probe whether stability under pressure is really the whole of knowledge's extra value, or whether there is something else (e.g., the ability to handle novel cases) -- the debrief should let students discover that the tethering metaphor may not fully capture what they just experienced.

Give me: concrete rules, roles, and materials; a payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't involve money; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief script mapping specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical positions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, and be honest about which feature of the knowledge/true-belief distinction the activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by other means. Think carefully, and briefly sketch two candidate designs before committing to one, saying why you rejected the other.
