THE QUESTION: "Explain what Scanlon means by saying an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject. Then explain why this framework resists justifying a serious harm to one person by adding up many small benefits to others (the aggregation problem), and say what Scanlon's own view implies about whether the number of people affected can ever matter, using the transmitter room case if helpful."

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER: "For Scanlon, morality is about justifiability to each person. An act is wrong when it would be forbidden by principles that nobody could reasonably reject, so the test is basically a hypothetical agreement test: we ask what principles people would in fact settle on if they sat down and negotiated under fair conditions, each trying to protect their own interests. Because each person assesses principles from their own standpoint, what matters is the strength of each individual's complaint against a principle, not the total amount of good or bad in the outcome. This is why the view blocks aggregation: in the transmitter room case, where a worker will be electrocuted unless we cut the broadcast of a big match that millions are watching, we may not add up the millions of small disappointments and weigh them against the worker's injury. Each viewer's complaint is trivial compared to his, so we have to shut off the transmitter. Since the size of the group therefore can't enter the reckoning, Scanlon concludes that numbers don't count: if it's one person on each track we should flip a coin, and adding a sixth person to one side changes nothing. So his view rules out standard triage, where emergency doctors treat the larger group first. The general lesson is that moral justification runs to each person separately, not to the group as a whole."
