I am a second-year undergraduate currently taking a course in philosophy of mind, in which we have spent several weeks on the extended mind thesis — the view, defended by Andy Clark and David Chalmers, that cognitive processes can partly be constituted by objects outside the skull, illustrated by their contrast between Otto, who stores information in a notebook he carries everywhere and consults automatically, and Inga, who stores the same information in biological memory.

I also serve as the student representative on my faculty's academic integrity panel, and we have just heard a case that seems to instantiate the thesis in real life. A fellow student sat the closed-book final examination for this very course and was found by the invigilator consulting a notes application on his phone. He does not dispute the facts. His defence is philosophical. The notes, he points out, were written entirely by him, in his own words, across the semester; he consults the app constantly in daily life, retrieves from it fluently, and endorses its contents automatically rather than cross-checking them — which, he argues, satisfies the standard criteria for a resource to count as part of one's own cognitive process rather than as an external aid. On the extended mind view, he says, consulting his app is relevantly like consulting biological memory, so the closed-book rule, as applied to him, is either incoherent or arbitrarily biased toward one kind of memory substrate. Two further facts complicate matters: he has a documented mild impairment of working memory, and his existing accommodations cover extra time but not access to materials; and the examination rubric states explicitly that the assessment measures unaided reconstruction of arguments. Our regulations, meanwhile, prohibit the use of unauthorised materials but nowhere define where a student's materials end and the student begins.

The panel chair has asked me to draft the written opinion we will vote on. Please write it. It should reach a clear, actionable bottom line — uphold the misconduct finding, dismiss it, or uphold it with mitigated sanctions — and it should take the extended mind argument seriously on its philosophical merits rather than treating it as a mere loophole, including by stating the strongest version of the point that ends up on the losing side. Finally, because whatever we decide will be cited in future cases, the opinion should propose a general rule the faculty could actually administer — one that neither collapses into permitting any well-integrated notebook nor simply stipulates that cognition ends at the skin — and should address whether the answer changes when what is being assessed is, by design, unaided performance.
