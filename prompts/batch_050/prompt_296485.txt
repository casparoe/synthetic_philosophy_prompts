I teach a survey course on the ancient world, and every year the unit on the end of the Western Roman Empire produces the same frustrating seminar. My students read a range of modern scholars and discover that the experts cannot even agree on whether there is anything to explain. One camp, drawing on archaeology -- the disappearance of mass-produced pottery, roof tiles, and coinage from provincial sites, falling skeletal stature, the collapse of long-distance trade visible in shipwreck counts -- insists that something genuinely catastrophic happened to ordinary people's living standards in the fifth century, and that calling it a mere "transformation" is a euphemism. Another camp, the late antiquity school, stresses continuity: Roman law, senatorial culture, Christianity, and Latin persisted for centuries, the Eastern empire did not fall at all, and the "fall" is a rhetorical construction we inherited from Gibbon's eighteenth-century narrative of moral decay. A third group now points to climate and disease -- volcanic dust veils in the 530s, the Justinianic plague, shifting weather regimes -- and a fourth still defends external shocks, arguing that the empire was functioning reasonably well until Hunnic pressure cascaded barbarian groups across the frontiers. Meanwhile the old lead-poisoning hypothesis keeps resurfacing in popular treatments despite most specialists treating it as dead.

What strikes me is that each side's evidence seems real. The pessimists' pottery really did vanish; the continuists' texts really were still being copied; the plague really did happen. So I have a hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis: this debate has stalled because "the fall of Rome" is not one explanandum but several -- the collapse of a political apparatus, the decline of material living standards, and the transformation of collective identity -- and the camps are each answering a different one of these questions while treating their answer as a verdict on the others. Political history says something fell in 476; economic archaeology says decline was regionally uneven and partly preceded the political end; cultural history says nothing fell, it changed. If that is right, the disagreement is largely about which proxy counts as "Rome," and no amount of new evidence would settle it, because the evidence is not what divides the sides.

Please diagnose this disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is my hypothesis correct that the sides are answering different questions under one name, or is there a genuine shared question on which they differ about facts, about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data? Note, for instance, that the pessimists lean heavily on material proxies (ice-core lead and copper emissions as indices of mining, shipwreck frequency as an index of trade) while the continuists lean on texts -- is that a difference about what counts as evidence, or does it follow from the different questions? And is there a hidden empirical issue doing work here, such as whether the Eastern empire's survival shows the West's collapse was contingent rather than structural? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a stipulative distinction the field should adopt, a specific kind of evidence that would arbitrate a real sub-dispute, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-formed and should be retired.

In your answer, please be careful to separate what is actually settled among people who work on this period (for example, that the Western imperial administration ceased to function in the fifth century, and that the Eastern empire continued) from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any one school as the consensus when the field is in fact divided.
