I'm in a second-year course on the history of economic thought, and we're on the socialist calculation debate. In last week's seminar, one of the other students presented what she called the standard modern verdict on Mises and Hayek versus Lange, built around an analogy I've since seen repeated in essays and online discussions. Her version ran like this: Lange's planning board was essentially a computer before computers existed, tasked with solving a huge system of simultaneous equations to find market-clearing prices. On this picture, Mises's calculation argument and Hayek's knowledge argument amount to the claim that no 1930s bureau could do the sums fast enough -- an engineering limitation, not an economic one. But, she continued, we now live surrounded by institutions that do exactly this kind of large-scale optimization: a big retailer like Walmart or Amazon runs an internal logistics system that coordinates millions of products without any internal prices, and firms like this are, in effect, planned economies the size of small countries, adjusted hourly by software. So the debate should be closed in Lange's favor: the calculation problem was a compute problem, the compute problem has been solved, and Mises and Hayek turn out to have been right only about the temporary inadequacy of filing cabinets.

I have to write a response paper on this, and I suspect the analogy is smuggling in more than it earns, but I'm struggling to say precisely where. What I'd like is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the debate itself. Specifically: please lay out the mapping explicitly -- what in the planner-solving-equations picture is supposed to correspond to what in the retailer-running-logistics picture, including the roles assigned to equilibrium prices, the planner's iterative trial-and-error procedure, and the firm's internal resource allocation. Then separate the disanalogies into those that are fatal to the conclusion my classmate drew and those that are real but don't actually touch her argument -- I suspect, for instance, that the objection that a firm still buys its inputs on a market matters enormously, while the objection that algorithms make mistakes is beside the point, but I'd like this argued rather than asserted. In particular, I'd like you to address whether Hayek's point about knowledge that exists only dispersed, locally, and often inarticulably is genuinely a claim about processing power, or whether the analogy misclassifies it -- and whether the prices the retailer's software relies on are themselves outputs of the very market process the analogy says could be replaced. Finally: does her conclusion (that the Mises-Hayek objection has been overtaken by technology) survive once the mapping is on the table? And if the retailer analogy is the wrong one, is there a better analogy for whatever truth it contains -- something that captures what modern computation actually changes about the planning problem without overstating it?
