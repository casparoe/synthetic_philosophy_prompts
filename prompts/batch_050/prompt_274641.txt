Consider two well-known experiments in superrational reasoning. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty people each privately decide whether to submit a single entry; if exactly one entry is submitted, its submitter wins an enormous prize, and otherwise no one wins anything. In the Luring Lottery, anyone may submit as many entries as they like, and a fixed jackpot is divided by the total number of entries received, so every additional entry dilutes the prize for everyone. In both cases, the superrational line of thought runs: since every participant faces the same situation and will reason much as I do, I should choose as I would want all of them to choose — which is usually taken to recommend submitting with probability 1/20 in the Platonia dilemma, and submitting very few entries or none in the Luring Lottery. The general moral drawn is that, among like-minded reasoners, this style of thinking reliably outperforms straightforward causal self-interest, which in such games tends to produce overcrowding and ruin.

Construct a new scenario in the same family — symmetric players, no communication, no enforcement, each participant aware that the others reason similarly — in which this moral reverses: agents who choose by asking what it would be best for everyone-reasoning-like-me to do end up foreseeably worse off than agents who simply maximize expected payoff while treating the others' behavior as fixed (but correctly anticipated). "Foreseeably" matters: the superrational players must lose in expectation, not merely in unlucky realizations.

Constraints: the scenario must be statable in one paragraph; the correlation between players' choices may arise only from their similar reasoning about a shared situation — no perfect predictors, no stipulated identical copies, no mind-reading; the payoffs should be mundane (money, time, safety, grades), with no torture or utility monsters; and the superrational players must not lose merely because they are swamped by a population of straightforward defectors — the disadvantage should arise even when everyone in the scenario reasons superrationally.

After presenting the scenario, show that it meets the specification: state exactly what the superrational players do and what the causal self-interested players do, and derive each behavior rather than stipulating it; identify which feature of the game is doing the work — that is, which feature, if removed, restores the usual superiority of superrational cooperation; explain what happens to your scenario if the correlation between like-minded reasoners is assumed to be perfect rather than merely strong, and whether it survives; and finally, give the best response available to a defender of superrationality who objects that your scenario refutes not the idea itself but only a careless application of it — and say honestly whether you think that objection succeeds.
