Since retiring from a career in experimental physics — thirty-odd years in condensed matter, most of them spent ruling things out — I have been reading philosophy of science, and I keep returning to the dispute over string theory and multiverse cosmology. One camp holds that a theory no conceivable observation could test cannot be confirmed at all. The other argues that confirmation can proceed without empirical tests, typically on three grounds: that an exhaustive search has turned up no alternatives to the theory; that the theory unexpectedly explains or unifies things it was not built to explain; and that, as a matter of the discipline's track record, theories enjoying the first two features have tended to be vindicated empirically later on.

What I want to know is whether such non-empirical confirmation is confirmation at all, or merely a policy for allocating research effort. For that I need a thought experiment sharp enough to force the issue, built to the following specifications.

The scenario. Devise two rival fundamental physical theories, developed by separate research communities, that are empirically equivalent not merely in practice but in principle: engineer the world of the case so that no observation accessible to embodied inquirers in it could ever distinguish the theories (you may stipulate energy scales, spacetime regions, or measurement limits that the laws of that world place permanently beyond reach). Then arrange the non-empirical facts so that a defender of non-empirical confirmation must judge one theory better confirmed, while a strict falsificationist or empiricist must judge the two on a par — equally confirmed or equally unconfirmed.

Constraints. The discriminating evidence must be genuinely non-empirical, but it must not be aesthetic: no appeal to beauty, elegance, naturalness, or simplicity as the deciding factor. Only the three grounds named above may discriminate, since those are at least in principle checkable by historians of the field rather than by taste. Note that the second ground, unexpected unification, sits uncomfortably close to the excluded aesthetic virtues; your construction must either keep them clearly apart or concede the point honestly. And there must be no smuggling: the theories may not differ in any actual or possible observation, including low-energy consequences, cosmological signatures, or retrodictions — if they differ empirically in some way the characters simply have not got round to testing, the case fails. Sketch the communities and theories plausibly enough that a working scientist could take the episode seriously, in at most a page.

The demonstration. After the scenario: state each side's verdict and derive it from the position rather than stipulating it. Identify the single feature of your construction whose presence forces the two verdicts apart, and say what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse into agreement, and on which verdict? Give the strongest attack the empiricist could mount, presumably that whatever discriminates between permanently equivalent theories cannot be confirmation but only a heuristic about where to invest. Finally, tell me what your case is actually a case of: does it show that non-empirical confirmation is genuine confirmation, or only that rational scientists would behave as if it were, and what further consideration would settle that?
