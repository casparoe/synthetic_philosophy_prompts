Two colleagues in my department's reading group on classical Chinese philosophy, Elena and David, have been arguing for weeks about whether Mencius and Xunzi actually disagree about human nature or only appear to. I have stayed out of it, but they have now asked me to referee, and I would like a more careful adjudication than I can produce myself. The exchange, reconstructed as best I can:

(1) Elena opened with the deflationary case: the dispute is terminological. Mencius reserves the term xing for whatever distinguishes human beings from other animals -- the incipient moral responses he calls sprouts -- while Xunzi reserves it for whatever is present at birth and cannot be acquired through learning. Each can grant everything the other says: Xunzi need not deny that humans have some native responsiveness to others' suffering, and Mencius does not deny that spontaneous desires lead people into conflict. They attach the same label to different things and then talk past each other.

(2) David answered that the label is the least of it. For Mencius, becoming good is the growth of something already inside us: the sprouts are genuine moral beginnings, and cultivation extends them. For Xunzi, becoming good is wei -- deliberate artifice imposed on recalcitrant material, the way warped wood is steamed straight or clay is shaped on the potter's wheel. Those are two different pictures of what moral effort does to a person, and of whether virtue realizes the self or disciplines it.

(3) Elena replied that metaphors are not doctrines. Compare the practical upshot and the two converge: both prescribe study of the classics, the guidance of teachers, deliberate choice of environment, and sustained ritual practice; both hold that essentially anyone can become good and that most people fail. If anything, Mencius's own admissions narrow the gap further -- his sprouts are tiny, fragile, and easily destroyed by bad surroundings, which is the point of his own example of barley flourishing or failing depending on the soil it is planted in. Two philosophers who agree about the diagnosis, the treatment, and the prognosis disagree only about what to call the patient.

(4) David's final move was that the disagreement reappears one level down, in moral epistemology. For Mencius, consulting the heart's spontaneous responses -- the alarm anyone would feel at seeing a small child about to fall into a well -- is a route to knowing what is right; the heart, properly attended to, is itself a standard. For Xunzi, the untransformed heart is precisely what cannot be trusted, and the standard lies outside present motivation, in the ritual order instituted by the sage kings. Hence also their different accounts of moral failure: for Mencius it is the starvation of the sprouts, for Xunzi the failure to impose form on the raw material. A dispute about whether moral knowledge extends what we already feel or corrects it, David said, is not a dispute about words.

Please adjudicate. Assess each of the four moves on its own terms -- where it succeeds, where it overreaches -- rather than merely declaring a winner. Then answer the underlying question: is the Mencius--Xunzi dispute verbal, substantive, or does that very distinction break down here (for instance, if a disagreement over which human capacity deserves to be treated as authoritative is itself a normative claim rather than a lexical one)? Finally, what, if anything, are both of them missing?
