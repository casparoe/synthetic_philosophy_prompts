I am a PhD candidate in philosophy, working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and this term I am TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice (12 students, one 90-minute meeting, no prior exposure to non-expected-utility theories). For our session on Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, I want a runnable in-class activity that makes the students produce the phenomenon themselves before they ever hear the theory's name.

The design constraint I care most about is this: I want the activity to rule out the standard dodge. When students' choices violate expected utility, the first thing a clever student says is that their utility over the stakes is nonlinear, and then the whole demonstration collapses. So please build the activity around lotteries over a small fixed set of prizes — say, three concrete, non-monetary prizes of clearly different desirability that I bring to class, plus real probability mixtures implemented with physical randomization devices (dice, a spinner, or a deck) so that the probabilities are transparent and trusted. Over a sequence of pairwise choices with small real stakes, their revealed preferences should be jointly unrepresentable by any utility assignment to the three prizes, and the activity should include the moment where they verify this themselves: a stage where each student plots or tabulates their own choices in a way that makes the linearity required by expected utility visible, so that the class can see, from their own responses, that no rescaling of prize utilities can save them. Only after that impossibility is on the board should the notion of weighting outcomes by their rank in the gamble be introduced, ideally by having the students fit a simple risk function to their own data.

Please give me: the exact choice problems with numbers, sequenced so the modal pattern of responses does the work; the incentive structure and how to keep the stakes small but real; a facilitation script with timing, including what to do with the votes/choices before any theory is mentioned; and a debrief plan that maps each stage of the activity onto a component of the risk-weighted view.

The debrief section should also anticipate, concretely, what will predictably go wrong and how I should handle it: the student whose choices really are expected-utility-consistent (and whether that is a problem for the activity or an asset for the debrief); noisy or indifferent responders; the student who insists they just like gambling or variance, in a vocabulary that predates the theory; and the student who has encountered prospect theory and claims this is all just probability weighting — I need a crisp way to show, using the activity's own materials, what distinguishes rank-dependent weighting from reference-dependent probability distortion, since I expect this conflation and I do not want to resolve it by fiat.

Finally, be frank about the limits of the exercise: tell me which features of the theory the activity cannot reproduce — I assume the axiomatic content, the normative claim that risk-weighted preferences can be rational rather than merely descriptively common, and the global character of the risk function are candidates, but tell me if I am wrong — and give me a short way to supply each of these in the remaining discussion time without lecturing for more than ten minutes.
