I am a novelist, and I am trying to finish a book in a voice I can no longer consult. For twenty years my first reader was my oldest friend, a philosopher, now dead; every plot I devised that turned on whether some character was still the same person, she would rule on, and her rulings became the private canon of my fiction. She liked to ground her view in a sentence from John Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII (Of Identity and Diversity), where Locke writes: "In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity: an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse." Her conviction, much in the spirit of Douglas Hofstadter's later writings, was that a person, like Locke's oak, is a persisting pattern rather than a parcel of stuff — and that such a pattern can survive, wholly or in part, in substrates and skulls other than the original. Her recorded verdicts: (a) a woman who loses every autobiographical memory in an accident but keeps her skills, her wit, and her way of attending to people is entirely the same person, and her family wrongs her by treating her as gone; (b) a dying man whose brain is scanned and instantiated in two robotic bodies at once survives twice over — each successor is fully him, and their later divergence changes nothing retrospectively; (c) a dead writer whose protégé has so internalized his books and letters that she argues, jokes, and notices as he did genuinely persists in her, as a partial, coarse-grained continuation; (d) a prisoner whose memories are left intact but whose dispositions, humor, and characteristic style of thought are erased by re-education is no longer the man his wife loved, whatever the witness-box facts. My new plot presents a case I never put to her: by a freak accident — a bolt of lightning in a marsh, let us say — matter assembles into a being molecule-for-molecule identical to a woman who died that morning, with her memories, her style of mind, her everything, yet with no causal connection to her whatsoever. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, with no introduction or conclusion and no more than two sentences each: (1) the verdict my friend would most likely have given, and with what confidence; (2) which of the four earlier cases is the closest precedent, and why it beats the runner-up; (3) the principle that best unifies her four verdicts and what it predicts here; (4) the strongest rival principle equally consistent with all four verdicts, how it diverges on my plot, and what further case would have told the two principles apart; (5) whether her view reads Locke's "something else" faithfully or extends it past breaking — that is, whether a pattern with no continuous history remains the same living creature in Locke's sense.
