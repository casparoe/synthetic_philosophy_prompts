Two of my graduate students have been arguing about the Pasadena game, and I've stayed out of it so far. The setup: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if it first lands heads on flip n, the player receives (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars, so the prize alternates in sign while the payoffs' magnitudes shrink harmonically. Daniel says the expected value of the game is undefined -- the sum is conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem it can be made to equal anything, meaning no number summarizes the prospect, and any stated price to play is therefore ungrounded. Maya says that's too fast: the game strictly dominates not playing only in one direction, and more importantly, decision theory has non-expected-value resources -- she points to symmetry arguments and to the fact that truncating the game at any finite cap gives a positive expectation, so rationality still delivers a verdict even where expectation does not. Daniel replies that positive truncated expectations at every finite cutoff prove nothing, since the limit of those expectations is exactly the undefined sum, and that "every finite version favors playing" is compatible with the infinite version being genuinely indeterminate. Maya counters that this makes the infinite case an artifact: any game actually played terminates within the lifetime of the universe, so the conditional-convergence pathology only appears when we idealize, and a decision theory that returns no verdict precisely in the idealized case is failing at its job. Daniel's last move: by that logic all St. Petersburg-style reasoning collapses into claims about bankroll constraints, and we've lost the original puzzle rather than solved it.
