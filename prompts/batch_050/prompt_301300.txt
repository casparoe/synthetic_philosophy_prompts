I am a novelist at work on a book in which several characters are judged — by one another and, I hope, by the reader — for outcomes that were only partly in their hands: a driver who kills a pedestrian on a wet road while his equally careless brother drives home without incident; a woman who abandons her family for a vocation and is vindicated by success in a way her stay-at-home counterpart never could be; a man whose cruelty everyone condemns, though his temperament was forged entirely by a childhood he did not choose. My early readers keep disagreeing about whether the book condemns these characters fairly, and I have realised the disagreements are not about my prose but about where my readers stand on moral luck.

I would like a decision tree that I can put to my characters (and, mischievously, to my readers) to sort them into the main positions on moral luck. Please construct it as follows.

First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: a sequence of diagnostic questions, each a concrete case or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that someone could answer it without ever having heard of Nagel or Williams. Include cases of resultant luck (identical recklessness, different outcomes), circumstantial luck (the collaborator who would have been a resister had the war not come), and constitutive luck (temperament one did not choose), plus at least one case that separates blaming practices from moral assessment itself. Where it helps, a question may take the form: would your verdict on this character change if the story ended one chapter earlier, or with one different roll of the dice?

Second, locate the actual positions as leaves of the tree: strict adherence to the control principle (no moral luck, full stop); the view that luck infects outcomes but never blameworthiness as such; the Nagelian concession that moral luck is pervasive and ineliminable; a broadly consequentialist willingness to assess by results; a virtue-theoretic focus on character that absorbs constitutive luck but resists resultant luck; and a sceptical leaf that denies robust blameworthiness altogether. Note any combinations of answers that no defended position occupies, and say briefly whether each empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can devise, say so explicitly rather than forcing a distinction.

Third, give me a one-paragraph author's note on which leaf my own plotting instincts, as described above, most plausibly occupy, and what I would have to change in the manuscript to relocate it elsewhere.

Please indicate how confident you are that the tree actually partitions these positions cleanly, and single out the single diagnostic question or leaf assignment you are least sure of.
