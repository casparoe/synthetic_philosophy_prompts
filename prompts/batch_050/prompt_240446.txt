I am preparing a graduate seminar on consequentialism and want to put the so-called cluelessness objection under sustained pressure. The objection, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: the total consequences of any action ramify indefinitely into the future in ways that are utterly unpredictable — a trivial kindness today may, through causal chains no one can trace, contribute to outcomes of enormous significance centuries hence. If consequentialism judges actions by their total consequences, and those consequences are largely unknowable, then consequentialism cannot guide action, and the objection concludes that it thereby fails as a moral theory (or at least as a practical one).

The standard responses I already have on hand, which I do not need rehearsed, are these:

1. The tu quoque response: cluelessness afflicts every action-guiding theory, since any action whatsoever has untraceable ripple effects, so it cannot count specially against consequentialism.
2. The symmetry response: for any distant catastrophe we might unknowingly cause, there is a roughly equiprobable symmetric benefit, so the unknown consequences cancel out in expectation and we may simply act on the foreseeable near-term effects.
3. The criterion-versus-decision-procedure response: consequentialism supplies a standard of rightness, not a method of deliberation; cluelessness shows at most that we cannot know which acts are right, not that the standard itself is defective, and an agent may rationally rely on rules of thumb while the standard stands untouched.
4. The epistemic modesty response: we are not entirely clueless — we possess non-trivial evidence about the long-run effects of broad classes of action (institution-building, education, catastrophe prevention), so the objection exaggerates our ignorance.
5. The inversion response: cluelessness, combined with the vast scale of the future, does not paralyze consequentialist deliberation but redirects it toward actions whose effects are robust across a wide range of contingencies, such as reducing the risk of premature human extinction.
6. The proves-too-much response: taken seriously, cluelessness would equally undermine all moral praise, blame, and decision-making, and a consideration that sweeps away morality wholesale is better treated as a general puzzle about uncertainty than as an objection to one theory.

I would like one further response to the cluelessness objection that is not on this list — and, to be precise about what counts, it must not be a rewording, special case, or conjunction of any of the six above, nor a mere strengthening of one of them with more detail. After presenting it, explain how it relates to the responses already listed (does it undercut one of them, presuppose one, occupy a dialectical gap they all leave open?), and then give your honest assessment of whether it is any good — including the strongest reason to doubt it.
