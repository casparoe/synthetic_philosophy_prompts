At a philosophy reading group last week we discussed Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer — in the standard version, a person has a device implanted with settings running from 0 (no pain) up to 1000 (unbearable agony); each step upward increases the pain by an amount too small to be felt, and each step upward earns a cash payment. After the session, two members, Mara and Dev, argued for an hour about what the puzzle actually diagnoses. I stayed out of it, but I keep going back and forth, so I'd like a referee.

(1) Mara opened by claiming the puzzle shows that rational preferences can be intransitive. At every setting, the self-torturer prefers the next one up — the money difference is real and the pain difference is imperceptible — yet no sane person prefers setting 1000 to setting 0. Since each local preference is rational and the global preference is rational, the consistent reading is that rationality simply does not require transitivity, and the agent's real mistake lies elsewhere, perhaps in never having formed a stopping plan.

(2) Dev replied that the preferences need not be intransitive once the options are individuated properly. The genuine options at each moment are not 'setting n' versus 'setting n+1' but whole trajectories: 'keep turning and live with the consequences' versus 'stop here.' Preferences over trajectories can be perfectly transitive — the agent ranks stopping at 5 above stopping at 50 above stopping at 900 — and the irrationality consists in treating each turn of the dial as an isolated, costless act rather than adopting a policy for the sequence as a whole. The puzzle targets myopic, stepwise choice, not the logic of preference.

(3) Mara answered that the trajectory maneuver dissolves every paradox by redescription and therefore proves too much: any sequence of choices can be relabeled as one grand option, which would make diachronic rationality failure impossible by definition. She then offered a variant. Make the dial freely reversible, but charge a small fee each time the direction of turning is reversed. Now, she argued, the agent can be pumped: he turns upward collecting payments while the pain stays imperceptible, reverses downward once the pain becomes salient, and repeats indefinitely, bleeding fees. A real money pump, she said — and money pumps only get traction against agents with cyclic preferences, so intransitivity is the correct diagnosis after all.

(4) Dev's response was that the reversible variant changes nothing. A transitive agent with one stable ranking never cycles, fee or no fee; what cycles is an agent whose evaluations are recomputed locally at each moment, so the pump exploits the local-evaluation procedure rather than any intransitivity. He added that Mara's original case does not even contain intransitive preferences — it contains intransitive indifference. Because adjacent settings are indistinguishable in pain, the agent is pain-indifferent between n and n+1 all the way up, but not between 0 and 1000; and indifference generated by just-noticeable differences (the stock example being grains of sugar in coffee) is notoriously non-transitive without any irrationality. Strip that out and what remains is a gap between what is better at each step and what is better overall — a problem about the separability of choices, not about preference logic.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't.
