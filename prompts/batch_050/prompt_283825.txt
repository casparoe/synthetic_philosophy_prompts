Since retiring (I was a physical chemist, mostly spectroscopy) I've gotten sucked into philosophy of mind via a reading group, and there's a pattern in the mind-uploading debates that keeps bugging me. I want to know if it has a name, because I keep needing to refer to it.

Here's the pattern. Pretty much everyone in the group accepts some version of the thought experiment where your neurons get swapped out one at a time for little silicon devices that do exactly the same job, spread over years — and almost everyone says you're still you at the end, since you never noticed anything and there was no single moment where "you" stopped. Fine. But then someone says: okay, identical devices, identical operations, only we do them all in one afternoon while you're under. Now most of the group flips — that's death, and whatever wakes up is a copy. What strikes me is that the only variable that changed is the schedule. No individual replacement differs, and nobody has proposed any physical mechanism by which the rate could matter — in my old field, when slow and fast versions of a process give genuinely different results, you can point to the mechanism (think quasistatic vs. irreversible), but here there's nothing like that on offer. One friend bit the bullet and said there must be some critical frequency where you cross from survival into death, like a phase transition, and of course nobody can say where it is or why it would be anywhere in particular. And it gets worse: anasthesia and deep sleep look like little gaps in the "unbroken stream" that's supposedly doing the justificatory work in the slow case, so the slow case's own rationale seems shaky too.

So, three parts: (1) Is there an established name for this — the phenomenon, or the position, that verdicts about whether you survive flip purely on the rate or schedule of replacement while every individual operation is held fixed? I know the general neighborhood is covered by teleporter-style arguments, and someone mentioned a philosopher called Parfit, but I mean this specific rate-dependence, not the whole debate. (2) If there's no name, coin one, define it precisely, and give me some boundary cases — does the same thing show up in how people think about cryonics, split-brain patients, organ transplants? (3) If you have a view, say where the intuition actually comes from psychologically. Please keep the whole answer under 500 words; my group meets Thursday and I can't inflict an essay on them.
