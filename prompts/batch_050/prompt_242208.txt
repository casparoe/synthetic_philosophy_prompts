In the fourth century BCE the state of Qin, then a marginal frontier kingdom, adopted the reforms associated with Shang Yang: ranks and land granted for military merit, agricultural production rewarded and idleness penalized, households registered in mutually responsible groups liable for one another's crimes, and uniform, publicly promulgated law applied regardless of birth. By 221 BCE Qin had conquered its six rivals and unified China. Fifteen years later the dynasty lay in ruins: the First Emperor died in 210 BCE, the succession was manipulated at court, the administration turned erratic and punitive even by Qin's own standards, and widespread revolt brought the empire down by 206 BCE. The succeeding Han dynasty publicly condemned Qin's harshness while quietly retaining most of its administrative machinery. Analyze this episode as a test of Han Feizi's Legalism, the doctrine that order depends not on the virtue of rulers or ministers but on clear law, impartial rewards and punishments, and techniques of control that make officials' private interests align with the state's. Run the analysis in both directions. On the theory's side: what does Legalism say the Qin reformers faced and did right, and does the dynasty's meteoric rise confirm the claim that incentives and institutional design outperform rule by virtuous exemplars? On the episode's side: is the collapse an anomaly Legalism can absorb — the standard reply being that the machinery was only as good as the man operating it, and that after 210 BCE the operator either would not or could not run it — or does the collapse expose a structural gap in the theory itself, namely that a system designed to make everyone accountable except the ruler has no answer to the succession problem and no defense against capture of the apparatus from within? Address the rival Confucian reading, on which Qin fell because harshness without benevolence forfeits legitimacy, and say whether the Han synthesis — Confucian rhetoric layered over Legalist structure — vindicates Han Feizi, refutes him, or shows that the two positions were never as opposed as their partisans claimed.
