I sit on the board of a charitable foundation I helped found twelve years ago. Our charter says we exist to relieve severe poverty, but in practice everything runs through an internal scoring system: program officers are promoted based on the measured cost-effectiveness of their grants, and grantees are renewed based on hitting pre-agreed targets. Two things trouble me. First, our grantees have become strikingly good at hitting their targets — yet when I visit their projects, I often struggle to see the connection between what is being counted and the suffering we originally set out to relieve. Second, the foundation's own sense of its mission seems to have quietly migrated: nobody ever voted to change it, but the way we now talk in meetings about what counts as "impact" would be unrecognizable to the people who drafted the charter, and every step of that migration seemed, at the time, like a small and sensible refinement. When I raise these worries, my colleagues point out that our numbers have never been better. I suspect they are answering the wrong question, but I cannot articulate what the right question is — nor how I could even tell the difference between an organization whose measures are being gamed and one that is genuinely succeeding. What, if anything, is actually going wrong here, and what concretely should I do about it?
