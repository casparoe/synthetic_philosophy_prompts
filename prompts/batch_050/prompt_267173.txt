I'm giving a forty-minute seminar next month at an animal welfare research institute, followed by a thirty-minute Q&A, defending a thesis I expect most of the room to dislike. My thesis: the standard question "does beef or chicken cause more harm per calorie?" has no diet-independent answer, because the ranking flips once you do the accounting honestly. On my accounting, the comparison must charge each diet not only for the farmed animals raised and killed, but for (i) the wild animals killed or displaced by the crop cultivation that feeds the farmed animals (which falls much harder on chicken, given feed conversion), and (ii) what I call a counterfactual habitat ledger: the wild-animal welfare, positive or negative, that exists on grazing land relative to whatever that land would realistically become if the beef weren't produced (reforestation, row crops, continued grassland). Since (ii) inherits all the uncertainty about whether wild animal lives are net negative on average, my conclusion is deliberately deflationary: the honest output is a conditional ranking — a table of who wins under which assumptions about nature's valence and land-use counterfactuals — rather than a verdict.

The audience has three factions. About half are quantitative welfare researchers who think in welfare-range-adjusted suffering-years and cost-effectiveness estimates, and who will be hostile to anything that looks like motivated reasoning toward a beef-friendly conclusion. One senior conservation ecologist is visiting; she regards claims about net wild-animal suffering as speculative overreach and will say so. Three or four moral philosophers with abolitionist sympathies will resist the entire framework of comparing harms rather than objecting to use.
