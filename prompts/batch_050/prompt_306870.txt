So I'm the "poetry person" in a small philosophy reading group (we meet in someone's garage, it's very informal) and we're finishing Epicurus next week. I got tapped to write a closing poem, and I want it to be about the passage in the Letter to Menoeceus that got under my skin more than the famous "when we are, death is not" bit -- it's this one:

"Foolish, therefore, is the person who says that he fears death, not because it will pain when it comes, but because it pains in the prospect. Whatever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation."

Here's the thing I want the poem to capture. I believe the argument. I've gone over it a dozen times and I can't find the hole in it (my friend Dave insists the hole is that being deprived of future goods is bad even if nobody's around to feel deprived, but that's a separate fight). And yet I still lie awake at 3am with my heart pounding about it. The argument wins on paper every single time and the fear keeps showing up anyway, like it never got the memo. That mismatch -- being completely convinced and completely unconsoled at once -- is the poem I want.

You're an expert on Hellenistic philosophy who also publishes poetry, so take this seriously as both. Write me a villanelle where the two refrains ARE the two sides of this: one refrain should be Epicurus's point that the fear of death is groundless because the thing feared will never be present to hurt us, and the other should be the voice of the 3am fear itself. The whole point of choosing a villanelle is that the fear-refrain keeps reasserting itself no matter how many stanzas the argument gets -- the form should enact the way a refuted fear keeps coming back. And in the closing quatrain, when the two refrains finally sit side by side, I want that juxtaposition to land as the real philosophical question Epicurus's sentence dodges: is the prospective pain actually "groundless" just because its object never arrives? Brainstorm a few candidate refrain pairs before you commit, then give me the final poem. The quoted passage doesn't count toward anything; the poem itself should be a proper villanelle, 19 lines.
