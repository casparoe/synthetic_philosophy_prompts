Yesterday I spent several hours arguing with a friend about consequentialist cluelessness. Her view was that consequentialism is practically paralyzing: because any apparently good action may have enormous, unpredictable effects on the far future, we often cannot know whether its total consequences are better or worse than those of doing nothing. I replied that this conclusion is too strong. Some actions may be robustly good under many causal stories, even if their very long-term effects remain unknowable. To keep the dispute precise, distinguish three forms of cluelessness: (1) **sign cluelessness**, where we do not know whether the total value of an action is positive or negative; (2) **ranking cluelessness**, where we know that several options are good but not which is best; and (3) **compositional cluelessness**, where an action has a foreseeable near-term benefit but also an unpredictable “causal residue” that may dominate its total value. Write an essay examining whether these forms of cluelessness show that consequentialism cannot guide action in the cases that matter most, or whether they instead reveal a need for more sophisticated decision rules.

Organize the essay around the following case. A government can fund either a well-tested malaria-prevention program, whose immediate benefits are substantial and highly predictable, or a research institute whose work has a modest chance of producing technology that would greatly improve humanity’s long-term future but also some chance of causing severe harm. Call the first a **locally robust intervention** and the second a **speculative trajectory intervention**. Assess whether the unknowable indirect effects of malaria prevention—on population, institutions, economic development, later technologies, and countless future lives—make it no more rationally choosable than the institute. In doing so, consider whether symmetrical uncertainty really cancels out, whether the possibility of extraordinarily large future effects should swamp reliable present benefits, and whether an agent may rationally rely on proximate evidence while remaining clueless about total consequences. You should also address the objection that any supposedly robust intervention merely pushes uncertainty farther down the causal chain rather than eliminating it.

Reach and defend a definite conclusion. You may argue that cluelessness is devastating for consequentialist decision-making, that it matters only in a restricted class of comparisons, or that it supports some hybrid standard—such as giving extra weight to interventions whose value is insensitive to speculative causal assumptions. Whatever position you take, explain exactly which form of cluelessness does the philosophical work, and state what your conclusion implies for the malaria-versus-institute choice. The essay should not merely summarize the problem; it should identify the strongest version of the paralysis argument, expose its central assumption, and either vindicate that assumption or show where it fails.
