Last weekend I had a long and somewhat heated argument with an old friend about how to think about transformative AI, and I want to put the crux of it to you. His view is that the nuclear analogy is the one to organise policy thinking around: a small number of actors racing towards a capability with civilisation-level destructive potential, where the historical lessons are that arms-control treaties, test bans, export controls on the key input, and an international inspection regime are the right instruments, and that the shameful thing would be to sleepwalk into a race the way the great powers did before 1945. I pushed back that AI looks far more like the printing press -- a general-purpose technology that diffuses widely, gets built by thousands of independent actors, and reshapes institutions over decades in ways no treaty could have governed -- and that his analogy flatters the idea that a handful of states and laboratories can simply agree to hold the thing still. He replied that the printing-press analogy is what people reach for precisely when they want an excuse to do nothing, and we more or less stalled there.

What I'd like is an audit of his nuclear analogy specifically, since it's the one doing real work in our dispute. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of fissile material, of the weapons test, of deterrence, of the non-proliferation treaty, of the inspectorate. Then the disanalogies, separating the ones that actually matter for his governance conclusion from the ones that are merely interesting -- I'm particularly unsure whether it matters that AI capability is diffused knowledge rather than a scarce physical input, and that there's no analogue of a weapons test, no observable moment at which someone 'has the bomb'. Say what the analogy quietly imports that the AI case lacks (state-centric control being feasible, secrecy being possible, capability being countable), and whether his conclusion -- arms-control-style governance now -- survives once those are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it. If there's a better historical analogy for the specific question of governance instruments, name it; if you think the whole business of reasoning from historical analogy here is unsound and my friend and I were arguing about the wrong thing, say that plainly rather than finding a diplomatic middle position. At most 600 words.
