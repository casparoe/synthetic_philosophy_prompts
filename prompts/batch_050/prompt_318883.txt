I teach a jurisprudence seminar for third-year philosophy majors, about 14 students, and we get one 90-minute session on self-amending legal systems before we read anything on it. I want a game, not a lecture. Design me a playable activity where the students run a tiny legal system whose rules can be amended by the rules' own procedures, and where the rules are engineered so the group stumbles into the paradoxes themselves rather than me pointing at them: the amendment clause that can be used to amend itself, the moment two rules both claim to be valid and contradict each other, and the moment they realize that "is this amendment legal?" has no answer inside the system and someone has to just... decide.

What I'm after concretely:

- The initial rulebook. Small enough to fit on one page, but rigged so the trouble is latent in it, not scripted by me. I want them to discover that the amendment rule covers itself, ideally mid-game when someone tries a "coup" move like entrenching their own favorite rule as unamendable. Tell me the actual starting rules, in the actual wording, because the wording is where the loopholes live.
- Mechanics: turns, proposals, voting, how a rule change gets "enacted," what players are playing *for* (some lightweight win condition or point scheme so they have incentives to exploit the rules, not just discuss them -- otherwise it'll turn into a seminar discussion with dice).
- The referee question, which I think is the heart of it: when players dispute whether an amendment is valid, who rules on it? I suspect the right design is that the game starts with a referee but the referee's authority is itself conferred by a rule, so a sufficiently bold coalition can try to amend it away. Tell me whether to let that happen or block it, and why.
- A debrief plan mapping what happened onto the theory: the coup attempt onto the entrenchment/self-amendment puzzle, the validity dispute onto why every system bottoms out in a practice rather than another rule (I'll connect it to Hart's rule of recognition after), the referee problem onto ultimate interpretive authority. For each, one or two discussion questions that start from "remember when X tried to..." rather than from the reading.
- What will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it -- I'm guessing deadlocks, one loud student speedrunning the coup in minute five before anyone understands the stakes, and someone asking "but is that allowed?" and the room going quiet in the good way vs the bad way. Tell me how to handle each without killing the mood.
- And be honest about what the game can't reproduce: real systems have coercion, stakes, and a social practice that predates and outlasts any rulebook, and my students will have none of that. What do I need to say in the debrief to keep them from over-generalizing from the game?

No readings, no bibliography -- I'll do the connecting-to-the-literature part myself. Just the game, run-tested in your head.
