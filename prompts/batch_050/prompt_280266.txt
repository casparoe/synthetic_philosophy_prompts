A few months ago I joined a small reading group on the metaphysics of science, and we are currently working through the debate between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature. I find myself drawn to the Humean best-system picture — roughly, the idea that the laws of nature are those generalizations that would figure in the ideal summary of all particular matters of fact, where the ideal summary is fixed by the best overall balance of simplicity and informativeness — but I have run into a worry that nobody in the group has been able to settle for me, and I would appreciate a careful resolution.

The worry is this. If the laws are nothing over and above the best systematization of the particular facts, then it seems to me that the laws must be in a state of continual revision. Every time something new happens — a coin lands heads in my kitchen, a star goes supernova in a distant galaxy — the totality of particular facts grows, and so the summary that best balances simplicity and strength over that totality presumably shifts, even if only by a vanishingly small amount. The laws that held yesterday are therefore not quite the same laws that hold today. This leads me to two questions. First, how can Humeans coherently maintain that the laws of nature are stable across time, given that their own account ties the laws to a body of particular fact that is constantly expanding? Second, does the picture imply that sufficiently unusual events literally rewrite the laws — so that, for instance, if experimental physicists observe a genuinely unprecedented kind of particle decay next year, the laws of physics will be slightly different after the observation than they were before it? If so, that strikes me as a steep price to pay, since it seems to undermine the sense in which laws could ever be relied upon in advance.

I should mention that several members of my reading group think I have misunderstood something fairly basic about the account, but they disagree among themselves about what exactly the mistake is. So beyond answering my two questions as posed, I would be grateful if you could identify where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does, and explain how the best-system analysis is actually meant to conceive of the relationship between the laws and the unfolding of particular events.
