I help run a small film discussion group in my town — none of us has any academic background, we just like arguing about movies — and our next pick is The Bridge on the River Kwai. I want to prepare something more interesting to say about it than the usual remarks about honor and madness, and I keep coming back to an idea I ran into recently, the so-called dollar auction. As I understand it: an auctioneer offers a dollar bill under ordinary-seeming rules, with one twist — the highest bidder wins the dollar, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid and gets nothing. Bidding starts innocently, but once two people are in, each faces the choice between losing what they have already bid and bidding again, and so a dollar can sell for five, and both bidders end up ruined. What grips me is that every individual step is, in a narrow sense, the reasonable one, and the disaster comes from the accumulation of steps nobody quite chose. I should say I have never studied game theory or philosophy; I just find this structure uncomfortably familiar.

What I'd like is a reading of the film through this lens, done seriously in both directions. Colonel Nicholson begins by resisting the Japanese on a point of principle, and ends up so invested in building the best possible bridge that the bridge matters more to him than the war it serves. Is his arc an escalation trap in the dollar-auction mold — each act of commitment raising the cost of walking away, until he is defending the bridge against his own side? Or is something different driving him — craftsmanship, pride, a code of duty — something the auction model flattens rather than explains? I'd also like to know whether the frame illuminates the other side of the story: the commando team sent to destroy the bridge, and Shears, who escapes and is sent back, make their own escalating investments, and the film seems to set the two escalations on a collision course. Where does the analogy break down — is the dollar auction's trap essentially about irrecoverable costs, while Nicholson's investments seem to him to be building something of real value? And in the final scene, when he seems to recognize what he has done, does the film vindicate the economic reading or rebuke it?

Most of all, I'd like to know what the film understands about escalation that the abstract game cannot. The auction explains the trap from outside, mechanically; the film makes us feel, I think, why Nicholson cannot see it from inside, and what it costs others — his men, Saito, the commandos — to be caught in someone else's commitment. If you think the reading fails at some point, or that I'm forcing it, please say so honestly rather than rescuing it; and point me to the particular scenes a discussion group should linger on.
