I direct the performance office of a mid-sized American city — roughly 300,000 residents — and four times a year I chair the review that decides which departmental indicators appear on our public dashboard and, more consequentially, which get tied to budget allocations and the city manager's scorecard. I was burned last spring: after two years of steady improvement in our reported police response times to priority calls, a local reporter demonstrated that a large share of the gains came from dispatchers reclassifying calls into lower-priority categories at intake. The council hearing was unpleasant, the police chief felt ambushed, and I have concluded that I can no longer evaluate proposed indicators by instinct. I know the broad shape of the problem — the principle usually credited to the economist Charles Goodhart, that a measure conscripted into service as a target stops measuring what it once measured, and the sociologist Donald Campbell's parallel warning that leaning on a social indicator for decisions corrupts the very process it was monitoring. But a principle is not a procedure, and a procedure is what I need.

A colleague sketched for me four distinct ways a metric can fail once pressure is applied, and I would like the procedure built around testing for each in turn: first, the metric may only ever have correlated with the real goal in an environment where nobody was trying to move it, so the correlation itself is an artifact of the unobserved regime; second, the correlation may hold in the middle of the range while the pressure pushes the metric to extremes where it no longer tracks the goal; third, the metric may be a downstream symptom rather than a cause, so forcing it to move leaves the goal untouched while destroying the metric's value as information; and fourth, the people being measured understand the metric and will find the cheapest available way to move it, which is rarely the way that serves the goal. Give me a two-stage instrument: a pre-deployment screen that any proposed indicator must pass before it reaches the dashboard, and an annual audit for indicators already in use. Each stage should be an ordered sequence of tests or questions I can run in about ninety minutes per indicator using what a city performance office realistically has — historical time series, department self-reports, the occasional spot audit — not an econometrics lab. Mark explicitly which steps are genuine judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, flag which of the four failure modes each test is and is not capable of detecting, and at each step anticipate what a department head who wants to keep a bad metric will say, because that is who I am actually arguing with across the table.

Then apply the procedure to three cases from my current docket: (a) police response time to priority calls, now under formal suspicion of reclassification gaming; (b) the share of pothole complaints closed within thirty days, which the public works director wants promoted from a published indicator to a budget-tied target and which strikes me as comparatively safe; and (c) the share of cardiac-arrest patients whom ambulance crews deliver to hospital with a pulse, which the fire chief insists is beyond gaming because a pulse cannot be faked — though I note he has so far resisted my questions about who counts as a patient in the first place. Run each case through the full sequence, and make sure at least one comes out in a way I will dislike: the procedure should either recommend keeping or promoting a metric I suspect, or retiring one I have publicly defended, and it should say plainly which and why rather than hedging into a verdict I can interpret however I like. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — which classes of failure it will structurally miss, and whether the right response to that residue is rotating indicators, randomized audits, pairing each metric with an adversarial counterpart, or simply conceding that certain municipal goals resist quantification and saying so to the council in public.
