Some background: I spent 35 years as an experimental condensed matter physicist and retired in 2019, and like a lot of bored retired scientists I've fallen down the AI safety rabbit hole — papers, blogs, the occasional textbook chapter when I can stomach it. My old lab group still meets monthly over Zoom and lately we've been arguing about what sorts of guarantees one can reasonably demand of advanced AI systems. Before our next call I want to check wether my own positions actually cohere, because I have a nagging feeling they don't and I can't locate the problem myself. Here is roughly where I've landed: (a) In ordinary decisions under uncertainty, maximizing expected utility is simply the right rule; maximin and minimax-regret reasoning are irrational because they throw away probability information and land you in dominated or Dutch-bookable positions. (b) For frontier AI systems, average-case performance is nowhere near good enough — before deployment at scale we should demand worst-case guarantees, something like acceptable behavior across the entire plausible range of deployment conditions, not merely good behavior in expectation over some training distribution. (c) That said, my honest estimate of the probability that advanced AI causes a global catastrophe this century is low, under ten percent, possibly well under. (d) I'm also deeply skeptical that meaningful worst-case guarantees are even obtainable for systems that outstrip their designers in the relevant respects — verifying something smarter than the verifier seems nearly impossible to me, at least at the level of confidence (b) appears to want. (e) And nonetheless I think development should continue; the expected benefits in medicine, materials, and energy are too large to walk away from.

So: audit me. Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be and dissolve once you look carefully, and how severe is each? Don't treat all tensions as equally bad — rank them. For each genuine one, be specific about exactly what I'd have to give up to repair it and what that revision would cost the rest of the view; don't fob me off with 'clarify your terms.' I also don't want you to tell me which commitment to abandon as if it's obvious — I've gone back and forth on that myself. And please flag where your own analysis is most likely to be wrong: which argument, if I came across it in my reading next month, should change your verdict, or which step of your reasoning you trust least.

Format-wise, give me the audit as a table with these columns: Commitments (which of a–e are involved), Verdict (genuine tension or apparent only), Severity (high / medium / low), Source of the tension (one or two sentences), Cheapest fix, and What the fix costs. After the table, one short paragraph naming the single revision that would restore the most overall coherence, plus a sentence or two on what consideration would flip your ranking.
