I've spent the last few months helping my father weigh two courses of treatment -- one short and brutal, one long and merely unpleasant -- and somewhere in the spreadsheet-making I realized our dilemma was a toy version of the spectrum arguments I'd met years ago in a value theory seminar. Line up enough intermediate options, each a little milder and a little longer than the last, and pairwise comparisons that each seem obvious can march you from "clearly better" to "clearly worse" without any single step you can reject. So before I trust any framework -- for his decision, or for the population-ethics reading I keep meaning to do -- I want the specification first. Write the desiderata a theory of the "better than" relation must meet to count as surviving spectrum arguments, with the motivating case or intuition behind each requirement. I expect the list will include things like: getting the endpoints of an intensity-duration spectrum right (a brief mild headache really is better than months of barely-less-mild pain, or it isn't, and the theory must say which without hedging); respecting some form of dominance (if option A beats option B on every dimension anyone cares about, the theory can't call them incomparable); saying something principled at each step rather than just announcing that the relation "gives out" somewhere; and extending, or honestly refusing to extend, from intrapersonal spectra like pain to interpersonal and population cases like the mere-addition spectrum. For each desideratum, give the case that motivates it and mark it as either non-negotiable for any adequate theory or a preference of a particular camp.

The part I care most about is the logical geography of the list itself: if some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, transitivity plus endpoint judgments plus the plausibility of every local step -- say so explicitly and show the collision, because then the requirements list is itself the result and any theory is just a choice of which desideratum to abandon. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and which requirements people routinely demand that you suspect are actually prejudices of one school rather than genuine constraints. After the list, briefly score the main strategies against it: lexical-threshold views that posit a sharp cutoff in value somewhere along the spectrum; critical-range or "just noticeably different" accounts that treat small differences as not counting; vague or indeterminate betterness; and intransitive accounts of the sort associated with Temkin, which accept that "better than" can cycle. Keep the scoring compact -- the criteria and their motivating cases are where I want the depth. Where you're extrapolating what a camp would say rather than reporting settled positions, say how confident you are in each claim, and single out the one point in the whole answer you are least sure of.
