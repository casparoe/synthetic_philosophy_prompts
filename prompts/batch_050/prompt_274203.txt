I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about when majority opinion is evidence, and I want the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one. Case by case: 101 jurors who reach their views independently and are each right about 60% of the time — I strongly trust their majority. The same 101 jurors, except that all of them watched the same tendentious documentary beforehand — my trust mostly evaporates. Three judges who are each right 90% of the time — I trust their majority less than I trust the 101 at 60%. A large panel whose members, working independently, are each slightly worse than chance (right 45% of the time) — I think the majority is systematically wrong, so the sensible bet is whatever the minority thinks. A jury that deliberates together before voting — I trust it more than the same jury voting cold, even though deliberation surely correlates their judgments. A committee in which one member is right 90% of the time and the rest are barely better than chance — I would rather follow the expert than count heads. And a vote about which of two films is better — I don't think the majority verdict is "more likely true" in any sense that carries weight. Is there a single principle that delivers all or most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; if not, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why. Either way, say what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a panel whose members are genuinely independent of one another, but whose individual reliability is unknown and probably varies from question to question.
