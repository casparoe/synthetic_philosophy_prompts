I teach philosophy of science, and I have spent the last two summers working through category theory with a reading group of mathematicians — mostly because I wanted to understand the structuralism debate in philosophy of mathematics, but the vocabulary has begun to colonize how I think about my own field. I now find myself wanting to translate the structural realism debate about scientific theories — roughly, the view descending from Poincaré that what survives scientific revolutions is structure rather than content, developed today in "epistemic" versions (we can know only the structure of the world, not its intrinsic nature) and "ontic" versions (structure is all there is, and objects are at best derivative of relations) — into the vocabulary of objects, morphisms, isomorphism, and functors. The question was put to me afresh by a passage I assign every year, from Henri Poincaré's Science and Hypothesis (1905), Chapter X, on the theories of modern physics:

"That a given periodic phenomenon (an electric oscillation, for instance) is really due to the vibration of a given atom, which, behaving like a pendulum, is really displaced in this manner or that, all this is neither certain nor essential. But that there is between the electric oscillation, the movement of the pendulum, and all periodic phenomena an intimate relationship which corresponds to a profound reality; that this relationship, this similarity, or rather this parallelism, is continued in the details; that it is a consequence of more general principles such as that of the conservation of energy, and that of least action; this we may affirm; this is the truth which will ever remain the same in whatever garb we may see fit to clothe it."

Take this passage as your text and restate its central claim in category-theoretic vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then do the same for the contemporary epistemic and ontic structural realist positions that claim Poincaré as an ancestor. For each of the three, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in a language where objects are individuated only up to isomorphism and where "what a thing is" is exhausted by its pattern of morphisms. Some specific pressure points I would like addressed: Poincaré's "images we substitute for the real objects" sounds like it wants to be a claim about isomorphic representations of a fixed structure, but he also insists the real objects themselves are hidden — can a framework that is constitutionally silent about objects-beneath-isomorphism even state that humility, or does it collapse the distinction between the real object and its image? And the ontic structural realist's slogan that there is structure without relata is often mocked as incoherent in ordinary metaphysical vocabulary — is it equally strained when put in category-theoretic terms, or does the translation make it look better than it deserves by importing mathematical precision the metaphysical claim never had?

Finally, the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Is what fails to translate — Poincaré's epistemic hedge ("neither certain nor essential"), his residual realism about hidden objects, the empirical bite of the pessimistic induction that motivates the whole position — something category theory cannot say, or something the structural realists were never saying clearly? I would rather you be honest about where the mapping fails than force the translation through, and I would like your verdict on whether a colleague could use this exercise to argue that the epistemic/ontic structural realism dispute is partly an artifact of object-first vocabulary.
