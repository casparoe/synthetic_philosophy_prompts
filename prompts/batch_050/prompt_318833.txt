I've been chewing on this for a seminar I'm teaching on collective responsibility, and I want to assign a paper on it, so help me think it through first.

Official state apologies for historical wrongs: a head of government formally apologizes, on behalf of the nation, for something the state did generations ago -- slavery, the forced removal of indigenous children, colonial massacres, wartime internment. Standard features of the practice: the person speaking had nothing to do with the wrong; nearly all the direct victims are dead; the apology is largely addressed to descendants; and, the part I find philosophically weirdest, these apologies routinely come with explicit disclaimers that they create no legal liability and no obligation to pay compensation. Some jurisdictions even have statutes saying an apology can't be admitted in court as evidence of fault. Meanwhile the same legal order treats inherited guilt as anathema: we don't punish children for their parents' crimes, collective punishment is prohibited, no criminal liability without individual wrongdoing.

So tell me what this practice presupposes. Concretely: (1) what must be true about the state's identity over time for "we did this" to make sense across a century -- is that a metaphysical claim about corporate agents, or a legal fiction doing moral work, and does the practice care which? (2) ordinary apology seems to require the wrongdoer, so what theory of apology lets a bystander, or an office, deliver one, and what gets lost in the transfer? (3) the addressees: descendants weren't wronged directly, and many of them exist only because of the history being apologized for -- can an apology presuppose a wrong to people whose very existence is downstream of that wrong, or must the real addressee be the dead, or the community taken as a corporate entity? (4) can an apology that's legally inert -- admits nothing, costs nothing, can't be used against you -- still be an apology, or does the disclaimer quietly convert it into something else, an expression of regret or a piece of political ritual? If the latter, what theory of what an apology even is is the practice actually running on?

And like any good seminar paper: seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, flag where it's underdetermined (several rival theories might license the practice equally well), and give me a verdict on whether the apology practice and the no-inherited-guilt criminal law can be made jointly coherent, or whether the state is just talking out of both sides of its mouth.
