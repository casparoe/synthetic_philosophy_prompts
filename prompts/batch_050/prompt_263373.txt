I presented the following argument at a workshop on game theory and, to my embarrassment, could not answer the charge that it proves far too much. Help me find where it goes wrong — or tell me honestly if it doesn't.

First, the setup. In the centipede game, two players alternate moves. A pot grows at each stage, and at her turn a player can either take the larger share of the current pot, ending the game, or pass, which increases the total but hands the same take-or-pass choice to the other player. After a fixed, commonly known number of stages the game ends with some default split. Backward induction delivers a grim verdict: the player at the final decision node takes; the player at the second-to-last node, anticipating this, takes; and the reasoning unravels all the way back, so the first player takes immediately and both walk away with a fraction of what continued passing would have produced.

Now the argument:

(1) Whatever backward induction prescribes in a finite game of perfect information is what ideally rational players, with common knowledge of each other's rationality, will do.
(2) The centipede's structure is not exotic. Any finite sequence of exchanges in which each party's continued cooperation is worthwhile only if the other keeps cooperating at later stages — lending with repayment over time, a business partnership, an employment relationship, a marriage — has at its final stage a party who profits by defecting, and so unravels exactly as the centipede does.
(3) Every actual human relationship of trust is finite.
(4) Therefore ideally rational agents could never rationally sustain trust in any real relationship: the rational move is to "take" at the first opportunity — never lend, never front the capital, never commit.
(5) But lending, partnership, and marriage are not mass irrationality. People enter them with open eyes and are, on the whole, vindicated. (Tellingly, experimental subjects do not take at the first node, and those who pass early earn more than those who follow the equilibrium.)
(6) So either (1) is false — backward induction is not a requirement of rationality at all — or its background idealizations are so remote from actual agents that it has no normative grip on us.
(7) And since the standard solution concepts for finite games are built on precisely this reasoning, game theory is normatively irrelevant to real strategic life: an elegant mathematics of beings nothing like us.

My audience agreed that (7) is false, but no two of them agreed on which earlier step to reject. My own preference is to keep both (1) and (5): I would hate to surrender either the claim that rational players under common knowledge of rationality follow backward induction, or the claim that trusting relationships are generally rational. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling when I wrote it, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? In particular: if the culprit is (2), I need to be told which structural feature of the centipede my real-world examples lack, and whether the difference is one of degree or of kind; if it is (4), I need to know which shift in scope or modality carried me across; and if (1) must go after all, I want the replacement account of what rational play in the centipede actually consists in, and why it doesn't simply relabel equilibrium violation as rationality.
