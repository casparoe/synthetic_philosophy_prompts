I serve on the review panel of a small private foundation that funds theoretical work in physics and pure mathematics. We evaluate roughly forty proposals per funding cycle, with two cycles a year; each proposal receives about ninety minutes of panel discussion, funding is decided by majority vote, and there is no appeal. Panel members are working scientists, not philosophers, and our deliberations keep running aground on the same dispute: one faction argues that a theory's elegance -- its simplicity, its symmetries, the sense that it could not have been otherwise -- is itself a reason to believe it is on the right track and therefore a reason to fund it, even in the absence of new empirical confirmation. The other faction treats such arguments as taste dressed up as evidence, and points out that the community's recent beauty-guided bets (for instance, confident expectations of new particles just above the electroweak scale on grounds of naturalness) have not been vindicated by experiment. Meanwhile, both factions sometimes dismiss as ugly, ad hoc models that are in fact matching the data.

Please write a decision procedure the panel could actually run when an elegance-based argument surfaces during deliberation: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure instructs the panel to discount the aesthetic consideration entirely; and where it must hand the question back to judgment because no rule can settle it. In constructing it, preserve the distinctions that seem to matter philosophically: whether the aesthetic criterion was fixed in advance or reverse-engineered from the model under review; whether the theory's beauty consists in compressing previously independent phenomena into one scheme or merely in its internal coherence; whether and how the track record of a research community's prior elegance-guided bets should modulate the weight given to its current aesthetic judgments; and the difference between treating elegance as evidence for truth and treating it as a heuristic for allocating scarce attention and funding. Mark explicitly which steps encode a substantive philosophical commitment (and therefore could be contested) rather than neutral administration.

Then run the procedure on three cases drawn from our recent cycles:

1. A particle-theory proposal motivated chiefly by a naturalness argument and a new symmetry. Two successive generations of experiments have returned null results in the regions where the symmetry's simplest versions predicted signals; the applicants respond that the surviving versions are, if anything, more beautiful, and request funding to develop them.

2. A pure-mathematics proposal aiming to establish a conjectural correspondence between two distant subfields. There are no applications, no prospect of empirical check within a decade, and the case for funding rests on the unification the correspondence would achieve plus the strong aesthetic enthusiasm of several senior figures.

3. An openly inelegant phenomenological model with many fitted parameters, which has nonetheless correctly anticipated three subsequent experimental results that its prettier rivals missed. Its author concedes it is almost certainly not the fundamental theory, but requests continued funding on predictive grounds.

At least one of these applications should yield a recommendation the panel's prevailing sentiment would dislike -- say which one and why. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements about beauty and evidence it merely relocates rather than resolves.
