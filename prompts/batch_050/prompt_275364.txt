I'm a PhD student and next month I'm presenting a paper at our department's grad conference, which this year is themed around environmental ethics. My argument, in short: if you take the welfare of wild animals seriously at all, then fertility control -- things like immunocontraception (the PZP-style vaccines already used on wild horses and deer) -- is the most defensible large-scale intervention going, and I defend the stronger claim that preventing future wild animals from being born can itself be a welfare improvement, not just a regrettable side-effect of one. My reasoning leans on life-history strategy: most wild animals come from r-selected species where the overwhelming majority of individuals die very young, often of starvation, cold or predation, so on plausible views about welfare, reducing the number of such lives being started reduces total suffering. I explicitly do not want to defend the edgier version of this where habitat destruction looks good because fewer animals get born -- I was workshopping the talk with my housemate who does ecology and she told me flat out that if I leave that door open I'll be eaten alive, so part of my prep is figuring out how to shut it.

The audience is about fifteen people: four or five environmental philosophers (at least two of whom are sympathetic to holistic/ Leopold-style land ethics and will bristle at anything that treats populations as containers of welfare), a few conservation science postdocs who think in terms of population viability, trophic cascades and unintended consequences, one animal welfare scientist, and my supervisor, who is a population ethicist and definitely has views about the non-identity problem and replaceability. Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this particular crowd, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each person would actually phrase them (a conservation biologist doesn't object like a land ethicist). For each one: what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is weakest. I also want to know which objection does the most damage to the stronger claim specifically (that fewer future wild lives can be good in itself), and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, in British English.
