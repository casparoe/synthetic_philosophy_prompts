I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about people who report wrongdoing inside their own organizations. Across the interviews I keep running into the same pattern, and I want to know whether philosophy already has a name for it.

Here is the pattern, in three instances. A nurse discovers that a senior colleague — someone who trained her, vouched for her, and is genuinely beloved on the ward — has been falsifying medication records. She reports him. Everyone who reviews the case, including the nurse herself, concludes that reporting was not merely permissible but required: patients were at risk. And yet the colleague has a grievance against her that a stranger who reported him would not have. He doesn't just say the outcome was bad for him; he says she did something to him. And she agrees, in a way: she says she would do it again, and also that she owes him something — an apology, an acknowledgment, she isn't sure what — and that these two convictions do not cancel each other. I have parallel accounts from an engineer who reported safety violations at a firm whose partners had gone out on a limb to hire him, and from a woman who reported her brother's accounting fraud to the regulator. In every case the person insists the act was right, equally insists that someone specific was wronged, and treats the wronging as part of what made the act hard rather than merely what made it sad.

An editor at my magazine suggested the phrase "dirty hands," but it doesn't fit: as I understand it, dirty hands describes doing something wrong because the stakes demand it, whereas my subjects did something right. The nursing literature's "moral distress" is closer in register but names a psychological state, not the interpersonal fact that one particular person has a legitimate complaint against another.

So, three questions. First: does this configuration — an act that is justified, even obligatory, all things considered, yet that nonetheless constitutes a wrong done to a specific person in virtue of a prior relationship — have an established name, and is there a recognized analysis of it? Second: how does it relate to the nearby ideas I can gesture at but cannot cleanly distinguish — regret that remains appropriate even without wrongdoing, obligations that survive being overridden rather than evaporating, and the thought that some duties are owed to particular people rather than to people in general? Which of these is doing the real work? Third, the boundary question my editor will certainly ask: does it matter that the wronged party was himself at fault? Intuitively the brother's fraud does not erase the sister's betrayal of him, but it does seem to change it — how, exactly?

Please answer as a moral philosopher who has thought carefully about loyalty, betrayal, and the structure of interpersonal wrongs. Take your time and reason step by step. Begin by brainstorming the candidate concepts a specialist would reach for, state precisely what each one covers, and show where the pattern I've described falls outside each of them. Only then give your verdict: an existing term if one genuinely fits, or — if none does — a coined term with a precise definition, the cases it includes, and the cases it excludes. I don't want citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual carving done properly.
