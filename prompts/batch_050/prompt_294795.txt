My half-formed thought: Camus's essay runs two arguments that are usually read as complementary, but they pull in different directions. The first is empirical and conditional -- capital punishment fails as a deterrent, and once deterrence goes, nothing justifies it. The second is structural and looks unconditional -- execution is a disproportion between a fallible, uncertain judgment and a certain, irreversible punishment, and the state in executing commits a calculated, premeditated version of the very act it punishes. My suspicion is that only the second strand is really doing abolitionist work, and that taking it seriously has an uncomfortable consequence Camus himself gestures at but does not pursue: if the objection is that the state replicates the crime in deliberate, cold-blooded form, then life imprisonment without parole -- which Camus describes as arguably worse than execution -- may stand condemned by the same argument, in which case his position is not a verdict on the death penalty but the start of a critique of punishment as such.
