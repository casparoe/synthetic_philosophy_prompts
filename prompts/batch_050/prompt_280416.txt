You are a philosopher with expertise in epistemology and the law of evidence. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing to one. Here are my verdicts on whether purely statistical proof can ground liability, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one. (1) A bus forces a car off the road at night; the only evidence is that 80% of buses on that route belong to the Blue Bus Company. The plaintiff should lose. (2) Of 1,000 attendees at a concert, organizers prove only half paid, then sue one attendee picked at random for the ticket price. He should win. (3) Police run crime-scene DNA through a database and get a cold hit on a man with no other connection to the case; the random-match probability is one in ten million. I am inclined to convict, though this verdict troubles me, since the evidence is "merely statistical" too. (4) A factory ships a batch of vaccine vials; testing establishes that 90% of the batch was contaminated, and a patient harmed by a vial from that batch sues. I want the patient to win, which seems to conflict with (1). Is there a principle that captures all or most of these — individualized evidence, sensitivity, normic support, a probability threshold with exceptions? State it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and say honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then apply it to two cases I have not decided: a partial fingerprint match with a one-in-five-hundred random-match probability, offered together with proof that the defendant lived nearby; and the use of actuarial recidivism scores at sentencing.
