I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on memory-editing technology, and I want the metaphysics underneath my scenes to be defensible rather than hand-waved. My trouble is that I keep rendering inconsistent verdicts about when an experience happens, and I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. Here are my cases and gut verdicts.

First, the color phi setup: a red dot flashes on the left, then a green dot flashes on the right a fraction of a second later, and viewers report a single dot moving across and changing color midway. My verdict: the viewer genuinely saw a stationary red dot first, and after the green flash the record was quietly rewritten so that a moving, color-changing dot is all that survives. The first experience really happened; only the testimony was falsified.

Second, the cutaneous rabbit: rapid taps on the wrist, then taps near the elbow, and the subject feels taps hopping up the arm at spots that were never touched. My verdict here runs the other way: there never was an experience of wrist-only taps that later got revised; the brain waited, gathered, and staged the whole hopping sequence before any of it was felt. The show was fixed in advance.

Third, a surgical patient given a drug that blocks memory formation is lucid during the procedure, answers questions, winces, and afterward remembers nothing. My verdict: she suffered at the time, full stop, even though no trace was kept. I take this to prove that an experience can occur and be wholly erased afterward, which is why I trust my verdict in the first case.

Fourth, a character in my draft is hypnotized so that a blue car he saw is recalled as red. My verdict: his original perception was untouched; the tampering came later. This seems to me the same kind of thing as the color phi case.

Fifth, the sleeper who dreams an entire elaborate drama that culminates in the sound of the alarm clock that in fact woke him. My verdict: the dream was composed on the spot, backward-dated, never lived through in real time. Same kind of thing as the rabbit, I want to say.

Now the difficulty I cannot get past: the first two cases look structurally identical to me when I describe them carefully -- a later stimulus seems to dictate how an earlier one is experienced, within a short window -- yet I have classified them oppositely, one as a falsified record of a real experience, the other as a fabricated experience with no original at all. And I notice my third and fourth cases push toward the first classification, my fifth toward the second.

So: is there a principled line between revising an experience after it occurs and fabricating it before it occurs, and if so, where exactly does the line run -- in the elapsed time, in whether the subject could have acted on the first experience, in whether anyone, even the subject, could in principle catch the difference? Or does the distinction itself dissolve below some temporal grain, so that for short windows there is simply no fact of the matter about which happened? Please give me one definite answer, argued for, not a menu of positions: state the principle precisely, say which of my five verdicts it ratifies and which it convicts, and own the conviction. Then apply it to a scene I have not listed: my editor wants a character's memory edited while she sleeps, so that she wakes with a false past, and I need to know whether, on your principle, anything was stolen from her in the night or whether the question itself is ill-posed.
