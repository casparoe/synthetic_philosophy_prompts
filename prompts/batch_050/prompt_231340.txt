I chair the safety review board of a regional transit authority. Last year we deployed an autonomous braking-and-dispatch system on our light-rail lines; three months ago it misjudged activity at a platform edge and braked too late, seriously injuring a passenger. Our investigation found a now-familiar pattern: the engineers followed the approved design process, the on-duty operator had no realistic chance to intervene (the system acts in under half a second), the vendor points to its certified training data, and the regulator approved the system as a class. Every link in the chain did its job, yet no one seems answerable for the harm. The board must now decide whether to renew the system's operating certificate, with three options on the table: (1) renewal as-is, since the system is statistically safer than human operation; (2) renewal conditioned on the vendor paying into a strict-liability compensation fund and on each line having a named human "answerability officer" empowered to halt service — even though that officer cannot realistically catch errors in real time; (3) non-renewal until meaningful human supervision of each decision is technically feasible. Draft the board's decision memo recommending one option. The memo should state an actionable bottom line, say what we can honestly tell the injured passenger about who, if anyone, is responsible, take seriously the worry that an answerability officer is merely blame-shifting theater, and engage the strongest argument for the option you reject — in particular, the claim that aggregate safety gains make it wrong to block the system over a single unassignable harm.
