So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm fact-checking a sidebar a colleague wrote for a piece on how language shapes reasoning. Here's what she drafted about conditionals:

"Logicians distinguish two kinds of if-then sentence. Indicative conditionals, like 'if it rained last night, the match was cancelled,' and counterfactuals, like 'if it had rained last night, the match would have been cancelled.' The classic illustration is the Kennedy pair: compare 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did' with 'if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have.' The first is clearly true and the second is a coin toss, and all that separates them is verb tense -- which shows the difference between the two kinds is purely grammatical, a matter of mood rather than meaning. Ernest Adams then proved that an indicative conditional is assertible exactly when the corresponding conditional probability is high, so 'if A then B' really just means P(B given A) is high, and the indicative conditional has no truth value at all, only a probability. Counterfactuals, by contrast, do have truth values: Lewis and Stalnaker analyzed them using possible worlds, and 'if A had been the case, B would have been' is true iff B holds at the closest world where A holds. So indicatives live in probability theory, counterfactuals live in metaphysics, and the two projects never meet."

My gut says the Kennedy bit can't be right -- if the two sentences genuinely differ in how confident we should be, that sounds like a difference in meaning, not just tense, so concluding it's "purely grammatical" feels like she's drawn the opposite lesson from the example.
