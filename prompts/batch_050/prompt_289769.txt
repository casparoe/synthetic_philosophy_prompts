I am a professor of philosophy serving as an external member of the clinical ethics committee of a regional health trust. The committee votes next month on whether to approve a pilot programme, and I have undertaken to circulate a written opinion beforehand. Please draft that opinion for me.

The proposal concerns adult patients who lack decision-making capacity and have left no advance directive. At present, treatment decisions for such patients are made by a relative or an independent advocate attempting 'substituted judgement' — reconstructing what the patient would have wanted. The trust proposes instead to use a software tool that constructs what its developers call a laundered preference profile. The tool assembles evidence about the patient — medical records, correspondence, past statements, structured interviews with relatives — and then outputs the treatment preference the patient would supposedly have if their actual dispositions were corrected until they were: (a) fully informed about the relevant medical facts; (b) internally coherent, with no intransitivities or preference reversals; (c) stripped of documented cognitive biases such as framing effects and unrealistic optimism; and (d) stable under calm, sustained reflection. Where several corrected profiles are each consistent with the evidence, the tool reports the preference shared by the majority of them. The developers describe this as implementing idealised-preference theories of welfare — roughly, the view that what is good for a person is what they would want if ideally placed to want it — and they invoke, by analogy, proposals in the literature on artificial intelligence about honouring a person's extrapolated volition rather than their raw, unreflective preferences.

The committee is split. The pilot's supporters argue that substituted judgement by relatives is already guesswork dressed up as fidelity, and that an explicit, auditable procedure is an improvement on a spouse's hunch. Its opponents make two points: that 'correcting' a patient's preferences risks correcting the patient away — they cite a current case in which the tool discounted a lifelong needle phobia, and hence the patient's consistent refusal of a burdensome but marginally life-extending treatment, as a bias to be laundered; and that the majority-among-consistent-profiles rule conceals indeterminacy, since a 55–45 split among the completions is reported to clinicians as a single settled preference. I should add that I am torn myself: I find the idealised-preference framework philosophically respectable in the abstract, yet I suspect the proposal equivocates between two quite different things — repairing the evidence we have about a person, and revising the person the evidence is about.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, in British English, structured as follows: (1) Recommendation — the bottom line, stated in the first sentence: approve, reject, or approve with conditions (roughly 100 words); (2) What the proposal gets right — the strongest version of the supporters' case (roughly 200 words); (3) The strongest objection — which of the opponents' two points cuts deeper, and why (roughly 200 words); (4) Where the idealisations do the work — an analysis of whether the four correction conditions are innocent epistemic repairs or substantive commitments about who the patient is, with attention to the needle-phobia case (roughly 250 words); (5) Conditions — the specific safeguards under which a pilot would be defensible, including how the tool should report indeterminacy (roughly 150 words).
