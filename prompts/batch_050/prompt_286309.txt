I've become convinced that acausal threats are basically a non-problem: a threat only gives you a reason to act if the threatener can actually make you better or worse off, and with no causal channel the "threat" is just some distant agent writing fanfic about you. So my position is that a rational agent with a stable utility function can adopt a blanket policy of ignoring acausal blackmail, and that this policy is self-enforcing, since any would-be threatener reasoning about me would predict the refusal and not bother. What would count against this? I've seen the rejoinder that threateners can route around a refusla policy via surrogate goals -- e.g. rewarding whatever would have been served by my cooperation, or punishing simulations or models of me that my own decision theory treats as decision-relevant -- but I don't see why I should care what happens to surrogates that aren't me. Tell me what observations, arguments, or formal results would show this policy actually loses utility, which of those would be decisive versus merely weird, and whether the surrogate-goal move is a real defeater or just a re-description of the thing I already dismissed.
