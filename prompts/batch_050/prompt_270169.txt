The exam question was: "The Mu'tazila and the Ash'arites disagreed about whether human reason can discern good and evil independently of revelation. State each side's position, explain how the dispute maps onto the Euthyphro dilemma, and assess whether the Ash'arite position amounts to a version of divine command theory." Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"The Mu'tazila held that good and evil are objective features of actions that unaided reason can discover, even before any revelation arrives, so that someone who never encountered scripture could still know that injustice is evil. On their view God commands what is good because it is good, and since God is wise and just He would never command evil; indeed they held He is bound to act justly. The Ash'arites rejected this. They held that reason cannot know good and evil on its own and that acts become good or evil only because God commands or forbids them. Since moral properties are constituted by God's will, God stands entirely above morality: had He commanded murder or lying, those things would be good, and He could command them arbitrarily, since no standard exists above His will. That makes the Ash'arites divine command theorists in the strict sense, stuck on the arbitrary-will horn of the Euthyphro. To preserve God's omnipotence, the Mu'tazila also taught that God creates all human acts (the doctrine of kasb), since acts created by humans would limit His power. And because the Ash'arites ground morality in will rather than reason, their ethics collapses into relativism, since different gods could command different things."
