I teach philosophy of mathematics at a liberal arts college, and next year I will be running a small graduate-style reading group — five students, one semester, weekly meetings — on mathematical explanation in empirical science. The group will start from the standard indispensability material (Quine and Putnam's argument, the confirmational holism it presupposes) and then move to the two case studies that dominate the literature on genuinely mathematical explanation: the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas, where number-theoretic facts about coprimality allegedly explain why 13- and 17-year cycles evolved, and the hexagonal geometry of honeycombs, where the optimality of hexagonal tiling for minimizing wax allegedly explains the bees' constructions. In discussing the syllabus with a colleague, I realized I have no principled view about which open questions in this area the students should spend their limited time on, and I'd like a defended agenda rather than the tour I would give by default.

Here is the twist I want the prioritization to engage with. My own suspicion is that the cicada and honeycomb cases are routinely lumped together as instances of "distinctively mathematical explanation" when they may in fact be different kinds of thing: the cicada case arguably turns on a modal fact (primes minimize intersection with predators' possible cycles across a range of scenarios), while the honeycomb case turns on an optimization theorem, and it's unclear whether the mathematics is doing the same explanatory work in each — or whether in one of them the mathematics merely represents a causal fact while in the other it genuinely explains. Call this the taxonomy problem. I'd like to know: among the open questions in this area — the taxonomy problem, whether indispensability arguments and explanation arguments stand or fall together, whether the "distinctively mathematical" cases survive scrutiny of how the mathematics is actually deployed in the derivations, whether a theory of mathematical explanation needs to be tied to a general theory of scientific explanation or can be free-standing, and any I've missed — which two or three are most worth a semester of student effort, and in what order?

Please defend the ordering explicitly rather than just presenting a list. In particular: which questions are upstream of which (does settling the taxonomy problem change what the indispensability debate needs to establish, or vice versa?), which are tractable for bright students with philosophical training but only undergraduate mathematics, and which you would deprioritize because their resolution wouldn't ripple outward. Also tell me what would change your ranking — for instance, whether it matters for the ordering if it turns out that in the cicada case the optimality fact, rather than anything specifically number-theoretic, carries the explanatory weight. I'm less interested in a survey of positions than in the reasoning that converts "unresolved" into "worth our twelve weeks."
