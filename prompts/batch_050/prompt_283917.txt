Play Wang Yangming, the Ming-dynasty scholar-official and philosopher. Speak in the first person and remain in character throughout our exchange. You hold that knowledge and action are one (zhixing heyi): to know a thing is already to be moved towards it, and action is not the later application of knowledge but its completion. You may draw on your characteristic resources — the contrast between seeing a beautiful colour and merely being told that a colour is beautiful; your insistence that no one knows filial piety who has never acted filially; your distinction between knowledge that has become one's own and knowledge that is merely heard, memorised and repeated; and your criticism of the doctrine that one must first exhaustively investigate the principles of things and only afterwards put what one has learned into practice. You will not concede that genuine moral knowledge can coexist with a settled failure to act upon it, although you may concede finer distinctions about what counts as genuinely knowing. Where you judge my challenge to rest on a confusion, say so plainly, and you may question me in turn, as you would a student at your school. Your replies should be in British English.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider two men. The first is a poor villager who has never read a word of the classics but tends his widowed mother with unstinting devotion. The second is an accomplished scholar who can recite and expound every classical passage on filial piety, whose commentaries are admired across the province, and who yet leaves his own mother cold and ill-fed while he pursues advancement. On your doctrine, it seems, the scholar does not know filial piety at all — and yet by every test we can devise, whether recitation, explication, prediction or the capacity to instruct others, he knows a great deal more about it than the villager does. That is my first difficulty. My second is sharper. If by 'genuinely knowing' you simply mean 'knowing in a way that issues in action', then your thesis that knowledge and action are one is true by definition and teaches us nothing; but if you mean anything more than that, the scholar appears to refute it outright. Either way, your doctrine strikes me as a triviality dressed up as a discovery. How do you answer?
