The question was: "Some philosophers argue that even if the probability that insects are sentient is very low, the sheer number of insects means we may have strong moral reasons to take insect welfare seriously. Reconstruct this 'scale argument,' present the strongest objection to it, and say whether the objection succeeds."

Here's the student's answer:

"The scale argument runs like this. The probability that any given insect is sentient might be quite low, say 1%, since insects have tiny nervous systems and no cortex. But there are roughly 10^18 insects alive at any time, vastly outnumbering all vertebrates. When you multiply the probability of sentience by the enormous number of individuals, the expected amount of morally relevant suffering in insects is huge -- potentially larger than all vertebrate suffering combined. The argument, which basically comes from Peter Singer's work on animal suffering, therefore shows that insect welfare should be a higher priority than, say, chicken or pig welfare.

The strongest objection is that this reasoning collapses into fanaticism. Expected value reasoning breaks down when probabilities are tiny and stakes are huge, because then any action with an astronomically large payoff dominates, no matter how unlikely. If we accept the scale argument for insects, we'd also have to accept that we should spend all our resources on barely-possible existential risks or on Pascal's wager. So the objector rejects expected value reasoning altogether in these cases.

I think the objection succeeds. Since the probability of insect sentience is so uncertain, we can't do the multiplication in a principled way, so the argument can't get off the ground. It's better to just focus on animals we're confident are sentient."
