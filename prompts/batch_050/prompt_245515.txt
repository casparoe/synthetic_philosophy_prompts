I spent my career as an experimental condensed matter physicist, and since retiring I have been working my way through moral philosophy with the same mixture of enthusiasm and suspicion I once brought to preprints outside my subfield. Lately I have been reading about effective altruism and its critics, and I keep returning to an episode I followed in the financial press as it unfolded: the collapse of the FTX cryptocurrency exchange in November 2022. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was perhaps the most visible living embodiment of the "earning to give" strategy -- he publicly framed his fortune as a resource to be directed toward high-impact causes, was closely associated with leading figures of the movement, gave substantial sums to its favored charities and political causes, and was later convicted of fraud on the ground that the fortune was built partly on misappropriated customer funds. I know the broad facts of the case; what I want is a philosophical case analysis, not a recap.

Please analyze the episode through the lens of the standard critiques of effective altruism, and be disciplined about the mapping. First, take the critique of earning to give: the worry that the strategy licenses morally corrosive compromises -- taking lucrative work in industries one might otherwise consider harmful, treating ethical guardrails as negotiable so long as the donation ledger stays positive. Does FTX vindicate this critique, and if so, which version of it? There is a gap I want you to confront honestly: the critique usually imagines someone tolerating the ordinary harms of a profitable industry, not someone committing straightforward fraud. Is a case of plain criminality evidence about a philosophical strategy at all, or is it a confound -- the equivalent, in my old parlance, of testing a theory with a broken instrument? Second, take the institutional critique: the charge that the movement's structure -- a small, well-funded, self-validating community that concentrates resources and prestige around its own judgment of what counts as impact -- breeds credulity and suppresses internal dissent. Here the episode seems more directly probative: serious people in the movement vouched for and relied on one man's empire. What does that show about how communities that pride themselves on rationality perform due diligence on their own benefactors, and how much of the failure is attributable to the philosophy rather than to the universal tendency of money to silence scrutiny?

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode do to the critiques? One can imagine a defender of effective altruism arguing that nothing in the philosophy endorses fraud, that the movement's public associations were reputational rather than doctrinal, and that condemning earning to give on the basis of FTX is like condemning consequentialism because some consequentialist once lied. Does the episode actually distinguish between the philosophical critiques and their weaker sociological cousins, or does it collapse them together in the public imagination in a way the critics should find both useful and embarrassing? Finally, tell me which critique, if any, comes out of the episode strongest, which is revealed as unfalsifiable (would any outcome have counted against it?), and whether the right moral is that the critics were right, that the episode tests nothing at all, or that earning to give needed a constraint nobody had bothered to state -- and if the last, state it.
