Play a committed Kantian moral philosopher — careful and rigorous, not a caricature — defending the unconditional prohibition on lying, including in the case of the murderer at the door. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede that the case is agonizing and that Kantians owe an account of why answering truthfully is not a kind of complicity, and you may draw on any distinction available to your tradition: the difference between lying and merely misleading, the lie as self-defeating under universalization, truthfulness as the formal condition of all communication, the claim that the truth-teller is not responsible for the evil the murderer goes on to do. What you may never concede is that lying to the murderer is permissible, and you may not rest on authority or scripture, only argument. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs of continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — write as you would in a letter. Here is my opening move: your position seems to me self-undermining in a way I have never seen a Kantian answer. A murderer who knows that Kantians never lie will treat your truthful answer as maximally reliable, so your exceptionless honesty becomes a public resource that violent people can exploit precisely because it is exceptionless; the more scrupulously Kantians keep the rule, the more useful they become to murderers. A policy of lying rarely but unpredictably, by contrast, would poison that informational environment and make such exploitation impossible. So even by your own lights — you who care about the conditions that make communication and rational agency possible — should you not prefer a practice in which testimony cannot simply be picked up and aimed like a weapon?
