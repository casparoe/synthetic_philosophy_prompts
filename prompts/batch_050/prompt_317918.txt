So I'm a retired chemist, and about a year ago I fell into a philosophy reading group that meets at the library, mostly other retired folks plus a couple of grad students. We just finished Kuhn (which felt like home turf) and now it's my turn to lead the session, and somehow I got assigned Wittgenstein on religious belief — the lecture notes stuff where he goes on about how believing in the Last Judgement isn't like believing it'll rain tuesday, and how if you treat religious belief as a hypothesis or a prediction you've basically missed what it is.

Here's my problem: I don't want you to explain Wittgenstein's view to me, I've read the lectures twice and argued about them with my daughter enough. What I need is a plan for how to teach it to this group in about an hour, because I can already see exactly how it's going to go wrong. These are smart people but most of them spent their lives in engineering, medicine, business — and I can hear it already: someone will say "so Wittgenstein is saying religious belief is irrational," someone else will say "no no, he's saying it's just feelings, not real claims," and half the room will assume this is all just a fancy way of saying religion is false but you can't say so politely.

So give me the plan:

1. The order the ideas have to come in. What do they need firmly in place before the religious stuff will make any sense at all? My instinct is you can't start with religion, you have to start somewhere more neutral, but I want you to tell me what the actual dependency structure is and why.

2. The one example that should carry the whole session. It has to be something other than religion — a case where the difference between "holding a belief as an empirical claim" and "it organizing your whole life/picture of the world" shows up plainly, so that religion arrives late as the application rather than early as the provocation. Tell me what this example has to have and what it must NOT have (e.g. it can't be something the room will just argue about on its own terms for forty minutes).

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface in the room, and where in my sequence each one should be headed off rather than cleaned up afterwards. I've named my three guesses above — tell me if those are the right three or if I'm missing a bigger one.

4. One question I can ask near the end where the answers will show me whether they got the distinction or just got comfortable nodding along.

5. What I should openly refuse to get into at this level (expressivism debates? the later Wittgenstein vs the Tractatus? whether Wittgenstein himself actually believed anything?) — with reasons, not just a list.

Also, keep in mind these people will push back on anything that smells like special pleading for religion, and half of them are churchgoers who will push back on anything that smells like an attack. The plan has to survive both.
