I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy preparing a dissertation chapter, and I have provisionally committed to a position I want properly stress-tested before I build on it. My view is that the cluelessness objection to consequentialism fails: granted, we cannot know the remote ramifications of our acts, but our ignorance about those effects is symmetrical across our options, so unknown distant consequences cancel out in expectation and we are justified in deliberating almost entirely on the near-term, foreseeable effects. Consequentialism thus remains action-guiding even in a causally opaque world.

Tell me what would count against this position. I do not want a rehearsed statement of the original objection; I want a map of where my reply is exposed. Which assumptions about evidence and expectation does the symmetry claim require, and are there plausible structural asymmetries -- say, acts with irreversible civilizational-scale effects, or systematic biases in which remote harms we can and cannot foresee -- that break it? Does my defense presuppose a decision theory that delivers well-defined expected values under radical uncertainty, and would a formal result showing it cannot be a defeater? Might the reply prove too much, licensing disregard of low-probability high-stakes outcomes generally, or prove too little, extending equally to every rival theory and so rescuing consequentialism only by tying the race? Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what defenders of my view would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics press, and close with an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into something untouchable -- and if so, whether that matters for a view of this kind.
