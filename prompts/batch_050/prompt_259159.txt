The topic: what adult children owe their parents, and specifically Jane English's friendship model — roughly the idea that grown kids owe their parents the same thing friends owe each other, and that parental sacrifices don't generate some ongoing debt that has to be repaid. Here's my angle, which I havent seen treated head-on: dementia. If the ground of filial obligation is an ongoing friendship (mutual recognition, a shared history both parties can actually access, some reciprocity), then late-stage dementia looks like it dissolves the ground while the obligation intuitively persists — most people think you'd owe your demented mother more, not less. So the friendship model seems forced into one of three moves: (a) quietly smuggle in a non-friendship ground (gratitude, vulnerability, a special-relationship duty) and thereby stop being a pure friendship model; (b) defend some kind of carryover principle where obligations outlive the relationship that generated them — but then why doesn't the same carryover logic rescue the debt models English wanted to reject?; or (c) bite the bullet and say the obligations really do fade, which sounds monstrous, though maybe the bullet is cheaper than it looks once you separate what we owe from what a decent person does anyway.

In the proposal I need: the question stated precisely (my framing: can a pure friendship model of filial obligation accommodate duties to parents with severe dementia without collapsing into one of its rivals?); why the standard menu of answers (debt/gratitude, special goods, friendship) leaves this open rather than already settling it; the method — I'm thinking of using the non-relative case, a lifelong friend with dementia where intuitions seem noticeably weaker, as a control, to test whether any parent/friend asymmetry the model produces is actually an explanation or just a restatement of the problem; what the possible conclusions are and what would count as the project failing (e.g. if the asymmetry ends up tracking something purely psychological about recognition rather than anything normative, the paper kind of collapses); and a realistic scope for one semester — I definately can't also relitigate the entire filial obligations literature on top of this.
