Here is the exam question:

"Consider the argument: (1) If tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. (2) Tormenting the cat is wrong. (3) Therefore, getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. Use this example to explain the Frege--Geach problem for expressivism. Your answer should: (a) state precisely which feature of expressivist semantics generates the difficulty; (b) explain why no parallel difficulty arises for the descriptivist; (c) say whether the problem is best understood as a refutation of expressivism or as a challenge, and what shape an adequate expressivist response must take; and (d) briefly assess whether the same problem arises for normative vocabulary generally, such as 'ought', and whether that generality helps or hurts the expressivist."

And here is the student's answer:

"The Frege--Geach problem arises because moral predicates can occur in both asserted and unasserted contexts, and expressivism seems forced to say they mean different things in each. When I utter premise (2), on the expressivist view, I am performing a speech act: I express my disapproval of tormenting the cat, somewhat as I might boo it. But in premise (1), the phrase 'is wrong' occurs in the antecedent of a conditional, and there I am plainly not expressing disapproval of anything -- I am not condemning cat-tormenting at all, since for all I've said the antecedent may be false. Yet the argument from (1) and (2) to (3) is obviously valid, and its validity requires that 'is wrong' have the same content in both premises. If the meaning of 'is wrong' is constituted by the disapproval it is used to express, then in the antecedent it cannot mean what it means in (2), and the argument commits a fallacy of equivocation. So the expressivist must choose: either deny the validity of an intuitively watertight piece of moral reasoning, or admit that the meaning of moral vocabulary is not exhausted by the attitudes expressed. This, as Frege originally argued against the emotivists, shows that meaning cannot be identified with the speech act performed, since a thought must be able to occur unasserted without changing its identity.

The descriptivist faces no parallel problem. On a descriptivist semantics, the antecedent of a conditional is still asserted, albeit conditionally: to assert 'if P then Q' is to assert P under the supposition that it holds, so the predicate 'is wrong' does the same work in (1) as in (2), attributing the property of wrongness in both places. Because descriptivism assigns moral predicates a constant descriptive content, the validity of modus ponens arguments involving them comes out as straightforwardly as it does for any empirical predicate.

As for (c), the problem is best seen as a challenge rather than a knock-down refutation. What it demands of the expressivist is a compositional semantics: an account of what moral sentences contribute to conditionals, negations, and belief reports that explains why the argument above is valid without equivocating on 'is wrong'. Blackburn's quasi-realism and Gibbard's norm-expressivism can be read as attempts to supply exactly this, by treating accepting a moral conditional as a higher-order attitude toward combinations of attitudes and factual beliefs. Whether these constructions fully succeed is contested, especially for negation, but the dialectical point stands: Geach's argument by itself shows only that the crude identification of meaning with expressive force is untenable, not that no sophisticated expressivism could work.

On (d), the problem is perfectly general. 'Ought' embeds in conditionals and negations just as 'wrong' does, so if embedding is a problem for moral predicates it is equally a problem for the whole normative vocabulary. This generality arguably helps the expressivist rather than hurting him: he can embrace expressivism about normativity across the board instead of offering a patchwork treatment, and the uniform treatment of 'ought', 'wrong', and 'good' is a point in the theory's favor rather than an embarrassment."
