I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about AI personhood that keeps collapsing in interviews because everyone uses Daniel Dennett's phrase "counterfeit people" to mean somthing different. Dennett coined it in a late essay where he argued that AI systems mimicking humans should be treated the way governments treat counterfeit currency -- not merely regulated but stamped out, with serious penalties for making them, because fake money destroys trust in the whole monetary system and fake people would do the same to whatever trust holds human communication together. Since his death the phrase has gone fully feral. At a workshop I attended last month, one panelist used it for deepfaked videos of real individuals, another for chatbots that claim to have feelings, a third for any sufficiently fluent LLM output at all, and a policy person cited it to justify a licensing regime, all in the same afternoon.

Here's what I actually need, basically as a sidebar for the piece. Lay out the distinct precise claims "counterfeit people" could be making -- like, separate the thesis that these systems deceive users into thinking they're human, from the thesis that they counterfeit something else (agency? inner life? accountability? being the kind of thing that can make a promise?), from the pure trust-erosion claim that doesn't even require anyone to be fooled in the moment, just for our default willingness to take words as coming from a someone to degrade. For each reading, say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false -- the deception reading, for instance, seems to get weaker every year as people get used to talking to bots they know are bots, but maybe that's wrong and the interesting version doesn't need conscious deception at all. The counterfeit-money analogy itself deserves scrutiny: money is only valuable because it's scarce and verifiable, and persons aren't like that in obvious ways, so where exactly does the analogy do work and where does it just borrow the criminal-law intuition for free?

Finally, tell me which reading Dennett himself most plausibly meant given the argument as I've paraphrased it, and -- this is the part my editor cares about -- which readings would actually support the strong policy conclusion (creation should be punished, like counterfeiting) versus which only support wimpier stuff like labeling requirements or disclosure norms, and whether anyone at that workshop was using the slogan in a way that could bear the weight they put on it. I suspect the phrase survives precisely because it equivocates between a modest point about deception and a radical point about trust, and people slide between them mid-sentence, but I'd like the menu laid out properly rather than my suspicion confirmed.
