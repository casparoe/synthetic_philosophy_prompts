I am a high school student preparing a capstone project on anomaly detection in complex systems. I keep seeing detectors described as identifying not merely unusual data, but behavior produced by an unusual mechanism. Before comparing methods, I want a rigorous account of what such a claim would require.

Please act as both a professional philosopher of science and an experienced machine-learning researcher. Think carefully and begin with a short brainstorming section listing every requirement that comes to mind. Then consolidate those ideas into a final list of eight to twelve distinct desiderata that a theory of mechanistic anomaly detection must meet. Avoid citations, bibliographies, and appeals to authority.

Use this scenario to anchor your answer. A school network has many ordinary sources of traffic: students submitting assignments, teachers streaming videos, software updating itself, printers malfunctioning, and attackers trying to steal passwords. Two events can produce nearly identical traffic patterns while being generated by different underlying processes; conversely, the same malicious process can look different after a change in timing, device, or software version. The central question is therefore not just whether an observation is statistically rare, but whether the process that generated it counts as the same mechanism as a previously understood process or as a different one.

For each desideratum, explain:

1. What the theory must be able to do or distinguish.
2. Why that requirement matters, using a concrete case from the school-network scenario or another ordinary example.
3. What would count as satisfying it.
4. Whether it is non-negotiable, important but context-dependent, or merely preferred by a particular approach.

Make sure the list addresses at least the following issues, while adding any important ones I have missed:

- Distinguishing an anomalous mechanism from ordinary noise, rare events, and measurement errors.
- Handling different levels of description, such as packets, user behavior, software activity, and institutional goals.
- Deciding which changes are merely changes in a mechanism’s expression and which changes make it a different mechanism.
- Separating knowledge about causes from knowledge about correlations.
- Accounting for mechanisms that interact, overlap, share components, or deliberately imitate one another.
- Explaining how background knowledge and incomplete observations affect the judgment.
- Remaining usable when the available data are logs or other traces rather than direct access to the underlying process.
- Avoiding a standard so strict that every small variation counts as a new mechanism, but also avoiding one so loose that genuinely different mechanisms are grouped together.

After the list, identify any requirements that may be jointly unsatisfiable. I am especially interested in tensions between causal accuracy, computational practicality, robustness to deception, and independence from human labeling. Explain whether each tension looks unavoidable or whether it depends on a particular conception of mechanism.

Finally, briefly compare three broad strategies against your requirements: a statistical strategy based on unusual patterns, a causal-model strategy based on interventions and dependencies, and a narrative or process-tracing strategy based on reconstructing how the event unfolded. Do not merely declare one strategy the winner; state which requirements each handles best, which each sacrifices, and what a promising hybrid would need to preserve. Conclude by naming the single desideratum that would eliminate the greatest number of superficially plausible theories and explaining why.
