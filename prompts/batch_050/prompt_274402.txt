I hold: (a) a modest Lucas-style argument succeeds — for any consistent formal system we can specify, we can in principle recognize the truth of its Gödel sentence, so human arithmetical competence is not captured by any single formal system, and in that sense the mind is not a machine; (b) physicalism — the mind is the brain, and anything a brain does can in principle be reproduced by a sufficiently detailed computational simulation; (c) fallibilism about our own arithmetical reasoning — we have no guarantee that the totality of our arithmetical commitments is consistent, and even careful, idealized reasoners might unknowingly endorse an inconsistent set of arithmetical claims; (d) the second incompleteness theorem carries no skeptical upshot — our justification for trusting elementary arithmetic does not depend on a consistency proof carried out in a stronger system; (e) Gödel's theorems are results of mathematical logic, so any conclusion about minds must rest on substantive bridging premises and cannot simply be read off the theorems themselves.
