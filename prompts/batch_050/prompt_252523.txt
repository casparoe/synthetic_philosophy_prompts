For an ethics reading group I'm part of, one member circulated her own summary of Bernard Williams's famous distinction between internal and external reasons, which she wrote up from memory of a lecture. The summary runs as follows:

Williams argued that a claim of the form "this person has a reason to act in a certain way" is true only if acting that way would serve some desire the person has right now. So if I tell you that you have a reason to keep your promise, what I say is true only if you happen to want to keep it, or happen to want something that keeping it would get you. It follows that a person who genuinely does not care about anyone else has no reason whatsoever to refrain from cruelty or deceit: morality's demands reach only those already motivated to heed them, and the rest are simply beyond its jurisdiction. Williams therefore concluded that blaming the thoroughly selfish person is irrational, on a par with blaming someone for bad weather, and that so-called external reasons — reasons that would bind a person no matter what they are motivated to do — are an invention of moralists unwilling to accept this conclusion, since any such alleged reason would have to influence someone ex hypothesi immune to its influence.

Something about this makes Williams's position sound cruder than I remember it being, but I can't locate the trouble precisely, and the person who wrote it is usually reliable. Please audit the summary. Separate, as clearly as you can, (i) what accurately captures Williams's actual view, (ii) what is a defensible simplification of the kind one finds in competent secondary discussions, and (iii) what distorts or overstates his position. In particular, I would like to know whether his notion of an agent's "motivational set" is really equivalent to the agent's present desires, what role deliberation and the correction of error play in determining what reasons a person has, and whether the conclusions about the selfish person and about blame actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary — or whether Williams himself drew a more cautious conclusion on those points.
