For the final session of my undergraduate seminar on the concept of liberty, I traditionally set a short satirical reading alongside the serious ones, and this year I would like a new piece written for the occasion. Compose an Onion-style news article — headline plus roughly 500 to 600 words — reporting that a local man has come to the devastating realization that he has never been free, despite the fact that no one has interfered with him at any point in his life. His employer has never disciplined him, though she could dismiss him at whim and on a moment's notice; his spouse adores him, though every plan in the household proceeds only for as long as she continues to permit it; and the authorities leave him entirely alone, while reserving the right to do otherwise whenever they see fit. The humor must come from getting the theory right rather than from inventing absurdities: the article should enact, without ever naming its terms, the distinction between freedom as the absence of interference and freedom as the absence of domination — the problem of the kindly master, the arbitrariness condition, why power held in check by mere goodwill dominates just as surely as power exercised, and why suitably constrained, law-tracked power arguably does not. Include at least one quote from a concerned colleague who insists the man is obviously free because nobody is stopping him from doing anything he likes, one from a self-help author who suggests his real captor is his own undisciplined will, and one from a neighbor who has quietly begun to reexamine the benevolent arrangements of her own life. The piece should remain deadpan throughout — no winking, no editorializing — and a reader versed in the republican tradition should be able to reconstruct from the jokes alone exactly which conception of liberty is being defended, which two rivals are being deflated, and to feel that the satire is ultimately fair to all three.
