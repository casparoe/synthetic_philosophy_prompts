Write a dialogue between two decision theorists, Dr. Rao and Dr. Lind, who have been retained to advise the same client, and who disagree about whether she should finish what she started.

The client: Maya, a boatwright, has spent four years and €180,000 of her savings restoring a historic sailing vessel from which she plans to run a charter business. One year and €40,000 of work remain. A market analysis that both advisors accept as sound concludes that the finished business would be worth about €30,000; meanwhile Maya could sell the half-restored hull today for €5,000 and return to salaried employment. So a rational stranger, offered the boat and the business plan for free, would decline to put in the remaining €40,000 — and Maya faces exactly the same prospective choice. One further fact complicates things: Maya has abandoned three previous multi-year projects midway, each time on locally impeccable calculations much like this one, and she plausibly believes that abandoning a fourth will leave her psychologically unable to commit to any long-term plan ever again.

Dr. Rao holds that honoring sunk costs is always irrational: only prospective costs and benefits can bear on a choice, and whatever a rational newcomer would refuse to do, Maya should refuse to do. Dr. Lind defends resolute choice: rational agency extended over time sometimes requires executing a plan one would no longer adopt afresh, because an agent who re-optimizes at every decision node cannot carry out any plan whose later stages are locally unattractive — and Maya's history shows her to be exactly such an agent.

Requirements:

- Give the two roughly equal speaking time, and make them equally competent. Rao must grant that the capacity to follow through on plans has genuine value and that human agents are not frictionless calculators; Lind must grant that most real-world appeals to sunk costs are straightforward fallacies and that her view had better not license throwing good money after bad in general.
- The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth: each speech should engage the other side's most recent point, not restate a fixed position.
- Over the course of the dialogue, work through the following, in any order: (i) whether the fact that a rational stranger would walk away settles the question, or whether that test smuggles in the assumption that an agent's history of abandoned plans is decision-irrelevant; (ii) Rao's objection that Lind's appeal to preserving her future plan-executing capacity is simply forward-looking reasoning on a wider ledger — and therefore no defense of sunk costs at all — together with Lind's reply, which should address whether a policy of completion can do its work only if it sometimes binds even when the wider-ledger calculation comes out negative; (iii) the objection that Lind's reasoning proves too much, since it would have rationalized keeping the Concorde flying, and Lind's best available response, which must distinguish honoring sunk costs ex post from adopting, ex ante, a completion policy whose expected costs are priced in before the plan begins; (iv) whether Maya's self-knowledge about her own weakness of will is a legitimate input to her decision or itself a bias to be corrected.
- End without a winner: either the two converge on a criterion separating rational perseverance from the sunk cost fallacy, or each states in a single closing sentence the one proposition on which their disagreement finally turns.

Length: 600–900 words of dialogue, with only minimal stage directions.
