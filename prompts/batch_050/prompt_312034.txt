I produce a small podcast on the philosophy of decision theory, and next month I'm interviewing a guest who defends an unusually strong thesis he calls geometric rationality: the view that the Kelly criterion, the rule of staking the fraction of one's bankroll that maximizes expected logarithmic growth, is not merely a clever heuristic for gamblers and investors but the correct general norm for rational choice under uncertainty. His signature move is to argue that maximizing expected value is the rationality of the multiverse, optimizing an average across infinitely many copies of the agent that will never exist, whereas maximizing the growth rate is the rationality of a single life lived once, because compounding agents experience time averages, not ensemble averages. I've already done a soft episode where he got to state all this unchallenged, so this time I want a harder conversation.

Draft seven to nine interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view and instead press where it is weakest. I want at least one question on each of the following. First, whether the ergodicity argument actually settles anything for a mortal agent: the asymptotic results that seem to favor growth-maximization concern what happens as time goes to infinity, so what force do they have for someone with a finite horizon and no reincarnation clause? Second, whether the long-run theorems guaranteeing that a Kelly bettor will almost surely overtake any other bettor carry real normative weight, given that the overtaking may take centuries and the guarantee is silent about what happens if the competitors adjust their strategies in response. Third, the standard objection that maximizing expected log wealth is just expected utility maximization with one particular, seemingly arbitrary degree of risk aversion baked in, so the view either collapses back into orthodoxy or illicitly elevates a taste into a theorem. Fourth, what the criterion could even mean outside wagers: a career change, a scientific theory choice, or a marriage has no obvious bankroll from which to take a fraction, and I want him pinned down on whether geometric rationality is a general theory or a metaphor wearing one.

For each question, also write the follow-up I'll need given the answer he is most likely to give, since he is practiced at deflecting. To seed one of these: when pressed on the utility objection, he tends to reply that logarithmic growth is not a preference but a fact about how multiplication works, so the follow-up there should force him to explain why the arithmetic of compounding binds someone whose preferences genuinely differ from log. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would draft notes in a long email to oneself before recording: no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just the questions and their follow-ups woven together so that I can read it aloud and improvise from it.
