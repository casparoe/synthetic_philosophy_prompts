Something has been nagging at me about how my university (and honestly most universities now) handles interpersonal conflict, and I'd like some help making it philosophically precise. Here's the setup as I see it. There seem to be three broad logics for responding to being wronged. In the first, call it the honor logic, a slight is a challenge to your public standing and you have to answer it yourself, visibly, or you lose face -- your worth is on the line and its your job to defend it. In the second, call it the dignity logic, your worth is supposed to be inalienable and not up for grabs in everyday interaction, so the mature response to a minor slight is to shrug it off, maybe have a direct word with the person, and only call in authorities for genuinely serious stuff. In the third, the one that seems to be winning on campus, slights get documented, reported, and taken to third parties -- bias response teams, ombuds offices, formal grieivance procedures, HR, sometimes public statements rallying support -- and being recognized as wronged by the institution is a big part of what remedies the harm. What interests me is that the same institution runs the third logic for student conflicts while still preaching the second logic elsewhere: we tell grad students to talk to their advisor directly before escalating, we insist grading disputes start with a conversation with the instructor, and every orientation speech mentions resilience.

So: what does the third logic, as an institutional practice, actually presuppose? Does it presuppose a view on which harm is partly constituted by social recognition -- i.e., a slight isn't fully a harm until an authority validates it as one? Does it presuppose a picture of persons as lacking the standing or the capacity to settle their own conflicts, and if so is that a descriptive claim (people are fragile) or a structural one (power asymmetries make self-help unjust)? Does it presuppose that equality among members is so precarious it needs constant external enforcement rather than being assumed as a baseline? I also want to know how much of this is strictly presupposed versus merely a good fit -- could, say, an orthodox Kantian dignity view itself license heavy institutional protection, so that the practice is underdetermined between theories? And then the consistency question: can one institution coherently presuppose dignity-theory in its advising norms and victimhood-theory in its grievance procedures, or is one of them going to have to give? Finally, sketch the redesign: what would a university look like that went all-in on the dignity logic, or (god forbid) the honor logic, and which of the three, if any, comes out morally ahead once you've seen what each presupposes?
