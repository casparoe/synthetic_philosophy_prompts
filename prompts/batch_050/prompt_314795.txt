So I'm a novelist, and somehow I got talked into joining the board of a mid-sized literary prize (the money is good and the meetings have wine, that's my excuse). Anyway we're now in a mess and I have to draft the memo that settles it, meeting is in ten days.

Here's the situation. One of our five shortlisted authors has been accused of pretty serious misconduct -- years of emotional abuse and manipulation of a former partner, also a writer. Nobody on the board has any firsthand knowledge of any of it. What we have is: the ex-partner's public account, plus private calls from three people two of our board members know well and trust, all saying the account is accurate and that this is an open secret in their circle. There's no court case, no investigation, nothing to independently check, and the author categorically denies everything and has hinted at legal action if we drop him.

The board is split in a way that maps onto something I dimly remember from a philosophy podcast: half the board says acting on other people's moral testimony -- even from people we trust -- is illegitimate, that you can't outsource your conscience, and unless we can verify it ourselves we have to judge the book on the book. The other half says that's absurd, that virtually everything anyone believes about right and wrong comes from testimony one way or another, and that refusing to defer here is just cowardice dressed up as epistemic rigor. Both halves have a point and both halves are driving me crazy.

Write me the memo I'd circulate before the meeting. It needs an actual bottom line -- recommend we keep him on the shortlist, remove him, or some third option like deferring the decision with a defined process -- and it has to take a real position on the underlying question: when, if ever, is it legitimate to act on a moral judgment you hold entirely on someone else's say-so, and does the answer change when the decision is public and irreversible-ish like this one? Don't split the difference or give me a menu of options to choose from -- pick a lane and defend it. But do give the losing side its strongest version, because I'll be sitting across from those people, and I need to be able to answer the objection that whichever way we go we're either convicting a man on hearsay or letting prestige launder an abuser. Also keep it to something a board of novelists and one agent will actually read, so no jargon, and I'll handle any citations or references myself if it comes to that.
