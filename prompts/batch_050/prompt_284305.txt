I’m a high school junior helping run our school’s philosophy club, and our sponsor gave me one 70-minute meeting. There are usually around 16 kids, mixed grades, and most of them only know the trolley problem as a meme. Design an original activity or game about whether there’s a morally important difference between doing harm and allowing harm, especially killing versus letting die.

Please don’t just re-skin the trolley problem. I want something where a threat is already moving through some kind of system, and players can either stay out of it or intervene in a way that predictably changes who gets harmed. Across the rounds, mess with the details: sometimes a player redirects an existing threat, sometimes they create a new one, sometimes they had a special responsibility to act, sometimes helping costs them a lot, and sometimes they merely let someone else do the dirty work. The same outcome should look morally different depending on how it came about, but there should also be rounds where doing nothing feels awful.

It would be cool if observers had a limited pool of blame or responsibility points to hand out after each round, first with only partial information and then again after the full action history is revealed. I want people to notice their own judgments shifting before we explain the distinction. Use points or tokens instead of real stakes, and keep the scenario serious without getting weirdly graphic.

Give me actual runnable rules, materials, roles, setup, round order, timing, and a simple scoring or payoff system. The game should make both reactions feel plausible: actively causing harm seems worse, but refusing to lift a finger can also seem monstrous. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto doing vs. allowing, redirecting vs. creating harm, intention vs. foresight, and special duties. Don’t name big theories before the activity.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong with high schoolers, like people treating it as a strategy game, joking through the choices, or assuming there’s one correct answer, and how the facilitator should handle that. End with an honest note about what this kind of game can’t recreate about actual killing and letting die. It definately shouldn’t turn into a lecture with a game attached.
