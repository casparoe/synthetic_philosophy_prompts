I'm putting together a problem set for a philosophy of law seminar, and I've been frustrated by how the standard cases in the strict liability debate are rigged: either the defendant is obviously careless (so the mens rea debate never really gets going) or the defendant is so blameless and the punishment so pointless that strict liability looks absurd by construction. I want a case where the genuine argument for strict liability — the one its serious defenders actually give — has to do real work. So: construct a thought experiment, realistic and contemporary (a small business, a food producer, a contractor — not a trolley, not a science-fiction device, no omniscient regulators), in which a person is criminally convicted under a strict liability statute despite having taken every precaution a reasonable person could take, such that no fault-based standard could convict them — and yet the strict liability conviction is, on reflection, defensible as policy. Two constraints: first, the defense of the conviction must not rest on deterring the defendant from being careless, since they are by stipulation already maximally careful; it has to run through something else — incentivizing people to reconsider whether to enter the risky activity at all, forcing information about hidden risks into the open, spreading costs to those best placed to absorb them, or whatever you find strongest. Second, the harm must be serious enough that a fine or regulatory slap on the wrist would feel evasive, so the reader can't dodge the "punishing the innocent" discomfort by treating the liability as merely administrative.

Please begin with the scenario itself in the first paragraph, with no preface or commentary. After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification: explain why no mens rea standard (purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence) convicts this defendant, then walk through the strongest argument for why the strict liability conviction is defensible anyway. Finally, identify the single feature of the case doing the most work, say what happens to the argument if that feature is removed, and give the best response available to a retributivist who insists that punishment without culpability is unjust no matter how good the downstream effects — including whether you think that response succeeds or whether the case is really a reductio of strict liability in disguise.
