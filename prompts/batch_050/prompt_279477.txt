The final exam for my upper-level undergraduate seminar on G. E. Moore and early twentieth-century value theory needs one more essay question, and I want it on Moore's account of the ideal goods — the appreciation of beauty and personal affection — developed in the final chapter of Principia Ethica (1903). In past years my Moore questions have rewarded students for reciting the naturalistic fallacy and the open-question argument; I want a question on which that material earns almost nothing. The crux I want examined is the doctrine of organic unities and what it commits Moore to about these two goods.

Please draft the following.

The question itself. It should be answerable in roughly 75 minutes from a careful reading of the relevant chapters, without any secondary literature, and it should not quote Moore verbatim — paraphrase his claims in the stem instead. It must present two novel cases and require students to run Moore's machinery on them: (1) an unobserved-beauty case — say, a magnificent landscape on a planet no sentient being will ever visit, contrasted with a mediocre painting admired daily by thousands — which forces students to state what, on Moore's view, contributes what to the value of the whole; and (2) a misdirected-affection case with two variants — one in which someone loves a friend for virtues the friend does not in fact possess, and one in which someone admires a companion partly for his ruthlessness — which forces students to deploy Moore's distinction between affection directed at good qualities that are absent and admiration directed at bad qualities that are present. The question should then ask students to explain why Moore can rank personal affection at or above aesthetic enjoyment, given the kind of object each takes, and to confront the tension between the organic-unity principle and the method of isolation Moore uses to establish intrinsic value: if the value of a whole is not fixed by the values of its parts, what work can imagining a thing existing alone still do?

A model answer of about 600 words.

A marking scheme out of 25, built around five must-hit points with the marks each carries and a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates get each wrong. The five points I want targeted are: a correct statement of the organic-unity principle applied to both cases, not merely the slogan that wholes exceed the sums of their parts; Moore's verdict on unexperienced beauty and the reasoning behind it; the contrast between the two variants of the affection case and why one constitutes a diminished good while the other is, for Moore, a positive evil; the structural reason affection for a person can rival or exceed aesthetic appreciation; and an explicit reckoning with the isolation-method tension, including the reply that a part's value considered alone need not measure its contribution to a whole.

Finally, a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B answer. My expectation is that B answers will apply organic unities competently to the cases, while only A answers will notice the methodological tension and say something intelligent about whether Moore's own framework contains the resources to defuse it.
