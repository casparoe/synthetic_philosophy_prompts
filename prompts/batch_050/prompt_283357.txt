I'm stuck inside an argument that grew out of a seminar discussion of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and its conclusion is one neither I nor the colleague who assembled it can accept. I want a diagnosis, not a summary.

The setup: a fair lottery with one million tickets and exactly one winner. Separately, a meticulous historian finishes a long book and writes in her preface that, while she stands by each claim in the book individually, she is certain the book contains errors somewhere.

The argument:

(1) For each ticket i, it is rational to believe that ticket i will lose; the evidence is as strong as evidence gets short of certainty.

(2) Agglomeration: if it is rational to believe each of p₁, …, pₙ, then it is rational to believe their conjunction.

(3) From (1) and (2): it is rational to believe that every ticket loses.

(4) It is rational to believe that exactly one ticket wins.

(5) It is never rational to believe an outright contradiction; so something in (1)–(4) has to give.

(6) The reasoning in (1) is perfectly general: whenever your evidence makes a proposition overwhelmingly probable, outright belief in it is rationally permitted. Call this the threshold principle.

(7) Take all your current beliefs and conjoin them; call the conjunction T. Each conjunct is believed on empirical, testimonial, or memory-based evidence that makes it highly probable but not certain.

(8) By (6) and (2), it is rational to believe T.

(9) But the same statistical reasoning as (1) applies to T itself: given enough fallible conjuncts, the probability that they are all true is minuscule. So by (6), it is rational to believe ¬T. This is just the historian's preface generalized to a whole cognitive life.

(10) From (8) and (9): it is rational to believe T and rational to believe ¬T.

(11) Therefore (5) is the premise that fails: rationality does permit believing contradictions, at least of the distributed, large-scale kind. The preface is not a curiosity; it is the general human condition.

(12) Now the step I choke on. Every long chain of reasoning — a multi-page proof, a legal brief, a scientific derivation — is believed, if at all, conjunctively: you believe the premises and each intermediate step, and each of these falls short of certainty, since you might have misread, misremembered, or misderived.

(13) By the reasoning of (7)–(10), for any sufficiently long argument it is rational to believe each step, rational to believe their conjunction, and rational to believe that the conjunction is false.

(14) Therefore it can never be rational to be convinced by a long argument; rational conviction is available only for what can be taken in at a glance.

(15) (14) is absurd — mathematics and law run on long arguments — so something earlier has to give.

If I have to choose what to protect, my priorities are: the rationality of the historian's preface first, the rationality of long deduction second, the threshold principle a distant third. But I want to know what I am left with if the threshold principle goes — in particular, what outright belief even is, if not high confidence.
