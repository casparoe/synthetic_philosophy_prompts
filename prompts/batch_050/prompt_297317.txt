At a workshop on transitional justice last month, a colleague defended a truth-and-reconciliation mandate by reaching for a comparison I keep hearing in these circles: a nation that has committed atrocities stands to its past roughly as a person stands to theirs. On this picture, historical commissions are the collective analogue of psychotherapy — repressed memory festers, acknowledgment heals, and a society that refuses to confront its crimes is doomed to repeat them the way a patient who refuses to confront trauma stays stuck in it. I first ran into this analogy in readings around Avishai Margalit's The Ethics of Memory, where the obligation to remember is tied to the "thick relations" that bind members of a community to one another, and on the other side David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, which argues that the demand that societies remember tends to keep wounds open rather than close them. What strikes me is that both sides take the person–community analogy seriously even while pulling opposite prescriptions out of it.

What I'd like from you is an audit of the analogy, not a summary of either book. Map the correspondence explicitly: in the collective case, what plays the role of the individual's memory, of repression, of the therapist, and of the remembering subject itself? Then identify where the mapping breaks — for instance, whether a community has anything like a unified subject capable of suffering the analogue of trauma; whether institutional forgetting (shredding archives, letting witnesses die off, rewriting curricula) resembles psychological repression in the respects that matter, or whether it is more like deliberate lying by some members to others; and whether "the community remembers" reduces to "enough members remember, in the right positions" or names something over and above that. Finally, say which of the two conclusions — Margalit's, that certain communities have a duty to remember their dead and their crimes, or Rieff's, that forgetting is often the healthier collective course — the analogy can actually support once the disanalogies are priced in, and whether either author needs the analogy at all or could argue directly from the ethics of thick relations without it.

Keep your answer under 450 words. Lead with your verdict on how much argumentative weight the analogy can bear, then give the correspondence mapping, then the disanalogies, then the assessment of the two conclusions.
