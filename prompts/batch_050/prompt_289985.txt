I serve on the five-member committee that administers my employer's staff hardship fund, and this month we must rule on two claims that our founding documents do not clearly cover. The first is from a warehouse worker who developed a degenerative spinal condition in his forties despite a lifetime of reasonable habits; he can no longer work his shifts, and his medical and retraining costs far exceed our fund's usual grants. The second is from a technician who declined the subsidized flood insurance we all receive annual reminders about and lost most of what she owned when her riverside flat flooded in the spring storms. Half the committee wants to pay both claims in full, on the grounds that misfortune is misfortune; the other half wants to pay only the first, on the grounds that the fund exists to insure people against what happens to them, not to underwrite the gambles they take.

This has stopped being an abstract policy question for me. Last year my brother was refused assistance by a similar fund at his own workplace because his loss was judged, in the administrator's phrase, a consequence of his choices, and I have since gone back and forth on whether that judgment was coldly fair or simply cold. So I want the document I am asking for to be one I could defend at his dinner table as well as in our committee room.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition of both claims, either together or separately, and it should do so by articulating criteria the committee can apply to future cases rather than by resolving these two on their sympathy alone. In working out those criteria, the memo should grapple seriously with the distinction between bad luck that befalls a person and bad luck that results from a risk a person knowingly accepted, including whether that distinction can bear the weight the second half of the committee wants to put on it. It should also address the two strongest objections to relying on that distinction at all: first, that a fund which abandons imprudent claimants to destitution has smuggled cruelty in under the name of responsibility; second, that a fund which compensates people for being unlucky in their health or natural endowments risks treating them as deficient rather than as equals. Where the memo recommends distinguishing between the two claimants, it should say what we owe the one we turn down. Where it recommends treating them alike, it should say how we will answer the contributor who asks why her premiums should fund other people's declined insurance. Roughly eight hundred words, in the register of a document a skeptical colleague could vote against on stated grounds.
