The passage I am working on is from the second of the three "Impartial Care" chapters (chapter 15 in the standard arrangement). Mozi is answering the objection that impartial care is admirable but impossible to practice — the objector compares it to lifting Mount Tai and jumping across a river with it. Mozi replies by pointing to cases in which rulers' preferences reshaped the behavior of entire courts: King Ling of Chu favored slender waists, so his ministers cut themselves down to a single meal a day; Goujian of Yue honored reckless bravery, so his soldiers would charge into fire to prove themselves; Duke Wen of Jin wore coarse clothing, so his officials dressed in rough hides and hemp. Mozi's point is that people will adopt even painful, unnatural-seeming conduct when those above them model and reward it, so impartial care is not like carrying Mount Tai at all — it is the sort of thing a social order can make normal. 3. Incentive dependence: the ministers and soldiers acted from hope of favor and fear of displeasure, so the argument really supports a sanction-and-reward account of how impartial conduct is secured, and says nothing about motivation at all.
4. The psychological question is a red herring: Mozi's point is that what people do is a function of convention and institutions, so the Confucian worry about what people can feel is simply the wrong kind of objection to his doctrine.
