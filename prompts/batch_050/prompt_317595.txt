I am writing a long-form piece on the Great Divergence and the Needham question, and I want to know whether my editorial judgements about contrastive historical questions are coherent. I think “Why did sustained industrial growth first occur in Britain rather than China?” is a useful hook but too coarse to be the article’s central question. “Why Britain rather than the lower Yangzi region around 1750?” is well posed. “Why Britain rather than the Netherlands?” is also well posed, because the comparison can discriminate among wages, energy, trade and institutions. “Why did China fail to industrialise?” is badly posed, since it treats China’s path as a departure from an expected norm. “Why Europe rather than China?” is worse: it compares a continent of competing states with an empire and invites explanations at incompatible scales. Yet “Why did Song China’s commercial and technological advances not lead directly to industrialisation?” seems legitimate, despite the much larger temporal separation. By contrast, “Why Britain rather than France?” feels misleading if it asks why Britain was first, because it may turn small contingencies into profound national differences; asking why British and French industrialisation took different forms seems perfectly legitimate. Finally, “Why steam power in Britain rather than cotton production in China?” is plainly confused.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough to explain when a historical “why X rather than Y?” question has an appropriate contrast class, commensurate cases and a well-defined explanandum. Which of my judgements, if any, must be revised to achieve a simpler account, and what would that account predict about “Why Britain rather than Qing China?” and “Why industrialisation rather than continued commercial growth?”
