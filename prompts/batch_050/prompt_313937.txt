So I'm a novelist, and at Thanksgiving my brother-in-law (econ PhD dropout, insufferable) walked me into an argument and I still can't find the exit. Backstory: my publisher offered to buy out the royalty stream on my backlist for a flat lump sum. I was leaning toward yes — money now, no waiting — and he said I'd be a mark, and then he "proved" that basically nobody should ever accept any offer anybody makes them. Which can't be right, because I sell books to publishers for a living and people sell houses and take jobs every day and both sides walk away happy all the time. But his steps each sounded fine in the moment. Here it is as best I can reconstruct:

(1) You should only take a deal if it leaves you better off in expectation given everything you know, including the fact that the deal was offered to you.
(2) Anyone who offers you a deal is offering it because they expect to come out ahead — that's what offering is.
(3) In a deal like mine it's roughly zero-sum: dollars one way, royalty rights the other. So if they expect to gain from the trade, their expected gain is your expected loss — at least once you update on the fact that they chose to offer this number and not a higher one.
(4) So the offer itself is evidence against the deal, and a rational counterparty who hears (2) and (3) should just decline as a blanket policy. The publisher knows my sales data way better than I do, so this goes double for me.
(5) But run the same logic everywhere and almost no voluntary trade between informed adults should ever happen: no book deals, no house sales, no hiring at a salary the employer picked, no stock trades. Which is insane, because these deals happen constantly and clearly both sides frequently do fine.
(6) So the argument proves too much, therefore something in it is broken.

Where does it break? If I have to keep one premise I'd really like to keep (1) — updating on the fact of the offer just seems like basic rationality, and I don't want to give that up. Also, two follow-ups. First: is there a repaired version of the argument that IS right, i.e. is my brother-in-law accidentally onto something about my royalty buyout specifically, where the other side really does have private information and I really am the mark? Second: tell me what would change your verdict — which step you'd stop rejecting if some background condition held, or where your diagnosis of the flaw is most likely to be the wrong one. I want to know what I'd have to believe to end up back at his conclusion, becuase I'd rather know that now than at Christmas.
