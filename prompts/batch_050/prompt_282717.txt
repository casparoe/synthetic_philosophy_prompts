I'm revising a paper on evolutionary debunking arguments, and I keep finding that my seminar students -- and, frankly, my coauthor -- treat the question "does the debunking argument succeed?" as a single thing, when it seems to me to be a conjunction of several quite independent claims. I'd like your help forcing the issue apart. Here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage: evolutionary considerations give a non-naturalist moral realist -- someone who holds that there are stance-independent moral truths and that we have epistemic access to some of them -- a defeater for most of her substantive moral beliefs, such that she would be required, on reflection, to substantially revise or suspend them. Note what this rules out: the claim is not merely that some genealogy of morals is true (everyone grants that), nor that debunking works against a naturalist who builds tracking into her theory, but that it genuinely bites for the non-naturalist who insists her moral judgments were never supposed to be selected for truth.

I'd like your credence in that proposition, but more importantly the decomposition. Please identify the sub-claims it depends on -- I'd expect at minimum something about whether our moral dispositions were in fact shaped primarily by selection for reproductive fitness rather than by any truth-tracking process; something about whether a non-naturalist can supply a "third factor" or pre-established harmony that would explain a correlation between fitness and moral truth without circularity; something about whether the argument overgeneralizes (e.g., whether it would equally debunk our mathematical or epistemic beliefs, and whether that counts against it); and whatever other terms you think the final number actually multiplies through. Assign a rough probability to each sub-claim, say in one or two lines why, and then show how they combine -- noting, if relevant, that the conjunctive structure means the overall credence can be dragged down by the weakest link.

Two further requests. First, tell me which single sub-claim your final number is most sensitive to -- that is, which one, if you learned its truth value, would move the overall credence the most -- because my coauthor and I suspect our own disagreement lives in exactly one of these and we haven't been able to locate it. Second, here's a wrinkle I'm considering adding to the paper, and I'd like your reaction built into the estimate: suppose we someday encountered an independently evolved intelligent species whose members converged, without any cultural contact, on a recognizably similar core of moral judgments -- prohibitions on gratuitous harm to kin, norms of reciprocity, but also something like impartial benevolence. Would that discovery raise or lower your credence, and by how much? I have a suspicion that debunking arguments commit their proponents to predictions about such counterfactuals that nobody has made explicit, and I'd like to see whether your decomposition treats convergence as evidence for a third factor, as evidence that the relevant judgments are just cheap equilibria any social species lands on, or as evidentially neutral.
