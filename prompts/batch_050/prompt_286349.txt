I run a small team of analysts at a research shop (we do forecasting for clients, mostly markets and policy questions), and we have a recurring problem that I suspect Aumann's agreement theorem is relevant to, though I've only ever read summaries of it. Two analysts will look at the same question, both come across as smart and well-informed, and end up at genuinely different probabilities -- say 30% vs 65% on some geopolitical event -- and then just... leave it there. "Agree to disagree," they say, which if I understand the theorem at all is exactly the thing two honest Bayesians with a shared prior can't coherently do. The theorem seems to say that if each of them knows the other's estimate, and knows the other knows, and so on, and each trusts the other to be reasoning honestly, their estimates have to converge -- because the disagreement itself is evidence each side has to chew on. In practice though nothing converges and I'm the one who has to pick a number to send the client.

What I want is a working protocol we can run when two analysts disagree -- call it a disagreement audit -- that operationalizes whatever is actually true and usable in the theorem, without pretending my analysts are ideal Bayesians. It should be a sequence of steps they run together in an hour or less: how to surface what private information or different background assumptions each side is relying on, how to test whether each side actually trusts the other's competence and honesty enough for the theorem's logic to bite, what to do when they don't (is mutual distrust itself information? whose?), how to tell a disagreement about facts from a disagreement about priors, and what the procedure should output in each case -- a revised joint number, a documented split, an escalation to me. Please include the point where the procedure has to stop and admit that real humans aren't Bayesians and common knowledge is never literally achieved, and say honestly which steps are genuine procedure versus judgment calls in disguise. I want to run this maybe two or three times a month, stakes are real money but not company-killing, and the analysts involved won't have read any decision theory so the protocol can't lean on jargon.

Then apply the protocol to three worked cases: (1) two analysts at 30% vs 65% on an election where one has been quietly weighting a polling method the other distrusts; (2) two analysts who disagree but where it turns out each privately thinks the other is bad at this particular domain, even though they'd never say so; (3) a case where the procedure, run correctly, concludes the disagreement is a legitimately different prior and neither side should move -- an outcome I'll find deeply unsatisfying, since it means I still have to pick a number myself. For that last case, spell out what the theorem actually permits there, because I suspect people overstate what Aumann rules out. Also flag the known failure modes of the whole exercise -- e.g., analysts anchoring to the first number said aloud, or convergence that's just social pressure wearing a theorem's costume.
