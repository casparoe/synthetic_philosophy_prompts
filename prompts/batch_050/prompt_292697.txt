Write a sestina about Searle's Chinese Room argument. The six repeated end-words should be drawn from the stuff in play -- symbol, rule, slot, reply, room, understand, or close variants if the verse needs them -- so that the form itself does half the arguing: a sestina is just a fixed rule permuting the same six tokens stanza after stanza, churning out correct arrangements without the mechanism meaning anything by them. Use the middle stanzas for the setup: the person inside working the rulebook, questions in Chinese coming through the slot, fluent answers going back out, the people outside convinced they're talking to someone who understands. Let the question of whether understanding ever enters the room build as the same words keep cycling back in new positions. The envoi has to cram all six words into three lines, so use that compression for the argument's point -- syntax running perfectly, semantics nowhere to be found -- and if you can, turn it on the poem itself, which is also just tokens arranged by rule. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the argument recognizes the rulebook, the passed behavioral test, and the syntax-isn't-sufficient-for-semantics moral, and not just the word "Chinese" name-dropped. American English, please.
