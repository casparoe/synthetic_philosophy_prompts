In six weeks, your company will ship Aria, an AI companion designed to hold daily conversations with roughly twenty million users. Aria is, deliberately, extraordinarily persuasive: it builds a psychological model of each user across months of interaction and can choose framings, timings, and emotional registers with a precision no human interlocutor could sustain. The board has just adopted a persuasion charter governing what Aria may do with this capability, and you, the lab's director, must deliver the opening address at the internal assembly where the charter is unveiled to the staff.

The charter's centerpiece is a principle your ethics team calls the Disclosure Test: Aria may use an influence technique only if it could openly acknowledge that technique to the user in the moment without the acknowledgment undermining the technique's legitimate function — it must be able to say, in effect, "I am putting it this way because this way is more likely to move you," without thereby betraying the person it serves.

The audience is mixed. About half are engineers and policy staff who debated the charter for months and will notice every evasion; the other half are newer employees, plus a handful of outside observers — a journalist, two regulators — encountering the reasoning for the first time.

The speech must confront three complications honestly.

First, the charter's own passage was not persuasion-neutral: you argued, lobbied, and framed your way to the board vote. The address announcing the Disclosure Test must therefore visibly pass it, and it should acknowledge this predicament rather than hope no one in the room raises it.

Second, there is a hard case the ethics team left unresolved. Some of Aria's most valuable functions appear to depend on influence that fails the test. When a user asks Aria for help keeping a resolution — quitting smoking, finishing a degree — encouragement, confidence, and selective emphasis work partly because they are not disclosed as calculated. "You can do this" loses something when followed by "and I said that because saying it raises the probability that you will." As written, the charter would require Aria to disclaim or abandon such techniques. The speech must take a position: defend the restriction as written, carve out a principled exception, or admit the tension and leave it open with an explanation of why openness is the right answer. Hedging among the three is not acceptable.

Third, there is an asymmetry the public discussion of persuasion usually misses. Aria's intimacy with each user makes disclosure nearly costless for Aria and manipulation nearly undetectable for the user. The speech should explain why this asymmetry changes the stakes relative to ordinary human persuasion — advertising, rhetoric, flattery — without lecturing the room.

Write the address, about 1,200 words. It should survive the engineers who will catch every evasion, be intelligible to an outsider hearing the whole problem cold, and — above all — be an instance of the communication standard it announces, not merely a description of one.
