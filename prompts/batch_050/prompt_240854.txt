I'm a high school senior, and for my school's political theory seminar I put together an argument that now worries me, because its conclusion seems to dissolve a distinction I thought was load-bearing. The background is the familiar three-way contrast between negative liberty (freedom as the absence of interference), positive liberty (freedom as actually having the capacity to do and become things), and republican liberty (freedom as not standing under anyone's arbitrary power). Here is the argument:

(1) A person is negatively free to do X just in case no external obstacle prevents her from doing X.
(2) A person is positively free to do X just in case she has the real capacity to do X.
(3) These are two descriptions of one fact: every external obstacle can be redescribed as a missing capacity (a locked door is an inability to leave the room), and every missing capacity can be redescribed as an external obstacle (paralysis is one's own body refusing to cooperate; poverty is the barrier of prices and property rules standing between a person and the goods).
(4) So negative and positive liberty are coextensive — they classify every case identically — and the distinction between them is purely verbal.
(5) Republican liberty is just negative liberty with a condition on the source of the interference, namely that it must come from an arbitrary power; so if the distinction in (4) is verbal, republican liberty is the same condition yet again, differently described.
(6) A merely verbal distinction cannot ground real disagreements.
(7) Therefore the political disputes that are supposed to turn on these concepts — whether high taxation makes people less free, whether poverty is a kind of unfreedom, whether the subject of a kindly dictator is free — are not real disagreements at all, only differences in vocabulary.

I find (7) absurd, since those disputes feel like the most substantive ones in politics, but I cannot locate the step I would reject. I even ran a symmetry test of my own devising on the argument — checking whether every redescription in one direction genuinely has a mirror image in the other — and it passed every case I tried, including the tricky ones: the prisoner whose cell is unlocked but whose guard would lock it the moment he moved, and the citizen whose government could lawfully confiscate her passport at any moment but never does.

Two requests. First, name the premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked acceptable in the first place, and tell me what rejecting it costs. I would rather keep (1) and (2), since they are the definitions I was taught, and I very much want to keep the reality of the political disputes; so tell me what I have to give up instead. Second, if my symmetry test is itself the source of the error, explain what a better test would look like for deciding whether two concepts of liberty differ substantively rather than verbally, and run that better test on all three concepts.
