Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend who is a physics graduate student. Her view was blunt: gauge symmetry is merely redundancy in our description of nature, gauge transformations change nothing physical, and only the gauge-invariant content of electromagnetism and Yang–Mills theory is real. I pressed her with two cases I don't think she ever absorbed. First, the Aharonov–Bohm effect, where the measurable phase shift depends on the enclosed magnetic flux and can be stated gauge-invariantly only by appealing to holonomies around loops, which are nonlocal objects. Second, the phenomenon of edge modes: on regions with a boundary, certain gauge transformations appear to act on genuinely physical degrees of freedom at the boundary, which sits awkwardly with the claim that gauge transformations are always mere redescriptions. She kept replying that the holonomy formulation dissolves the first problem and that boundary phenomena are a technicality. I suspect both replies are too quick, and I want to find out whether my students can do better than she did.

I teach an upper-level graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics. Draft one essay exam question on gauge freedom and surplus structure, answerable in 75 minutes, built around this crux: the claim that gauge-variant structure is surplus does real work in the interpretation of gauge theories, but the Aharonov–Bohm effect and edge modes each expose a different cost of that claim, and a good answer must identify the costs rather than merely recite the slogan that only gauge-invariant quantities are physical. The question should be unanswerable by summarizing standard textbook material on gauge symmetry; it should require the candidate to take a position on whether the Aharonov–Bohm effect is best understood as evidence for the reality of gauge-variant potentials, as an argument for a nonlocal but gauge-invariant ontology, or as neither, and to explain what the edge-mode case adds that the Aharonov–Bohm case does not.

Provide:

1. The exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, with any necessary setup stated precisely enough that a candidate cannot hide behind vagueness about what "surplus structure" means in this context.

2. A model answer of roughly 600 to 800 words, at the level of a strong candidate under time pressure, not a polished article.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each totaling 20, and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates the marks there (for instance, treating the Aharonov–Bohm phase as straightforward evidence for the reality of the vector potential without addressing the holonomy reformulation, or conflating the claim that a representation is redundant with the claim that the degrees of freedom it describes are unphysical).

4. A short paragraph on what distinguishes an answer that merely passes from one that earns top marks, keyed to how the candidate handles the tension between locality and gauge invariance.

Where the question or model answer invokes specific physics, keep to material a well-prepared philosophy of physics graduate student can be expected to know: the Aharonov–Bohm effect, holonomies, gauge-invariance as a criterion of physicality, and large gauge transformations at boundaries. Do not lean on obscure technical results I would have to fact-check separately.
