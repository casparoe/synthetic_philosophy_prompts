I'm teaching an undergraduate epistemology seminar, and this past week we covered the KK principle — roughly, the claim that if you know something, you're in a position to know that you know it — alongside the standard anti-luminosity arguments against it, which use margin-for-error reasoning and sorites-like series of gradually changing cases (a slowly fading light, imperceptibly cooling coffee) to argue that no non-trivial mental condition can be luminous, i.e., such that whenever it obtains you're in a position to know that it obtains. My students followed the formal machinery well enough, but they kept asking a fair question: what would it actually look like to live inside the principle? To answer them, I'd like you to write a piece of satire that shows rather than tells.

Write an internal memo or employee-handbook excerpt, around 600–700 words, issued by the "Epistemic Assurance Department" of a corporation that has adopted the KK principle as binding policy. Under this policy, any employee who knows anything is required to know that they know it, and to certify that higher-order knowledge on Form KK-1 — which certification they must, of course, also know to have been completed correctly, via Form KK-2, and so on up the ladder. The memo should be deadpan and procedurally plausible throughout: an induction seminar for new hires on recognizing the inner glow of knowing, an escalation pathway for employees who report knowing something but cannot yet honestly report knowing that they know it, a graded disciplinary schedule for first-, second-, and third-order failures, and a special protocol for the night-shift surveillance operator watching a light fade very slowly, who must file a report at each moment stating whether she still knows the light is on, and whether she knows that she knows.

The satire must work from the inside: every policy should be something a sincere defender of luminosity or KK could actually endorse, pushed to absurdity rather than caricatured. A reader who knows the debate should be able to recognize the margin-for-error premise smuggled into the fading-light protocol, the iteration regress encoded in the form-numbering system, and the single unremarkable closure principle quietly doing all the damage — without the memo ever naming a philosopher or stating the argument outright. Ideally it should end on a beat that is funny precisely because it is the principle's own logic carried one step further than anyone intended.
