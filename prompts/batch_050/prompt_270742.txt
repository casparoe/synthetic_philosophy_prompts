Recently, after a string of exhausting night shifts, I drove home along a familiar route and realized at some point that I had no memory of the previous twenty minutes — in particular, no idea whether I had already passed the first of the two turnoffs I normally use. Nothing bad came of it, but the episode unsettled me, and a philosophically inclined friend pointed out that I had essentially lived through the absent-minded driver problem from decision theory.

In that problem, a driver must decide at each of two indistinguishable exits whether to turn off; taking the first exit is the worst outcome, missing both exits is bad but tolerable, and taking the second exit is by far the best. Because of her absent-mindedness, upon arriving at an exit she cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and she cannot leave herself reminders. It turns out that the strategy she would judge optimal before setting off no longer looks optimal to her once she is sitting at an exit and deliberating: given the beliefs her own plan induces about which exit she is at, turning off appears more attractive than the plan permits.

Please argue that the driver should nevertheless adhere to the strategy she would have endorsed from the planning stage. Your argument should identify precisely what the deliberation at the exit overlooks or illegitimately presupposes — for instance, the fact that her uncertainty at the exit is generated partly by her own past and future choices rather than by the world — and it should answer the strongest objection on the other side: that a rational agent must act on her current information, and that her earlier conclusion is now merely one more piece of advice she is free to weigh and discard. Finally, tell me briefly what your verdict implies for someone in my position: is adopting a rigid standing rule for such moments a rational safeguard, or does it simply smuggle the planning perspective back in under another name?
