For my ethics class final project, I'm building a decision tree that sorts people into positions on the question: are everyday conventions -- tipping, queueing, jaywalking, taking off your shoes indoors -- genuine moral obligations, and are visitors to a place morally required to follow local conventions even when those conventions strike them as pointless or even slightly wrong? I'd like your help designing it.

Please act as a professional moral philosopher who specializes in social norms and conventions, and who has real experience designing diagnostic instruments that sort people by what they actually believe rather than by the labels they give themselves. Think carefully about this. Before writing anything final, briefly brainstorm the logical space: what are the genuinely independent questions on which views about conventions can come apart, and which combinations of answers correspond to positions that real people actually hold?

Then produce a decision tree with the following features:

1. A starting commitment that everyone in the debate shares, stated neutrally -- something like "conventions often make social life go better," without presupposing that this generates obligations.

2. Branch nodes phrased as questions a respondent could answer without knowing which position they lead to. Use concrete cases rather than abstract formulations: for example, a traveler in a country where queue-jumping is tolerated who hates queue-jumping; jaywalking across an empty street at 3 a.m. where no driver could be startled; an American visitor in a country where tipping is considered mildly insulting; a local custom a visitor finds mildly unjust rather than merely pointless (say, a convention about who defers to whom on a sidewalk). The branches should turn on distinctions like: does the obligation depend on others actually complying, or only on the convention's usefulness; does it survive when no one is harmed by a violation; does it bind visitors at all, and if so, does a visitor's obligation come from the same source as a local's; and does a convention's being unfair to some group cancel the obligation to follow it.

3. At least eight leaves, which must include: a view on which conventions generate obligations only because people rely on them; a view on which conventions bind because violating them free-rides on others' compliance; a view on which conventions have no independent moral force at all and only underlying harms matter; a strong "when in Rome" view for visitors; and a view on which unjust conventions must be defied even at some social cost. Name each leaf in a way that describes the position, not just a philosophical -ism, since this is for high schoolers.

4. A scoring note for any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate -- say explicitly what additional question would be needed and why it might be hard to phrase neutrally.

5. A short section listing any combinations of answers in the logical space that no one seems to occupy, with a one- or two-sentence diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

End with the tree's instructions for a respondent, in second person, written so a classmate could walk through it in five minutes and land on a position they recognize as their own.
