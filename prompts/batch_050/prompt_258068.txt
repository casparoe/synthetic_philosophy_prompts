I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training who volunteers on Saturdays teaching programming to kids aged 9–12 through a local library program. I've been reading John Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education, which I picked up after a coworker mentioned it, and I was struck by three connected claims I hadn't expected from a 17th-century writer. In my own words: first, that whatever learning is imposed on a child as a task or burden quickly becomes irksome to them, even if it was previously a thing of delight or indifference, so the mind forms an aversion to it; second, that children should therefore be drawn into learning as though it were play — Locke even sketches schemes like putting letters on dice so reading becomes a game rather than a lesson; third, that esteem and disgrace are the most powerful incentives available to an educator, while material rewards and corporal punishment both corrupt the learner's motivation in different ways.

I'd like to pressure-test these claims experientially before I trust them with actual kids, so I want to run a 90-minute session at my local developers' meetup (about 16 adults, all programmers, none familiar with Locke) where participants themselves feel the force of that first claim — the way imposed tasks breed aversion even toward things we started out enjoying. Design the activity. It should involve participants learning some small, genuinely learnable skill (something like reading a novel text encoding or an unfamiliar notation would fit our crowd, but pick whatever works) under contrasting regimes that they rotate through, structured so that the aversion effect emerges from the rules and incentives themselves before anyone names it. Give me concrete rules, roles if useful, a timeline, a scoring or incentive scheme that doesn't involve real money, and a plan for when to reveal what the session was about.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the three claims as I've paraphrased them. Be honest with me about a worry I have: is this activity actually testing Locke's claim, or a nearby modern claim (I've heard of the overjustification effect) that Locke wouldn't fully endorse? Tell me where his position, as I've stated it, diverges from what my session would demonstrate. Also flag what will predictably go wrong with a room full of engineers, and which features of the view — say, long-run habit formation in children rather than one-session effects in adults — the activity cannot reproduce and I'd simply have to state.
