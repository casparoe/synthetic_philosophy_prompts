Background: I'm a software engineer with zero formal training in history or philosophy, and I've gotten nerd-sniped by the "was the Renaissance even a real thing" fight. As I understand the state of play: Burckhardt's The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860) argued that something genuinely new happened in Italy between roughly 1300 and 1500, above all the birth of the modern individual. Ever since, medievalists have been chipping away at it: there was a Carolingian renaissance, a renaissance of the twelfth century, courtly love, Abelard writing about his inner life, and so on; Huizinga painted the fifteenth century as the autumn of the Middle Ages rather than a new spring; these days people talk about a "long Middle Ages" that basicly runs into the 1600s. The other side just keeps insisting that something obviously shifted. This has been going for well over a century and, as far as I can tell, nobody has moved an inch.

Here's the passage from Burckhardt I keep circling back to. It's from Part II, "The Development of the Individual," where he's arguing that the fully private person first appeared under the Italian despotisms:

"The private man, indifferent to politics, and busied partly with serious pursuits, partly with the interests of a dilettante, seems to have been first fully formed in these despotisms of the fourteenth century. Documentary evidence cannot, of course, be required on such a point."

Two things jump out at me here. First, "seems to have been" plus "documentary evidence cannot be required" is a wild combination from an engineering point of view: if no document could settle it either way, what kind of claim is this even? Second, his evidence base elsewhere in that chapter is basicly novelists and famous oddballs, i.e. the loudest 0.1% of the population, and he sort of admits it.

My working hypothesis, offered as an outsider's guess: this debate is secretly verbal. The two sides are using "new" and "individual" differently, like two engineers arguing about whether a system was "rewritten" where one counts lines of code changed and the other counts architectural changes — both right, talking past each other forever. But I can also see it being a real disagreement about which data should count (elite self-description vs. law, demography, parish records), or about bedrock intuitions regarding how much continuity something can have before it's "a different thing."

So here's what I actually want: a diagnosis, not a survey. What kind of disagreement is this? Is Burckhardt's claim in that passage the sort of thing that could be true or false in a checkable way, or is it a gestalt/interpretive claim that evidence can't touch — and if the latter, is that a defect or just what all periodization claims are like? Use what the two sides actually say and do (what evidence they reach for, what they count as a counterexample, what they ignore) to back the diagnosis. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a cleaner distinction, an agreed operationalization of "individualism" (count autobiographies? portraits? given names? diaries?), a formal argument that period boundaries are just bookkeeping conventions, or should I accept that the question was ill-posed from the start and the stall is permanent?
