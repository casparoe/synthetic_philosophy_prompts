Since I was elected chair of our housing cooperative's residents' committee last spring, I have become painfully attentive to how arguments are lost before they begin: someone asks when we are finally going to stop wasting money on the laundry booking system, and by the time anyone objects, the meeting has silently settled that money was being wasted. I have now persuaded the committee to let me run a forty-minute session for the other members — intelligent adults with no background in linguistics or philosophy — on presupposition, accommodation, and conversational scorekeeping, so that they can spot this happening in the wild. Plan the session rather than deliver it. I want the order in which the three ideas must be introduced and what each depends on; the one meeting-room example that should carry the whole session, what it must contain (a premise that enters without announcement, and a natural opening for someone to push back) and what it must not be (a loaded question, which would teach manipulation as the default case); the misconceptions I should expect — that presupposing is just assuming, that accommodation is always underhanded, that the common ground is whatever has been said out loud, that a failed presupposition simply makes a sentence false — and precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off; the closing exercise that would show me the idea has actually landed; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
