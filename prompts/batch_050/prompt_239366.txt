A friend of mine classifies minds using Daniel Dennett's four-level taxonomy. Darwinian creatures have their behavioral repertoire fixed by natural selection; variation and testing happen across generations, not within a lifetime. Skinnerian creatures can learn within a lifetime, but only by generating behaviors blindly and keeping whatever is reinforced. Popperian creatures can represent candidate actions in an inner environment and evaluate them there, so that bad options are discarded without being acted out in the world. Gregorian creatures, named for the psychologist Richard Gregory, go further: they import mind-tools from their culture — words, concepts, methods, transmitted know-how — that restructure what the underlying machinery can do.

Her verdicts on six cases so far:

- A sea slug whose withdrawal response is wholly fixed from birth: Darwinian.
- A laboratory rat shaped by rewards into lever-pressing: Skinnerian, the textbook case.
- A dog that, confronted with an unfamiliar fence, paces its length, pauses, backtracks, and then slips through a gap without having first tried and failed at the fence itself: Popperian.
- A Portia jumping spider that scouts detours around obstacles to reach prey it can no longer see, revising its route as it scans the scene: Popperian as well, which she concedes is surprising for a spider.
- Honeybees performing the waggle dance: Darwinian — she insists the sophistication is installed by selection rather than achieved by the individual bee.
- A chimpanzee that acquired nut-cracking by watching its mother and whose group maintains its own local variant of the technique: Gregorian.

Now consider the following case, based on a real experimental finding. Individual bumblebees were shaped, step by step, to pull a string in order to drag an artificial flower within reach of its nectar. Bees that had never been shaped acquired the full technique simply by watching trained demonstrators, and the behavior subsequently spread and persisted within colonies as experienced bees died and naive ones took their place.

Please predict her verdict on the bumblebees, and justify the prediction. In doing so: first, state the rule her six prior verdicts suggest she is running, and identify which prior verdict is the closest analog to the new case; second, set out at least two alternative rules that fit all six verdicts equally well but diverge on the bees, explaining precisely where and why they diverge; third, say which rule you think best captures her actual classificatory practice, and why; and fourth, describe one further case — real or hypothetical — that would discriminate most cleanly between the leading candidates, stating what each candidate rule would predict about it.
