I am a high school student serving on my school’s student-faculty technology ethics committee. When an automated or AI-assisted system contributes to harm, we issue a nonlegal judgment about who, if anyone, is primarily morally answerable. We are now trying to make our decisions consistent.

Our previous verdicts were:

- An attendance-prediction program disproportionately flagged students from one neighborhood. The vendor had warned against making prior disciplinary referrals the main variable, but an assistant principal configured it that way. We held the assistant principal primarily responsible.
- An autonomous supply cart injured a student after its sensor gave a low-confidence obstruction warning. The supervising teacher understood the warning, had an emergency-stop control, and dismissed it as a glitch. We held the teacher primarily responsible.
- A tutoring platform began giving systematically misleading advice after the vendor silently changed its model. The counselor using it spot-checked some outputs but had received no notice of the change and had no particular reason to distrust it. We held the vendor primarily responsible.
- An automated greenhouse damaged plants through the interaction of a vendor’s default moisture model, a district privacy setting that limited sensor data, and an unusual but permitted student sensor arrangement. Everyone had followed their instructions, and no one understood the combined system. We concluded that there was a genuine responsibility gap: the institution had to redesign the system and clarify duties, but no individual deserved primary blame.

Our new case concerns an AI chemistry-laboratory assistant that tracks chemicals and adjusts ventilation. A recent vendor update, made without notice, treats shelves without scanned labels as empty. The school has disabled the assistant’s optional camera-based cabinet tracking for privacy reasons, although the vendor permits that setting. The regular teacher relies on the automated inventory because the manual describes it as more reliable than manual checks and the school has not trained her in a fallback procedure. During class, a substitute teacher temporarily puts an incompatible chemical on an unlabeled shelf, intending to scan it later, but forgets. The assistant issues no warning and does not increase ventilation. Several students are briefly exposed to irritating fumes.

What should our committee decide if we want to remain consistent with our precedents? Please identify several general principles that could explain all four earlier verdicts, state whom each principle would hold responsible in this case, and defend one verdict as the best fit. Is the closest precedent the silent vendor update, the greenhouse’s interaction of separately reasonable choices, or some combination of the earlier cases? Should we treat this as another responsibility gap, or is someone primarily answerable? Finally, describe one additional fact or hypothetical case that would best distinguish the competing principles. Please reach a definite recommendation rather than merely concluding that responsibility is shared by everyone.
