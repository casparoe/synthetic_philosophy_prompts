I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I am writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the Meno problem -- the question of why knowledge should be more valuable than mere true belief -- that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar story about the road to Larissa or by listing the standard menu of positions (reliabilism, virtue epistemology, and so on) without saying anything about what makes the problem hard.

The question I have in mind should be built around the swamping problem: the worry that if the value of knowledge is explained by the value of the reliable faculty or virtuous method that produces it, then that value is merely instrumental and adds nothing once the true belief itself is already on the table -- just as a good espresso machine contributes no extra value to a cup of espresso that is already good. I want candidates to be forced to say what kind of value knowledge could have that would survive this objection, and to test their proposal against a case rather than leaving it at the level of intuition.

To that end, please build the question around this case, which candidates have not seen before: Talia runs a meteorological station whose instruments feed her beliefs about the weather in a remote valley she has never visited; unbeknownst to her, a rival installed the instruments with a miscalibration intended to corrupt her readings, but by sheer coincidence the miscalibration exactly cancels out a systematic quirk of the valley's microclimate, so the readings -- and her beliefs -- are accurate. Ruth, by contrast, forms a belief about the same valley's weather by flipping a coin, and happens to be right. The question should ask candidates (i) whether Talia knows, (ii) whether Talia's true belief is more valuable than Ruth's, and (iii) whether their answers to (i) and (ii) can come apart -- and if they cannot, whether that vindicates or embarrasses the account of knowledge's value the candidate has defended.

Please deliver: (a) the question as it would appear on the exam, worded so that a candidate could answer it in about 75 minutes; (b) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (c) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, worth 20 marks in total, where each point is stated as a specific move a top answer must make (not a topic it must mention), accompanied by a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically bungle that point.

The model answer should take a definite position rather than survey the options, and it should at minimum grapple with the following: the distinction between instrumental and final value and why the swamping problem turns on it; whether treating knowledge as a success attributable to ability -- an achievement -- gives knowledge a value that is not swamped by the truth it contains, or whether the same objection simply reappears at the level of the achievement; the role that environmental luck plays in the Talia case, and why it is the right test case precisely because Talia's method is de facto reliable in her environment; and what it would show if the best account of knowledge's value turned out to predict that some items of knowledge are no more valuable than mere true beliefs.

The marking scheme should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer: name the specific moves an A answer must contain that a competent but superficial B answer will lack.
