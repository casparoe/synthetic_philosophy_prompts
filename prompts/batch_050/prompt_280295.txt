I retired six years ago after four decades as a geochemist, and since then I've fallen down the history-and-philosophy rabbit hole that seems to claim a lot of us ex-scientists. Here's the position I've landed on, after a running argument with my grandson (history undergrad, insufferable in the way only a 20-year-old can be) and a spreadsheet I probably spent to many evenings building: when it comes to forecasting the fate of large political entities -- empires, states, maybe whole civilizations -- the "inside view" is worthless. By inside view I mean reasoning from the particulars: our institutions are more resilient, our technology unprecedented, our economy too interconnected to go the way of Rome, etc. etc. My spreadsheet has a couple hundred empires and major states in it with their lifespans, and my claim is that a forecaster who knew nothing about a polity except its rough category and the base-rate survival curve for that category would, on average, beat every historian and pundit reasoning from the details. People inside every declining empire presumably had excellent reasons why their case was special. The details aren't signal, they're the noise that lets each generation feel exempt from the base rate.

I've even operationalized this into a personal rule: for any forecast about the medium-term future of a big institution, start from the historical survival curve of comparable entities, and only allow inside information to shift the estimate by a small fixed amount (I use 15%, arrived at by vibes, which my grandson finds hilarious). What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. Not the usual hand-waving "every situation is unique" -- I mean: what specific evidence, argument, or result would actually demonstrate that inside-view reasoning can beat base rates for entities like these? What would have to be true about history -- its stationarity, the comparability of eras, the nature of the "collapse" events in my data -- for the rule to fail? And here's the part that genuinely worries me after a bottle of wine with my neighbor last week: choosing which entities count as "comparable," i.e. picking the reference class, seems to require exactly the kind of judgment about particulars that my rule forbids. Does that make the whole position self-undermining, or is there some principled way to select reference classes that doesn't smuggle the inside view back in through the side door?

A few more asks. Distinguish what a committed base-rate forecaster would themselves concede as a genuine defeater from what they'd dismiss out of hand -- I trust the former more. Rank the defeaters you come up with from fatal to merely awkward. And if the honest answer is that I've insulated the position against refutation with my own vagueness about what counts as a comparable case, say so plainly: a position nothing could falsify is just astrology with a spreadsheet, as my wife puts it.
