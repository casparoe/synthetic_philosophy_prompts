Earlier this year my wife and I reached an impasse we lacked the vocabulary for until a friend supplied it. I had recently begun donating a tenth of my income to rigorously evaluated global-health charities; she volunteers every Saturday at a shelter and regards my donations as a paid exemption from genuine involvement, while I privately believe her Saturdays accomplish less than half of what that money, redirected, would achieve. Our friend suggested that rather than each trying to convert the other, we attempt what philosophers have called a "moral trade": I concede on a dimension she cares about and she concedes on one I care about — I volunteer one Saturday a month, say, and she donates a fixed sum — so that by each of our own lights the world ends up better than if we had both simply persisted, or both compromised toward some midpoint neither of us endorses.

I am now less interested in whether such a trade would settle our argument than in whether the idea is coherent at all. Draft the joint statement that a proponent and a critic of moral trade could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Take the proponent to hold that moral trade is ordinary cooperation extended across irreducibly different values, and that refusing it out of squeamishness forfeits real good that every party, by its own lights, wants. Take the critic to hold that a moral commitment bargained away ceases to function as a moral commitment, so the alleged gains are purchased by hollowing out the very values being served.

The statement should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. Include, at minimum, the distinction between a trade (each side concedes on a different dimension of value) and a compromise (both sides move toward the middle of a single dimension); agreement that informal trading of this kind is already ubiquitous in ordinary moral life; and agreement that there are concessions no one may offer — identify at least one such limit both accept, and state the different reasons each side gives for accepting it.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, each formulated as a single precise sentence that its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view. Where a disagreement dissolves, on inspection, into a dispute about words — I suspect at least one does — mark it as verbal and explain why.

3. A cruxes section: the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a case, argument, or observation both parties accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side concedes or predicts about that case. Consider, for example, how each side should describe the voter who trades away his vote on an issue he cares little about in order to prevail on one he cares deeply about, or the devout woman who, in exchange for a neighbor's large donation to her church's food bank, agrees to stop publicly picketing the neighbor's business.

Close with a short paragraph signed by both parties stating what the collaboration actually changed: which formulations each side abandoned, which apparent disagreement survived scrutiny, and the single question they now agree the debate turns on.
