I'm a philosophy professor and in a couple weeks I'm presenting a paper at our university's interdisciplinary social science colloquium, and I need a serious Q&A prep sheet. The paper argues that generational categories — Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z — pick out real social kinds rather than being marketing fictions.

Here's the argument sketch so you know what they'll be shooting at. First move: generations are homeostatic property clusters. Members of a cohort genuinely cluster on things like risk aversion, job tenure, marriage and fertility timing, and institutional trust, because shared formative conditions — entering the labor market in a deep recession, being the first cohort to get smartphones at age twelve — causally generate these regularities. No essence required, just a cluster held together by common causes. Second move: once a label is in wide circulation it feeds back on the cohort itself. Employers, advertisers, journalists, and the members themselves start orienting their behavior to the label, so the kind becomes self-reinforcing — real in roughly the way money is real. I also argue that fuzzy boundaries are no objection, since plenty of respectable kinds (species, social classes) have fuzzy edges.

The audience is about half quantitative sociologists and demographers, plus a few cultural sociologists and two philosophers of social science. One of the demographers does age-period-cohort modeling, so that line of objection is guaranteed. The cultural sociologists think generational talk is a media-driven discourse that flattens class and race differences, and they wont be polite about it. The philosophers are broadly sympathetic but will press the metaphysics.

What I need: the objections I'll actually get, grouped by which camp raises them and written in the vocabulary that camp would use, ranked from "this could sink the talk" down to "annoying but manageable." For each one, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, plus a flag on where that reply is thinnest. Then separately: what I should give away early before anyone even asks, which objection is worth preempting inside the talk itself, which one will show up dressed as a friendly comment rather than an attack, and which question I should be praying nobody asks. Last thing: if the self-reinforcement move is carrying less weight than I think it is, tell me now instead of letting me find out in the room.
