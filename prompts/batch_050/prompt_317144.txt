So this got personal for me this year: my sister was choosing between a guaranteed-but-mediocre treatment path and a trial with a tiny chance of a catastrophic outcome plus a decent chance of a much better one, and I caught myself defending an Allais-ish preference pattern that I would have called irrational in grad school. My current view, roughly: the independence axiom is the right default for lotteries over final wealth in one-shot, no-aftermath choices, but it is not obviously binding when the "sure thing" crosses an option-closure threshold -- e.g. guaranteed enough money/health means you can later refuse dangerous work, fund care, leave a bad situation, etc. On that picture the common-consequence effect is sometimes rational because C is not really the same C across the two lotteries once you model lives/projects rather than terminal prizes, and regret is not doing all the work.

What would count against that? Please don't give me a tour of Allais or a bibliography; I want a map of exposure. Which experiments, choice patterns, formal results, or everyday cases would be fatal versus merely awkward? In particular: if my view predicts that people should reverse Allais preferences when stakes are reframed as portable capabilities rather than dollar amounts, what would it mean if well-designed reframings mostly don't move them? If independence still seems normatively compelling even after threshold goods are modelled, is that a defeater or just intuition lag? Would a defender like me have to concede that dynamic inconsistency, status quo bias, or ambiguity aversion is secretly explaining the data? Would evidence that the threshold effects vanish under incentive-compatible elicitation kill the view, or only dent it?

Also tell me whether I've built an unfalsifiable "lives not prizes" escape hatch: what would the view have to predict that expected utility with a richer outcome space doesn't already predict? Rank the defeaters from fatal to annoying, say which ones a committed defender of my position would accept as real pressure and which they'd shrug off, and flag any place where my position collapses into just saying utility is nonlinear or outcomes are multi-dimensional. Please be pretty self-critical: separate what's basically settled in decision theory from what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff, and don't present a contested repair of independence as if it were consensus.
