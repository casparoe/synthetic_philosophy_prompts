I'm a PhD student in philosophy (working on prudential value), and at our reading group last week somone defended the Epicurean line on death with a mirror analogy: the stretch of nonexistence after you die is just like the stretch of nonexistence before you were born — two blank stretches flanking your life, symmetric in every relevant respect. Nobody mourns the prenatal blank, so mourning the post-mortem one is inconsistent, and the rational response to both is indifference. It did a lot of work in the room and I went home annoyed, because I feel like the mirror is hiding something but couldn't say what on the spot.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of what, and which features of prenatal nonexistence are supposed to transfer to post-mortem nonexistence.
2. Where does the mapping actually break? I have two suspicions: that our asymmetric attitudes toward past vs. future aren't just a cognitive error the analogy corrects, and that there's a difference between "you could have died later" and "you could have been born earlier" (something about which of these is even a metaphysically open possibility, given that your identity arguably depends on roughly when you were conceived) that the mirror flattens. Tell me whether those hold up.
3. What does the analogy quietly import about what would have to make death bad — e.g., does it sneak in the assumption that death could only be bad if the dead person somehow experiences the blankness? And which of the disanalogies actually matter for the intended use, versus being merely cosmetic?
4. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion "death can't be bad for you" survive, or was the mirror carrying it the whole time? And if you think a different analogy does better — a book with no sequel vs. a book missing its first chapter, say — sketch what it gets right that the mirror doesn't.
