Since retiring from laboratory work I have been reading aesthetics, and I have been trying to state my position on taste in a way a colleague could attack. I would like the attack. My starting point is David Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste (1757). Early in it he concedes that beauty is not a quality of things themselves but exists only in the mind that perceives them, and that each mind perceives a different beauty; yet most of the essay is spent arguing that some judgments of taste are genuinely better than others, vindicated by the verdicts of critics of delicate sentiment, practiced, practiced in comparison, and free of prejudice. He illustrates this with the tale of Sancho Panza's kinsmen, who pronounce a hogshead of wine excellent except for a taste of iron and of leather, are laughed at, and are vindicated when the barrel is emptied and a key on a leather thong is found at the bottom. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Hume is right that beauty is a sentiment in the perceiver, not a property of the object; there is no aesthetic fact out there for judgments to answer to.

(2) Some verdicts of taste are nonetheless mistaken, and the correct verdict on a work just is whatever the ideally qualified critics would converge on.

(3) The long durability of agreement — Homer and Milton still admired after centuries — is genuine evidence that the standard of taste is tracking something.

(4) Hume's parable of the wine shows why we should trust delicate critics: their discrimination is confirmed against an independent check, as with a thermometer against a known temperature.

(5) Where qualified critics still disagree, owing to differences of temperament or of age and country, there is a fact of the matter about which side is right even if we cannot find it.

Are these jointly tenable? Please go through them pair by pair where it matters, tell me which tensions are real and which only apparent, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. I am particularly unsure whether (4) and (5) sit comfortably with (1) and (2), but I would rather you found the problems yourself than take my word for where they are.
