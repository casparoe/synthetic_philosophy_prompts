I have been keeping a running list of cases in which I do or do not trust what people, myself included, say about their own minds, and I would like to know whether my verdicts rest on any coherent principle or whether I am simply deferring to people when it suits me. Here are the cases, with the verdicts I find myself committed to.

First, when I report a headache, I take it that I simply cannot be wrong about whether it hurts, whatever I might be wrong about concerning its cause. Second, when a friend insists, voice rising and fist clenched, that he is perfectly calm, I think he is straightforwardly mistaken about his current state, and I feel no obligation to defer to him. Yet I cannot see why his report about himself should get less authority than mine about myself -- both are reports of how things currently feel. Third, when someone explains why they chose one job candidate over another, I generally do not trust the explanation: I suspect people often settle on a choice for reasons they have no access to and then construct a plausible rationale afterward, sincerely believing it. Fourth, when the same person tells me they feel dread before a presentation, I fully trust that they feel dread, but not their further claim that the dread is about the presentation rather than, say, the argument they had that morning. Fifth, when a person who avows egalitarian convictions consistently interrupts women more than men, I think their behavior reveals what they actually believe better than their sincere avowal does. Sixth, when an experienced nurse says she cannot explain why but something is wrong with a patient, and she turns out to be right, I am inclined to credit her self-report despite its lack of reasons -- which sits uneasily with my third verdict, since she too is reporting a judgment without access to its basis. Seventh, I trust a trained contemplative's fine-grained reports of her own experience more than an untrained person's, which suggests I think introspective reliability is a skill that varies, but I also hold, in the first case, that my pain report is infallible with no training at all.

My tentative principle was roughly this: people have privileged and near-infallible access to what they are currently experiencing, but no special access to the causes of their states, their dispositions, or the grounds of their judgments. But this misclassifies at least the second case, and possibly the sixth and seventh. What I want from you is this. Give me the best principle, or a principled partition of cases into two or three categories each governed by its own rule, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, and tell me precisely which clause of the principle is doing the work in each case. If no tolerably simple principle saves all seven, tell me which verdict you would sacrifice and why that one rather than another. Then apply your final account to three cases I have not listed and am genuinely unsure about: whether a person can be wrong that they are imagining an apple rather than merely supposing it; whether to believe someone who says, after years of marriage, that they no longer love their spouse; and whether to credit a reformed person's report that their old desires no longer tempt them, as opposed to merely being dormant. I would rather have an honest statement of where the account strains than a tidy one that hides it.
