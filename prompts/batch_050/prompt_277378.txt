I have recently taken over as the methods editor of a generalist history journal, and I am drowning in a specific kind of referee dispute. Following the broader turn toward environmental history, our submissions increasingly treat non-human forces -- plagues, volcanic winters, drought cycles, El Niño oscillations, the Little Ice Age -- as historical actors in their own right. Half my referees complain that these manuscripts lapse into crude environmental determinism, as though a dust veil or a bacillus could simply replace politics, institutions, and choice. The other half complain that rejecting such manuscripts enforces an anthropocentric bias that treats the material world as mere scenery. Both complaints are sometimes right, and I currently have no way to tell referees when. What I need is a review procedure that my board can actually run on a submission: an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions that separates a legitimate causal claim about non-human forces from deterministic overreach, grounded in whatever philosophy of historical explanation is genuinely usable here -- the difference between a background condition, a trigger, and a sufficient cause; what counterfactual dependence really requires; what it means to attribute agency to something that cannot act.

The procedure should be concrete enough for a referee without philosophical training to apply in a single reading. I would expect it to include, at minimum, tests along these lines, though you should refine or reorder them as the theory demands: a counterfactual test (would the outcome plausibly have occurred, and when, absent the environmental event?), a mechanism test (is there an explicit causal chain running through human perceptions and decisions, or does the argument leap from isotope measurements straight to regime collapse?), a comparative test (did similar shocks elsewhere fail to produce the outcome, and does the manuscript explain why?), a timescale test (does the claimed cause operate on the same scale as the effect -- a frost can trigger a mutiny but not, by itself, a two-century decline?), and an agency-ledger test (does the manuscript preserve, redistribute, or silently erase human choice, and is that choice defended or just stylistic?). For each step, state what evidence counts as passing, what warning sign counts as failing, and what the referee should do on a failure -- reject, demand revision, or merely note the limitation. Be explicit about which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the referee simply has to decide.

Please present the procedure itself as a table with the columns: Step, Diagnostic question, What passes, Red flag, Action on failure. Then run the finished procedure on three hypothetical submissions: first, a paper arguing that the Justinianic Plague ended classical civilization as such; second, a paper arguing that a volcanic eruption in 1600 explains the precise timing of a specific peasant rebellion; third, a paper arguing that an arid climate fully determined the centralized political form of an irrigation society -- and please make sure at least one worked case yields a verdict an instinctive humanist reader will dislike, in either direction, and say so. Finally, append a brief JSON object for each worked case with the fields case, verdict (accept, revise, or reject), failed_step (or null), key_reasoning (one to two sentences), and residual_judgment_call (the point, if any, where the procedure handed the decision back to a person).
