I'm teaching an undergraduate metaphysics seminar, and we've just finished three weeks on the unity of the proposition, culminating in Bradley's regress. My students keep treating the argument as a word trick -- as if the problem were just that philosophers refuse to stop asking "and what relates that?" I want them to feel why the demand is forced rather than fussy: if the proposition that a stands in R to b were just its constituents sitting side by side, we'd have a list, not a proposition; but if relating requires a further relation R1 to tie R to a and b, then R1 itself needs tying down by R2, and so on without end. Bradley's own conclusion -- that no finite complex of constituents and relations ever adds up to a unified fact, so that unity, if it exists at all, must belong to an undifferentiated Absolute beyond relations -- struck them as melodrama, and I'd like to show them it's the honest terminus of the dilemma.

Write me a pantoum of at least five quatrains that stages this dilemma, using the form's machinery to do the philosophical work. (For clarity: in a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and in the closing stanza the poem's opening lines return, so that the poem ends where it began.) The recycling lines should enact the regress itself -- each stanza's attempt to state what unifies the proposition should be forced to reappear, verbatim, as new material that itself stands in need of unification -- while the two horns of the dilemma (the regress of ever-further relating relations on one side, the mere juxtaposed list on the other) should each get a recurring line that a reader who knows the argument can identify by its role. The traditional closure, in which the very first line returns as the very last, should land as Bradley's verdict: the starting problem is never escaped by piling on more relations, only deferred one stanza at a time.

Please don't mention Bradley, propositions, or relations by name in the poem itself -- the argument should be recognizable from its structure and imagery, not from label-dropping -- and don't let the poem pretend that the regress is benign or that adding one more relation somewhere quietly fixes things; that evasion is exactly the mistake I'm trying to cure. A specialist should be able to point to which repeated line carries which horn of the dilemma and why the poem's ending is the only ending the form allows, while a reader who has never heard of the problem should still find it a good, unsettling poem about things that refuse to hold together.
