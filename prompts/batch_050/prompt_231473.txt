So context: I've been reading Overcoming Bias and LessWrong since basically the beginning, and the metaethics sequence left me permanently convinced that the is-ought gap is massively overrated as an argument against moral naturalism. The short version of my view: "you can't derive an ought from an is" is just a point about logical form — of course a conclusion can't contain normative vocabulary that wasn't in the premises — the same way you can't derive claims about electrons from claims about furniture without bridge principles, and nobody concludes electrons are spooky non-physical stuff. Anyway, I mentioned this at my university's philosophy society (I'm a data scientist, not a philosopher, but I've been going to their public lecture series for years and they let grad-student-adjacent weirdos sign up for talk slots) and now I'm committed to a 30-minute talk next month defending the thesis that the is-ought gap is a shallow logical point that gets inflated into a deep metaphysical one, plus 20 minutes of Q&A. The talk leans pretty hard on this sentence from the Preface to G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), which I think gives away the game:

"It becomes plain that, for answers to the first question, no relevant evidence whatever can be adduced: from no other truth, except themselves alone, can it be inferred that they are either true or false."

My reading of that sentence: Moore's real master-argument isn't the famous open question stuff from Chapter I that everyone teaches, it's an epistemological thesis — intrinsic-value propositions are self-evident and can't be supported by evidence from anything outside themselves — and *that's* the part a naturalist should reject, since it's the is-ought gap restated as a theory of justification rather than argued for. (My friends at reading group told me this reading is either trivially obvious or crazy, and they couldn't agree which.) The audience will be maybe 15 people, mostly analytic philosophy grad students plus a few faculty: one prof who works on Moore and early analytic philosophy and will definately have read the Preface more carefully than me, a Hume scholar, two Kantians, an error theorist who thinks everyone in the room is confused including other error theorists, and a couple of normative ethics people who mostly want to get back to trolley cases. Nobody rationalist-adjacent, so moves that feel obvious to me will land as question-begging.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight-ish objections I'm actually going to get from *this* crowd (not the ones I'd get in a LW comment thread), ordered by how much damage they do to the talk if I fumble them, each phrased the way that specific audience member would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I should just concede gracefully. Also flag whether my reading of the Moore sentence survives contact with the Moore prof, since the whole talk kind of hangs on it — including whether he can hit me with the fact that Moore's "first question" is about intrinsic value rather than right action, and whether that's fatal or a flesh wound. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks. American English, please.
