So I run a small philosophy-of-mind reading group (spun off from our local LessWrong meetup years ago, but these days it's mostly actual philosophy grad students, which is its own problem). Next month I'm presenting and I've decided to go full Dennett: my thesis is that qualia, in the standard "intrinsic, ineffable, directly apprehensible" sense, are an incoherent posit, and that the Chase and Sanborn case from "Quining Qualia" shows it — the two coffee tasters can't settle whether the coffee tastes the same as it used to or whether their standards have drifted, and there's no fact of the matter beyond the dispositions, so the supposed further phenomenal fact is empty.

The passage I want to build the talk around is this one, where Dennett states his real ambition:

"I want to shift the burden of proof, so that anyone who wants to appeal to private, subjective properties has to prove first that in so doing they are not making a mistake."

My plan is to argue the burden shift is *earned* by the intuition pumps, not just asserted — Chase and Sanborn being the workhorse. But I have a nagging feeling this is exactly where the talk is vulnerable: shifting the burden of proof is the kind of move that sounds obviously legitimate to people who already agree with you (it's very "what do you think you know and how do you think you know it" coded) and obviously question-begging to people who don't.

The audience: about eight people. Two of them have basically memorized Chalmers' The Conscious Mind and think the conceivability argument is fine. One hardcore illusionist who'll think I'm being too soft. A couple of Wittgenstein people who mostly show up to be difficult. One cognitive scientist who does psychophysics and will have opinions about whether the Chase/Sanborn setup is even empirically coherent (e.g. memory of tastes, discrimination thresholds, adaptation effects).

Give me my Q&A prep sheet:

- The objections I'll actually get, in rough order of how likely they are, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them (the Chalmers fans will say "you're just denying the datum," the psychophysicist will say something about signal detection)
- For each: what I should concede, what I should hold, and a reply I can give in under two minutes
- A note on where each reply is soft
- Whether the burden-of-proof passage actually does the work I want, or whether I should lead with something else
- The question I'm hoping nobody asks

One typo-worthy worry: does the Chase and Sanborn argument secretly assume that if two dispositional stories are empirically indistinguishable then there's no phenomenal difference — because that's pretty much the conclusion, isnt it? If that's the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, say so plainly.
