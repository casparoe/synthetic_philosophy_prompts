I'm a PhD student in decision science and I got roped into mediating an argument between two people in my cohort (one does mathematical finance, one does philosophy of probability) at the pub last night, and I genuinely can't tell who landed more punches. Can you adjudicate it move by move?

Background: we were talking about the Kelly criterion -- bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth. Round by round it went:

(1) Marcus (the finance guy) said Kelly is special because it's not just another utility function: if you bet full Kelly repeatedly, you almost surely end up richer in the long run than anyone using any other fixed strategy, so the case for it doesn't depend on your risk preferences at all. It's objective, not subjective.

(2) Anya (the philosopher) said that's an illusion. Maximizing expected log wealth IS just expected utility theory with log utility, and the asymptotic result doesn't change that -- it just says that if you happen to already want to maximize long-run geometric growth, Kelly achieves it. She said you can prove similar asymptotic dominance results for other rules relative to their own criteria, so "it wins almost surely" smuggles in the thing it's supposed to justify.

(3) Marcus pushed back: no, the dominance claim is stronger than she admits. A Kelly bettor asymptotically beats any *essentially different* strategy almost surely, not just relative to some criterion -- pick whatever goal you like, you'll be glad you held more money than the alternative would have given you. Money is all-purpose, so growth-optimality is criterion-free in a way maximizing expected wealth isn't.

(4) Anya replied with two things. First, the dominance is only asymptotic and almost sure -- there's no finite horizon at which Kelly is guaranteed ahead, and for any fixed horizon someone maximizing expected value beats Kelly in expectation. Second, she said "all-purpose money" cuts the other way: the whole reason we don't just maximize expected value (which would tell you to bet everything on any favorable bet) is that wealth has declining marginal value to actual people, which is a preference fact. So Kelly's appeal rides on the same kind of subjective consideration she was accused of importing, just laundered through an infinite horizon nobody inhabits.

(5) Marcus's last move: he conceded the infinite horizon is an idealization but said it's a *robustness* argument, not a utility argument -- Kelly also has nice finite-sample properties, like minimizing the expected time to reach any large wealth target, and drawdowns don't refute it because fractional Kelly exists precisely for people who can't tolerate them. Anya said fractional Kelly is her whole point: the moment you go fractional you've admitted the "objective" answer needed adjusting by taste.

They both looked at me and I said something diplomatic about beer. Who's right about what? Specifically I'd like to know: is the almost-sure dominance result as criterion-free as Marcus claims, does the finite-horizon expected-value point actually undercut it or just trade criteria, and is Anya right that fractional Kelly is a concession that sinks Marcus's objectivity claim, or is that equivocating between two different things? Also, is there something both of them are missing?
