The set question was: what exactly does Descartes take the cogito to establish, and does it establish the existence of a thinking self? Assess Lichtenberg's objection.

Here's the student's answer:

Descartes begins the Meditations with hyperbolical doubt, setting aside anything that admits of the least uncertainty, from the testimony of the senses right up to the truths of arithmetic, which fall once he raises the possibility of a deceiving God or demon. The cogito is what survives this: a deceiver of whatever power could not deceive Descartes about his own existence, since he would have to exist in order to be deceived at all. As Descartes himself sets it out, the cogito is an inference: given the general premise that whatever thinks exists, and given that he is thinking, his own existence follows with certainty. In the Second Meditation he puts the point as the realisation that he is, he exists, whenever this is conceived by him.

All the same, the most the argument strictly delivers is that thinking is going on. Hume raised the celebrated objection that Descartes is not entitled to the 'I' in 'I think': introspection finds only a flow of particular thoughts, never a thinker who has them. The cogito therefore shows that something thinking exists for as long as the thinking lasts, but it cannot establish a persisting self.
