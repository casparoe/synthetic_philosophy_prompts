So I'm a PhD student in moral psych and I keep arguing with a professor in my department (I TA'd her grad seminar on Williams last year) about the "one thought too many" thing. She has a pretty stable pattern of verdicts on cases, and I want to test whether I've actually figured out the rule she's running. Here are the verdicts I remember:

1. The classic case: a man can save either his wife or a stranger, and saves his wife while thinking "she's my wife, and in situations like this it's morally permissible to save one's wife." She says: one thought too many.
2. A guy visits his sick friend in the hospital because he's worked out that visiting the sick is a duty and it's his turn to do his bit. She says: one thought too many.
3. The same drowning case as (1), but the man just thinks "she's my wife!" and dives in. She says: totally fine, that's exactly how it should go.
4. Someone with no personal connection to anyone involved sits down, thinks hard about the demandingness of morality, and donates a chunk of their salary to Oxfam on that basis. She says: fine, no complaint.

Now here's the new case I want to spring on her. A woman read Williams years ago, was convinced by the integrity objection, and decided she was moralizing her personal relationships too much. So she deliberately trained herself out of it -- worked on becoming spontaneously partial, stopped running moral checks on her motives toward loved ones, etc. Now, years later, she saves her husband over a stranger and the only thing in her head is "he's my husband." No deliberation at all. But the whole disposition she's acting from exists because of that earlier bout of moral reflection.

Which way does my professor go on this one -- one thought too many, or fine? I'm less interested in your own take than in: (a) what are the two or three most plausible rules that fit her four verdicts, (b) what each of those rules predicts about my new case (I want at least two of them to disagree with each other -- if your rules all give the same prediction you haven't found the interesting ones), (c) your best guess for her actual verdict and why, and (d) one further case I could put to her that would best distinguish the rules you're torn between.

Keep it casual. End with your prediction as the last line in the form \answer{one thought too many} or \answer{fine}.
