So I do a little moral philosophy podcast, mostly interviews, and next month I've got a guest on who's spent years defending Scanlon-style contractualism against the aggregation problem -- she's got a book out arguing that the "we can't count heads" objection is overblown and that a cleaned-up version of the individualist restriction can handle cases like choosing between saving one stranger from death and saving a hundred from death.

The trouble is she's done this material a thousand times, so the usual questions ("isn't it callous not to save the greater number?") will just get the standard talk. I want to actually press her. Draft 8-10 interview questions, and for each one give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she'll most likely give, so I'm not left stranded when she deploys her prepared line.

Concretely, I want the set to hit at least these:

- the transmitter/World Cup style case, where sparing one person from serious harm seems right even against a huge aggregate of tiny annoyances -- but push on whether her view actually delivers that result for the right reason, rather than smuggling in intuition
- the tie-breaking maneuver (when complaints are relevantly similar, the greater number breaks the tie) and the objection that this looks like a patch that contradicts the view's whole spirit -- why is it suddenly fine to count when the stakes are equal?
- the relevance problem: what decides which complaints are "the same kind"? one death vs one death is easy, but what about one death vs. many permanent paralysis -- and if there's a principled line here, where is it?
- the circularity worry about reasonable rejectability -- what makes a rejection reasonable already seems to assume a prior view about who's permitted to do what, so is contractualism explaining wrongness or just relabeling it?
- one question about what it would take to make her give the view up, since contractualists are notoriously good at absorbing counterexamples by adjusting what counts as a complaint

No softballs, no "how did you get into philosophy" stuff, and keep the questions askable out loud, not paragraph-long exam prompts. At the very end, add a last line in exactly this form: Verdict: question #N -- where N is whichever question you think she'd find hardest to answer cleanly.
