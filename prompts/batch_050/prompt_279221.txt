I help run a small reading circle (mostly nonfiction, a bit of philosophy when we can manage it), and after yet another week of data-breach emails and a long argument about whether our phones are listening to us for ad targeting, I sat down and wrote out, as carefully as I could, what I actually believe about privacy. The plan is to hand the list to the group and let them tear into it, but before I do that I'd like a dry run here. Here is where I've landed:

1. Privacy is valuable only because of what it protects -- safety from people who'd use information against you, the freedom to experiment with who you are, the ability to be a different person with your colleagues than with your oldest friends. There is no value in simply not being known, considered on its own. If being known about never leads to anything bad, nothing of value was lost.
2. Even so, someone who finds a stranger's diary, secretly reads it cover to cover, puts it back, tells no one, never uses a word of it, and takes the secret to his grave has still done something wrong to that stranger.
3. At bottom, a privacy violation is a loss of control: someone acquiring information about me without my say-so. If I hand information over freely, then no violation has occurred, whatever the recipient goes on to do with it.
4. Yet if a retailer pieces together from my purchase history -- every item of which I scanned and paid for voluntarily -- that I'm pregnant, or depressed, or in financial trouble, and quietly uses that conclusion against me, then I have been wronged, and privacy violation is the right name for the wrong.
5. My genetic data is mine the way anything else about me is mine, and if I choose to spit in a tube and mail it to a testing company, that's my right and nobody else's business.
6. People who shrug and say they have nothing to hide, and don't care who collects what on them, are making a mistake. Their privacy matters even though they don't feel that it does.

What I want is an audit, not an essay. Take the commitments pairwise and tell me which ones genuinely can't be held together and which only look at first glance like they conflict but actually survive scrutiny. For each genuine tension, tell me how deep it runs, what the cheapest fix would be, and exactly what that fix costs me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision -- one commitment amended, dropped, or re-scoped -- would do the most to make the whole set coherent. Please don't just announce that I should abandon whichever item seems weakest; I want to see what each of these actually commits me to before I decide what I'm willing to give up, because as far as I can tell I believe all six of them.
