Last night a friend and I argued late about whether failing to do something can cause an outcome, and we kept talking past each other. So here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they commit me to.

1. My neighbor promised to water my orchid while I was away and didn't. The orchid died. Her failure to water it caused the death.
2. The Queen of Denmark also didn't water my orchid. Her failure to water it did not cause the death.
3. A hospital's backup generator would have kicked in if a technician hadn't disabled it during maintenance; a patient died in the blackout. The disabling caused the death — though note this is an act, and I want to treat it the same as an omission, which my friend says proves too much.
4. A jailer simply doesn't unlock a door that would open onto freedom, and the prisoner remains imprisoned for an extra year. The jailer's inaction caused the continued imprisonment — but I hesitate here in a way I don't in case 1, and I'm not sure why.
5. I don't warn you about a step I know is broken, you fall. My silence caused your fall. But a passing stranger who never saw the step and so also didn't warn you caused nothing. Note that in my case the warning was withheld, in his it merely never existed, and I'm inclined to think that difference matters even though the physical upshot is identical.
6. A doctor omits to administer a readily available drug, the patient dies; the doctor's omission caused the death. But if the drug would only have raised survival odds from 10% to 11%, I'm suddenly reluctant to call the omission the cause, which seems to make causation degree-sensitive in a way that bothers me.

Is there a single principle that delivers all six verdicts — presumably something about normality, obligation, and the availability of alternatives — and if so, state it precisely, say which clause is doing the work in which case, and tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: a vending machine that fails to dispense my snack, where I feel genuinely wronged by the machine. If no simple principle survives all six, tell me which verdict I should surrender and why it's that one rather than one of the others.
