I co-teach an undergraduate great-books seminar, and this week we read Aristotle's Politics, Book I. After class, two of my strongest students fell into an argument over a passage from chapter 4 (1253b–1254a), where Aristotle explains why masters need slaves: "if every tool could perform its own work when ordered, or by seeing what to do in advance, like the statues of Daedalus in the story, or the tripods of Hephaestus which the poet says 'enter self-moved the company divine,' —if thus shuttles wove and quills played harps of themselves, master-craftsmen would have no need of assistants and masters no need of slaves."

Daniel read the conditional as quietly fatal to the natural-slavery doctrine of the chapters that follow. On his view, it shows slavery enters Aristotle's analysis only as a substitute for the self-moving tools nature failed to supply: the justification is instrumental and economic, so in a world where shuttles really do weave themselves, Aristotle's own argument would dissolve the institution. He added that this makes Aristotle less odious than advertised — a man describing a stopgap, not defending human subordination in principle.

Mara replied that Daniel is conflating two distinct claims Aristotle keeps apart: the slave as a living tool of the household (chapter 4's question about why households contain slaves) and the natural slave as a being who can apprehend reason without possessing it (chapter 5's question about who may rightly be enslaved). The automata conditional, she said, is only about the household's need for labor and says nothing about who is fit to rule; even a fully automated household would still contain people who, on Aristotle's view, can only flourish under another's direction. She conceded one point: Daniel's reading explains why Aristotle bothers to state the conditional at all, whereas on hers it is idle ornament.

Both asked me to settle it, and I could not. Whose reading of the passage is better, and what does the answer imply for how the chapter 5 doctrine should be understood? Please adjudicate point by point rather than simply declaring a winner, and say what, if anything, both students are missing.
