I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of logic, and I'm setting the final exam. I want one essay question on Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895) built around the following passage from the middle of the dialogue:

"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it
(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.
Have you entered that in your note-book?"
"I have!" Achilles joyfully exclaimed, as he ran the pencil into its sheath. "And at last we've got to the end of this ideal race-course! Now that you accept A and B and C and D, of course you accept Z."
"Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

Students will have read the whole dialogue, but this passage will be printed on the exam paper. Here is what I'm trying to test. Every weak student writes the same essay: the Tortoise generates an infinite regress, so premises can't justify a conclusion all by themselves, so we need a rule of inference like modus ponens, the end. That summary is correct but proves nothing about understanding. I want a question that forces students to say precisely what goes wrong with Achilles's move of writing the hypothetical down as an additional premise — and to grapple with the harder question the dialogue leaves open: if accepting premises and accepting a rule of inference are different in kind, then the rule itself cannot be justified by an argument (on pain of either regress or circularity), so what, if anything, entitles us to follow it? The best students should be able to connect this to the dilemma Dummett identifies for any attempted justification of deduction — that such a justification must either beg the question or fail to persuade a genuine sceptic — without my handing them that framing.

Please write:
1. The exam question itself, worded as it would appear on the paper, answerable in 45 minutes. It should require students to interpret the quoted exchange (why does Achilles think entering D in the notebook settles the matter, and why is he wrong?) and then extend it to the justification-of-deduction problem. It must not be answerable by reciting the regress alone.
2. A model answer of about 600 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around five must-hit points with the marks each carries, that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — including, for each point, a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates mishandle it (e.g., treating the rule of inference as one more true proposition the Tortoise ought to accept, or saying the regress shows deduction is unjustified rather than that justification can't take the form of an argument).
