At a recent policy workshop on redesigning our council's pension enrolment forms, I argued that defaulting new employees into the savings scheme, rather than requiring them to opt in, was a manipulative way of exploiting inertia. A colleague waved the objection off with the now-familiar line that choice architecture is unavoidable: every form has some default, the designer cannot opt out of designing, and so there is no neutral alternative against which the nudge could count as manipulation. Since then I have heard the same slogan deployed in other contexts — sometimes to defend nudging as obligatory rather than permissible, sometimes merely to excuse it, and once, curiously, to argue that critics of a supermarket's floor plan were being naive. I suspect the slogan is doing several quite different jobs under one wording, and I would like that laid bare.

Please enumerate the distinct precise theses that 'choice architecture is unavoidable' might be expressing, and for each one assess it. I have in mind, at minimum, discriminating among the following dimensions, though you should add any I have missed: whether the claim is that some structure of presentation must always exist (a trivial-sounding thesis about design) or that every such structure predictably steers behaviour (an empirical thesis about framing effects); whether the inevitability attaches to having a default or to having any particular default, since the choice between opt-in and opt-out itself looks anything but forced; whether the claim establishes only that influence is unavoidable, or the much stronger conclusion that no arrangement is less manipulative than any other; whether it yields a permission to nudge, an obligation to nudge well, or merely the cessation of a certain kind of criticism; and whether 'we' in 'we might as well nudge for good' picks out the designer, the state, or anyone at all.

For each reading you identify, I would like three verdicts: first, whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, or false, with a sentence or two of justification; secondly, whether it actually supports the use my colleague made of it — namely, dissolving the manipulation objection to the pension default — and if not, what further premise would be needed; thirdly, whether it is plausibly what people who repeat the slogan generally mean. Note in particular whether any reading commits the slide from 'some arrangement is forced on the designer' to 'this arrangement is no worse than the alternatives', and whether the strongest defensible reading leaves the manipulation worry intact in some reformulated version. A structured list I could take back to the workshop would be ideal.
