As a novelist, I keep relying on a particular device in dialogue and would like to know whether humour theory has an established name for it. The device: a character says something that is plainly inadequate to the situation, and the joke works only because the reader must do the correcting themselves. Two instances. First, a character tastes a disastrous soup at a dinner party and calls it a bold choice; the humour depends entirely on the reader supplying the accurate verdict the character withheld. Second, a character watches a bridge collapse and remarks that the council will be displeased; again, the gap between the remark and the event is where the laugh lives. Crucially, I have noticed that the device fails if I make the gap too wide, so that the reader cannot close it, or too narrow, so that there is nothing left to supply. There seems to be a band of optimal underdetermination. Please respond as an expert in the philosophy and empirical psychology of humour, thinking carefully and brainstorming before settling on your answer: does this configuration have a recognised name, and how does it differ from understatement, irony, incongruity-resolution, and benign violation? If nothing established fits, say so and coin a term with a precise definition and boundary cases. Kindly answer in British English.
