I'm a researcher in deep reinforcement learning with no formal training in philosophy of mind. I'm fluent in the standard formalism — reward functions, value and advantage estimates, temporal-difference errors, policy-gradient updates, reward shaping, intrinsic motivation bonuses, the training/deployment distinction — and I keep encountering the claim, both in and out of the literature on AI welfare, that RL agents might suffer, or at least that negative reward is a serious candidate analogue of pain. I want a careful translation exercise, not a verdict.

Please restate the three or four main philosophical positions on what suffering, or negative valence generally, consists in — at minimum: the phenomenalist view that suffering is essentially a felt, unpleasant experience; the functionalist view that valence is whatever plays a particular causal role in organizing perception, learning, and behavior; and desire- or preference-frustration accounts of what is bad for a subject — using only the vocabulary I work in. For each position, say precisely which of its claims map cleanly onto components of the RL formalism, which can be mapped only with distortion, and which have no expression there at all. Be honest about the distortions: for example, in RL the reward is a scalar defined by the designer and consumed by the learning algorithm, not necessarily an observation the policy conditions on; value is an estimate of expected return rather than a current state; and a parameter update driven by negative TD error is not obviously an event that happens to anyone. If mapping a philosophical claim requires treating one of these as something it isn't, flag that rather than smoothing it over.

Then give me the residue report. Of the things philosophers treat as central to whether a system can suffer — phenomenal character, the existence of a subject for whom things go badly, the distinction between a signal that guides learning and a state that is itself aversive, the putative asymmetry between pain and pleasure — which cannot be stated in my vocabulary at all? For each item of residue, give me your judgment on whether it indicates that the RL framework is missing something morally relevant, or that the philosophical claim was trading on something less precise than it appeared. I am also interested in two specific stress tests: first, whether the fact that reward often functions purely as a training-time signal — a policy may be shaped by rewards the deployed agent never encounters again, and may act to avoid states that were never themselves paired with negative reward — undermines the negative-reward-as-pain analogy or merely reflects an implementation choice; and second, whether anything in the philosophical debate corresponds to intrinsic motivation terms like novelty or curiosity bonuses, which shape behavior from inside the agent's own exploration rather than from the task designer, and whether that makes them better or worse candidates for proto-valence.
