I'm TAing a decision theory course this semester and I want to hand out a dialogue for the week we do Newcomb-adjacent stuff. The backstory: a friend and I went down a rabbit hole reading about Hofstadter's old Luring Lottery and the Platonia dilemma from his Metamagical Themas columns, and we cooked up a variant we've been arguing about for days without either of us budging. Here's the setup. Twenty grad students each recieve a letter from an eccentric donor: enclosed is a postcard, and each recipient may mail back at most one. If exactly one card arrives, its sender gets $500,000. If zero or more than one arrive, nobody gets anything. The letter explicitly says you may use any randomizing device you like, and it lists all twenty recipients by name, so everyone knows who else is playing. Two of the twenty, Mara and Dev, happen to be roommates, and talking isn't forbidden — but nothing they agree on is binding, and cards are due Friday.

Write the dialogue between them that night. Mara thinks the obviously right play is that each of the twenty rolls a twenty-sided die and enters only on a 20, since everyone faces an identical problem and any competent reasoner will converge on the same strategy, and that symmetric strategy beats everything else the group could land on. Dev thinks this is confused in a specific way: if the other nineteen really do roll at 1/20, then entering outright beats rolling by a factor of twenty, since you'd win whenever everyone else abstains — so the "rational strategy" is one it would be irrational for any individual to actually follow. Mara will want to say that Dev's reasoning is available to everyone, so his real choice is between a world where all twenty roll and a world where all twenty enter and the prize evaporates; Dev will want to say that correlation between his deliberation and everyone else's doesn't make his postcard causally reach into their dice cups, and decision theory has to evaluate outcomes holding the others fixed.

Make both of them genuinely sharp — no strawmen, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than reciting positions. Work out the arithmetic where it matters (e.g. what each person's expected value is under the everyone-rolls plan, and what Dev's deviation is worth). At some point one of them should try the proposal of designating a winner by some public convention — say, the alphabetically-first person enters and everyone else abstains — and they should fight about whether a causal decision theorist can coherently endorse abstaining there given that abstaining never strictly pays. Don't let either one win cleanly, and don't end with agree-to-disagree mush: end by having them state, as precisely as they can, the single proposition they actually disagree about. Around 800–1200 words.
