I am a retired hydrologist who, since leaving the institute, has been working my way through moral and political philosophy with a reading group of fellow amateurs. Last month, at a reunion dinner with some old colleagues, I witnessed an argument I have been unable to settle to my own satisfaction, and I should like a careful adjudication. The subject was a real-ish case of the sort our field now produces: a regional authority had deployed an autonomous flood-defence network — sluice gates and pumping stations governed by a machine-learned controller, trained to minimise expected property damage across the catchment. During an exceptional storm, in conditions unlike anything in its training data, the controller diverted water in a way that spared a town but flooded a hamlet whose defences it effectively sacrificed. No engineer had foreseen this particular allocation, and no human approved it in real time.

My colleague Anna argued that this is a clear instance of what philosophers call a responsibility gap: the harm to the hamlet is attributable to no one, because the controller's policy emerged from training rather than from anyone's decision, and neither the designers nor the authority intended, foresaw, or could have foreseen this trade-off. My colleague Marcus replied that responsibility need not attach to a moment of decision: the authority chose the objective function and chose to deploy the system, and so owns the consequences, much as a commander is responsible for the foreseeable effects of standing orders. Anna countered that standing orders are general and their implications surveyable in advance, whereas this was a novel trade-off the controller invented on the spot. Marcus answered that this proves too much — human experts also generalise beyond their training when circumstances demand it, yet we do not say their decisions are ownerless. Anna then shifted ground: the real difference, she said, is that a human decision-maker can afterwards be blamed, sanctioned, and made to answer, and these practices have traction; a controller cannot be punished in any sense that matters, so delegating the decision dissolves responsibility rather than transferring it. Marcus's final move was to say that punishment is beside the point — answerability, explanation, and compensation are what responsibility is for, and all of those can be assigned institutionally to the authority that procured and deployed the system.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of Anna's and Marcus's contributions, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in part, and justify your verdict in terms a philosophically literate but non-specialist reader can follow. Where both of them are missing something — for instance, a distinction that would dissolve a point of apparent disagreement — please name it. Conclude with an overall assessment of who is more nearly right about the case, and note what, if anything, would change your verdict (for example, if the authority had been explicitly warned that the system might behave unpredictably out of distribution). Kindly write your answer in British English.
