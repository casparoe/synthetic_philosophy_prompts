A few years ago I got properly into forecasting as a hobby — prediction markets, that whole superforecasting crowd — and somewhere down the rabbit hole I ended up reading about Keynes's Treatise on Probability, which he wrote ages before the economics stuff he's famous for, which surprised me. Anyway I've come out the other end with a view I'm fairly attached to, and I want someone to have a serious go at talking me out of it.

The view, roughly: some uncertainty genuinely can't be captured by a single number. Keynes draws a distinction between how probable a conclusion is and the 'weight' of evidence sitting behind it, and I think he's right that you can have two situations with the same probability but completely different amounts of evidence behid them — a coin you've flipped ten thousand times versus a coin a stranger just handed you — and that this difference matters for how you ought to act, not just for how confident you happen to feel. And I think the standard Bayesian response — just put a distribution over the probabilities, second-order probabilities, model it all — misses the point rather than answering it. The missingness is the whole thing, on my view.

So: what would count against this? Not generic 'Bayesianism works great in practice' stuff, but specifics. What are the strongest formal results on the other side — I keep hearing about Dutch book arguments, Cox's theorem, Savage's axioms, though I only half understand them — and which of their assumptions does the Keynesian view actually have to deny, and how costly is denying each one? If experiments like the Ellsberg paradox show people really do treat known and unknown probabilities differently, does that help my side or does it just show people are predictably irrational? And what concrete observation, result or argument would make me abandon the view altogether? I'd like the defeaters ranked from 'this would kill it stone dead' down to 'annoying but survivable'.

Two more things. First: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Is there anything it actually rules out, or does it just amount to saying 'sometimes numbers feel wrong', which no result could ever refute? Second, please be honest about what's settled versus genuinely contested among people who work on this for a living. If most decision theorists reckon the weight-of-evidence idea was dissolved decades ago and I've just rederived a known confusion, tell me that rather than pretending it's a live dispute — and equally, if the standard dismissal has a real hole in it, don't paper over that either.
