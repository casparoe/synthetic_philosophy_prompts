I supervise a doctoral student in jurisprudence who has spent years developing her own account of the rule of law, and I have a standing bet with a colleague that I can predict her verdicts on hypothetical legal systems before she delivers them. Her rulings so far, on a series of hypotheticals I put to her, were these. A regime that governs by unpublished decrees, issued retroactively and tailored to named individuals: no rule of law whatsoever, she said, and indeed doubtful whether it has law in the full sense at all. A regime with a published, prospective, clear, and general criminal code, but whose officials habitually disregard it whenever literal application proves inconvenient, so that citizens cannot rely on the code to predict how the state will treat them: a grave failure of the rule of law. A regime whose legislature enacts statutes that contradict one another and never resolves the contradictions, leaving courts to pick whichever provision suits the outcome they prefer: deficient in the rule of law, precisely because compliance with "the law" is impossible. A regime whose laws are revised almost daily, so that conduct lawful at breakfast is criminal by lunch: deficient, since law cannot guide action if it cannot be followed over time. And finally, a regime that satisfies every formal desideratum -- promulgated, prospective, clear, general, stable, mutually consistent, practicable laws, administered by independent courts in genuine conformity with their text, with full oral hearings at which parties confront the evidence and argue their case -- but which uses that impeccably legal machinery to persecute a religious minority, criminalizing its rituals and enforcing those prohibitions impartially: here she surprised me by saying that the rule of law is fully satisfied and that the regime's wickedness is a separate matter, that we should not build substantive justice into the concept of law's rule.

The next hypothetical I intend to put to her is this. A regime satisfies all eight of the familiar formal desiderata: its laws are promulgated, prospective, clear, general, stable, consistent, demand nothing impossible, and are administered in strict congruence with their texts by courts independent of the executive. But its procedure is entirely inquisitorial and written. Courts decide on dossiers compiled by examining magistrates; no party has a right to appear, to be heard orally, to confront witnesses, or to present argument; the rules of procedure are themselves published, prospective, and scrupulously followed, and any citizen can read exactly how his case will be decided and on what evidence. No substantive rights are violated beyond this: the substantive law is humane.

Predict her verdict on this final case. Then do the harder work: articulate the two or more general principles about what the rule of law is, each consistent with all five of her earlier verdicts, that diverge here; say which you take her to be running, and why, given her earlier rulings; identify which earlier verdict is the closest analog to the new case and what it shows; and finally, if her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, describe the further hypothetical that would most efficiently settle which conception she holds -- one that isolates the contested ingredient rather than piling on additional defects. I am less interested in the yes-or-no than in whether the divergence turns out to be about the point of the rule of law itself: whether its ideal is exhausted by making state power predictable and law-guided, or whether it additionally requires that those subjected to law be treated as agents entitled to answer for themselves.
