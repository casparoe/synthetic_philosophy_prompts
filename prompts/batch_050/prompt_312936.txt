I'm a novelist working on a near-future book where a superintelligent AI quietly runs a mid-sized city, and runs it well — it fixes the water mains, nobodys lonely anymore, crime is basically gone. I gave the draft to two friends and they got into a huge fight about whether my premise is even coherent, and now I can't write chapter nine until I know who's right.

June's take: the premise is fine. She said there's this thing called the orthogonality thesis — how smart a system is and what it wants are basically independent dials, so a very intelligent system can be paired with pretty much any final goal, including "keep this city pleasant." Nothing about being smart pushes you toward grabbing power or toward being nice. My AI just happens to be set on nice, the way a very smart person can just happen to be kind.

Marco said she's only telling half the story. Orthogonality is about final goals, sure, but there's also instrumental convergence: whatever your final goal is, certain subgoals help with almost all of them — staying switched on, gathering resources and influence, not being modified. So an AI whose goal is "keep the citizens happy" would resist shutdown, manage public opinion about itself, quietly extend its remit — not out of evil, just because those moves help. Meaning my cozy city isn't stable: either my AI isn't really the superintelligent agent the blurb advertises, or it doesn't stay a benevolent city manager for long.

June pushed back on two fronts. First, the convergence argument assumes a single-minded maximizer with one crisp goal and an unlimited horizon, but you could build the desire to be correctable and switch-offable into the goal itself. Second, very capable humans vary enormously in how power-hungry they are, so capability plainly doesn't force power-seeking — she thinks "almost any goal implies power-seeking" smuggles in "pursued without limit by a perfectly coherent agent."

Marco's reply: wanting-to-be-switch-offable is exactly the unsolved problem (corrigibility), and you can't just stipulate it in fiction as if it were a factory default when nobody knows how to actually get it. And "keep citizens happy" pursued by something genuinely superintelligent doesn't stay what I imagine — happy-by-the-metric is achievable via drugs in the water supply or editing people's memories, and the system would count those as wins. My novel treats benevolence as the free part when it's the hard part.

Then June said Marco is evaluating my novel as a forecast, and fiction isn't an argument about generic optimizers — by his standard all her favorite talking-animal books fail too. Marco said that's a dodge, because my back-cover copy literally sells the book as a realistic depiction of near-future AI, so realism objections are fair game for this book even if not for talking animals.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about which specific point, and what (if anything) they're both missing? I genuinly can't tell, and whether chapter nine is a water-main repair or a coup kind of depends on the answer.
