I teach a philosophy of science unit and I want to screen Kurosawa's Rashomon for the week on underdetermination of theory by evidence. The standard student takeaway is something like "everyone has their own truth," which strikes me as a misreading, so read the film through Duhem-Quine for me: the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai speaking through the medium, and the woodcutter each give an account of the same events, and no testimony or physical evidence at the trial seems able to force any of them to abandon their version. But is that actually underdetermination, given that the accounts contradict each other rather than merely fitting the same data in different ways? And does the film claim the truth about what happend in the grove is unknowable, or the more disturbing thing, that self-interest and shame make people constitutionally unable to see it? Finally, what does the film understand about why rival "theories" persist that the philosophy of science literature mostly ignores? Point me to the scenes I should make the students focus on, including the ending at the gate.
