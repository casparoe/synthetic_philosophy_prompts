An essay I am editing for an undergraduate philosophy magazine summarizes illusionism about phenomenal consciousness as follows:

"Illusionists like Daniel Dennett hold that consciousness is an illusion: there is really nothing it is like to see red or to feel pain, and our inner lives are a trick the brain plays on itself. Because experience is an illusion, the hard problem of consciousness turns out to be no problem at all — the apparent explanatory gap between brain processes and subjective experience dissolves once we stop taking the illusion at face value, and illusionists count this as settled. It also follows that some ordinary moral concern is misplaced: if pain is an illusion, then no one and nothing really suffers in the way we naively assume, so causes like animal welfare rest, at least in part, on a fiction. Critics object that an illusion of experience would itself be an experience, so the view refutes itself, but illusionists reply that the illusion is purely functional — a matter of the brain misclassifying its own internal states rather than of anything genuinely felt."

I am inclined to think this is unfair to the illusionists, especially the moral claim, but I cannot pinpoint exactly where it goes wrong, and I do not want to edit out something that is actually a fair statement of the view. Please audit the summary: which claims would a careful illusionist (say, Keith Frankish) accept as written, which are conflations or overstatements, and which does the view explicitly deny? In particular, I would like answers to three things. First, does illusionism deny that there is anything it is like to be us, full stop, or does it deny something narrower, and does the difference matter for how the summary should be worded? Second, does the self-refutation objection — an illusion of experience would itself have to be experienced — actually land, or is the essay's one-line reply roughly the right shape even if compressed? Third, does anything about the moral weight of suffering genuinely follow from illusionism, or could it? And as a practical matter: if I can only insist on two corrections to the essay, which two would do the most work in making it accurate, and why those rather than others?
