I am drafting a paper on conditional analyses of dispositions, and its organizing claim is that the enormous counterexample literature reduces to a small number of structural tricks played on the simple conditional analysis (roughly: an object is disposed to give response R to stimulus S just in case, were it to undergo S, it would give R). My current taxonomy of tricks runs as follows.

First, finks, in the style of Martin's electro-fink (1994): a live wire connected to a device that would cut the current the instant the wire was touched by a conductor. The wire retains its disposition to conduct electricity when touched, but the corresponding conditional is false, because the very occurrence of the stimulus activates the mechanism that removes the disposition. Martin's device also runs in the other direction -- a dead wire that would conduct if touched, because the device would switch it on -- which is the seed of my third category below.

Second, masks and antidotes: the fragile glass that would not break if struck because it is wrapped in protective packing; the lethal poison administered alongside its antidote; the reactor pile held below criticality by its control rods. Here the causal basis of the disposition is left entirely intact -- the packed glass is still fragile -- and only the manifestation is blocked. This is what distinguishes a mask from a fink, and it is why Lewis's repair (1997), which defuses finks by requiring that the object retain the intrinsic basis of the disposition through the stimulus, is generally taken not to defuse masks: the packed glass keeps its molecular structure, is struck, and still does not break.

Third, mimickers or reverse finks: an object that lacks the disposition but would produce the manifestation anyway, because something attached to it stands ready to imitate the disposition's work -- say, a sturdy concrete block with a device built in that shatters it when struck. The conditional comes out true, the disposition is absent. As noted, the dead-wire direction of the electro-fink is the original instance; this category generalizes the trick.

Fourth, the specificity and proportionality problem pressed by Manley and Wasserman (2008): even without finks or masks, fragility is not tied to one bare conditional but to breaking in a sufficient proportion of a suitably delimited class of striking-cases, since a concrete block would break under some strikes without thereby being fragile. Getting the analysans right seems to require quantifying over a well-chosen set of cases rather than stating a single conditional, and specifying that set without circularity is its own difficulty.

I have deliberately set aside two further strands: chancy or probabilistic dispositions, and explicitly higher-order dispositions (being disposed to acquire or lose another disposition), each of which raises complications my paper does not treat.

Here is what I need from you. Give me one more kind of problem case for conditional analyses, with a structure genuinely different from all four tricks above. It should not be a fink, mask, or mimicker dressed in new vocabulary; it should not merely combine the tricks (a masked fink, a finkish mask, a mimicker that also masks); and its force should not depend on the disposition being chancy or higher-order, since I have excluded those on independent grounds. Ideally it should put pressure on conditional analyses as a family -- including Lewis's retention-of-the-basis version -- rather than only on the naive simple analysis.

Then be honest with me about the result. Is your case really a fifth structure, or is it a variant I should file under one of the four? And what is the best response available to the conditional analyst? If your case is easily absorbed by some natural extension of the conditional strategy, say so plainly rather than overselling it -- the paper's thesis depends on my taxonomy being complete, and I would rather learn of a gap now than from a referee.
