I've been reading around the debate on aesthetic testimony and the so-called acquaintance principle for a course, and it keeps colliding with how I actually form judgments in daily life -- my sister is a music critic, so the question of whether you can believe an album is beautiful on someone else's say-so is a live one at our dinner table. So here are my gut verdicts on a run of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is generating them. (1) My sister tells me, in detail and with examples, that a record I haven't heard is devastatingly beautiful: I think I can come to believe it's beautiful on her word, but it feels like I can't properly *judge* that it is, and I certainly can't love it on her word. (2) A museum guide tells me the Vermeer in the next room is luminous: even fully trusting him, I feel I have to go and look; repeating "it's luminous" to a friend on that basis alone would be faintly fraudulent. (3) A sommelier tells me a bottle is superb: I'm happy to buy it on that basis, but if I then tell a dinner guest it's superb before tasting it, that again feels like cheating -- yet the buying didn't. (4) A friend tells me a joke is hilarious but won't repeat it: I cannot find it funny on testimony, full stop, and if I laugh later it's at the joke, not at his verdict. (5) I watch a film and find it tedious, then read a dozen critics I respect calling it a masterpiece: I think I should reconsider and probably rewatch it, but I can't simply adopt "it's a masterpiece" against my own experience, and it would be dishonest to say I believe it. (6) A mathematician tells me a proof I haven't worked through is elegant: oddly, I'm completely comfortable believing her and even passing it on, and I notice this doesn't feel like the Vermeer case at all, though structurally they look identical. (7) Someone tells me the sunset last night was gorgeous: I believe them without any sense of impropriety, maybe because sunsets are easy, though I'm not sure "easy" is doing principled work.

My tentative principle is something like: aesthetic judgment requires first-hand acquaintance with the object, except where the aesthetic property supervenes on features that can be fully conveyed by description or inference -- which is meant to explain why (6) is fine (elegance might be capturable structurally) and why (3)'s purchase is fine (acting on expected value isn't judging) while (2) and (4) aren't. But I'm not confident the principle actually delivers (1), where I want to split believing from judging, or (5), where my own acquaintance seems to *block* rather than enable deference, and I suspect (7) is trouble too.

What I'd like from you: take my tentative principle, or a better one of your own construction, and state it precisely enough that each clause is motivated by at least one of these cases. Tell me which of my seven verdicts it accommodates and which it forces me to give up, and be honest about whether the choice is between a baroque principle that saves everything and a clean one that overturns a verdict I care about. Then test it against three cases I haven't listed: being told a landscape photograph is beautiful when I've seen the landscape but not the photograph; a blind-from-birth person forming aesthetic beliefs about paintings entirely from trusted descriptions; and deferring to a majority verdict about a wine when my own palate says otherwise (a sensory case rather than a critic case). Finally, say whether the principle should generalize to moral testimony -- where my instincts run similarly -- or whether that apparent parallel is actually the sign that something has gone wrong.
