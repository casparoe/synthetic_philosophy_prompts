So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about a 19th-century naturalist -- genuinely foundational figure, I won't bore you with which one -- who also held views on race and hierarchy that were, well, bad even by the standards of his own contemporaries, some of whom were calling this stuff out at the time. My editor read the draft and killed about half of it with the line "you can't judge the past by today's standards," and when I pushed back she just repeated it slower, like that helped. I've heard this sentence my whole career, from editors, from sources, from angry letters, and I realized I actually have no idea what it's supposed to mean. Like, it's clearly doing some work for her -- it's supposed to show that my draft commits some kind of error -- but I can't figure out what error.

So here's what I want: lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making, from the most trivial to the most radical. Like, on one end maybe it's just the banal point that people in the past had different information than us, which, sure, but that can't be enough to kill the passages she killed. On the other end there's presumably some strong version where moral evaluation of anyone sufficiently long dead is just incoherent or off-limits, which seems wild but might be what she actually needs. In between I suspect there are several distinct positions -- something about fairness to the dead, something about our own epistemic situation, maybe something about moral luck? -- and I'd like them pulled apart cleanly rather than mushed together the way the slogan mushes them.

Give it to me as a menu: each reading stated precisely enough that I could tell whether it's true or false, with a verdict on which ones are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which a reasonable person repeating the slogan probably means. And crucially, tell me which readings, if true, would actually support my editor's move of cutting the criticism from the piece -- because I suspect the reading she'd endorse if pressed is too weak to justify the cut, and the reading that would justify the cut is one she'd never actually sign up to. I'm bringing this to our next editorial meeting, so the sharper the better.
