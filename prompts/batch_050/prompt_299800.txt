I'm co-teaching a 300-level seminar on formal epistemology this term, about 14 undergrads, and we've got a 75-minute slot on the Dutch book argument for probabilism. Last year I made the mistake of just presenting the theorem on the board, and the students nodded along in that glazed way where they accept the math but clearly don't feel why it matters -- one of them literally said "okay but I'd just not take the bet, so who cares." This year I want them to get actually money-pumped before anyone says the words "Dutch book." Design me an activity where the students' own credences get turned against them: they should write down degrees of belief about something, and then the structure of the game should let a "bookie" side extract a guaranteed loss from whoever's assignments violate the probability axioms, using bets the students themselves have to regard as fair or favorable by their own lights. I want them to feel the squeeze of "wait, every single bet looked good to me, how am I definitely losing?" in real time, with points or chips or something, not real money.

Give me the full runnable version: materials, the exact rules (including how you force students to actually engage with bets instead of just declining everything, since that's the obvious escape hatch), how the bookie side should spot and exploit incoherence (worked mini-example would help, like if someone puts 0.3 on rain and 0.5 on no rain, or prices a disjunction below the sum of its parts), a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and a payoff scheme that creates just enough sting to make it stick without anyone actually suffering. Then the debrief: how to map specific moments from the game onto the steps of the formal argument -- which axiom each pump exploited, how the "package of bets" works, the move from "you can be made a sure loser" to "your credences are irrational."

Also two honest notes. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- e.g., my worry is that half the students will just write down boring near-coherent credences like 50/50 on everything and no Dutch book will materialize, so tell me how to engineer the propositions so that incoherence arises naturally without me rigging it obviously. Second, tell me which feature of the actual philosophical argument the game structurally can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand in discussion -- I'm guessing the normative gap between "pragmatically exploitable" and "epistemically irrational" is the big one, plus the assumption that credences just are betting rates, but maybe there's more I'm missing. The students will have read a chapter covering the standard synchronic argument beforehand, so the debrief can use the vocabulary, but during the game itself nobody should hear the term Dutch book until the exploit has already happened to someone.
