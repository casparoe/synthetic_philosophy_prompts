Write me an agony-aunt page from an invented eighteenth-century periodical — call it whatever you like — in which the advice columnist is The Impartial Spectator, i.e. Adam Smith's moral theory from the Theory of Moral Sentiments given it's own problem page. Three readers write in and the Spectator answers each one strictly in terms of Smithian machinery: propriety as the correspondence of sentiments between the person acting and an imagined onlooker, the duty to flatten the pitch of your joy or grief down to whatever a bystander could actually go along with, the man within correcting the judgements of actual flesh-and-blood spectators, our habit of sympathising more readily with the rich and fortunate because their situation is simply nicer to enter into, and so on. No stepping out of voice, no winking at the reader.

Suggested letters (swap any out if you think of better ones): a man eaten up with envy over a colleague's promotion; a recent widow asking whether she grieves too much; someone seething over a trivial slight who wants to know exactly how much resentment he's entitled to and in whose company he may decently display it.

The humour has to come from the theory itself: the Spectator's advice should be scrupulously consistent with Smith and yet land, deadpan, on the standard objections — that the impartial spectator is just conventional opinion in a waistcoat, that propriety as Smith describes it amounts to emotional self-suppression, that sympathy flatters the well-off. Someone who knows the TMS should read it and think: yes, that really is what the theory commits him to here. But don't lift any actual sentences from Smith; paraphrase throughout.

Structure it as: the column itself (letter, reply, letter, reply, letter, reply), around 650-750 words total; then a separate section headed 'Where the jokes come from', roughly 120-150 words, saying which Smithian doctrine each reply leans on and which objection to his theory each one is quietly pressing. British English throughout please.
