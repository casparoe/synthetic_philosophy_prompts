Write a sonnet about Fitch's paradox of knowability: fourteen lines of iambic pentameter in the Shakespearean pattern (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG), with the volta falling at the ninth line rather than in the closing couplet.

The two quatrains of the octave should give voice to the knowability principle — the claim that every truth is, at least in principle, knowable — and should make it sound as modest and humane as it initially appears: no fact sealed off from us in principle, no corner of reality that thought could never reach. The volta should introduce the proof's decisive move: the witness proposition, a truth of the form "p, and nobody knows that p". The third quatrain should enact the derivation faithfully — to know this truth one would have to know both conjuncts, but knowledge is factive, so knowing the second conjunct (that p is not known) would require p genuinely not to be known, contradicting the knowledge of the first; the witness is therefore unknowable, and the apparently weak principle collapses into the absurdly strong conclusion that every truth is actually known. The closing couplet should deliver the deflated moral: if there is even one truth nobody knows, then some truths are unknowable of necessity — the price of our ignorance is that some of it is incurable.

Two further constraints. First, the reasoning must be rendered precisely enough that a reader who knows the proof can recognise each step in its place — the witness, the distribution of knowledge over the conjunction, the factivity contradiction, and the final inference from non-omniscience to unknowability — while a reader who does not should still encounter a compelling poem about the limits of what can be known. Secondly, the poem should not name Fitch or use the word "paradox"; the logic itself should carry the weight. Please write in British English.
