Write an essay on the following controversy. Take the view — call it threshold negative utilitarianism — that below some level of severity, pains and pleasures can be traded off against each other in the ordinary aggregative way, but that there exists suffering so severe that no amount of pleasure, for anyone, however large or widely distributed, could outweigh or compansate for it. On this view it's fine to trade a mild headache against a really good concert, but never fine to trade an hour of torture against any sum of happiness.

Here's the tension I want the essay to actually engage with, not just mention. Critics press a continuity or spectrum argument: unbearable agony differs from a dull ache by a long series of small increments in intensity, each one individually negligible, so any threshold looks like an arbitrary line drawn across a continuum — and arbitrary lines seem morally indefensible when this much hangs on them. Defenders reply that the threshold can be qualitative rather than quantitative: the line where pain stops being an experience you *have* and becomes something that takes over your entire mental life, the kind of suffering that defines a life rather than occurring within one. But this reply appears to rely on pleasure and pain being commensurable below the threshold and incommensurable above it, and it's not obvious what could justify that assymetry rather than just asserting it.

The essay should (1) state the view precisely enough that the spectrum objection genuinely bites; (2) assess whether a qualitatively defined threshold survives the objection, or whether the view is unstable and collapses into either full lexical negative utilitarianism (where any suffering at all is lexically prior to pleasure) or plain total utilitarianism with no thresholds; and (3) take a stand on the underlying question of whether pleasure and pain are even the sort of things that can be placed on a single common scale in the first place, and what hangs on that. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it — don't end on "it's complicated and both sides have a point."

One more thing: at the end, say what would change your mind. Name the specific argument or consideration that, if it turned out to hold up, would flip your verdict, and flag where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
