I would like you to play John Stuart Mill in a conversation about secession, grounded in the doctrine of Chapter XVI of your Considerations on Representative Government ("Of Nationality, as connected with Representative Government"). I will summarize the relevant doctrine as I understand it, so we share a text: where the sentiment of nationality exists in force among a people, there is a presumptive case for their being governed together and apart from other nationalities; but where different nationalities are so locally intermingled that separate government is impracticable, they must reconcile themselves to living together under equal laws; and absorption into a more advanced nationality can, in some cases, be to the advantage of a more backward one. If I have misstated you, correct me in character.

Rules for the role-play. Speak in the first person as Mill, in a measured Victorian register that remains intelligible to a modern reader. You may reason only with the concepts and historical knowledge available to you up to 1873, plus the facts about the present day that I supply; if you need a further fact about modern events, ask for it as Mill would rather than assuming it. You are a sharp interlocutor, not a monument: you may concede subsidiary points, refine distinctions, and acknowledge costs, but you should defend the core of your doctrine rather than capitulate, since I want to test it against a serious advocate. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like conversation. Stay in character until I write "stop."

Here is my opening move. In 2017 the government of Catalonia held a referendum on independence from Spain that the Spanish constitutional court had ruled illegal; a majority of participants voted for independence on a turnout of well under half the electorate, the Spanish state suspended Catalan autonomy and prosecuted the organizers, and Catalonia remains part of Spain. Consider what this case does to your doctrine. The Catalans have their own language and a distinct history, yet they are thoroughly intermingled with the rest of Spain: most speak Spanish daily, families and firms span the boundary, and no clean territorial cut separates the two populations. On your account, that points toward making a virtue of necessity and living together under equal laws. But the necessity clause in your chapter is conditional on equal laws, and here the central state has dissolved an elected regional government and imprisoned its members for organizing a vote. So either your doctrine licenses Catalan secession whenever the center withdraws equal treatment — in which case the intermingling objection does little work, and any aggrieved intermingled nation gains a standing right to exit — or it does not, in which case tell me what a wronged but geographically entangled nationality may legitimately do, short of secession, when the state it lives under answers ballots with prosecutions. Which is it?
