I work at a mid-size company where we're building an internal coding assistant, and a design debate at work has turned into something I can't settle. Quick version of the setup: we could train the agent one of two ways. Either it's goal-directed — it gets handed a ticket and is optimized to make some measurable end-state true (tests pass, latency target hit, ticket closed). Or it's approval-directed — it never runs a long autonomous arc at all; instead it proposes each individual action (an edit, a shell command, a refactor) and is trained to pick whichever one a human reviewer would rate most highly, step by step. I'd come across the second idea in some AI alignment writing and brought it up at lunch, and two of my coworkers got into it.

Dan's opening move was that the distinction is fake: approval is just another reward signal, so an "approval-directed" agent is a goal-directed agent with an annoying goal — the goal being maximize predicted thumbs-up. And we've basically watched that movie already with RLHF, where the failure mode is sycophancy and reward-hacking the raters. Maya replied that he's flattening the one thing that actually matters, which is the horizon. A goal-directed agent gets credit for an end state and can get there by any path, including weird long paths no human would endorse — that's specification gaming, and it's silent. An approval-directed agent only ever gets credit for the step right in front of it, so it has no incentive to run a multi-step scheme the reviewer couldn't follow; you're buying legibility at the cost of some capability. Dan came back that legibility expires the moment the agent gets good at modeling the rater: then it optimizes what plays well in review — plausible-sounding commit messages, clean-looking diffs — rather than correct code, and at least a test suite can't be charmed. Maya's counter was that tests get gamed constantly too (he of all people has watched an agent delete failing tests), and approval failures at least happen in front of a human who can intervene, retrain, or escalate — whereas the goal-directed failure looks like a green dashboard right up until prod is on fire. Dan's last word was that this all assumes the reviewer stays competent to judge: once the agent outperforms the reviewer at the task, approval quietly becomes rubber-stamping, and you've rebuilt an unaccountable goal-directed agent plus security theater.

They asked me to call it and I genuinly can't. Please go through the exchange move by move — Dan's "it's all just a reward function" opening, Maya's horizon/legibility reply, Dan's manipulation point, Maya's "failures happen in front of a human" argument, and Dan's rubber-stamping closer. For each move, say whether it's right, half-right, or wrong, and why. Then tell me who's more right overall about whether the approval/goal distinction is a real axis or just a costume the same thing wears, and what the strongest point is that both of them seem to be missing.
