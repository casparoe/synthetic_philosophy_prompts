I chair the ethics review board for a university decision-science center, and we need a written opinion on a study that has split us.

The researchers want 60 paid volunteers to take a licensed sedative that reliably produces a temporary gap in episodic memory. On Sunday night, while fully alert, each participant is told the whole setup: a fair coin will be flipped after they fall asleep. If heads, they will be awakened once on Monday. If tails, they will be awakened on Monday and Tuesday, but the Monday awakening will leave no accessible memory, so Tuesday will seem exactly like their first awakening.

At every awakening, the participant is offered a bet using part of their $100 participation payment: stake $10; if the coin landed heads, receive $30; if tails, receive nothing. They may decline, and because of the memory gap a tails participant makes the choice twice without remembering the first choice. Everyone is told this in advance.

The investigators’ consent form calls the bet actuarially fair because, from the standpoint of someone currently awake, the probability of heads is 1/3. Our statistician objects that this ignores duplicated exposure: if someone adopts the policy accept whenever offered, heads produces one $20 net gain, while tails produces two $10 losses, so the expected result for the enrolled participant is a $10 loss. A graduate student on the research team says the rational credence at each awakening is actually 1/2 and that a choice does not become coerced merely because the person will later make it again under conditions they cannot distinguish. Meanwhile, two clinicians on the board think deliberately arranging for a competent adult to consent twice while remembering only one consent is manipulative regardless of the arithmetic, which definately makes this more than a technical dispute about disclosure.

Draft the board’s decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Give a definite recommendation, and make the conditions operational rather than vague. In particular, say what the consent form must disclose about per-awakening versus per-participant expected value; whether consent should be understood as one advance policy, a fresh choice at each awakening, or both; whether there should be loss caps or separate payment for each awakening; whether debriefing afterward can mitigate anything; and what monitoring or stopping rules, if any, would be required. Explain how much the Sleeping Beauty disagreement actually matters to the ethics decision instead of just solving the 1/2 versus 1/3 question and leaving the institutional issue hanging. Include the strongest case for the side your memo rejects, especially the claim that the Tuesday participant is competent, unforced, and told everything that can still be told. Around 900 words.
