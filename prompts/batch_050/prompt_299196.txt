I'm in a small reading group on moral disagreement (mix of grad students and keen amateurs), and I've been drafting a short essay I want to submit to our philosophy society's little journal. An econ PhD friend of mine read it and said the whole thing is trivially true, which honestly made me worry it might just be trivial. Below is the draft, then what I want from you.

---

The Moral Gains from Trade

The standard picture treats moral disagreement as a cost: something to be managed, minimized, endured. I want to argue the opposite — that disagreement, when it has the right structure, is a resource. When two parties disagree morally, there is often a compromise available that leaves both better off by their own lights, and this is no coincidence. It is the moral analogue of gains from trade.

The economist's point is that trade isn't zero-sum because people value goods differently: each side gives up what it values less and receives what it values more. The same structure shows up in moral life whenever the disagreement is about priorities rather than flat contradiction. If you think issue A matters enormously and issue B is secondary, while I rank them in reverse, then a deal where you get your way on A and I get mine on B isn't a grudging midpoint. It's an improvement for both of us over splitting the difference on everything. Moral compromise, properly arranged, manufactures value out of disagreement itself.

I see three forms this can take. Priority swaps: each side concedes the issue it ranks lower. Moral side payments: one side yields on substance and is compensated with something else it cares about — funding, procedure, symbolic recognition. Domain partition: each side governs its own territory, federalism as a kind of moral arbitrage.

The objection I take most seriously: the trade model only works if moral commitments come with weights, like prices. But a conviction isn't a preference with an intensity dial. Someone who thinks X is wrong doesn't usually think it's a matter of degree how wrong it is to endorse X. So the very thing that makes trade rational — cardinal comparability — is the thing moral convictions lack. My reply is that we don't need exact weights, only rough rankings, and people demonstrably have those; everyone concedes that some issues matter more to them than others.

But there's a deeper worry, and this is where I want to end. There is a difference between tolerating the other side doing what you condemn and being asked to do it yourself. A compromise that requires you to perform the act — not merely permit it — consumes something the trade model can't price: your standing as the kind of person who doesn't do that. Integrity isn't one more good to be traded; it's arguably the thing that makes trading possible in the first place. So the gains-from-trade picture has an edge, and the edge is where compromise stops being commerce and starts being self-betrayal. Locating that edge precisely is, I think, the real unsolved problem.

What I'd like from you isn't general copyediting but actual philosophical pushback on three specific points: (1) Does my "rough rankings suffice" reply to the cardinal comparability objection actually do the work I need, or does it smuggle in exactly the weighting I claimed we don't need? (2) In the taxonomy, is domain partition really a compromise at all, or is it a refusal to compromise dressed up as one — and does that matter for my thesis? (3) At the end I claim integrity can't be priced, but earlier the whole essay rests on moral priorities being rankable — is that a flat contradiction, or is there a principled difference between ranking and pricing I could spell out in one more paragraph? Finally, be honest about whether the core analogy is interesting or whether I've just redescribed ordinary bargaining in econ vocabulary. If it's trivial, I'd rather hear that now.
