For a seminar on the problem of induction, I have tried to build the strongest version I can of the claim that inductive skepticism is not a philosophical mood but a theorem. The conclusion strikes me as proving too much, yet each step looks defensible, and I would like to know exactly where to push back and what it costs.

Fix a simple setting. Data streams are infinite binary sequences. A prediction method is a total computable function that, given any finite initial segment of a stream, outputs a guess at the next bit. Call a method successful on a stream if the limiting frequency of its errors on that stream is below one-half, and call a method justified if there is reason to expect it to be successful.

(1) Every inductive procedure we actually care about — enumerative induction, curve-fitting with a simplicity preference, Bayesian updating under a fixed prior — is, in this setting, a prediction method.

(2) No prediction method is successful on every stream. Given any method M, construct a stream adversarially: reveal the observations one at a time and, at each stage, append the bit M does not predict. On that stream M errs at every step. So for every method there is a possible world in which it fails as badly as a method can fail.

(3) A justification of induction must establish that some methods are more to be trusted than others without assuming anything contingent about which stream we are in fact observing; a justification that presupposed facts about the world would merely push the question back, since those facts would themselves have been established inductively.

(4) Weighted equally over all streams, every method fares identically: under the uniform weighting of the space of binary sequences, each method — including one that always predicts the opposite of what the observed pattern suggests — has a long-run error frequency of exactly one-half with probability one. There is no free lunch: what a method gains on the streams that suit it, it loses on the rest, and the construction in (2) guarantees that the rest is never empty.

(5) So, by (3) and (4), no method is more justified than any other: induction, counter-induction, and guessing by coin flip stand on precisely the same epistemic footing.

(6) But this is absurd. It implies that the practice of favoring methods that have worked — in science, in statistics, in ordinary life — is a superstition, and that someone who switches from reading tea leaves to running controlled trials has made no epistemic progress whatsoever.

I take (1) to be a stipulation, and (2) and (4) to be mathematics I have checked, so the pressure must fall on (3) or on the inference to (5). If I have to sacrifice something, I would rather weaken (3) than accept (5); but every weakening I have tried seems to concede the skeptic's underlying point — that trusting induction requires assuming, without argument, that our world is one of the streams on which induction works. Which step do you reject? Why did it look compelling, what exactly does rejecting it commit me to, and once the repair is made, does the skeptic keep a genuine consolation prize — something this argument really does establish?
