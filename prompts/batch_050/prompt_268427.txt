For about fifteen years I have run a small discussion circle — neighbors, former colleagues, a couple of relatives — that meets monthly to argue about current affairs. We are a mixed group, but everyone in it leans, loosely, toward one of the two great camps of our politics, and over the years I have noticed a pattern I cannot find a word for, though I feel sure someone must have studied it. Whenever a genuinely new issue arrives — I remember this with cultivated meat, with the contact-tracing apps during the pandemic, and most recently with a proposal in our town to let a data center be built on farmland — there is a strange interlude, lasting weeks or months, during which nobody, including the most opinionated members, actually has a view. People say things like "I haven't made my mind up yet," and they mean it; I have watched a strong-willed friend hold two incompatible positions on the same question a fortnight apart without embarrassment. Then, quite suddenly, the interlude ends. Positions settle, and they settle almost perfectly along camp lines — I can now predict, within a day or two of first hearing about any new controversy, where nearly everyone I know will land, including people whose eventual view I could not possibly have inferred from anything they previously believed.

What strikes me most is what happens after the settling. Once their position is fixed, each person experiences it as following obviously from their deepest values, tells a fluent story about why it does, and finds the other camp's parallel story strained and opportunistic — apparently without noticing that they themselves were persuadable of the opposite conclusion three months earlier, or that their camp has, within living memory, held the other side of comparable questions with the same air of self-evidence. There is also an odd asymmetry: everyone regards their own camp's position as reasoned from principle and the other camp's as somehow assigned to it, and everyone is half right about the second claim and, so far as I can tell, mostly wrong about the first.

I am not asking for the literature on why the two camps dislike each other, or on why people conform, or on hypocrisy; the thing I have in mind is not quite any of these, since the people I describe are sincere, are sometimes privately uncertain even after the public settling, and are not simply copying a leadership's line — occasionally the official line arrives after they do. What I want to know is whether this specific sequence — the open window of genuine uncertainty, the silent and remarkably coordinated assignment of a novel question to its owners, and the retrospective sense that the assignment was entailed by principle all along — has an established name or a dedicated literature. If it sits between several existing concepts, tell me which ones and exactly where my phenomenon differs from each, because I will not be satisfied with a bare label. And if it has genuinely gone unnamed, coin a term for it, define it precisely, and give me boundary cases: situations that look similar but fall outside it, and situations that fall inside it despite lacking some feature I mentioned.
