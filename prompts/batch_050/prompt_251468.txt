I am at work on a novel whose pivotal scene turns on a life-and-death choice, and to get the moral architecture of that scene right I have been studying the standard thought experiments. My notes so far contain four: (1) the switch case, in which one diverts a runaway trolley from five workers onto a side track where it will kill one; (2) the footbridge case, in which the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large man off a bridge into its path; (3) the transplant case, in which a surgeon kills one healthy patient to distribute his organs to five who will otherwise die; (4) the loop variant, in which the diverted trolley would circle back and kill the five anyway unless its progress is halted by the body of a large man who happens to be on the side track. Each of these, in its way, probes the distinction between intending a death and merely foreseeing one. Before I commit to a structure for my scene, I want one more case, genuinely different from all four rather than a redressing of one of them — ideally a case in which the intuitively permissible course of action involves intending a harm, or the intuitively impermissible course involves no intended harm, so that it pressures the doctrine of double effect itself rather than merely illustrating it. State the case precisely, report the verdict you expect most readers to reach, and explain what it reveals that my four do not.
