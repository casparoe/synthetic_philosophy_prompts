I've been going down a rabbit hole on self-driving cars and robot ethics after an argument with my brother-in-law, and I came up with this argument that machine ethics is impossible, but I think it accidentally proves way more than I wanted. Here's how it goes:

(1) Any machine's ethical behavior has to come from one of two sources: explicit rules a programmer wrote in, or patterns it learned from a pile of examples. There's no third option, it all has to get in there somehow.

(2) If it runs on explicit rules, then the moment it hits a situation the rules didn't cover, it either does something random or does nothing. It can't figure out what the rules were *for*, it just has the rules.

(3) If it learns from examples, then all it can ever do is replay whatever patterns were in the training data, prejudices and all. It has no way of knowing which of its examples were the good ones.

(4) Either way, the machine can never catch and correct its own moral mistakes from the inside. Every correction has to be delivered from outside, by a person.

(5) A system that can't correct its own moral mistakes from the inside isn't really doing ethics. It's just passing along someone else's ethics, like a very fancy note-passer.

(6) So there can be ethics *about* machines, and ethics *delivered through* machines, but no ethical machines. Machine ethics, as a project, is a dead end.

Now the problem. It seems to me that humans are in the exact same boat:

(7) Everything I know about right and wrong came from explicit rules (parents, school, church, the law) and from examples (watching people around me growing up). That's it. That's also only two sources.

(8) And steps (2) through (4) seem to apply to me just as much. I hit situations my rules never covered all the time, and my "examples" included plenty of behavior I'd now call awful, and I had no rule telling me which ones to keep.

(9) So if the argument works against machines, it works against me too, and no human has ever "done ethics" either. Which is ridiculous.

I'd really like to keep the conclusion that machine ethics is possible, or at least not obviously doomed, and I definitely want to keep that humans do ethics. But I can't find the step where this goes sideways, and my brother-in-law is insufferable about it. Which premise or step do you reject? And fair warning: don't just tell me the step is wrong, tell me why it *seemed* right, and what it costs to reject it, because if rejecting it means humans don't really do ethics either, I'd rather reject a different one.
