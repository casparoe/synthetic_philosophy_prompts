Last weekend, over a long dinner, a friend and I argued past midnight about deepfakes. Her position was that nothing fundamental has changed: photographs have been staged and recordings spliced for well over a century, courts have always authenticated evidence through chains of custody and witness testimony, and today's panic reflects a failure to remember that no recording was ever self-authenticating. My position was that she is missing what made audiovisual evidence special — not that it was unfakeable, but that faking it convincingly used to be expensive and risky, so recordings functioned as a cheap epistemic backstop: they let public disputes be settled without deferring to anyone's institutional authority, and they made flat denial costly for the powerful. Now that plausible fabrication is nearly free, the mere possibility of synthesis does the work an actual forgery once had to do, and the resulting dividend flows asymmetrically to whoever was recorded — usually the party with more power to lose. Neither of us budged, which is unfortunate, because I now have to write about exactly this question in my professional capacity.

I am one of five members of our national electoral disputes tribunal, and we have a live case. Eleven days before a general election, an anonymous source released a forty-second audio recording in which a leading candidate appears to negotiate the sale of a ministry appointment. The file's metadata has been stripped. Of two independent forensic laboratories, one reports artifacts consistent with synthesis; the other reports nothing conclusive either way. The candidate has neither confirmed nor denied the recording's contents and has moved to exclude it, citing the documented general capabilities of synthetic-audio tools but pointing to no specific evidence about this particular file. Our existing rules require that evidence be authenticated but say nothing about who bears that burden or by what standard, and whatever we decide will become the standing practice for future elections — including elections in which a disputed recording damages a candidate our current majority happens to favor, a fact I would like the opinion to confront directly rather than politely ignore.

The chair has asked me to draft, for circulation before Friday's sitting, the written opinion recommending the tribunal's decision. It should state the bottom line up front: whether this recording is admitted now, and which standing rule we should adopt for the next cycle — the status quo of unstructured case-by-case judgment; a provenance presumption under which only recordings with intact cryptographic provenance receive evidentiary weight; or a specific-challenge rule under which a party seeking exclusion must identify particular indicia of fabrication in the file itself rather than invoke the general possibility of fakes. It should engage honestly with the strongest argument against whichever rule I recommend — I suspect that is either the whistleblower objection (any provenance-based burden destroys anonymous leaking, which is the very mechanism that made recordings worth fighting over, and so hands incumbents a deniability shield) or my friend's deflationary objection (the asymmetry I describe is real but no admissibility rule can repair it, only redistribute its costs onto someone else). It should also address whether the imminence of the election, which forecloses definitive forensic resolution, should change the authentication standard at all — and if so, in which direction that cuts. Finally, I would like the operative paragraph drafted precisely enough that the tribunal could adopt it verbatim as rule text, since vague language here is how we got into this position.
