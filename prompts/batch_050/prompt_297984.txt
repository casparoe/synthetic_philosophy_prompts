My epistemology prof has been doing cases on when it's okay to just flat-out assert something, and I want to get ahead of her. Her verdicts so far: you assert "your keys are on the kitchen table" right after watching your roommate put them there — fine. You assert "the 9:03 train is cancelled" on a pure hunch with zero evidence, and it turns out to be cancelled — not fine, even though it was true. You sincerely assert "my team is going to win the final" purely out of loyalty and wishful thinking — not fine, and she said it still wouldn't be fine even if they ended up winning, because the problem is the basis. Next week she's doing this one: you have what seems like rock-solid evidence that Nogot, a guy in your office, owns a Ford — you've seen him drive it for years, he talks about it constantly, you even glanced at what looked like the registration papers — so you tell a friend "someone in my office owns a Ford." Twist: Nogot actually sold the car last week, but by sheer luck another coworker, Havit, who you've never discussed cars with, does own a Ford. Predict her verdict on the Ford case, tell me which rule she's implicitly running — a knowledge norm, a justification norm, a "justified true belief" norm, or something else — and tell me which case I should propose after this one to settle it definatively.
