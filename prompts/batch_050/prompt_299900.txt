EXAM QUESTION

Leibniz maintains that there cannot be two things in nature that differ in number alone: if two things were genuinely distinct, there would have to be some difference between them, and a reason for their distinctness could always be found in that difference. (a) State this principle — the identity of indiscernibles — precisely, and distinguish it from its converse. (b) Reconstruct Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment from his 1952 paper "The Identity of Indiscernibles," and explain why the two most obvious replies — that the spheres occupy different locations, and that they differ in their relational properties — do not straightforwardly rescue the principle. (c) Does Black's example refute the identity of indiscernibles? Defend a verdict.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The identity of indiscernibles says that if, for every property F, an object x has F if and only if an object y has F, then x and y are numerically identical. Its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals, says that if x and y are identical, they share all their properties. Both directions are really consequences of the definition of identity, so the principle as a whole is analytic: to say that two putatively distinct things share absolutely every property is just to misuse the word "two."

(b) Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres — same size, same composition, same temperature, qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect — floating some distance apart. The reply that they occupy different locations presupposes absolute space, but the thought experiment contains no spatial facts beyond the spheres' relations to each other, so "location" cannot be specified independently. And relational properties do not help either: each sphere has the property of being a certain distance from a sphere of precisely that size, composition, and temperature, so their relational profiles are identical too. Hence the spheres are two, yet share all their properties.

(c) Yes, the example refutes the principle. The sphere universe is coherent and clearly conceivable, which establishes that it is a genuine metaphysical possibility, and in that world the identity of indiscernibles fails. This is the conclusion Black himself reaches at the end of the dialogue. At minimum, the case shows that the principle cannot be a necessary truth, whatever its status in the actual world.
