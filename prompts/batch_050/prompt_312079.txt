I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've gotten myself into a situation. My new book is narrated by an actuary whose job is pricing flood insurance, and early on he does the math everyone does: his own flights, his own burgers, his own ballot, all of it rounds to zero -- no plane stays grounded, no chicken is un-slaughtered, no election is swung because of him. Over the course of the book I have him argue himself to the conclusion that it's still wrong to keep buying factory-farmed meat, roughly on the grounds that (a) if his purchase has even a tiny chance of being the one that tips a producer's order threshold, the expected harm isn't zero at all, and (b) even setting that aside, there's something separately bad about knowingly being one of the small contributors to a harm you'd condemn if one person did it alone -- he keeps coming back to those old Parfit-style cases, the harmless torturers and the drops in the bucket, which he read in college. I wrote the arguement as well as I could, but it's dramatized, not defended, and now the local university's philosophy society has invited me to do an event billed as "the ethics of the book" where apparently I'm expected to actually stand behind the narrator's position in Q&A.

The room will be: two consequentialist grad students who I gather think the threshold/expected-value move is the only live option and will want to litigate the empirical details; one Kantian faculty member who will probably think both my prongs are beside the point; a political economist who's publicly snarky about "consumer ethics" as a distraction from structural change; and a bunch of ordinary book-club readers who liked the sister subplot and will ask soft questions. Prep me. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come and in the vocabulary each of these people would actually use (the economist will not say "inefficacy objection," he'll say something about margins and collective action; the Kantian will not grant my framing at all). For each one: what I should concede cheerfully, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes without notes, and where that reply is soft so I don't get greedy. Also tell me the one question I'm praying nobody asks -- I suspect it's something about whether the threshold math actually works for voting, where the tipping probability is astronomically smaller than for a chicken order, and whether my narrator's two prongs secretly contradict each other -- and give me my best available answer anyway. If it matters: I'm allowed to say "he's a character, not me" at most once before it becomes cowardly.
