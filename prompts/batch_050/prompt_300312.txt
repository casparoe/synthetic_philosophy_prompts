In an introductory political philosophy course I help teach, a student recently argued on a discussion board that citizens owe the state obedience because the state is, as he put it, like a parent: it raised us, educated us, protected us from harm, and kept us safe when we were vulnerable, so refusing to obey an unjust law is like repaying a parent's care with contempt. He was responding to our unit on civil disobedience, and his conclusion was that acts like those of the civil rights era were, whatever their cause, a form of ingratitude that undermines the very relationship that made the protesters' freedoms possible. Several other students found the analogy compelling; several found it obviously defective; nobody could articulate exactly what it was doing or where it gave out.

I'd like you to audit the analogy properly, so I can give the class something better than my ad hoc answer. Please do the following: first, lay out the correspondence mapping explicitly -- what plays the role of the parent, of the child, of being raised and fed, of filial piety and gratitude, of leaving home, of adulthood itself -- and note where the mapping already strains (a table is fine). Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the argumentative use from the ones that are merely cosmetic: in particular, address the facts that states are not agents with intentions or love, that the benefits in question are non-optional and non-requested, that the child-citizen never reaches the adulthood in which parental authority lapses, that the parent-child relation is not chosen while political membership sometimes is, and that parents do not fine or imprison their children. Third, identify what the analogy quietly imports -- for instance, a picture of obligations as unchosen and gratitude-based rather than consent-based, and a default presumption that the benefactor's commands deserve deference -- and ask whether that picture can be defended independently or whether the analogy is doing the load-bearing work.

Finally, and most importantly for the class: does the anti-disobedience conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If the gratitude the analogy invokes grounds anything, does it ground obedience to particular laws, or only something weaker -- a duty not to free-ride, a duty to acknowledge debts, a duty to reform rather than exit? And if you think a better analogy for the citizen-state relation is available (a long-standing club membership, a business one has contracted with, a gift-giver, a lifeguard, or something else), say which one, what it captures that the parent picture misses, and what it would imply about civil disobedience differently.
