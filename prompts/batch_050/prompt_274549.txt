Here is the situation. I host a philosophy podcast, and my upcoming guest has just published a book defending a refined conditional analysis of dispositions. Her claim is that something like David Lewis's reformed analysis -- roughly, an object has a disposition just in case it has an intrinsic property which, if the stimulus occurred and the object retained that property for a sufficient time, would causally contribute to the manifestation -- can absorb the classic counterexamples: Martin-style finks (a live wire connected to a device that would instantly render it non-conductive the moment current touched it), masks (a fragile glass wrapped in protective packing so it wouldn't break if struck), and mimics (a styrofoam cup rigged with an explosive that makes it shatter on impact as if fragile). Her book argues that the intrinsic-property clause handles all three uniformly, and she is a smooth, practiced speaker who tends to answer each objection with the same move: point out that the case works by changing or removing the relevant intrinsic basis, so the conditional simply doesn't apply.

I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that makes that uniform move progressively harder to make. End the sequence with a question about what result, in principle, would make her abandon the conditional analysis rather than patch it again.
