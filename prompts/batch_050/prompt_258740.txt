I've been teaching myself the contingency version of the cosmological argument, and a member of my reading group put together a companion argument that seems to prove far more than anyone should want to accept. I'd like your diagnosis. Here it is, as he wrote it down:

(1) Every contingent fact has a sufficient explanation of why it obtains rather than not. (This is the principle of sufficient reason in the form the contingency argument needs: it is what lets us ask why there are any contingent beings at all.)

(2) Consider the conjunction of all contingent facts -- every contingent truth, conjoined into one large fact. Call it C. C is itself contingent, because a conjunction is contingent whenever at least one of its conjuncts is.

(3) By (1) applied to (2), C has a sufficient explanation. Call this explaining fact E.

(4) E cannot itself be a contingent fact. Every contingent fact is a conjunct of C, and no fact can be a member of the very totality it explains -- that would make the explanation circular, with C partly explaining itself. So E is a necessary fact.

(5) A sufficient explanation of a fact entails that fact. That is what "sufficient" means: given the explanation, the explanandum could not have failed to obtain. An explanation that leaves it open whether the explanandum obtains is merely partial.

(6) E is necessary (from 4) and E entails C (from 5). Whatever is entailed by a necessary truth is itself necessary. Therefore C is necessary.

(7) But C contains every contingent fact, including the fact that I had coffee this morning and that the dinosaurs went extinct. So nothing is contingent. Every truth is a necessary truth.

The conclusion is absurd, and worse, it is self-undermining in a specific way: the argument begins from a premise that is supposed to launch the contingency cosmological argument, and it ends by abolishing the contingency that premise was meant to explain. If (7) is right, there was never any contingent realm for a necessary being to explain.

Here is where I stand going in. I would strongly prefer to keep something like (1), because without it I don't see how the contingency cosmological argument gets off the ground at all. And I am obviously not willing to pay for (1) with the necessity of my morning coffee. My friend insists the argument is valid and that theists who rely on (1) are committed to necessitarianism whether they like it or not; I suspect something slides between (4) and (6), but I cannot name the slide.
