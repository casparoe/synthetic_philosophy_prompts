Write a parody corporate brochure for a fictional consulting firm, "Premise & Conclusion LLP," whose entire business model is built on the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. The firm advises corporate boards, tenure committees, appellate panels, and prize juries on which aggregation procedure to adopt -- and, discreetly, on which procedure will deliver the outcome the client already wants. The brochure should open with a mission statement about "restoring confidence in collective decision-making," then move through service tiers with names like Outcome Engineering, Agenda Design, and Path-Dependence Audits, and close with testimonials from satisfied invented clients (a dean, a general counsel, a literary prize chair).

The humor must come from getting the actual problem right. Include one worked case study, presented as an impressive client success story, built on the classic three-voter structure: a committee must decide whether a candidate gets tenure, where tenure requires both research excellence and teaching excellence; each premise passes 2–1, but the voters who dissent on each premise are different people, so a conclusion-based vote on "both, therefore tenure" fails 1–2 while a premise-based vote yields tenure. The brochure should treat it as perfectly natural that the same ballots produce opposite verdicts depending on where the vote is taken, and the firm's only anxiety should be about pricing this service, never about whether something has gone wrong. Bonus deadpan touches: a footnote acknowledging that no aggregation rule can satisfy all the obvious fairness conditions at once, offered as a selling point ("flexibility"); an FAQ entry on "What if a committee member notices?"; a junior analyst fired for asking which procedure is the right one.

The target is the temptation to treat the choice of aggregation rule as a technicality when it is often the decision itself -- so a reader who knows the doctrinal paradox literature should feel the satire is fair, while a reader who doesn't should still grasp, from the case study alone, why the firm's services work. About 700 words, formatted as a real brochure with headers, and strictly deadpan throughout: no winking, no moment where the firm concedes it is doing anything but solving a genuine problem.
