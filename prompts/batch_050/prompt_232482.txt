So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on prediction tech -- companies that claim they can forecast what specific people will do. Reporting it has surfaced a wierd thing: my gut gives me different answers on cases that all seem to have the same shape, and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually driving me. Here are my verdicts.

One: a near-infallible predictor -- thousands of trials, basically never wrong -- plays this game with me. She's already put a million dollars in an opaque box if and only if she predicted I'd take only that box, and a thousand in a clear box either way. I take just the opaque box. Obvious call, I'd think less of anyone who grabbed both.

Two: same game, but the predictor is only right about 55% of the time, barely better than a coin flip. Now I take both boxes. Feels totally different.

Three: researchers find a gene that causes both a nasty disease and a craving for chewing gum, and the chewing itself does nothing. I have the craving. Verdict: chew away and enjoy it, I can't change my genes, so why suffer twice.

Four: I have an identical twin raised with me, same wiring, and we each have to independently choose cooperate or defect in a one-shot game with no communication. I cooperate, partly because whatever I end up doing, he's almost certainly doing too.

Five: back to the box game with the great predictor, except this time both boxes are transparent and I can see the million sitting right there in the opaque one. Verdict: take both. The money's already placed, my choice can't reach backward and change it.

What I can't make consistent is one and five against three and four -- sometimes I treat my choice as evidence about something already fixed and act on that, sometimes I refuse to. Is there one principle that captures most of these, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does which case? Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon, and can I defend keeping the pair that pulls hardest against each other? And what does it say about the case I'm stuck on for the article: same box game, but the predictor is an AI trained on ten years of my own data, right 90% of the time -- does it matter that it's just reading my own history back at me rather than doing anything spooky?

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or wrap-up.
