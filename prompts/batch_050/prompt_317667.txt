In our evolution reading group at uni we've spent basically a whole term going round in circles on the adaptationism debate and I want to understand why it never resolves. The set-up, as I understand it: ever since Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper in the late seventies, one side says biologists are far too quick to assume that any trait you point at is an adaptation, shaped by selection for the job it currently does, and that loads of traits are actually byproducts of something else, developmental constraints, drift, or features later co-opted for a new use. The other side says adaptationist hypotheses are pretty much the only game in town for generating testable predictions, that "it might be a byproduct" isn't an explanation, and that the critics never actually demonstrate their alternatives in particular cases. What frustrates me is that both sides seem to agree on all the actual cases — everyone knows the human chin discussion, everyone accepts that drift and constraints and spandrels exist — and nobody is accusing anybody of getting a fact wrong. Yet the argument just loops.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement at all, but a disagreement about what an explanation in biology has to do: the adaptationists treat "what is it for?" as the default question and think progress comes from testing functional hypotheses, whilst the critics think the default itself is the mistake because it biases the whole research programme. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real factual question buried in there — something like how often selection actually beats drift in practice — which neither side can currently measure. Or maybe it's mostly verbal, with "adaptationism" meaning a research heuristic for one side and a claim about how nature actually is for the other, and everyone keeps sliding between the two.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Diagnose it using what each side really argues, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a decision procedure for telling adaptations from byproducts in particular cases, better population genetics data, an agreed default for the burden of proof, or nothing at all because the question is ill-posed. And be honest about whether you think it can be settled or whether the two camps are just commited to different pictures of what biology is for.
