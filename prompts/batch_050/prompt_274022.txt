So here's something that's been bugging me. Inside basically every country, freedom of movement is total and nobody even thinks about it -- if I want to move from Ohio to Oregon nobody asks me for a permit, Ohio can't charge me an entry fee, and if a state tried to wall itself off we'd call that insane, maybe unconstitutional. But the exact same countries run enormous apparatuses for stopping people crossing international borders: visa lotteries, deportations, border walls, the works. And there's a second asymmetry that seems related: a country that stops its own citizens from *leaving* (think East Germany) is condemned as a prison, but a country that stops outsiders from *entering* is just exercising normal sovereignty. So leaving is a right, arriving is a privilege.

What picture of political community does all this presuppose? Like, does the practice only make sense if a country is something like a club or a homeowners association, where current members get to decide on new members -- and if so, why doesn't Oregon get to be a club vis-a-vis Ohio? Or does it presuppose something about coercion instead, that the state owes justification to the people it governs and "the people it governs" just is whoever's already inside? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out -- I've seen the border regime compared to feudal birthright privilege, and the practice sure doesn't look like it takes that seriously. And where is the practice actually underdetermined, i.e. where would several different theories of membership all license exactly the same border policy we have? If we swapped in whatever theory best justifies *internal* free movement, what concretely would have to change at the external border?
