I’m a software engineer with no formal philosphy training, and I’m presenting to a mixed CS-theory/ML reading group next week. My claim is that Solomonoff induction is a great idealized model of prediction, but it doesn’t give engineers a content-free Occam’s razor: on any finite amount of data, the choice of universal machine can change which hypothesis counts as simple, and computable approximations inherit biases that the asymptotic convergence result doesn’t justify.

My toy example is compiler drift: two universal machines are identical except that one has a cheap primitive for transformer weight matrices while the other has one for finite-state controllers. They asymptotically converge on the same sequence, but across every dataset our lab will ever actually collect, they systematically favor different models. The audience has one complexity theorist who thinks the invariance theorem makes machine-dependence a constant-factor technicality, one ML theorist who sees this as ordinary MDL or PAC-Bayes model selection, and one decision theorist who’ll ask what predictive accuracy has to do with choosing actions.

Prepare me for their objections in the order they’re most likely to come up, phrased the way each person would actually phrase them. For each one, say what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and a concise reply. Include objections to the compiler-drift setup itself, especially whether it confuses encoding bias with epistemic bias, and end with the objection I’m hoping nobody asks plus the weakest point in my own position.
