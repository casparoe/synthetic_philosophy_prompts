Teaching Nietzsche again this term and I want a consistency check on where I've landed with the eternal recurrence. The passage that anchors my reading is Zarathustra talking to the dwarf at the gateway in Thus Spoke Zarathustra ("On the Vision and the Riddle", Thomas Common translation):

"Must not whatever CAN happen of all things have already happened, resulted, and gone by? ... And are not all things closely bound together in such wise that This Moment draweth all coming things after it? CONSEQUENTLY--itself also?"

My commitments, roughly:

1. The recurrence is best read as an existential test — could you will this life, exactly as it was, again and again — not as a cosmological hypothesis. The notebook "proofs" are a side-show.
2. The test has real normative bite: failing it shows you something about how you should change your life.
3. The gateway passage commits Nietzsche to fatalism, and he's right about it: every moment draws the next along, nothing could ever have gone otherwise, so desert-based guilt is just a confusion.
4. Amor fati is the ideal the whole thing aims at — wanting nothing different, backward or forward.
5. What makes a life go well is its narrative shape, not the sum of its pleasant moments.

Does this package hang together? Give me the tensions ordered by severity, and for each one say which commitment has to give and what the repair costs. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection — I suspect at least one of mine does, and I dont want to abandon a commitment over a mere word-confusion. 450 words max.
