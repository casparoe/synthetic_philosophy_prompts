I sit on my university's apparel licensing committee, which decides which factories may produce clothing bearing our logo. Our largest supplier is a factory in South Asia that pays roughly forty per cent above the local legal minimum and well above what agricultural work in the region pays; the factory has a waiting list of applicants, and independent monitors confirm that workers freely choose these jobs over their alternatives. At the same time, wages fall short of what a widely used living-wage benchmark calculates for the region, shifts regularly run to sixty hours, overtime is effectively compulsory at peak season, and supervisors have dismissed workers who tried to organise. Half the committee argues that cancelling the contract would harm precisely the workers we claim to protect, since their next-best options are worse and they have, in effect, voted with their feet; the other half argues that benefiting both parties is compatible with the arrangement being deeply wrong, and that our logo on the product makes us complicit in it.

Acting as a professional philosopher with expertise in applied ethics, draft the committee's decision memo. Think carefully first: before drafting, privately sketch the strongest version of each side's case, including the question of whether consent and mutual benefit settle the matter or merely complicate it, and whether the university's position as a wealthy buyer changes what it owes. The memo itself should recommend one course of action — terminate, renew unconditionally, or renew with specified conditions — state the conditions concretely enough that the supplier could actually respond to them, give the reasoning in terms a non-philosopher trustee could follow, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than as a straw man. Please write in British English.
