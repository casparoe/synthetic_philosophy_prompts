I am a professor of philosophy, and in three weeks I will present a paper at a two-day workshop on longtermism and the ethics of existential risk. I would like you to act as a philosopher with deep expertise in decision theory, the philosophy of existential risk, and the literature surrounding the simulation argument — someone who has sat through many such workshops and knows how this particular audience behaves in a question period. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming a longer list of objections than I will ultimately need before selecting and ordering the ones that matter.

The paper defends the following thesis: assigning non-negligible credence to the hypothesis that we are living in a computer simulation dampens — without eliminating — the "fanaticism" of standard longtermist reasoning about the far future. By fanaticism I mean the willingness, under expected-value maximization, to let astronomically large but improbable future payoffs swamp all near-term and certain considerations. My argument turns on a corollary that Nick Bostrom draws in "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (Philosophical Quarterly, 2003), where he writes:

Therefore, if we don't think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears.

I read this as creating a dilemma for the longtermist. Either we are not simulated, in which case — by Bostrom's corollary — our entitlement to believe in the vast, computationally rich descendant civilization whose trillions of lives ground the astronomical-waste calculus is itself undermined; or we are simulated, in which case the causal leverage the fanatical calculus presupposes is compromised, since whether any far future exists at all depends on the continued operation and purposes of the simulators rather than on our present choices. I argue that on both horns the expected-value case for fanatical far-future tradeoffs is weaker than longtermists typically assume, though I stop short of claiming it collapses entirely.

The audience will be roughly twenty people: several moral philosophers sympathetic to longtermism who accept total utilitarianism or something near it; a handful of decision theorists who have defended fanatical verdicts against the usual objections (Pascalian worries, tiny probabilities, infinity) and will know that literature cold; two or three philosophers of mind; and a few researchers from existential-risk institutes who are not philosophers but who take the simulation argument seriously and may know Bostrom's paper better than anyone else in the room. Nobody in the room will object that the simulation hypothesis is mere science fiction — take that as given.

Prepare me for the question period. Specifically:

1. Give me the objections this audience — not a general philosophy audience — will actually raise, ordered by the combination of how likely they are to be raised and how much damage they would do to the paper if unanswered. Phrase each as the person raising it would phrase it, and note who in the room it will come from (the decision theorists, the institute researchers, the total utilitarians).
2. For each objection, indicate what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, flagging the weakest point in that reply so I know where follow-up questions will probe.
3. Tell me which objection I should be hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the deepest problem in the dilemma structure of my argument — and how I might respond if it comes anyway.
4. One thing I am unsure about: whether my reading of the quoted corollary is doing legitimate work or whether I am leaning on it harder than its actual content supports. Address that directly.

Finally, an epistemic request: do not be polite at the expense of being useful. If you think the premise of the paper is confused — for instance, if you think the simulation hypothesis is simply irrelevant to the fanaticism debate, or that it amplifies rather than dampens fanaticism (since simulators might be running ancestor-simulations precisely to evaluate moral conduct) — say so plainly and explain why, rather than producing an elegant prep sheet for a talk that should not be given. I would rather cancel the presentation than deliver it in a defensible-seeming but hollow form.
