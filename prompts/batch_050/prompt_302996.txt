So I run a little reading group at work (we're all software engineers, zero formal philosopy training, we just pick weird papers and argue about them over lunch) and next month we're doing Pascal's mugging. For anyone who hasn't seen it: some guy walks up and says give me five bucks or I'll use my matrix-lord powers to torture an astronomical number of simulated people, and naive expected value math says you should pay, which seems insane.

I want to turn this into a one-page flowchart that walks a reader from "yeah, expected value is basically the right way to make decisions" to a named response to the mugging. Here's what I need:

- Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. First figure out which yes/no questions genuinely split the responses (does the utility function have to be bounded? do you treat small enough probabilities as zero? does it matter whether the mugger could actually causally deliver on the deal? does the hypothesis get penalized for being suspiciously specific or unfalsifiable?), and only then place the actual positions in that space.
- The leaves I want to end up at: bite the bullet and pay; bounded utility; a probability threshold below which you just ignore outcomes; discounting the mugger's claim because of how specific or unverifiable it is; and the move where you argue your five bucks can't influence the harm anyway so EV never gets off the ground. If I've misdescribed one of these or two of them secretly collapse into the same position, tell me that instead of forcing them into seperate boxes.
- Phrase every branch as a question someone could answer without knowing the jargon — a concrete case or a bet they'd take or refuse, not "do you believe in bounded utility?" The whole point is to sort people by what they actually think, not by which label sounds smart.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate, and for any empty corner of the space, say whether it's empty because the position is incoherent or just because nobody happens to defend it.
- Don't stay neutral at the end. Pick which response you'd actually mark as the "recommended exit" of the flowchart and argue for it, even if it's a judgment call. I'd rather get a definite wrong-ish opinion with reasons than six options and a shrug, and I can push back in the thread if I disagree.
- Keep it to maybe 8–10 branching questions so it fits on one printed page.

Write the whole thing in American English, and feel free to use programming analogies where they genuinely fit (the audience is engineers), but flag it if an analogy is doing real argumentative work rather than just illustrating.
