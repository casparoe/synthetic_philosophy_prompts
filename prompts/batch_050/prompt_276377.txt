Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who is also genuinely fluent in reinforcement learning and affective neuroscience. I want careful thinking, not a survey: before composing your answer, briefly brainstorm the candidate translations, then commit to the ones that hold up and say why the rejected ones fail.

Some background, because it shapes what I'm asking. For about a decade I was a behavioral neuroscientist studying affect in rodents: conditioned place preference, effort-based choice, and especially the dissociation between "wanting" (incentive salience, largely dopaminergic) and "liking" (hedonic impact, tied to opioid signaling in hedonic hotspots). Eight months ago I joined a lab that trains reinforcement learning agents, mostly for robotics. During a debugging session a colleague said the agent "really hates" a penalty term we'd introduced, everyone laughed, and I realized I couldn't articulate why the joke was only a joke. Since then I've been reading philosophers arguing about whether a reward signal could constitute or ground valenced experience — whether sufficiently sophisticated RL agents could suffer or enjoy — and I keep having the disorienting feeling that I can't tell whether they're saying anything beyond what my old field already knows, or something my old field is constitutionally unable to say.

So here is my request. Restate that debate — the claim that reward signals in an RL agent are, or could become, valence, and the main objections to it — in the vocabulary I actually work in: reward prediction error, value functions, policy gradients, phasic versus tonic dopamine, wanting versus liking, nociception versus pain, motivational trade-offs and homeostatic priorities. Be faithful: don't let my vocabulary's assumptions do quiet argumentative work and call it translation.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets distorted? Which have no counterpart in my framework at all? For each residue item, I want your honest judgment about whether it exposes something my framework is missing — a concept that neuroscience needs and lacks — or whether the philosophical claim turns out to be less clear than it looked once you try to operationalize it.

Two specific tensions I'd like you to engage rather than gesture at. First: my field established that reward prediction error is not pleasure; dopamine tracks wanting, not liking. Does that distinction by itself settle whether RL "reward" could be valence, or does it merely show that the word "reward" is a misnomer in both fields? Second: in animals, our best evidence for suffering is flexible, costly trade-off behavior — the animal treating some state as worth paying to avoid. A standard RL agent optimizes a fixed scalar and trades nothing internally. Is that the real disanalogy, and if so, does the philosophical debate quietly assume an architecture nothing like the agents I actually build?
