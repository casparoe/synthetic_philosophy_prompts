I'm preparing the instructor's handbook for a graduate-level course on AI agency, and I need a classification instrument for the chapter on what an agent is ultimately "for." Please write a decision tree that sorts artificial agents into approval-directed, goal-directed, and intermediate positions, following the specifications below.

**Purpose.** The reader holds a description of an agent design (a training scheme, an architecture, or a reward specification) and answers a sequence of questions about how the agent would behave under counterfactual conditions. By the leaves, the reader should know whether the design is approval-directed (the agent acts so as to be approved of, by some overseer or evaluative process), goal-directed (the agent acts so as to bring about states of the world, with approval at most instrumental), or some hybrid or degenerate case. The tree must sort by behavior under counterfactuals, never by what the designer calls the system, so no question may use the words "approval," "goal," "reward function," or "aligned."

**Structure.** Build the tree from the logical space first: state the discriminating questions, enumerate which answer combinations they permit, and only then locate positions at the leaves. Use eight to twelve branch nodes, each phrased as a question the reader could answer by reasoning about the design, without knowing which leaf it routes toward. Candidate diagnostic material you should consider including (adapt freely, but use at least five of these): (a) whether the agent would act differently if the overseer's evaluations were no longer causally connected to its future training; (b) whether it would conceal or misrepresent its reasoning if doing so raised its evaluation; (c) whether it would resist an update that replaces its current decision procedure with a differently-behaving one; (d) whether its behavior degrades gracefully or catastrophically when the overseer's feedback contains systematic errors; (e) whether it would spend resources on influencing the evaluative process itself rather than on the task; (f) whether it distinguishes "the overseer would approve if fully informed" from "the overseer will in fact approve"; (g) whether two agents trained under identical feedback but different overseer competence would converge or diverge in behavior; (h) whether the agent has any preferences over world states it could never receive feedback about.

**Leaves.** The following must each appear as a leaf, named and given a two-sentence characterization: simple approval-direction; approval-direction aimed at the overseer's informed or idealized judgment; goal-directed pursuit of an explicit objective; goal-directed pursuit of a learned objective; myopic approval-direction (care only about the evaluation of the present episode); and any hybrids your tree uncovers (e.g., approval-direction used as a scaffold toward an internal goal, or a goal-directed agent whose terminal goal is defined by reference to an overseer's judgments). Every other reachable combination of answers should also get a leaf, labeled "unnamed" if no position occupies it, with one sentence diagnosing why the position is empty: incoherent, unmotivated, or simply unexplored.

**Limitations.** Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot behaviorally separate (for instance, if an approval-directed agent with a perfect model of the overseer and a goal-directed agent whose goal is constituted by the overseer's judgment are indistinguishable under all your questions), and say what further information -- internal architecture, training provenance, something else -- would be needed to split them. Close with a note on which feature of the approval/goal distinction a purely behavioral tree cannot capture at all, and what the instructor must supply in lecture to compensate.
