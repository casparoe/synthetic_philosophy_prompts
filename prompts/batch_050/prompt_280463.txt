A friend and I spent most of last Saturday arguing about superrationality, and I would like an outside referee. The occasion was Douglas Hofstadter's pair of thought experiments from his Scientific American column in the 1980s. The first, the Platonia dilemma: twenty ideal reasoners, unable to communicate, are each invited to mail in a card bearing their name; if exactly one card arrives, its sender wins a prize, and otherwise nobody wins anything. The second, the Luring Lottery: anyone may submit entries, as many as they like; one winner is drawn at random from all entries, and the prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries submitted. Hofstadter's own suggestion, roughly, was that rational players in such perfectly symmetric situations should recognize that everyone will reason as they do, and should therefore adopt the policy it would be best for everyone to converge on — in the Platonia case, each player sending a card with probability 1/20, which gives the group about a 37 percent chance that exactly one card arrives. My friend, who was trained as an economist, thinks this is confused, and he laid out the following argument, which I reproduce with his numbering. (1) In the Platonia dilemma, a player can win only by sending a card; abstaining pays zero whatever the others do. (2) Sending therefore weakly dominates abstaining: it pays strictly more whenever there is any chance no one else sends, and it never pays less. (3) Rational choice does not forgo a weakly dominant option, so every rational player sends; the prize lapses, but that is no one's fault, since given that the others send, any individual who abstained would still gain nothing. (4) Hofstadter's alternative asks each player to send with probability 1/20 instead, on the ground that this is the best common policy. (5) But evaluate that policy correctly: if the other nineteen genuinely randomize at 1/20, a player who simply sends raises his own chance of winning from under 2 percent to (19/20)^19, about 37.7 percent — a roughly twentyfold increase in expected payoff, available to each player individually. (6) A player who declines that increase, not out of uncertainty but because the others will have reasoned as he did, is treating his private deliberation as though it controlled nineteen causally independent deliberations in other rooms, which is a straightforward confusion about causation; his coin flip does not move theirs. (7) So the superrational policy is irrational, and the all-send outcome, collectively dismal as it is, is the only rational one. (8) The reasoning is perfectly general: in the Luring Lottery, flooding the drum with entries is each entrant's best response to whatever the others do, even though universal flooding shrinks the prize to nothing; and whenever a symmetric group would all do better under common restraint but restraint is no one's best response to the others, rationality prohibits restraint — ideally rational agents cannot secure collectively available goods without contracts, enforcement, or irrationality.

What unsettles me is that the argument seems to prove too much. The 1/20 policy is sitting right there in plain view: it offers every player a positive expected payoff and the group a better than one-in-three chance of success, and my friend's conclusion is that twenty flawless reasoners are rationally required to walk past it into an outcome where winning is impossible — and moreover, by (8), that this is a permanent structural fact about rationality rather than a quirk of one puzzle. I have a persistent suspicion that something slipped in around (5) or (6), but whenever I press there, he says I am merely reasserting Hofstadter's premise, and I cannot articulate the difference between reasserting it and diagnosing a mistake. Please tell me which step of the argument you reject, if any; explain why that step looked compelling in the first place; and say what it costs to reject it, weighed against what it costs to accept (7) and (8). If I am forced to choose, I would rather give up the claim that rationality can never require leaving expected payoff on the table than give up the claim that rationality is not self-defeating in these games — but I want to know what I am paying either way. Please write your answer in American English.
