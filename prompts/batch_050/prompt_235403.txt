Two friends of mine, both of whom donate substantially to charity, got into a long argument about earning to give — the strategy of taking a lucrative career one has no particular moral enthusiasm for in order to donate a large share of the income. I stayed out of the dispute and would like an outside verdict on it.

Lena defends her own choice: she works in quantitative trading and gives away roughly a third of her salary. Her argument ran as follows. (1) Had she instead taken a job at a charity or in policy, the position would simply have gone to the next-best candidate, so her counterfactual contribution through direct work would have been small — only the difference between her performance and the runner-up's. Her donations, by contrast, are fully additional: no one else would have given that money. So once replaceability is taken seriously, the comparison favors earning to give, even though the direct work looks more intrinsically meaningful.

Oren replied (2) that this deploys replaceability selectively. If it is true that someone else would have done the charity work anyway, it is equally true that someone else would have taken the trading seat and executed the same strategies, including whatever harms the firm causes. Lena therefore faces a dilemma: either she applies replaceability across the board, in which case the argument proves too much — anyone could justify taking any harmful job on the grounds that the firm would otherwise have hired someone equally willing — or she rejects replaceability for the harm side, in which case she must reject it for the direct-work side too, and her case against the charity career collapses.

Lena answered (3) that the supposedly unacceptable conclusion is simply correct. The right test is always the counterfactual net effect: if a harmful job would have been done equally well by someone else, and her donations foreseeably do more good than the job does harm, taking it is the right choice, however corrosive that may sound. Moral unease about occupying a replaceable harmful role is a feeling in search of an argument, not an argument.

Oren then shifted ground (4). Even granting the arithmetic, he said, the whole exercise illustrates the institutional critique of effective altruism: it treats moral problems as allocation problems for private money, channels talented people into finance rather than into building political movements and institutions, and privileges whatever donations can measurably purchase over structural change that cannot be counted in this way. Lena called this a motte-and-bailey move: the institutional critique is a critique of cause selection, not of earning to give as such; nothing in her position commits her to funding only measurable interventions, and she could just as well donate to political reform efforts out of her trading salary.

Both asked me to settle the matter and I could not. Please go through the four moves in order; for each, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in part, and justify your assessment. In particular: does Oren's consistency objection in (2) actually land, or is there an asymmetry between the cases he treats as parallel? And does Lena's motte-and-bailey charge in (4) accurately characterize the institutional critique, or does the critique have force against earning to give even when the donations themselves target structural causes? Conclude with an overall verdict on who has the better of the exchange, and note anything important that both parties overlooked.
