I recently became a father, and the experience changed my moral outlook in a way I did not expect. Before my daughter was born I was a fairly strict impartialist: I believed, and argued, that my child's welfare should count for no more in my deliberations than any stranger's. Within weeks of her birth I found myself convinced -- it feels like insight, not mere sentiment -- that a special and deep partiality toward one's own children is not just excusable but morally right. Then I read about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and the experience has unsettled me, because my new conviction is a textbook candidate for a selection-based explanation (kin favoritism is about as fitness-conducive as a disposition gets), whereas my old impartialism seems to have no such story behind it.

I have now collected five standard replies to evolutionary debunking arguments, stated here in my own words:

1. The genetic-fallacy reply: the etiology of a belief is irrelevant to its justification; only the reasons one can now offer for it matter.
2. The third-factor reply: sometimes there is an independent explanation of why evolution would align our beliefs with moral truth (for instance, avoiding pain is fitness-conducive, and pain really is bad, so evolution selected a true belief).
3. The overgeneralization reply: if evolutionary origins debunk moral beliefs, parallel arguments would debunk our mathematical and perceptual beliefs, which nobody is prepared to give up.
4. The selective-debunking reply: the argument only undermines beliefs whose content matches plausible selection pressures; beliefs that run against fitness interests (say, strong impartial concern for distant strangers) are more trustworthy precisely because no evolutionary story explains why we would hold them.
5. The self-defeat reply: the debunking argument itself rests on normative and epistemic judgments whose reliability is equally open to evolutionary suspicion, so the argument undermines its own premises.

Please do the following. Rate each reply from 0 to 10 on how much rational confidence it restores specifically in my situation -- not in the abstract dialectic, but for a person trying to decide which of his two conflicting moral selves to trust. Rank the five replies, and give two or three sentences of reasoning per reply. Then address what I take to be the uncomfortable twist: reply 4, if it works at all, seems to vindicate the impartialism I abandoned and debunk the parental partiality I now hold. Say whether you think the five replies, taken together, give me grounds to trust the new intuition, the old one, or neither, and be honest if your answer is that the evolutionary point, properly understood, does less work in my case than I fear -- or more.
