I am in the final year of my undergraduate degree and currently taking a course on the metaphysics of modality. For the assessed essay, our instructor has set us an unusual exercise: rather than rehearsing the standard case against Lewis's modal realism, we have to construct an objection that is not already in the standard repertoire and then evaluate it ourselves. I would like a sounding board before I commit to a direction.

Here is what I take to be the standard repertoire, so please do not offer me any of these or variants of them: the incredulous stare; the epistemological objection that causally isolated worlds are in principle unknowable to us, so the theory undercuts its own justification; the parsimony complaint about the sheer extravagance of the ontology; the island-universes problem, namely that the spatiotemporal criterion of worldhood rules out intuitively possible scenarios involving two spacetimes that never connect; the complaint that counterpart theory fails to capture what we actually care about when we say that a person could have done otherwise, since strictly it is only a counterpart who does so; the expressive-limitation objection that the theory cannot consistently express certain modal claims about the plurality of worlds themselves, such as that there could have been more worlds than there are; and the objection that treating 'actual' as an indexical misdescribes the sense in which our world seems to us to be absolutely actual.

Can you supply an objection to modal realism that is genuinely different from everything on this list? By genuinely different I mean not a rewording, not a special case, and not a combination of items already there; it should rest on a distinct underlying thought. After stating it, I would like your honest assessment of whether it is any good. Specifically: does it target modal realism as such, or only Lewis's particular commitments to the spatiotemporal criterion of worldhood and counterpart theory? Would it equally trouble an ersatzist about possible worlds, or does it depend essentially on worlds being concrete? And does Lewis have the resources to absorb it by biting some bullet, and if so, how costly is that bullet compared with the bullets he already bites against the strongest item on my list? I would rather hear that a clever-seeming objection is weak than be told every objection is powerful.

Please write your reply as continuous prose only, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
