I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, working on representational format, and I've been reading a trade book on the science of imagination for background. Its chapter on the mental imagery debate of the 1970s and 80s summarizes the controversy like this:

"In the 1970s, the psychologist Stephen Kosslyn argued that the brain stores mental images as literal pictures -- essentially photographs filed away in visual memory, which the mind retrieves and inspects. His opponent, Zenon Pylyshyn, argued that mental imagery doesn't really exist at all: what we call 'seeing in the mind's eye' is just reasoning with sentences, and the vividness of imagery is an illusion. Kosslyn's experiments settled the matter empirically. When subjects memorize a map and then mentally scan from one landmark to another, the time it takes them increases with the distance between the landmarks, exactly as it would if they were moving a spotlight of attention across a real picture. Pylyshyn objected that these results show only that subjects know how perception ordinarily works and unconsciously mimic the appropriate reaction times to please the experimenter -- but this objection was refuted decades later, when neuroimaging showed that visualizing a scene activates some of the same regions of visual cortex as actually seeing it. With that finding, the scientific community recognized that Kosslyn had won: the mind really does think in pictures."

My inclination is to think this is slanted toward Kosslyn in ways that misdescribe both sides, but I don't trust my own sense of the debate's history enough to be sure, and the author is a respected science journalist. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? In particular: Is it accurate to say Kosslyn posited "literal pictures" stored in memory, and what did his actual theoretical framework commit him to? Did Pylyshyn really deny that imagery exists, or is something subtler going on in his position -- and did he think the alternative to pictures was "reasoning with sentences"? What exactly did the map-scanning experiments establish, and would Pylyshyn's objection to them be fairly rendered as a charge that subjects were merely trying to please the experimenter? Finally, is it right that neuroimaging resolved the debate -- does finding that imagery recruits visual cortex bear on the question the two sides were actually disagreeing about, or does it leave the central issue untouched? I'd like to come away knowing which parts of this summary a careful historian of the debate would accept, which parts each protagonist would object to, and what the debate was genuinely about once the caricatures are stripped away.
