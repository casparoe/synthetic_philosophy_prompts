In June 2016, a narrow majority of British voters chose to leave the European Union in a referendum marked by contested factual claims on both sides — most famously the leave campaign's figure of £350 million per week sent to Brussels, which it later retreated from — and by surveys suggesting many voters had little grasp of the economic and institutional stakes. Analyze this episode as a test case in the epistocracy-versus-democracy debate. First map it onto the epistocratic critique associated with thinkers such as Bryan Caplan and Jason Brennan: widespread factual ignorance and systematically biased reasoning among voters as grounds for restricting or weighting political power rather than distributing it equally. Then press the standard democratic replies against this application: that the leave vote arguably expressed values rather than errors (sovereignty, community, distributive grievances are not the kind of thing a voter can be simply wrong about); that credentialed experts are themselves fallible and interested parties; and that any epistocratic filter faces the question of who certifies competence. Finally, address the harder question: does Brexit actually discriminate between the two positions, or can each side redescribe it to fit its prior commitments — the epistocrat pointing to the misinformation and buyer's remorse, the democrat to the legitimacy of a decision whose costs fall on those who chose it? If the episode is underdetermining, explain what a genuinely discriminating case would have to look like.
