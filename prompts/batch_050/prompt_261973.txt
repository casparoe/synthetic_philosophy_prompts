I am a novelist by trade — three literary novels published, a fourth in progress — and I have been asked by a general-interest magazine to write a personal essay about my notebooks. I have drafted the essay below. I am not a philosopher, and before I submit it I would like comments from someone who actually knows the territory, because I have leaned on the extended mind thesis and I am sure I have got parts of it wrong or stretched it past what it licenses.

Here is the draft.

---

THE OTHER HALF OF MY HEAD

I have kept a notebook since I was nineteen. Not a diary — a working notebook: plot scaffolding, half-scenes, lists of what a character wants and refuses to admit, floor plans of houses I have never seen. When I was twenty-six I lost one on a train, eighteen months of work on a novel that never got written, and I want to describe what that felt like, because I think the feeling is philosophically loaded. It did not feel like losing a tool. It felt like waking up with a piece of my memory gone — except that I could not even tell you what I had forgotten, which is not how ordinary forgetting works.

Years later I learned that philosophers have a name for what I was groping at. In 1998 Andy Clark and David Chalmers published a paper called "The Extended Mind," arguing that mental states need not stop at the skull. Their famous case involves Otto, a man with memory impairment who carries a notebook everywhere and consults it automatically — when he wants to go to a museum he looks up the address in the notebook the way you or I would simply recall it. Their claim, as I understand it, is that anything that stores information you rely on counts as part of your mind, full stop. The notebook's entries are Otto's beliefs, just as the neurons' patterns are yours.

If that is right, then my notebook is not a record of my mind. It is a piece of it. And I want to go further than Otto. Otto's notebook holds settled facts; mine holds a novel that does not yet exist. When I write that my character "would never forgive her brother, though she doesn't know this yet," I am not remembering something — I am making it true, and the notebook holds the truth until I am ready for it. The novel's memory lives there, not in me. Left alone with a blank page and no notebook, I could not tell you half of what I "know" about my own book. So I want to say: some of my beliefs about my characters exist only in the notebook, and I am their custodian rather than their container.

And if the notebook, why not the rest of the apparatus? My laptop's search function finds things I wrote a decade ago faster than I can recall them — by the logic of the thesis, the search index is part of my memory too. My editor has read my drafts more recently than I have; when I ask her "did I already use the image of the burned orchard," her answer functions exactly like recollection. Even my readers' marginalia, once I have read them, feed back into what the book means to me. The mind, it seems, has always been a committee with external members; the skull is just where the meetings happen to be minuted.

This has, I think, a moral upshot. If my notebook is partly constitutive of my mind, then the stranger who took it off that train did not merely steal paper. He injured me — not metaphorically, but in the same sense in which a blow to the head injures you, though without malice and without a victim able to name the wound.

The obvious objection is that a notebook is a tool, like a hammer, and nobody thinks the hammer is part of the carpenter's arm. My answer is that I rely on my notebook constantly, automatically, and with complete trust, which is exactly the relationship I have to my own biological memory and not at all the relationship a carpenter has to a hammer. Where the reliance is that deep, the distinction between "using" a thing and "thinking with" it dissolves.

---

Now, the comments I am hoping for:

1. Accuracy first. Where have I misstated the extended mind thesis as philosophers actually hold it? I have a suspicion that my one-line gloss in the third paragraph is too crude — that the thesis attaches conditions to when an external resource counts, and that I have flattened them. Tell me what the real conditions are and whether my notebook, by my own description, satisfies them better or worse than Otto's satisfies them.

2. Which of my extensions in the fifth paragraph are licensed by the thesis and which go beyond it? The laptop, the editor, the readers' marginalia — I wrote that paragraph with mounting confidence and I am no longer sure the confidence was earned. For each one: does the thesis cover it, does some nearby position cover it, or have I left the rails entirely? And is leaving the rails a defect for my purposes, given that I am writing a personal essay rather than a journal article?

3. My central move beyond the original thesis is the claim that my case is stronger than Otto's because my notebook is generative rather than archival — it does not store what I think, it changes what I think, and the drafts shape the author as much as the author shapes the drafts. Does this cut in my favor, against me, or is it a distinction without a difference for the question of where my mind ends? I genuinely cannot tell, and it is the paragraph I care most about.

4. My reply to the hammer objection. A friend who read the draft said I slide from "I rely on it constantly" to "it constitutes my mental states," and that no amount of reliance, however deep, by itself shows constitution. Is she right that there is a slide? If so, what would my answer to the hammer objection need to look like to avoid it?

5. Finally, the moral conclusion about the theft. Flag anything in the inference from "the notebook is part of my mind" to "taking it injured me the way a head injury does" that a philosopher would find glib — I would rather cut the paragraph than publish a howler.

Please be specific about which paragraph each comment attaches to, and do not be gentle about the philosophy; be gentle only about the prose.
