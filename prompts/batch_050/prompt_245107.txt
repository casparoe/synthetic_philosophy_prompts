I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and for months I have been arguing about capital punishment with a colleague. We recently both read John Stuart Mill's 1868 speech to the House of Commons, "Speech in Favor of Capital Punishment," and we got stuck on this passage:

"For it is characteristic of all punishments which depend on duration for their efficacy—all, therefore, which are not corporal or pecuniary—that they are more rigorous than they seem; while it is, on the contrary, one of the strongest recommendations a punishment can have, that it should seem more rigorous than it is; for its practical power depends far less on what it is than on what it seems."

To me this reads like designing a deterrent system around perceived rather than actual cost. In my field we would call that a dark pattern, and I think it has a failure mode Mill does not address: if the public came to believe that death is in fact the lesser suffering compared to lifelong imprisonment, the deterrent advantage should collapse, so the argument seems to depend on preserving a public misperception. My colleague thinks I am over-reading a rhetorical flourish and that the passage just says punishments should be efficient in the impression they make.
