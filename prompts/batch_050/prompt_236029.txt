For my philosophy elective, I'm writing a paper on whether emotions can ever be called rational or irrational, fitting or unfitting, and before I read further I want to know what I should be demanding of any theory of what emotions are. Write the desiderata: the phenomena a theory must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it must respect, with the case or example motivating each requirement. Two cases I keep tripping over, which I suspect belong on the list: my fear of flying persists even though I sincerely judge flying to be safe, so an emotion can apparently contradict my own settled judgment; and my anger is about something in particular, like what my brother did, so an emotion seems to have a kind of content that a mere bodily sensation lacks. Tell me which of the requirements people standardly impose are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp, whether any pair on the list looks jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. Then, briefly, score the three main families of views — emotions as judgments, emotions as perceptions of value, and emotions as bodily feelings — against the list, without letting the scoring crowd out the criteria themselves.
