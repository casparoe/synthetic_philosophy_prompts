I'm in Year 12 and I'm writing an extended essay on the philosophy of mathematics, and I've run into something in my reading on epistemic logic that I can't get past. The standard treatment of knowledge in modal logic, as I understand it, takes knowledge to be closed under logical consequence: if an agent knows p, and p logically entails q, then the agent knows q. This is usually justified by the possible-worlds picture, where knowing p means p holds in every world compatible with what you know, and anything entailed by p holds in all of those worlds too.

Here is my problem. The axioms of arithmetic presumably entail every true theorem of arithmetic, including ones nobody has proved yet and ones nobody will ever prove. So on this account, every one of us who knows the basic axioms already knows whether, say, every even number greater than two is the sum of two primes. It seems to follow that mathematicians cannot actually be gaining knowledge when they prove things, since the knowledge was there all along. My question is twofold. First, what is a proof actually for, then? Is it just a psychological device that changes our confidence without changing what we know? Second, if closure under consequence really does imply that schoolchildren know all of arithmetic, shouldn't we conclude that the possible-worlds account of knowledge is simply false, rather than an idealisation? I don't see how a model can get something this basic wrong and still tell us anything about knowledge.
