I chair the invention review commitee at a mid-size robotics company (~250 engineers). Once a month we get a stack of invention disclosures, usually 12-15, and for each one we decide three things: whether the company should file a patent, who gets named as inventor (there's a bonus attached, so people care a lot), and -- the ugly one -- whether the thing belongs to the company at all or to the person (side projects, contractor work, stuff that came out of our public hackathon). We're not lawyers. Legal signs off after us, but we're the ones making the actual call, and we get maybe 20 minutes per disclosure.

My little reading group at work just did chapter V of Locke's Second Treatise of Government ("Of Property") and we got properly stuck on one passage, §28:

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

Here's what trips me up. Locke's whole move is that the servant's turf-cutting counts as the master's labor -- "the labour that was mine" -- so the turfs are the master's, no consent needed. Take that seriously and it's basically the theory behind every employment agreement ever signed. But ideas aren't turfs. Me owning your idea doesn't leave you with less of it, nothing spoils if someone hoards it, and in most of our disclosures the employee did the genuinely clever part while the company's "labor" was paying a salary and providing a GPU cluster. So I honestly can't tell whether Locke's move survives the jump from turfs to ideas, or whether this is the one passage in the chapter that completely breaks on contact with IP.

So: turn this passage into a procedure I can actually run. I want a step-by-step test the committee can apply to a disclosure inside our 20 minutes, something that operationalizes "whose labor was it, really" for ideas. Start with a definite verdict on whether the servant move even transfers to ideas, and on what reading of the passage. For the procedure itself, say which parts of Locke have to survive the translation (the enough-and-as-good proviso, the spoilage limit, the no-consent point) and which you drop or patch -- and flag clearly where you're patching Locke rather than applying him, because I don't want to cite him in a memo when I'm really citing you.

Then run it on these three cases, which are anonymized versions of real ones:
1. An engineer prototyped a grasping algorithm at home on weekends, on a company laptop, and filed a disclosure once it worked.
2. A contractor on a work-for-hire agreement supplied what everyone privately admits was the actual inventive step; our employee mostly managed the project and wrote it up.
3. At our public hackathon, a visiting grad student suggested the key trick in a hallway conversation and an employee spent the next month building it out. The student signed the standard hackathon waiver.
If the procedure spits out an answer my company won't like on one of these, don't soften it -- that's half the point of the exercise.

Also: mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and end with what the procedure flat-out can't settle -- places where Locke is simply silent and I'd need a different principle entirely.

Please actually commit. I don't want a survey of Locke interpretations or a menu of IP theories with pros and cons. Pick one reading of the passage and one procedure, defend the picks, and where reasonable people would read the passage differently, say so in a sentence and move on. And write it in American English, please.
