You are a philosopher of mind consulting for a cognitive science lab, and you should think carefully, beginning with a brief brainstorm of candidate implications before settling on the most important ones.

Background: in the color phi phenomenon, two differently colored dots are flashed in rapid succession at two locations, and observers report seeing a single dot move across the screen and change color midway along its path — even though the color of the second dot, which determines the experienced change, appears only after the motion has apparently begun. Philosophers distinguish two explanations: an Orwellian revision, in which the subject really did experience the first color at the midpoint but that memory was instantly rewritten, and a Stalinesque revision, in which conscious experience was constructed only after a short delay, so the midpoint content never occurred in experience at all and exists only in the fabricated record. The claim of interest is the stronger deflationary one: that there is simply no fact of the matter about which revision occurred, because nothing — not introspection, not reaction time, not neural measurement — could in principle decide between them.

The lab you are advising studies moment-to-moment conscious experience using probe-caught sampling: participants are interrupted at random times and asked what they were experiencing just before the probe. Please describe the most important implications of the deflationary claim for how this lab should design studies, interpret reports, and write up results. In particular: which of their current inferential practices does the claim actually undermine, and which survive untouched? What should the lab stop claiming its data show? If the deflationary claim is correct, does it follow that the timing of experience is merely a verbal question, and would that conclusion license ignoring timing altogether in study design? Distinguish carefully between implications for first-person reports, for real-time behavioral measures, and for neural decoding, and flag any place where a tempting implication does not in fact follow.
