Right, I need some help designing a teaching case for my third-year seminar on moral revolutions. We've spent the term on why entrenched practices — Atlantic slavery, duelling, foot-binding, that sort of thing — actually came to an end, and my students keep collapsing three genuinely different explanations into one vague story about "progress". I want to force them apart. The three candidate drivers:

1. Arguments: the practice ended because the moral case against it finally persuaded people (note that on this view the timing is awkward — the anti-slavery and anti-duelling arguments were mostly available, and made, for generations before anything changed).
2. Interests: the practice ended because it stopped paying. This is the Eric Williams line on abolition — slavery was abolished when it ceased to be economically central to the empire, and the humanitarian campaign was more symptom than cause.
3. Honour: the practice ended because it flipped from conferring status to costing it. This is Appiah's line on duelling — the practice died fast once the right sort of people started being laughed at for doing it, not when new arguments arrived.

Here's what I want you to build. Construct a thought experiment: a fictional but realistic society (something like an early nineteenth-century maritime power, no magic, no mind-reading, no invented social science tech) in which a long-standing practice — pick something with the right shape, like judicial duelling or debt-bondage — comes to an end. Engineer the case so that the three hypotheses can be distinguished *by the observable evidence*, not just by stipulation. A few hard constraints:

- Give the case temporal and regional structure (e.g. the practice ends at different times in different provinces, or in stages) so that the arrival of arguments, the shift in economic incentives, and the shift in status incentives can come apart from one another. That's the whole point — if all three change at once the case is useless to me.
- The evidence available to the characters and to us as observers must be limited to what real historians actually have: pamphlets and sermons with dates, trade and price figures, diaries, who among which class keeps participating and who quietly stops. No narrator whispering the true cause.
- Keep the scenario itself tight — a few short paragraphs at most.

After the scenario, do the analytic work: (a) state what each of the three hypotheses predicts the evidence should look like, and point to the specific features of your case that confirm or embarrass each prediction; (b) identify which single structural feature of the case is doing the discriminating work, and tell me what happens to the case if you remove it (does it collapse back into an undifferentiated "things changed" story?); (c) steelman the hypothesis your case treats most unkindly — how would its defender attack your construction as rigged or as relying on an unrealistic separation of causes that are actually entangled in real history? I suspect (c) is where the interesting pushback lives, since defenders of the honour view will say status and interests can't be cleanly prised apart, and I want my students to feel that pressure rather than have me just assert it.

Please write the whole thing in British English — I'll be circulating it to students and I'd rather not have to weed out American spellings afterwards.
