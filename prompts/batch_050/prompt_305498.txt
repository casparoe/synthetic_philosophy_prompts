I edit the book-review desk at a mid-sized history magazine, and my single biggest source of fights with reviewers is counterfactual claims. Someone always wants to write that without some one person, everything would have been different -- no Hitler, no Nazi Germany; no Princip, no First World War; no Jobs, no smartphone in every pocket. Other reviewers write the exact opposite, that the structural forces made the outcome inevitable and the individual was basically interchangeable. Both camps write with total confidence and neither ever has to show their work. I went to grad school for history (dropped out, long story) so I know there's a real literature underneath this -- the whole Great Man vs. structural explanation debate, plus what philosophers say about legitimate vs. illegitimate counterfactuals -- but my reviewers are working journalists, not philosophers, and I can't send them off to read modal metaphysics before deadline.

What I want is a vetting procedure I can paste into our style guide: an ordered set of tests a reviewer's counterfactual claim has to pass before it goes to print, with instructions for what to do at each step (revise the claim, downgrade it to speculation, or let it stand). It should force the author to separate the questions that usually get mushed together -- how big a rewrite of the historical record does the counterfactual require, what mechanism actually connects this particular person to the outcome, whether someone else was positioned and motivated to do roughly the same thing, and how many steps downstream the claim runs before it becomes uncheckable. It also needs to be honest about which tests are mechanical and which are judgment calls, because I'll get pushback either way. One constraint: I'm personally a structuralist by temperament, and I know that biases my editing, so flag explicitly where in the procedure someone like me is most likely to rig the outcome, and build in a safeguard.

Please format the response with these sections, in this order: "The Procedure" (the tests in order, ~400-500 words), "Where Judgment Can't Be Eliminated" (~150 words), "Worked Examples" (~150-200 words each: run it on the no-Hitler-no-Nazi-Germany claim, on no-Princip-no-WWI, and on no-Jobs-no-smartphone-revolution, and make at least one of these verdicts be one a structuralist like me won't enjoy), and "What This Procedure Cannot Do" (~100 words).
