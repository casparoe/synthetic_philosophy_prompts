I am a retired biochemist who, in recent years, has taken to reading the history of science, and I keep stumbling over a pattern in how historians apportion blame that I cannot find a word for. The usual issue I have read about is whether one should judge past figures by today's moral standards or by those of their own era, and I gather the first option is sometimes derided as presentism. But what I observe is something stranger than either. Consider two researchers working in, say, 1905, both holding essentially the same views we now find repugnant — on race, say, or on heredity — views that were, at the time, unremarkable or even on the progressive end of the available spectrum. One of them publishes work that, decades later, gets taken up and built into programs with genuinely catastrophic consequences. The other's work leads nowhere in particular, or into harmless applications. What I notice is that historians, and especially popular histories and the committees that decide whose name comes off a building, condemn the first man and pass over the second in near silence — even when their actual beliefs, their actual conduct, and their actual culpability as individuals were indistinguishable. The judgment seems to track not what the person thought or did, nor how far they deviated from their contemporaries, but what later people chose to do with their legacy.

So my question is twofold. First, does this configuration have an established name? It is not plain presentism, since the verdict is not simply 'we judge them by our standards' — it is 'we judge them by the downstream causal footprint of their work', which is a different and, to my mind, philosophically more precarious principle. It resembles what I understand moral philosophers call moral luck, the business of holding people responsible for outcomes partly beyond their control, except that here the luck unfolds over generations and is mediated entirely by other agents' choices. And it has an odd kinship with consequentialism run in reverse: the dead are graded by results they never witnessed and could barely have influenced. Second, if no term exists, I would ask you to coin one, define it carefully, and — this is the part I care most about — say exactly where it differs from presentism, from whig history, and from ordinary moral luck, with a boundary case or two: for instance, the scientist whose repugnant views were idiosyncratic for his era but whose work did no downstream harm, and the scientist whose views were benign but whose methods became a template for later abuses. I am less interested in a label than in a clean carving of the phenomenon, so please do not stop at naming the nearest literature.
