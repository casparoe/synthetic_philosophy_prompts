In my seminar on collective decision-making, two classmates argued after the last session about whether our student society is vulnerable to the unilateralist's curse, and they have asked me to adjudicate. The setup: the society has about thirty members, and any one of them can, acting alone, publish an open letter in the society's name on a current controversy. Whether publishing would be good on balance is genuinely uncertain, and each member forms a private judgment.

Leila opened by saying the curse clearly applies: even if publishing is in fact a bad idea, with thirty independent judgments it is very likely that at least one member mistakenly concludes it is good and presses send, so the society will publish far more often than the judgment of its typical member warrants. Aaron replied that this only follows if members are naive: a rational member knows her estimate is one of thirty and that publication happens exactly when the most optimistic member favors it, so she conditions on being that maximum and raises her threshold accordingly; aware agents self-correct, and the curse dissolves. Leila answered that self-correction overshoots: if everyone inflates their threshold, the letter never goes out even when it should, so awareness merely replaces a systematic bias with an erratic one, and in any case the correct adjustment is uncomputable, since nobody knows the others' thresholds or how noisy their judgments are. Aaron's final move was that the curse then proves too much: flip the default, so that the letter is sent automatically unless someone vetoes it, and the very same reasoning now predicts systematic inaction; since reversing the architecture reverses the verdict, the phenomenon is an artifact of institutional design rather than a deep fact about individual judgment, and calling it a curse mislocates the problem.
