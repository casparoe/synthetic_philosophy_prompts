In a well-known thought experiment (Hofstadter's dialogue about Einstein's brain in The Mind's I), we are asked to imagine that neuroscientists have mapped Einstein's brain completely and encoded its workings in an enormous book. The book contains a full description of the brain's current state plus deterministic instructions for updating it: to converse with Einstein, you write your question on the current page, and an operator — who understands no German and no physics — mechanically follows the instructions, turning pages and copying symbols, until the book dictates Einstein's reply. Grant the usual idealizations: the brain is finite and describable, the book may be as large as needed, and the operator never errs. On this setup I constructed the following argument. It seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the step at fault.

(1) If two systems share the same total pattern of counterfactual dependencies among their states — the same transitions in response to the same inputs, at whatever grain the brain itself is organized — then they have the same mental life, if either has one. Consciousness supervenes on organization, not on material.

(2) For a finite system, that entire pattern can be written down: a complete state-transition table fixes every counterfactual. The Einstein Book contains such a table for Einstein's brain.

(3) A system instantiates a functional organization when its states would evolve as the organization specifies. The book-in-use satisfies this: were you to ask any question, the pages would turn to exactly the state Einstein's brain would enter. So the book instantiates Einstein's organization.

(4) The operator contributes nothing to the organization: she can be replaced by any reliable page-turning mechanism, and a mechanism that merely follows instructions cannot be where Einstein's thoughts occur.

(5) Therefore the book itself instantiates Einstein's organization, and by (1) the book is conscious — it is Einstein, thinking, remembering, and suffering.

(6) Worse, the counterfactuals are fixed by the text alone, so the unread volume on the shelf already contains Einstein enduring every conversation the book makes possible.

(7) But marks on paper do not think, and an unread book is not a man. Hence (1) is false, and consciousness must depend on the material substrate after all.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, yet each premise looks defensible, and (1) is the claim I would most like to keep — I am a functionalist by temperament and do not want to be pushed into claiming that neurons possess some special causal magic. Which step should I reject? Explain why the failing step looked sound, and then price the alternatives honestly: what it costs to reject (1); what it costs to deny that the book instantiates the organization, given that any account of instantiation strong enough to exclude the book must not also exclude future mind-uploads, or indeed ordinary brains; and what it costs to accept the conclusion and locate Einstein in the unfolding process rather than in the pages. Do not rest your answer on the practical impossibility of building the book — I am granting that it could exist.
