I organize a casual monthly "research lunch" at the software company where I work, and next month's slot is mine. I've chosen Libet's experiments on the timing of conscious intention, because my colleagues have all absorbed the pop-science version -- that neuroscientists showed the brain "decides" hundreds of milliseconds before we feel we've decided -- and a couple of them now treat it as settled that free will is an illusion. One even cited a follow-up study using brain scanners that reportedly predicted people's choices several seconds in advance, which he takes to close the case. My audience is bright engineers with no neuroscience or philosophy background, and I have about forty minutes plus a whiteboard. I don't want the lecture written for me; I want a plan for how to give it.

Please lay out the sequence the explanation has to follow and justify the ordering by what depends on what. My instinct is that nobody can evaluate "the brain decides before you do" until they understand what a readiness potential actually is (and isn't), and what Libet's clock-watching method for timing the felt urge can and cannot measure -- but tell me if there's a better place to start. Identify the one concrete demonstration or walkthrough that should carry the whole session (I was wondering whether having everyone perform a simple version of the wrist-flex task themselves would work, or whether that invites more confusion than it's worth), and say what properties that example needs to have.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect, roughly in the order they'll arise, and mark where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. The ones I'm anticipating: that the readiness potential just is the decision, rather than a slow buildup of preparation whose meaning is contested; that the reported time of the urge is a clean readout of an inner event rather than a noisy retrospective estimate; that results about spontaneous, inconsequential finger movements generalize to deliberate choices with real stakes; that Libet's own "veto" observation is a desperate patch rather than a substantive finding; and conversely, that the newer interpretation of the readiness potential as noise crossing a threshold means the original experiments showed nothing at all. Finally, suggest one question I can pose at the end that would tell me whether they've grasped why the experiments are genuinely puzzling without being the free-will refutation the headlines promised -- and tell me what I should openly decline to get into at this level.
