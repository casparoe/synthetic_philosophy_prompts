I've been reading into the debate between nationalists and cosmopolitans over whether compatriots have special claims on us, and I keep encountering an argument that seems to settle the matter quickly. It runs as follows. Nearly everyone, including committed cosmopolitans, accepts that parents may — indeed, are obligated to — give priority to their own children over strangers' children: feeding them first, sacrificing for them, devoting resources to their education rather than to needier children abroad. But if partiality toward one's own is legitimate in the family case, the argument continues, then it is legitimate in the national case as well, since a nation is simply a family writ large — an extended community bound by shared history, common sacrifice, and accumulated mutual obligation. The cosmopolitan therefore faces a dilemma: either condemn ordinary parental love as morally arbitrary favoritism, or concede that compatriots have special claims and thereby abandon cosmopolitanism. The position appears trapped between absurdity and surrender.

I want to stress-test this argument before relying on it in a paper I'm writing. My question is twofold. First, is there a principled way for the cosmopolitan to accept the parental case while rejecting the national one — that is, some morally relevant difference between the two kinds of relationship such that endorsing the first doesn't commit one to the second — or is any such distinction just special pleading dressed up as theory? Second, if the two cases genuinely stand or fall together, should we conclude that any cosmopolitan who defends parental partiality has already lost the argument? Please give the strongest available version of the cosmopolitan's reply, evaluate whether it survives the family-nation analogy, and say where you think the analogy, if anywhere, actually breaks down.
