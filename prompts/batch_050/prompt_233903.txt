So I'm a third-year PhD student working at the intersection of philosophy of action and AI safety, and in three weeks I'm giving a talk in our department's general colloquium series. The room will be mostly moral philosophers and philosophers of mind, a couple of action theorists, one very sharp philosopher of science who hates speculative AI stuff, and maybe a stray AI safety person or two from the tech policy center if I'm lucky. Nobody there works on alignment formally, but a bunch of them have heard of "the paperclip thing" and are a bit tired of it.

My thesis: wireheading and reward tampering aren't a quirky engineering failure mode unique to artificial agents -- they're the general, structurally inevitable case of what goes wrong whenever an agent's goal is specified through a signal rather than the state of the world the signal was supposed to track. I argue the same structure already shows up in familiar human cases (addiction, p-hacking as reward tampering by scientists against their own epistemic norms, Goodhart-style institutional collapse) and in classic thought experiments like experience machines, and that the AI case is just the one where there's no built-in messiness to slow the process down. So the payoff is supposed to be: philosophers should stop treating wireheading as sci-fi and start treating it as a lens on a bunch of problems they already care about.

Prep me for the Q&A. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from THIS audience -- not from the AI safety crowd, I've heard those -- ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, phrased the way an ethicist or philosopher of mind would actually put them (so expect stuff about whether I'm smuggling in consequentialist assumptions, whether reward in RL is even the right analog of desire or pleasure, whether p-hacking is really the same structure or just ordinary weakness of will, that kind of thing). For each: a two-minute reply I can actually deliver, and a note on where the reply is soft or where I'd just be asserting. Flag which objections I should concede a chunk of up front to keep the room on my side.
