Thomas Hobbes held that in the state of nature covenants are void wherever no common power exists to enforce them; that whoever performs his side of a bargain first under such conditions merely betrays himself to his enemy; that nobody can be bound by a covenant with himself, since whoever has the power to bind also has the power to release; and that the sole exit from the war of all against all is an irrevocable joint submission to a sovereign.

Suppose we could put to him a phenomenon from modern strategic analysis, sometimes called a commitment race. Two adversaries — states arming, or firms competing to develop a powerful technology — can each gain a decisive bargaining advantage by being the first to bind themselves irreversibly: not by promising the other side anything, but by engineering their own situation so that backing down later becomes impossible, for instance by delegating decisions to automatic systems or destroying their own capacity to retreat. Because being first to commit is so valuable, each side rushes to lock itself in before it has reflected on what it is locking in, and the commitments that work best are precisely the crudest and least reversible.

How would Hobbes analyze this? In particular: would he treat a technologically self-enforced commitment, one the committed party cannot itself undo, as a covenant in his sense at all, given that it binds exactly where no common power exists? Given his verdict that first performance in the state of nature is self-betrayal, would he judge racing to commit first rational or self-destructive? And since his own remedy for the state of war is itself an irreversible commitment, does the logic of commitment racing vindicate that remedy, or does it instead reveal the race to build private commitment devices as the war of all against all prosecuted by other means?
