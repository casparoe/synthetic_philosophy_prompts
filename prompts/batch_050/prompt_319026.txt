A podcast I listen to did an episode on how to make decisions when you have no clue about the odds, and the host summed up the philosophy angle like this: "When you're choosing under total uncertainty, there are two main rules. Maximin says pick the option whose worst case is least bad, and minimax regret says pick the option that minimizes how much you'll kick yourself afterward. John Rawls argued that if you had to design society without knowing who you'd be in it, rationality forces you to use maximin, so you'd make the worst-off group as well-off as possible. John Harsanyi disagreed and said behind the same veil you'd just assume you're equally likely to be anyone and maximize average happiness, which is basically utilitarianism. So the whole Rawls-Harsanyi fight is really just a fight about whether maximin or averaging is the rational rule, and most economists think Harsanyi won because maximin is obviously too paranoid to be rational."

I've been chewing on this and a few things bug me. Why would maximin be "obviously" irrational when the whole setup is that you don't know the odds? And I thought Rawls's thing was about justice, not about a general decision rule for everything. Is this summary fair? Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or wrap-up, saying which parts are accurate, which are common oversimplifications, and which are just wrong -- and whether the host's "Harsanyi won" conclusion actually follows.
