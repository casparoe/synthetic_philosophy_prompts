At an ethics seminar I recently attended, two participants, Renata and Marcus, disagreed about whether there is a principled difference between a moral preference and a moral bias. The discussion began with ordinary cases: caring more about identifiable victims than statistical ones, giving priority to members of one’s community, and finding some harmless taboo violations intuitively wrong.

Marcus opened by proposing a reflection test: a response is a genuine moral preference if the person would continue to endorse it after becoming calm, informed about its origins, and aware of the strongest objections to it; it is a bias if they would repudiate it under those conditions. He said this avoids deciding from the outset which substantive moral views are correct.

Renata replied that the test confuses authenticity with justification. A person might calmly endorse partiality toward their own family or even a discriminatory attitude after reflection. Reflection can also produce rationalizations, so the fact that a reaction survives scrutiny does not show that it was not a bias. Conversely, some defensible moral insights may be fragile under reflection because people lack the vocabulary to articulate them.

Marcus then abandoned the appeal to endorsement and proposed a procedural test instead. A response is biased when it is improperly sensitive to irrelevant factors, unstable under accurate redescriptions of the same case, or influenced by order, salience, framing, or social pressure. A preference, by contrast, is stable when the morally relevant features are held fixed. For example, if someone condemns an act when it is described one way but permits the very same act under a different description, without being able to identify a relevant difference, that is evidence of bias.

Renata objected that Marcus had merely moved the substantive dispute into the notion of relevance. Whether geographic distance, kinship, personal involvement, or statistical identifiability are morally relevant is precisely what competing moral theories disagree about. Calling one factor irrelevant assumes the correctness of one theory rather than providing a neutral way of sorting preferences from biases. She added that apparent inconsistency can indicate a subtle distinction that has not yet been articulated rather than a defective response.

Marcus conceded that no procedural test can settle every case, but argued that Renata was demanding too much. A distinction can be evidential without being decisive. Instability under redescription, unexplained scope restriction, and sensitivity to manipulation do not prove bias, yet they lower a response’s credibility. Likewise, an inability to explain a reaction does not prove that it is biased, but it matters when the reaction also changes predictably with obviously incidental features. He proposed that preference and bias are not sharply separated categories but endpoints on a continuum of epistemic credibility.

Renata answered that this language of credibility still risks asymmetry: we treat reactions supporting our preferred moral theory as insights and construct debunking explanations for reactions opposing it. Unless there is an independent standard, the preference–bias distinction becomes a rhetorical device for dismissing disagreement. Marcus replied that avoiding all debunking explanations would be equally question-begging, since some causal influences really do distort judgment. The difficulty, he said, is not whether origins can matter, but how much they can matter without assuming a complete moral theory.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point rather than declaring a simple overall winner. For each major proposal and objection, state who has the stronger position and why. In particular, assess: the reflection-and-endorsement test; the procedural tests of stability, relevance, and susceptibility to manipulation; whether inability to articulate a reason counts against a moral reaction; whether the preference–bias distinction necessarily presupposes a substantive moral theory; and Marcus’s final continuum proposal. Conclude by explaining what both speakers may be overlooking, and specify what kind of evidence or conceptual distinction could actually help distinguish genuine moral preferences from biases without circularity.
