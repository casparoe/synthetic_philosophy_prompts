Ok so I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been circling around writing a piece on how we even *evaluate* whether an AI system is "aligned" enough to deploy. Here's my position, which I've more or less committed to in my head and want stress-tested: the only alignment evaluations that matter are worst-case ones. If a model behaves fine on average but there's a known (or findable) set of conditions under which it does something catastrophic, the average-case numbers are basically a distraction -- worse, they're actively misleading, because they make deployment look safe when it isn't. I think of it like structural engineering: nobody certifies a bridge by noting that it holds up on a typical Tuesday.

My editor pushed back with something I haven't been able to fully dismiss, and it's why I'm asking. Her line was roughly: worst-case analysis has a hidden cost, which is that the "worst case" for almost any system is a scenario an adversary (or an evaluation team) had to actively *construct*, and once you're constructing scenarios you're not measuring the system anymore, you're measuring your own imagination. On her view, average-case behavior under realistic deployment conditions is the only thing that's actually evidence about the world, and worst-case probing is closer to fan fiction -- you can always *find* a failure if you get to search long enough, for literally any system including spreadsheets and seatbelts. So my worst-case purism, she says, would either certify nothing or certify things based on which eval team has the cleverer red-teamers.

So: what would count against my position? I want a genuine map of its exposure, not a summary of the debate. Specifically -- what observations about how deployed AI systems actually fail (in practice, not in thought experiments) would embarrass my view? What formal or engineering-style results -- say, from robustness research, or from how worst-case guarantees have worked out in cryptography or formal verification -- would show that worst-case certification either can't scale to systems like LLMs or doesn't buy you what I think it buys? Which cases would my own side (the people who take this worst-case line seriously in the alignment community) concede as real trouble, versus which ones would they wave away, and who's right? And the falsifiability question: has "worst-case" been quietly redefined over time -- from provable guarantees, to red-team findings, to "we tried hard to break it" -- in a way that means my position no longer risks anything? If so, is that a problem for a stance like mine, or just what prudence looks like when you can't get proofs? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest if the answer is that both my view and my editor's are unfalsifiable in different costumes.
