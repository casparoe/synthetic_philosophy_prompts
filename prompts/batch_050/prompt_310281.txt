I sit on the strategy committee of a small grantmaking foundation, and for three consecutive meetings we have been unable to settle whether our ten-year plan may describe the long-term decline of global violence as a measurable backdrop for our work. The disagreement on the committee has calcified into two camps, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The first camp argues the declinist case: measured per capita, deaths from war and homicide have fallen steeply over the centuries, and by the standards of, say, 1650 or 1942, our era is remarkably peaceful; the trend is the fact, and our planning should ride it. The second camp replies that the trend is an artifact of bookkeeping. Their objections cluster around four points, and I have tried to state them as strongly as I can: (i) rates of realized death tell us little about risk when the distribution of war deaths is heavy-tailed, since a single nuclear exchange would erase the entire trend retroactively, so the claim "violence is declining" confuses a lucky draw with a property of the process; (ii) the per capita denominator is itself contestable, since dividing by the living population makes a war that kills the same absolute number look smaller simply because the species has grown, and it is unclear why the moral ledger should care about the share rather than the count; (iii) the historical record before modern statistics is so patchy that early atrocities are systematically undercounted, inflating the contrast with the present; and (iv) what I will call the substitution hypothesis: overt killing has not so much declined as been converted into forms the statistics do not register as violence — incarceration, border regimes that outsource drowning to the sea, sanctions whose mortality is attributed to disease, and institutional coercion generally — so the decline may be partly a relabeling.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two camps are not disagreeing about the same proposition at all: one side is making a claim about realized frequencies in the historical record, the other a claim about the underlying risk-generating process, and past frequencies underdetermine risk precisely when the process is heavy-tailed. If that is right, the impasse is a category error wearing the costume of an empirical dispute, and no amount of additional battle-death data will move it. But I can also see less charitable readings: that the dispute is really conceptual (whether "violence" should cover structural and institutional harm, in which case the substitution hypothesis is doing all the work), or really normative (which metric is morally relevant rather than which is descriptively accurate), or genuinely empirical but stalled only because neither side has specified in advance what the record would look like if their view were false.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, in fact? Please diagnose it against the four objections as stated, saying which of them are empirical, which conceptual, which statistical, and which normative, and whether the camps are answering different questions under one heading. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn (for instance, between frequency claims and tail-risk claims), an operationalization to be agreed, a test to be specified in advance, or a reformulation of the question itself. Finally, give an honest answer on the meta-question the committee actually needs: once every term is operationalized, is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or does the question dissolve into several questions that no longer contradict one another — and if it dissolves, what should a body like ours put in its strategy documents instead?
