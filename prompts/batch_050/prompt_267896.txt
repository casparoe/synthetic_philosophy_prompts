Got into it with a friend over the two-sphere universe (a world containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres a mile apart) and he shut me down with "there are no two things exactly alike" -- apparently some Leibniz principle that settles it. But the more I stare at that sentence the less I know what it actually claims. Does it mean that necessarily, numerically distinct things differ in some qualitative respect, or just that as a matter of fact nothing in our world is perfectly duplicated? Does being at a different location count as a "difference," and if so is that cheating? Are properties like "being this very sphere" allowed in, because then everything comes out distinct trivially? Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, say which are platitudes, which are substantive, and which the sphere case genuinely threatens -- and then tell me which reading my friend needs for it to beat the counterexample, and whether that reading is independently plausible or just tailored to win?
