I've been reading philosophy of mathematics and physics on my own for years — the usual rationalist-adjacent curriculum, where Zeno comes up mostly as a cautionary tale about trusting intuitions over the calculus — and I recently went back to Aristotle's *Physics* to see the original discussion. In Book VI, Part 1 (Hardie and Gaye translation), Aristotle writes that "a line cannot be composed of points, the line being continuous and the point indivisible." Reading that crystallized something for me, and I wrote down my current position on the Zeno cluster. Before I inflict it on my reading group, I'd like an audit. Here are my commitments:

1. Aristotle's claim in the passage above is simply false. The Cantorian continuum — a line as an actual, complete set of points with the structure of the real numbers — is coherent, it is the continuum of all our best physics, and the convergence of geometric series fully dissolves the Dichotomy and the Achilles. Once you've shown the runner's total distance and time are finite, there is nothing left to explain.

2. The Arrow is best handled by the at-at theory of motion: to move is just to occupy different positions at different times, and there is no further intrinsic "state of motion" a body has at an instant. The paradox arises only from demanding such a state.

3. Supertasks are logically possible. Thomson's lamp does not show otherwise, because the switching history up to the limit simply doesn't fix the lamp's state at the limit — underdetermination is not contradiction.

4. Aristotle's distinction between actual and potential infinity nonetheless still does real work, and this is part of why I don't think Zeno is *merely* a math exercise: the runner does not complete an actual infinity of sub-motions, because the divisions of the interval exist only potentially — they are there to be made, not already made — so there is no question of finishing infinitely many tasks.

5. Instantaneous velocity is a genuine physical magnitude that does explanatory work — it's part of why a body leaves its position rather than staying put — and any account of motion that treats it as a mere bookkeeping device derived from position records is missing something physically real.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the history. Specifically: which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, ordered by severity, and for each, what exactly would have to give and what that concession costs. Which pairs only *look* like they conflict but dissolve on inspection — say why. And if you had to pick a single revision that cleans up the most, name it. One thing I specifically can't tell myself: whether my dismissal of Aristotle's claim in (1) can coexist with the use I make of his framework in (4), or whether I'm treating the passage as refuted when convenient and as deep when convenient. Don't tell me which commitment to abandon based on what's fashionable — tell me which revision is actually cheapest.
