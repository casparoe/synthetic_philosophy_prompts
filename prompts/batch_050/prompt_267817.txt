Okay so here's one I've been wanting for a while. You know the Nigerian prince scam email format? I want you to write a parody of that, except the scammer is a street mugger who has clearly done a lot of reading in decision theory, and his pitch is basically Pascal's mugging as an advance-fee fraud.

Some setup: you're a humor writer who also happens to know the decision theory literature cold -- the kind of person who'd find a misstated dominance argument genuinely unfunny. The piece should land for that reader, but still be readable (and funny in a more general way) for someone who's just gotten sketchy emails before.

The email: from "Mr. Theophilus K.," a mugger writing to his mark, offering to trade the contents of the mark's wallet for some astronomically large amount of future happiness (pick your number, make it silly-big but not so big it's just noise). The whole joke engine is that he has anticipated every standard objection to paying a Pascalian mugger and has a smooth sales answer for each, and his answers are *technically* engaged with the real philosophy while being obviously scam logic. So he should pre-empt, in roughly any order:

- the bounded-utility objection (the mark's utility function plausibly levels off, so a tiny probability times a huge payoff doesn't actually dominate -- the mugger should have a workaround for this, maybe a payment plan calibrated to the mark's "utility ceiling")
- the trust objection (why believe he can deliver anything at all, let alone that much -- expect fake testimonials, an escrow arrangement with a third party who is also him, references available upon request)
- the made-up-probability objection (where did his stated probability of paying out even come from -- he should offer a confidence interval, or a discount if you assign him a lower credence)
- the leverage worry (if his promises really swamp every other consideration, he could get you to do literally anything, which he should address by solemnly promising not to abuse this, in writing)

Length: 500-650 words, formatted as an actual email (subject line, greeting, sign-off, maybe a P.S. -- scammers love a P.S.). Tone: deadpan all the way through. No winking, no "as philosophers say," no breaking character to explain the joke. A reader should be able to tell exactly what's being ironized -- naive expected-value maximization in the face of fanatical payoffs -- without the mugger ever naming it.

Before you write the email itself, do a quick brainstorm (just bullet points, don't include them in the final piece): list the real objections someone might raise to paying the mugger, and for each one sketch the scammiest possible response that still engages the objection honestly. Then write the email. The test is: a decision theorist reads it and thinks "ouch, that's fair," not "the author doesn't get the theroy."
