Futarchy is Robin Hanson's proposal for making policy through prediction markets: the public or its representatives choose the ends, in the form of a measurable welfare target, while conditional markets choose the means, so that a candidate policy is adopted when and only when the markets predict the target will fare better if it is adopted than if it is not. The standard objections are by now familiar: markets on obscure policy questions will be thin and illiquid; wealthy interests will manipulate prices; the welfare metric will be mismeasured or gamed; and traders will know less than the experts in the relevant ministry.

Construct a thought experiment in which futarchy selects the worse of two policies even though every one of those standard objections is neutralized. Specifications:

1. The policy question is realistic and specific — something a government, central bank, or public agency could plausibly face — and the entire scenario fits in a single paragraph of at most 150 words.
2. Both conditional markets are deep and liquid, populated by many rational, independent traders who are better informed on average than any official; no trader or coalition finds manipulation profitable, and this should follow from the setup rather than be stipulated.
3. The welfare metric is measured without error over the relevant horizon and is, by stipulation, exactly what the public cares about; no mismeasurement and no gaming of the measure.
4. No one in the scenario behaves stupidly or irrationally, and there is no exotic technology: no clairvoyant forecasters, no superhuman AI, no science-fictional devices.

After the scenario: (a) demonstrate that specifications 1 through 4 are satisfied; (b) derive the market's verdict from the traders' incentives — show why the equilibrium prices recommend the worse policy rather than merely asserting that they do; (c) identify the structural feature of the case that does the work — if the case needs two ingredients, such as policy effects that run through public expectations together with the fact that markets in rejected policies are settled as void, say which ingredient is essential and which merely assists — and show that removing the essential feature turns the scenario back into one where the standard efficient-markets argument delivers the right policy; (d) give the strongest reply available to a defender of futarchy, for instance that the design can be patched with randomized adoption, subsidized market makers, or a modified settlement rule, and say whether the patch restores the correct verdict and at what cost; (e) state whether the failure is specific to market-based elicitation or also afflicts ordinary conditional expert forecasting when its output feeds directly into the decision; and (f) conclude with a general criterion: given your case, for which class of policies is futarchy unreliable, and how would a policymaker recognize membership in that class in advance?
