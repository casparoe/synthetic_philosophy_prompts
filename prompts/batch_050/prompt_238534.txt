Two friends of mine got into a long arguement over beers last week about the 1999 NATO bombing of Serbia over Kosovo (no UN authorization, justified as stopping ethnic cleansing), and it ended up turning on this passage from J.S. Mill's 1859 essay "A Few Words on Non-Intervention," which one of them pulled up on his phone:

"When the contest is only with native rulers, and with such native strength as those rulers can enlist in their defence, the answer I should give to the question of the legitimacy of intervention is, as a general rule, No. The reason is, that there can seldom be anything approaching to assurance that intervention, even if successful, would be for the good of the people themselves. The only test possessing any real value, of a people's having become fit for popular institutions, is that they, or a sufficient portion of them to prevail in the contest, are willing to brave labour and danger for their liberation. [...] But the evil is, that if they have not sufficient love of liberty to be able to wrest it from merely domestic oppressors, the liberty which is bestowed on them by other hands than their own, will have nothing real, nothing permanent. No people ever was and remained free, but because it was determined to be so."

Dan says this settles it: Kosovo was Serbia's internal affair, the Yugoslav army was a native ruler's own strength, so Mill's answer is no, and he says the last bit predicts exactly what happened in Iraq after 2003, where freedom got delivered by outsiders and didn't take. Ana says he's reading a rule of thumb as a ban -- Mill only says as a general rule, and elsewhere in the same essay he treats it as a different case when a government can only stay on top of its own people because of outside backing, which she says describes Milosevic's security machine versus a basically defenseless population, so evening the odds is permitted. Dan shot back that Mill's exception is for governments propped up by foreign troops, nobody was sending troops to prop up Belgrade, and anyway Mill's point is that if the Kosovars were fit for freedom they'd have won it themselves -- so the bombing was either unnecessary or doomed. Ana's last move was that Mill's test is about whether freedom lasts once gained, not about whether you're allowed to stop a massacre, and air strikes backing people who'd been resisting for years isn't the same as handing freedom to passive bystanders.

Neither will budge and now it's my problem. Who's right at each step? Does the passage actually support Dan's reading, does Ana's use of Mill's exception hold up, and is there something both of them are missing about applying an 1859 essay to stuff like this? Please answer in American English.
