So I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy background, but I have a 3 year old and somehow ended up in a late-night argument with my brother-in-law (a high school civics teacher, so he thinks he knows stuff) about how much the state should be allowed to second-guess parents. He brought up the idea of a child's "right to an open future" -- I think it goes back to a philosopher named Feinberg -- and the old US Supreme Court case where Amish parents won the right to pull their kids out of school after eighth grade even though the state wanted them in school longer. Fine. But then I tried to actually state the principle carefully, and it seems to prove way to much. Here's my version of it:

(1) Kids have a right to an open future, meaning the state can and sometimes must step in when parents make choices that irreversibly cut off big chunks of the child's adult life-options before the kid can choose for themselves. (2) What makes such a choice a rights-violation is that it's irreversible and closes off a lot of options -- not which domain it's in (religion, school, the body, whatever). Irreversibility plus magnitude is what matters. (3) But by that standard, tons of completely normal parenting qualifies. Being raised speaking only English basically forecloses ever being a native speaker of another language, thanks to the whole critical period thing. Growing up in one culture forecloses really belonging to another. Even stuff like where you grow up, or whether your parents make you do music or sports as a kid, permanently shapes which careers and communities are realistically open to you. Plenty of these close off as many life paths as an Amish upbringing does, arguably more. (4) So the open-future principle commits the state to intervening in ordinary parenting on a massive scale -- mandatory multilingual education at minimum, maybe communal childrearing, definitely not letting parents just raise their kid monolingual in their own religion and culture. (5) That conclusion is insane, so one of the earlier steps has to go.

Here's what bugs me: I *want* to keep something like (1), because I do think the state should stop parents from, say, refusing their kid basic schooling or needed medical care. And (3) seems to just be... true, empirically? Like, the linguistics on native acquisition seems pretty solid. So I suspect (2) or the move to (4), but every fix I try either makes the principle too weak to justify the interventions I liked, or just amounts to "intervene in the cases I happen to disapprove of," which isn't a principle at all. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it actually cost me -- especially given the cases I said I want to keep? And if the whole request is confused somehow, like if I've misunderstood what the open-future right was ever supposed to be, just tell me that straight up instead of politely playing along.
