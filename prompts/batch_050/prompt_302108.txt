So I'm writing a novel where the villain, more or less, is a company -- a mid-sized property developer that keeps buying up council blocks, finding the legal loopholes, and leaving the tenants scattered. Nobody inside it is a moustache-twirler; each person just does their little bit. What I want is for the reader to feel that the company itself deserves blame, not just this or that executive. But a philosopher friend read my draft and told me the whole conceit is confused because organisations can't be guilty, only people can, and now I'm worried my book rests on a category error.

So: give me your actual credence that a corporation -- taken as an entity, not as shorthand for its employees -- can be genuinely morally responsible for what it does, in a way that would make blame stick to the firm even if every individual involved was only doing a defensible-in-isolation job. I don't want a tour of positions, I want a number and where it comes from. Decompose it for me: how much depends on whether a company can have something like intentions (policies, decision procedures, internal culture -- does that count?), how much on whether moral responsibility requires consciousness or just the right kind of decision structure, how much on whether group agency is real or just a useful fiction, and anything else you think the credence actually rides on. A rough probability for each, a line on why, then how they combine into your final number.

Then tell me which part of the decomposition you're shakiest on, and what would shift it -- and finally, as a storyteller's favour: if your credence is low, is there any way I can write the company so that blaming it feels true anyway, or am I stuck making the finance director the real villain?

Oh and answer in British English please, I'm trying to soak up the register for my narrator.
