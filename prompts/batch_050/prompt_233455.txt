For a philosophy-of-biology seminar, write a specification that a proposed mechanistic explanation must meet before it counts as explaining a biological phenomenon, rather than merely listing its parts, reporting correlations, or telling a plausible causal story. Present the specification as numbered requirements, each followed by the consideration or test case motivating it, and classify every requirement as non-negotiable, negotiable for some explanatory purposes, or characteristic mainly of one theoretical camp.

Use this hypothetical system as the running case. In cells exposed to blue light, receptor R recruits adaptor A; A brings kinase K into contact with transporter T; K phosphorylates T, after which phosphorylated T assembles into dimers and imports a nutrient. Scaffold S normally accelerates K–T contact, although a related protein can replace S when S is absent. Deleting K or T abolishes nutrient import, while a version of K that cannot catalyze phosphorylation also abolishes it. Overexpressing A partially restores import in cells lacking S. Increasing temperature changes the rate of import without altering the light-responsive pathway. These findings are supported by knockouts, targeted mutations, and direct intervention, but the molecular interactions have not been observed in continuous time.

Address, at minimum:

- the difference between entities, activities, states, and causal arrows;
- why organization and productive continuity matter in addition to necessity and sufficiency;
- how the explanatory phenomenon and the mechanism’s boundaries should be individuated;
- what decomposition into lower-level components contributes, and why recomposition into a working whole is needed;
- how a mechanism sketch differs from a mechanism schema and from a complete description;
- how interventions, redundancy, compensation, and robustness complicate claims that a component belongs to the mechanism;
- how temporally ordered evidence and dynamical information should matter;
- when omissions, simplifications, and idealizations undermine the explanation rather than merely leaving it incomplete;
- whether universality, conservation across organisms, and predictive precision are genuine desiderata or demands appropriate only in some contexts;
- how a mechanistic explanation should be judged against a parts inventory, a causal-network model, a covering-law explanation, and a purely interventionist causal graph.

Identify any requirements that appear to be jointly unsatisfiable, such as complete lower-level decomposition, manageable explanatory scope, sensitivity to higher-level organization, and coverage of all biologically permissible variants. Explain what should be given up when they conflict. Conclude by applying the specification to the blue-light system: identify which parts of the proposed mechanism are established, which remain schematic, and whether the S–A compensation result shows that S is not part of the mechanism, is context-dependent, or belongs to a family of mechanisms.
