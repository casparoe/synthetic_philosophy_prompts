I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in ethics, and I want to open our unit on moral responsibility with a diagnostic quiz titled "Where do you actually stand on moral luck?" Most of my students walk in assuming they hold the commonsensical view that people are only assessable for what was under their control, and most of them turn out not to hold it once you press them with cases. The quiz should do that pressing.

Write the quiz: ten questions, each built around a concrete case with three or four answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto five positions. The leaves I need are these, described by content rather than by label. First, the control-principle purist: only the quality of the will is assessable, and our tendency to blame the unlucky driver more than the lucky one is either an epistemic artifact (outcomes are evidence about the will) or a plain mistake. Second, the resultant-luck acceptor: identical negligence can warrant different blame depending on what in fact results, so the reckless driver who kills a child is more blameworthy than the counterpart spared by chance. Third, the split position: circumstantial and constitutive luck are real and morally significant, but resultant luck is not; outcomes never add blame, even though the situations we face and the temperaments we have shape what our wills confront. Fourth, the view that luck permeates agency so thoroughly that the purist's ideal of a luck-free core is incoherent, so instead of quarantining luck we should reconceive moral assessment itself, as something more like a way of relating to agents than a ledger of desert. Fifth, the thoroughgoing skeptic: since everything about us is ultimately luck, no one is responsible in the basic-desert sense, and practices of blame stand or fall on their forward-looking effects.

Constraints on the questions. None may use the phrase "moral luck," the phrase "control principle," or any philosopher's name; my students must be sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label appeals to them. Each answer option should be written as a first-person verdict about the case, not as a description of a theory. Use at least one matched pair of cases differing only in outcome (the two reckless drivers), at least one circumstantial-luck case (the person who would have collaborated under occupation but happened to live somewhere uneventful), at least one constitutive-luck case (the person whose callous temperament was never chosen), and at least one institutional case (whether the law should punish attempts less severely than completed crimes, other things equal). The remaining questions are yours to design, but each must turn on a distinction on which at least two of the five positions genuinely come apart; do not include filler questions where every position gives the same answer.

On the scoring key, three things. First, the purist and the skeptic converge on many case verdicts, so the key must include at least one question that separates them — presumably one probing whether a fully formed malicious will is blameworthy even when the will itself was entirely shaped by factors beyond the agent's control. Second, some answer patterns will be internally unstable (a student who accepts resultant luck in the driver case but denies it in the sentencing case); the key should flag these patterns and name the tension rather than forcing them onto a leaf. Third, build the key from the logical space, not from my list of five: treat the three kinds of luck as three dimensions, enumerate which combinations are occupied and which are not, and for each unoccupied combination say briefly whether it is vacant because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If any two of my five leaves turn out to be inseparable by any ten-question instrument of this kind, tell me so plainly instead of papering over it.
