Here's a puzzle I keep chewing on. Everyone reaches for chaos when they want to show determinism doesn't imply predictability -- sensitive dependence, butterfly effect, fine. But chaos only blocks *practical* prediction given measurement error; a Laplace-style demon with perfect data still predicts a chaotic system fine. I want a thought experiment where a system is fully deterministic AND we stipulate perfect knowledge of its current state and its laws, and yet an agent *inside* the system still can't predict some future fact about it -- and where the obstruction isn't chaos and isn't quantum anything. Constraint: keep it mundane-ish (computers, billiard balls, whatever), no exotic physics, and the impossibility has to be structural, not just "too much compute" in the casual sense -- though if computational limits end up being the answer, say why that's in-principle and not in-practice.

After the scenario, walk through why each standard escape fails on it: chaos (stipulated away), quantum indeterminacy (stipulated away), ignorance of initial conditions (stipulated away). Then say which feature is actually doing the work -- I'm guessing it's something about the predictor being part of the thing predicted, but maybe I'm wrong and you should say so. Also: be clear about what's settled here (self-reference/diagonalization stuff is a century old) versus what's genuinely contested, like whether this kind of unpredictability tells us anything deep about free will or determinism itself -- don't present a contested philosophical moral as if it were the math.
