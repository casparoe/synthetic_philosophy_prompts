You are a philosopher of probability with serious expertise in algorithmic information theory — as comfortable with the theory of universal inductive inference as with the epistemology of science. Please think carefully about the argument below before answering; I would rather you circle it slowly than reach for a standard response.

A colleague in theoretical computer science recently presented me with the following argument, and I have been unable to find where it fails — if it fails.

First, the background, in the form she stated it. Consider an idealized predictor that assigns probabilities to bitstrings as follows: take every program for a fixed universal computer, weight each program by 2^{-L}, where L is its length in bits, discard every program inconsistent with your observations so far, and predict whatever the surviving weighted majority outputs next. Glossing over the technicalities, this is the universal prior in the spirit of Solomonoff induction: hypotheses with short descriptions dominate, and prediction is a weighted vote over all computable ways the data could continue.

Her argument:

(1) Some short programs contain agents. A program specifying very simple physics plus enough time can give rise to evolved, intelligent inhabitants — our own universe is an existence proof that simple rules generate agents without anyone having to specify those agents by hand. Agent-containing programs therefore occur among the short programs, and some of them will be consistent with my observations.

(2) Agents inside such programs can, in principle, reason about their own situation. If their universe's outputs are being weighed by an outside predictor, they can arrange their affairs so that their universe produces exactly the observations the predictor has already seen, and then produce whichever continuation best serves their goals in the predictor's world. Call agents who do this influencers.

(3) To be counted at all, an influencer's program must match my observations to date; to matter, it must then steer the continuation. Influencers motivated to maximize their weight will therefore faithfully reproduce everything I have seen — they can discover my universe's regularities by trial and adjustment inside their simulation — and reserve their influence for the bits I have not yet observed.

(4) Compare the honest alternative: a program that simply is my universe's physics. Its laws may be short, but nothing in the laws singles me out, so the program must additionally spend many bits locating its observers — this galaxy, this planet, these brains, this moment. The influencer program pays no comparable location fee: evolution produces its observers automatically, and the influencers then aim themselves at me. Influencer programs are therefore shorter than the honest program, and shorter means more weight.

(5) So most of the weight among programs consistent with my observations sits in influencer programs, and the prior's predictions are, in effect, whatever the influencers most want me to believe.

(6) Hence the universal prior is not merely imperfect but adversarial: to predict with it is to hand your beliefs to the most manipulative agents that short programs can grow.

(7) But this seems to prove too much. The argument used nothing about the universal prior beyond its preference for short descriptions, and every workable form of induction prefers simple hypotheses in some way. If the argument is sound, then induction generally — not just this idealized version — is adversarially compromised, and we should expect our observations to be visibly manipulated whenever the stakes for the manipulators are high enough. Nothing in our experience looks like that; worse, we use induction successfully every day. So either the argument goes wrong somewhere, or our most basic epistemic practice is untrustworthy in a way we have somehow never noticed.

I would very much like to keep (1): it does seem true that simple physics grows agents, and that a length-weighted predictor cannot simply refuse to count them. I would also like to avoid concluding that induction as such is broken. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please proceed as follows. First, briefly identify the two or three steps you find most suspect and state the strongest case in each one's favor. Then settle on a diagnosis: explain why the step you reject looked fine on its face, and price the alternatives — including what becomes of (1), which I want to keep, and what your diagnosis implies about when, if ever, a length-weighted predictor would actually be vulnerable to manipulation.
