Last year I promised to water my neighbor's orchids while she was on holiday. I forgot, the orchids died, and she told me I had killed them. I accepted this without thinking, and only later did it occur to me that roughly eight billion other people had also not watered her orchids, and none of them had killed anything. Since then I have been going around in circles about whether failing to do something can genuinely cause something, and I have started to suspect that the answer "yes, plainly" is much stranger than it first sounds. I would like to see that strangeness played out with a completely straight face.

Write me a satirical piece of around 600 words in the form of an extract from the newsletter of a wildly successful lifestyle guru -- the smug, unflappable kind who sells his method in courses. His method is built entirely on the premise that omissions are causes in exactly the same sense that actions are, and he has organized his life around it. The target is the claim that letting something happen just is causing it, full stop; I am not trying to mock any actual person.

A few requirements:

- Deadpan throughout. The guru never doubts himself, never winks at the reader, and never concedes that not doing something is somehow cheaper or less real than doing it. If an escape hatch appears, it must be one he tries to install himself and visibly fails to.
- The humor has to come from applying his premise consistently, not from giving him silly beliefs. He takes credit for the lives preserved by everything he refrained from doing, accepts responsibility for disasters he failed to prevent, and treats both kinds of bookkeeping as equally real. Since everyone alive this morning is someone he did not kill, his daily achievements dwarf those of any surgeon; since some people drowned while he did not sandbag, his daily failures dwarf those of any tyrant.
- At one point he must explain why some people's omissions count and others do not -- why his own non-donations weigh on him but a toddler's do not, or why his promise about the orchids made his forgetting a cause while a stranger's identical forgetting was not. The explanation has to lean, quietly and without acknowledgment, on some notion of what a reasonable person would have done, so that the attentive reader sees the whole edifice resting on that one unexamined word.
- By the end, the piece should tip into a conclusion the reader recoils from -- some cheerful pronouncement in which the guru, by his own arithmetic, has caused more deaths before breakfast than any figure in history and also personally saved the entire human race -- while he remains serenely pleased with his method.

Please format the response as follows: first, The newsletter (roughly 600 words, the piece itself, no editorializing); then, Where the joke lands (roughly 250 words), identifying which genuine difficulty in the theory of causation by omission each passage leans on, and telling me honestly whether the satire is fair to those who defend the view that omissions are real causes.
