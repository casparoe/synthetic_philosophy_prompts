I'm putting together a talk for a film-and-philosophy night and want to do Memento through Dennett's Orwellian vs Stalinesque distinction from the color phi discussion. For anyone rusty: Orwellian revision is when the experience happens veridically and memory rewrites it afterward, Ministry of Truth style; Stalinesque is when the brain stages the fabrication *before* anything reaches experience, so the fake version was all you ever got. Dennett's point with the color phi case is that at small enough timescales the distinction collapses — there's no fact of the matter about wich one happened.

So: Memento looks at first like a pure Orwellian story. Leonard has his experiences fine, he just can't retain them, and then he actively edits the record — burning the photograph, writing himself misleading clues, setting Teddy up to be the next John G. He's his own Ministry of Truth. But then there's the Sammy Jankis material, and the suggestion that the story Leonard tells about his wife's death is itself a reconstruction, and that starts to look Stalinesque: the "memories" he consults were fabricated exhibits all along, staged for the only jury that matters, namely present-tense Leonard. Do the film's central deceptions actually fall into one category, or is sorting them the wrong question?

Second thing. The reverse chronology does something to the audience that I think is philosophically loaded. We watch a scene, form an interpretation of it, and then a later-shown-but-earlier scene recontextualizes it, so our memory of what we saw keeps getting revised — the film puts us in Leonard's condition. Is the viewer's position Orwellian, Stalinesque, or some third thing where revision happens because the film *withholds* rather than fabricates?

And the hard question I actually want to build the talk around: the movie's gut-punch depends on there being a fact about what Leonard originally experienced (the insulin, his wife) that his current beliefs diverge from. But Dennett's argument implies that once you're inside the revision machinery, "what you actually experienced" can stop being well-defined. Does Memento secretly need a naive realist picture of original experience that Dennett is trying to talk us out of? Or does the film survive if you grant that there's no fact of the matter — and in that case, what exactly is tragic about Leonard? Be honest about where the film resists the philosophy; I don't want a reading that just uses the movie as an illustration for a paper.
