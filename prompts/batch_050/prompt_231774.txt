I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a course in the environmental humanities, and I've run into a problem with a term paper I'm writing on geological time. Like many people, I've been using "the Anthropocene" to refer to our current epoch, and I had drafted the paper around the idea that it began around 1950, when radioactive fallout from nuclear weapons testing left a globally synchronous marker in sediments and ice around the world. I even found that a small lake in Canada had been selected as the reference site where this boundary is preserved most cleanly, which seemed to me like the closest thing to an official starting line. But then I read that in 2024 a committee of geologists voted against formally recognizing the Anthropocene as an epoch, which I take to mean that we are, strictly speaking, still in the Holocene after all.

This has left me unsure how to write the paper, and honestly a bit unsure about the science itself. First, since the experts have now decided against the Anthropocene, does that mean the geological evidence for humanity's impact on the Earth system turned out to be weaker than advertised — for example, that the plutonium signal wasn't as sharp or as globally detectable as its proponents claimed? Second, if the vote wasn't really about the strength of that evidence, then what exactly was being decided, and why would geologists settle a question about the world by voting rather than by measuring? Third, now that the proposal has been rejected, is there any realistic prospect of the decision being revisited and 1950 being reinstated as the official start date, or should I just stop using the term "Anthropocene" in academic work altogether? I'd appreciate it if you could untangle what the vote actually settled, what it left open, and how much weight the concept still carries outside the formal business of naming epochs.
