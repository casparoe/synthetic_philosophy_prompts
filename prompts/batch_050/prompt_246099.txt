For my metaphysics seminar I tried to sharpen Kripke's old complaint against David Lewis's counterpart theory -- the one he pressed with the example of Hubert Humphrey, roughly that what happens to a merely possible lookalike of Humphrey is no concern of Humphrey's -- into a rigorous argument. Here is what I came up with. (1) According to counterpart theory, a claim of the form "a could have been F" is made true by the existence, at some other possible world, of a counterpart of a who is F. (2) A counterpart of a is numerically distinct from a; no individual exists at more than one world. (3) If what makes a claim apparently about an individual true is a fact wholly about a numerically distinct individual, then the claim was never really about the first individual at all. (4) So on counterpart theory, "Humphrey could have won the election" is not really about Humphrey, and more generally no merely possible fortune or misfortune of mine -- the crash I narrowly escaped, the career I nearly had -- is really about me. (5) But nothing in steps (1) through (4) turns on modality specifically: the argument works against any theory that grounds claims apparently about one individual in facts about a numerically distinct but appropriately related individual. (6) Perdurantism is exactly such a theory: it grounds "I was once a small child" in facts about a past temporal part, numerically distinct from the person-whole I am, related to me by the same genidentity or counterpart-like relations that unify persons over time. (7) So, by the same reasoning, on perdurantism "I was once a small child" is not about me; the child was, strictly, someone else. (8) Generalizing: anyone who accepts the Humphrey-style objection to counterpart theory must also accept that no one was ever a child, that nothing I did yesterday is something I did, and that my dread of tomorrow's surgery attaches to a stranger. (9) That is absurd, so the Humphrey objection must fail and counterpart theory survives unscathed.

The awkward part: I set out to refute counterpart theory, and the argument now seems to show that the only available objection to it proves too much and must be abandoned. I am a committed perdurantist, so I cannot accept (7) and (8); but I also don't want to lose whatever was right in Kripke's complaint, since counterpart theory really does seem to me to change the subject. Note that Lewis himself held both counterpart theory and perdurantism, so presumably he thought the two cases come apart somewhere -- or that the whole line of reasoning misfires at a shared step.

Which step do you reject, and why does it look so convincing given that it fails? Please be precise about whether the trouble lives in (3)'s principle linking truthmakers to subject matter, in (5)'s claim that the modal and temporal cases are parallel, or elsewhere. Then price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject (3) -- in particular, does anything survive of the Humphrey objection once we grant that a claim can be about one individual while being made true by another? And what does it cost to accept (3) but deny (5) -- that is, what exactly is the relevant difference between the counterpart relation across worlds and the unity relation across time, and can a defender of transworld identity state that difference without simply begging the question against Lewis?
