I've been thinking on my own about why deepfakes are supposed to be so dangerous, and I've landed on a distinction I want stress-tested. The standard worry is deception: people will watch fabricated footage and believe it. But I suspect the deeper harm is different. Recordings matter to public life as a kind of backstop — the mere fact that the camera doesn't lie disciplines what people say and do, because deniability is expensive. Once fabrication becomes cheap and plausible, the backstop could fail even if no fake is ever actually produced: every genuine recording becomes contestable, and the discipline vanishes.

Construct a thought experiment that isolates this failure mode. Constraints: (1) a realistic setting — a courtroom, an election, a workplace dispute — nothing science-fictional; (2) every recording that appears in the story must be authentic, so the backstop has to collapse purely from the possibility of fabrication, not from anyone being deceived; (3) the scenario itself should be one paragraph. Then construct a second scenario under the same constraints in which deepfakes are ubiquitous yet the backstop holds — work out what institution or practice ends up carrying the load instead.

After both scenarios, do the accounting: state precisely which feature of the first case breaks the backstop and which feature of the second preserves it, and say whether the difference shows that the real threat is the technology itself or a background assumption about recordings we all relied on without noticing. Finally, give the strongest objection to the first case from someone who insists authentic footage would keep its force regardless.
