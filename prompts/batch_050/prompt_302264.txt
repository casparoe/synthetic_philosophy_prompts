You are a veteran instructor of undergraduate epistemology who has run this seminar many times and knows exactly which classroom gimmicks fizzle and which ones leave students genuinely unsettled in a productive way. I need your help designing one of the latter. Think carefully before you commit to a structure; sketch a couple of candidate designs in your head and pick the one that best survives contact with real students.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, about sixteen students, and I have one seventy-five-minute session for this. We are about to discuss the so-called dogmatism paradox. The rough idea: suppose I genuinely know that p. Any future evidence I encounter that tells against p must, since p is true, be misleading evidence. But then I seem to be in a position to know, in advance, that any such evidence is misleading -- and knowing that a piece of evidence is misleading seems to license me in simply disregarding it. So knowledge appears to come with a rational entitlement to close my mind: the better my epistemic position, the more entitled I am to ignore anything that might correct me. My students reliably nod along to the reasoning and feel nothing, because on the page the move of disregarding evidence looks obviously disreputable and they assume they'd never do it. I want them to discover otherwise about themselves.

Design an activity that makes the pull of the paradox happen to them before anyone names it. No lecture up front. I want a setup in which some or all of the students genuinely come to know some concrete, checkable fact inside the classroom (by proving it, counting it, verifying it together -- your choice, but the knowledge has to be earned on the spot and be real), after which the rules of the activity confront them with counterevidence against that fact that they are in a position to rationally dismiss precisely because they know. The structure should make dismissing the counterevidence the apparently correct move -- so that when they do it, they have enacted the paradox rather than merely understood it. Please specify: the exact rules and sequence, any materials I need to prepare in advance (including fabricated evidence, if that's your method), a minute-by-minute timeline, and what roles if any students play. Small stakes or points are fine if they sharpen the choice.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the paradox's structure -- the step from knowing p to knowing future counterevidence is misleading, and the step from there to licensed disregard -- and only at the end gives them the name. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students who refuse to commit to having known anything, a confederate who overplays their part, someone who spots the trick early) and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce: for instance, in my game the knowledge will concern a trivial checkable fact, whereas the real bite of the paradox is supposed to concern weightier beliefs -- tell me what the facilitator has to supply in discussion that no game can stage.
