So I teach a lower-division metaphysics course and every semester we do the Ship of Theseus, and every semester some student asks "okay but which one is ACTUALLY the real ship" and we go around in circles, which is fine for a seminar. But here's the thing: a friend of mine sits on the collections committe of a maritime heritage museum, and she pointed out that for them this is not a seminar exercise, it's a recurring administrative decision with money attached. They meet quarterly and have to rule on things like: a restored 1890s fishing boat whose hull planks have been swapped out one by one over forty years of maintenance; a wreck that was salvaged, disassembled, warehoused, and now someone wants to reassemble the original timbers and claim it's the same vessel on the registry; a full-scale replica built from original blueprints that the donors want to display under the historic name; and a flagship where basically only the keel and some fasteners are original but it's never once left service. These rulings affect insurance valuations, whether pieces keep their heritage designation, and what the donor plaques say, and the board signing off on them are mostly not philosophy people.

What I want from you is a reusable decision procedure the committee can apply in maybe twenty minutes per case, that translates whatever the philosophical debate about persistence actually delivers -- spatiotemporal continuity, sortal-dependence, gradual replacement vs. reassembly, the social/institutional dimension -- into ordered tests a layperson can run. Be honest about which philosophical positions have to get baked in as policy choices rather than derived, and mark explicitly where the procedure bottoms out and the committee just has to vote. Include in your answer how the procedure handles at least two of the cases above, including one where it gives an answer the museum will hate.

Format-wise: give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion -- item one should be the procedure's core rule or ordering of tests, the middle items the key distinctions the committee must apply in order, and the last item the list of case types where the procedure admits it can't decide.
