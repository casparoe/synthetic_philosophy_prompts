So I need help settling an argument between my two roommates, Priya and Marcus, that started over our robot vacuum and got completely out of hand.

Background: we have this new vacuum with a little voice module, and when it gets stuck under the couch it says stuff like "I'm trying to reach the dust under here, could you give me a hand?" and when it's done it announces it "checked every room." The other day it spent twenty minutes bumping a chair leg that someone had moved, and I said "it doesn't know the chair moved" without thinking about it.

Priya's position, roughly: that sentence was perfectly fine and literally true. She leans on Dennett's old intentional stance idea -- her version of it is that once treating something as a believer with goals lets you predict it better than treating it as parts, there's nothing more to ask for, the belief talk isn't a metaphor layered over some hidden fact of the matter. She said the vacuum believes the chair is in its usual spot, wants the corner clean, full stop, and that Dennett's whole point was that our own beliefs get their status the same way, so it's not some downgrade version for robots.

Marcus said that proves way too much. He said by that logic the river "wants" to reach the sea and the thermostat "believes" it's cold, and Priya said fine, cheap beliefs for cheap systems, the stance is graded, and honestly the thermostat case doesn't scare her.

Then Marcus shifted to what I think was his better move. He said even on Dennett's own terms the stance only earns its keep when the belief attributions are *stable* -- same system, same beliefs, tracking over time -- and with the vacuum's voice module you can get it to "believe" anything by rephrasing the situation to it. He asked it whether the hallway was clean and got one answer, asked it whether it had checked the hallway and got a slightly different one, and said there was no fact about what it believed, only facts about what it outputs given the prompt. So the intentional stance doesn't actually pay for itself there; you predict it better by thinking about its code and sensors, which he said proves the beliefs were never real.

Priya's comeback: human beliefs are just as wobbly and framing-dependent (she brought up how poll answers flip when you reword the question) and we don't conclude people don't believe anything, so Marcus is holding the vacuum to a standard humans would fail too. Marcus said the wobbliness in humans is bounded by something -- there's a fact of the matter that I do actually believe the chair moved -- and in the vacuum there isn't anything playing that role, it's wobbles all the way down. Priya said that "something playing that role" is exactly the kind of magical extra ingredient Dennett spent his career arguing doesn't exist.

They also had a side skirmish I couldn't follow: Marcus said that if believing is just being-usefully-predictable, then whether the vacuum believes anything depends on how smart *we* are at predicting it -- a better engineer who sees the code loses the vacuum's beliefs, which is absurd. Priya said that's not absurd, it's just what the view says, and our beliefs are hostage to nobody being able to predict us mechanistically too.

Can you go through the whole exchange and adjudicate point by point -- not just who "wins"? Specifically: (1) is Priya's reading of the intentional stance fair or is she stretching it, (2) does the thermostat/river objection actually land, (3) who takes Marcus's instability argument, and is Priya's polls-rebuttal a good parallel or a false one, (4) is there really a "bounded wobbliness" fact in humans that has no analogue in the vacuum, and (5) is Marcus's "it depends on the observer's smarts" objection the bullet Priya says it is or the reductio he says it is?
