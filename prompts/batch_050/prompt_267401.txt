I retired last year after four decades as an experimental physicist, and I have lately been attending the philosophy seminar at my old university. At the last meeting, a colleague dismissed a worry about a theoretically elegant but empirically unconfirmed framework with the line 'if it isn't beautiful, it isn't true'. I have heard versions of this throughout my career — at conferences, in refereeing disputes, in hiring debates — usually deployed to justify pursuing a theory past the point where the evidence warrants, or to rank one formalism above another without further argument. I want to know what the slogan could actually be claiming. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses it might express — ranging from a mere report of how scientists happen to feel, through a heuristic claim about how research ought to be directed, to a metaphysical claim that nature itself is simple or harmonious in a way our aesthetic sense can detect. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, note what would have to be true about past scientific practice for it to hold, and indicate which readings would actually license the dismissive use my colleague made of it. Please write your answer in British English.
