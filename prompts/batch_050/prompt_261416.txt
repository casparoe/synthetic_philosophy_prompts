I'm a policy researcher rather than a machine-learning engineer, and at a workshop last month a speaker I respect made an argument I have been chewing on ever since. Advanced AI systems, she said, are best thought of as corporations: entities assembled from many components, no one of which understands or endorses the whole, which pursue objectives that no single part was explicitly given, and which humans must steer through incentives, monitoring, and audit rather than through direct control. Her conclusion was that prosaic alignment — aligning systems built from current methods, without awaiting any new conceptual insight — is less a novel science than an extension of a craft we have practised for centuries on firms, bureaucracies, and markets, and that the catastrophists are panicking because they have mistaken an old problem for a new one. The economists in the audience nodded along; the one alignment researcher I spoke to afterwards called the analogy dangerous, though he struggled to say precisely why. I would like you to adjudicate.

Please audit the analogy properly. Work out what is meant to map onto what — what plays the role of the shareholders, the managers, the incentive scheme, the audit, the regulatory environment — and then identify where the mapping breaks. Pay particular attention to features of corporations that the analogy quietly imports and that trained models may lack: that corporations are made of human beings with values of their own and with levers of exit and voice, that they operate at human timescales, that their failures tend to be legible to the humans inside them, and that the law co-evolved with them over centuries rather than being designed in advance. For each disanalogy you raise, say whether it actually matters for the intended conclusion or is merely decorative, since I suspect some of the standard objections do no real work. Finally, tell me whether the speaker's relaxed conclusion — that alignment is an old craft rather than a new science — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If you think a better analogy exists for what prosaic alignment is actually attempting, propose it and defend it against the same treatment.

One request concerning format: please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email or a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, even though a correspondence table would be tempting here. And please write in British English throughout.
