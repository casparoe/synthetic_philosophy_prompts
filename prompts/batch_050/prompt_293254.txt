So I was arguing with a friend who thinks the whole bootstrapping / easy knowledge literature is massively overblown. His line: sure, reasoning like "the gauge says the tank is half full, the tank is half full, so the gauge got this one right... and after enough of these, the gauge is reliable" looks fishy, but we do apparently circular stuff all the time and nobody blinks. His favorite example is roughly: you double-check your vision by comparing what your two eyes report, or you confirm a clock is running correctly by noticing its ticks line up with your own sense of duration. He says if epistemologists condemn the gauge case they have to condemn half of ordinary reasoning too, and I suspect he's wrong but I couldn't articulate exactly where the line falls.

So here's what I want: construct a pair of scenarios that settle the argument by triangulation. Build two cases that use the *same* inferential skeleton as the classic bootstrapping pattern -- an instrument or faculty delivers a verdict, you take the verdict on board, then you use the verdict (or a string of such verdicts) to conclude that the instrument or faculty is trustworthy -- but where one case is intuitively perfectly fine reasoning and the other is intuitively worthless, as worthless as the gauge case. Constraints: both scenarios must be ordinary-life (no evil demons, no brains in vats, no perfect predictors, no stipulation that the subject is hallucinating), each no longer than a paragraph, and the two cases should be as structurally parallel as you can make them -- same number of steps, same closure-style move at the end, ideally both involving checking a source against its own deliverances. The whole point is that whatever flips the verdict can't be something crude like "in the bad one the source is actually broken"; both sources should be working fine, so the difference has to be epistemic, not factual.

After the two scenarios, do the analysis: run each case under (a) the view that basic perception/instrument-reading gives you justification without any antecedent warrant for thinking the source is reliable, and (b) the view that you need some independent grip on a source's reliability before its deliverances can justify anything. Say which structural feature is doing the work in separating the good case from the bad one (candidate features might be: whether the confirming evidence is independent of the source being tested, whether the test could possibly have come out negative, whether reliability of the source is part of what's being inferred versus part of what's being assumed -- but figure out which one actually holds up). Then tell me what happens if you remove that feature from the good case: does it collapse into the gauge case, or does something else save it? And give the best response a defender of basic knowledge would make to stop your good case from licensing bootstrapping everywhere.

At the end, give me a JSON object summarizing the whole thing with these fields: "good_case" (one-sentence summary of the fine scenario), "bad_case" (one-sentence summary of the worthless one), "shared_inferential_form" (the skeleton both share), "differentiating_feature" (the feature that flips the verdict), "basic_knowledge_view_verdict" (what view (a) says about each case and whether it can tell them apart), "antecedent_warrant_view_verdict" (same for view (b)), "collapse_test" (what removing the feature does), and "who_wins_the_argument" (me or my friend, in one sentence).
