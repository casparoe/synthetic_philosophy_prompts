So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about campus speech fights. One of my interviewees, a political theorist, defended disinviting speakers with what she called the immune system argument. Her line, roughly: a tolerant society is like a body's immune system, and an immune system that attacks nothing just dies. So refusing a platform to intolerant speakers isn't a failure of tolerance, it's tolerance defending itself, and the so-called paradox of tolerance dissolves once you see that. She also mentioned Germany's "militant democracy" setup as the policy version of the same idea.

My editor loves the immune system line and basically wants it in the headline, but something about it nags at me and I don't want to print an arguement that falls apart the moment a philosopher reads it. Can you audit the analogy for me before I go to print?

Concretely, what I'd like:

1. The mapping, laid out explicitly -- what corresponds to the pathogen, what's the immune response, what's the health of the organism, what's death. A table is fine.
2. Where the mapping breaks. Some things I keep half-remembering from a biology beat I used to cover: immune systems produce allergies and autoimmune disorders, i.e. they attack the wrong stuff all the time, which feels like it might cut *against* her rather than for her. And I think immunologists talk about immune *tolerance* toward gut bacteria and such, so suppression isn't even the immune system's default strategy. Do either of those actually matter, or am I nitpicking?
3. The part I really need: whether the disanalogies matter for the specific work she's making the analogy do, namely licensing institutions to exclude people without that counting as intolerance. Distinguish the disanalogies that are fatal for that use from the ones that are just imprecise.
4. Whether the conclusion survives in some weaker form once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy for what she wants to say. I dimly recall that Popper's own treatment of the paradox was more hedged than "suppress the intolerant" -- something about reserving force for when the other side has given up on argument entirely -- so if there's a framing closer to that, I'd rather use it.

If I've misremembered any of the science or the Popper bit, say so -- that's exactly the kind of error I'm trying to catch.
