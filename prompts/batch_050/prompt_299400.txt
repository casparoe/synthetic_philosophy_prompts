I'm preparing for a debate in my political theory seminar on Robin Hanson's proposal for futarchy, the idea that we should keep democratic voting for choosing our values and goals but delegate the choice of policies to prediction markets, on the principle often summarized as voting values but betting beliefs. My teammates keep leaning on an analogy I want stress-tested: they say a policy market under futarchy works like the stock market, so that letting prices pick policy is just doing for government what investors already do for companies -- prices aggregate dispersed information, people with knowledge put money behind it, fools get separated from their cash and stop distorting the signal, and the resulting number is a better guide than any poll, pundit, or committee. They then conclude that opposition to futarchy is just status-quo bias, since we already trust market prices with decisions at least as consequential as legislation.

Audit the analogy. Set out the mapping explicitly -- a table of correspondences is fine: what in the stock market corresponds to the policy market, what corresponds to the firm's earnings, to the investor, to insider trading, to a market crash, to the exchange's regulators. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and -- this is the part I most need -- separate the disanalogies that actually matter for my teammates' intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Candidates I'd like assessed include: that stock traders bet on outcomes they don't control while policy-market traders bet on a variable the government itself sets after reading the price (so the price is trying to forecast its own cause); that a stock's value exists whether or not you buy it, while a conditional policy price has no ground truth if the policy is never adopted; that stock markets are deep and subsidized by index funds and noise traders, while a policy market might be thin, manipulable by interested parties, or dominated by a Keynesian beauty-contest dynamic; and that weighting judgment by money staked is arguably a feature in finance but smuggles a plutocratic epistemology into a democracy.

I'd also like your verdict on what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, and whether my teammates' conclusion -- that resistance to futarchy is mere status-quo bias -- survives once the material disanalogies are on the table. If the stock market is the wrong source domain, tell me what a better analogy would be for what futarchy is actually doing, and whether that better analogy makes the proposal look stronger or weaker. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
