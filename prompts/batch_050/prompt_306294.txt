There's a line that keeps coming up in my corner of the internet and, annoyingly, in my actual life: "you can't agree to disagree." It got deployed on me last week. My friend Dana and I were going back and forth for like an hour about whether our company should drop its main product line and pivot, and after we'd both laid out our reasoning she goes "ok, we're both smart, we've both heard everything the other has to say, and rational people can't agree to disagree — so one of us is being irrational here, and I don't think it's me." Which felt less like an argument and more like a trap, but I couldn't articulate why on the spot.

I poked around afterwards and it seems to trace back to a theorem by Aumann, something about common priors and common knowledge. I get the vague gist — if two agents share a prior and each knows the other's estimate, the estimates have to coincide. But the gap between that and what Dana did with it seems enormous, and I want the gap mapped. So: lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be expressing, as a menu, from the near-trivial end (something like: same priors plus same evidence gives same posteriors) to the maximalist end (any persistent disagreement between honest inquirers means someone is epistemically broken). For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, an actual theorem, a plausible heuristic, or just false — and flag where the technical machinery is doing work that the slogan hides. Like: what exactly is a "common prior," what does *common* knowledge of our estimates add over merely both of us knowing them, and does the theorem say anything about the normal human case where we've exchanged arguments for an hour rather than announcing exact credences?

And then the part I actually care about: does any defensible reading get Dana her conclusion? Because near as I can tell we obviously don't share a prior (whatever that would even mean for two actual people with different life histories), I don't know her exact credence and neither does she, and the theorem seems silent on whether listening to each other's reasoning simulates the kind of knowledge it requires. But I have a nagging worry that there's some spirit-of-the-theorem version — maybe about how updating on the mere fact that an informed, honest peer disagrees should move you — that survives the technical caveats and still has teeth in real disputes. If there is, state it precisely and tell me how much it would actually force me to concede.
