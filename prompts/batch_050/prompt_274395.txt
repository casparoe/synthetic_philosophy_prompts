As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last two years reading philosophy of science and causal inference, I keep returning to an episode I first encountered as a piece of statistical folklore: the 1973 graduate admissions figures at UC Berkeley. The aggregate numbers, as usually reported, showed about 44 percent of male applicants admitted against about 35 percent of female applicants; but when Bickel and his colleagues disaggregated by department, the apparent bias largely disappeared or even reversed in women's favor, because women had disproportionately applied to departments with low acceptance rates. I know this story well; what I want is not the story but a disciplined analysis.

Please take Simpson's paradox as your theoretical lens and work the episode through it in both directions. First, the formal side: what exactly does the paradox show here, and what does the standard resolution -- condition on the third variable, and the association flips -- presuppose about the causal structure among sex, department, and admission? Second, the part I find undertheorized in the usual retellings: conditioning on department is the correct move only if department choice is a confounder of the sex-admission relation. Suppose instead it is a mediator -- suppose, for instance, that the competitiveness of departments reflects long-standing differences in funding and prestige that are themselves downstream of the exclusion of women from those fields, or that applicants were steered toward or away from departments in gendered ways. Then conditioning on department blocks a causal path and the per-department figures are precisely not exculpatory. My question is whether the Berkeley data, as a case, can adjudicate between the confounder and mediator readings at all, and if not, what further evidence would.

Above all, I want a committed answer, not a survey. Take a definite position on whether the reversal is exculpatory in this episode -- and if your verdict is that the data underdetermine it, commit to that as a position and defend it against the obvious objection that it is a philosopher's evasion. Likewise, commit to a general criterion for when disaggregation is obligatory and when it is misleading, state the criterion as a rule one could actually apply, and say honestly what the episode shows Simpson's paradox, considered purely as a formal result, cannot settle. Where you are uncertain about the historical facts as opposed to the logic, flag the uncertainty explicitly rather than papering over it.
