Write a dialogue between Thomas Hobbes and a modern formalist mathematician (think Hilbert-style: geometry is just the consistent manipulation of symbols from axioms) who get stuck together at a conference dinner. Hobbes holds that geometry is genuinely demonstrable because we *make* its objects ourselves -- a circle is something generated by moving a body, and we can know what we've constructed in a way we can't know things we merely observe. The formalist thinks this is quaint: the meaning of "point" and "line" is exhausted by the axioms, physical motion is irrelevant, and Hobbes is confusing the psychology of discovery with justification. Have them actually argue, not lecture: the formalist should press Hobbes on his embarrassing circle-squaring episode (if geometers know what they construct, what went wrong there?), and Hobbes should press back on whether pure symbol-shuffling can really count as *knowledge* of anything, or whether the formalist has just defined away the question of why geometry applies to the world. Neither should win cleanly.
