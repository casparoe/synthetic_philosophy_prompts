I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has taken up philosophy in earnest over the past two years, and I have been working through the conservative tradition in political thought. In a recent popular book on political ideas (I will not name it, as my question concerns the substance rather than the author), I came across the following summary of Burke's conservatism and of Chesterton's fence. I would like you to respond as a professional philosopher specializing in the history of political thought, with a firm command of the primary texts and of contemporary discussions of tradition and practical reason. Think carefully before answering, and begin by briefly brainstorming the strongest version of what this author is trying to say before you subject it to scrutiny.

The summary runs as follows:

"Burke's core insight is that individual human reason is too feeble an instrument to redesign society from first principles. Institutions that have endured for many generations embody more practical knowledge than any single mind can hold, because they were shaped by innumerable adjustments to real circumstances, most of which were never recorded and some of which no one now living could even state. Chesterton captured the same point with his fence: if you come across a fence across a road and cannot see what it is for, you have no business removing it until you have discovered why it was put there. Since an institution that has survived was presumably serving some function — whether or not we can now identify it — inherited arrangements carry a presumption in their favor that abstract argument cannot by itself defeat. This is why Burke held that society is best understood as a partnership across the generations, and why genuine reform, as opposed to revolution, must proceed gradually, work with existing materials rather than against them, and treat large-scale institutional change as a last resort."

My questions, in order of importance to me:

First, the accuracy audit. Which parts of this would a careful defender of the conservative tradition endorse as stated; which are common simplifications that gesture at something true; and which, if any, are misrepresentations a scholar of Burke or Chesterton would object to? I am particularly unsure about two things: whether the fence parable licenses anything as strong as the closing sentence's "last resort" language, and whether the inference from "has survived" to "was presumably serving some function" is one either man actually makes, or whether it is the author's own gloss.

Second, whether the practical moral follows. Suppose everything accurate in the summary is granted. Does "treat large-scale institutional change as a last resort" actually follow, or does the argument support only something weaker — for instance, a rule about where the burden of proof lies, or about the manner in which reformers should proceed? If the stronger conclusion requires additional premises, please name them and indicate whether the conservative tradition supplies them.

Third, an angle I cannot suppress, given my background. The author's reasoning is structurally identical to adaptationist reasoning in evolutionary biology — inferring function from persistence — and my field spent decades cataloguing the failure modes of that inference: vestigial structures, exaptations, traits fixed by drift rather than selection, and functions that once obtained but no longer do under changed conditions. Do these failure modes transfer to social institutions, or does the analogy break down at some crucial point — for instance, because institutions, unlike organs, are continuously maintained by agents who can articulate, contest, and revise their purposes?

Please organize the first part under three headings — accurate, defensible simplification, misrepresentation — and indicate concretely where each sentence of the summary falls. Where the popular picture of what Burke actually argued diverges from the scholarly one, I want the scholarly version, not the cocktail-party version, even if it makes the author look better or worse than I have assumed.
