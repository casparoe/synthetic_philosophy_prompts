I'm grading finals for my upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and want a second pair of eyes on one answer before I finalize marks. The question I set was: State van Fraassen's Reflection principle and explain its relation to conditionalization. Is Reflection a defensible requirement of rationality? Discuss with at least one apparent counterexample.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on her credence in that proposition at some later time being r, should itself be r. Schematically: Cr(A | Cr_later(A) = r) = r. The motivating idea is that your present opinion should defer to the opinion you expect your future self to hold, since your future self will typically be better informed.

Reflection follows from conditionalization. If I know I will update by conditionalizing on whichever member of a partition of possible evidence turns out true, then my current credence in A is the weighted average of the credences I'd end up with, so the expectation of my future credence equals my present one. Anyone who accepts conditionalization as the rational update rule should therefore accept Reflection as a derived constraint.

One upshot is that a rational agent can never be sure in advance that her credences will change. If I am certain now that tomorrow my credence in rain will be 0.8, Reflection forces me to adopt 0.8 already. So confidently expecting any change of opinion at all is irrational -- a rational agent must expect her opinions to remain as they are.

There is also a Dutch book argument in favour of the principle: an agent who violates Reflection can be offered a series of bets, at her current and future credences, on which she loses money come what may. So violations of Reflection are irrational for the same pragmatic reason violations of the probability axioms are.

An apparent counterexample: I am about to drink several glasses of wine at a party. Right now I am nearly certain I will be unfit to drive home afterwards, but I know that in a few hours drunk-me will be quite confident of being fine to drive. Reflection seems to demand that I already adopt whatever credence drunk-me will have, which is absurd, since drunk-me is less reliable, not better informed. The natural fix is to restrict Reflection to situations where I expect my future self to be rational and at least as well informed as I am now; whether the principle stays substantive under that restriction is a further question.
