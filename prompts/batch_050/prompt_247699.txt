Among those who campaign for legalised assisted dying, few rhetorical moves are more common than the veterinary analogy: we regard it as cruel, and in some circumstances positively unlawful, to keep a terminally ill dog alive while it suffers, and we consider it a final act of kindness to have it put to sleep — so how can we justify refusing the same release to a terminally ill person who begs for it? I am preparing a paper for a medical ethics society in which I want to use this analogy, but only if it survives scrutiny, and I would therefore like it audited rather than simply endorsed or dismissed.

Please set out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the vet, of the owner or family who decide, of the animal itself, of the judgement that the suffering is no longer worth enduring, and of the act of putting the animal to sleep. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in the disanalogies that are uncomfortable for my side: the dog cannot request death, so the analogy's logic appears to rest on a third party's judgement that a life is no longer worth living, which would seem to license non-voluntary rather than voluntary euthanasia — a conclusion almost no advocate of assisted dying wants. Does the analogy in fact prove too much, or can it be repaired by treating the patient's request as doing work that has no counterpart in the veterinary case? Equally, audit it from the other direction: opponents say the analogy ignores that human suffering has narrative and anticipatory dimensions (fear of what is coming, the meaning attached to one's decline) that can be addressed by palliative care and cannot be read off outward signs, and that a society's willingness to kill its members carries a significance that a household's decision about a pet does not. Which of these disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion the analogy is used to support, and which are real differences that leave the argument intact?

Please also say what the analogy quietly assumes: that death can be a benefit to the one who dies, that suffering's intolerability can be judged accurately from outside, and that the moral work is done by the suffering rather than by the consent. Finally, tell me whether the pro-assisted-dying conclusion survives the audit, whether it survives only in a weakened form, and whether there is a better analogy I should reach for instead. Where you deliver a verdict, state what consideration or argument would lead you to a different one, and identify the point in your reasoning where you think the analysis is most likely to fail.
