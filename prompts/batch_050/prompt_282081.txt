I've been teaching our undergrad probability seminar this term and I keep hitting the same wall: the students ask, reasonably, what the probability numbers *mean*, and I find my gut answers don't line up with any of the textbook interpretations I put on the syllabus (frequentism, subjective Bayesianism, propensities, the logical view). So I figured I'd just write down my raw verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if you can tell me what theory, if any, I actually hold.

Case one: I flip a fair coin and slap my hand over it before looking. I'd say the probability it's heads is 1/2, full stop, even though the outcome is already settled and some god's-eye observer would assign 0 or 1. Case two: someone asks me the probability that the 10^9th digit of pi is 7. From my armchair this is *exactly* like the coin — I have no information favoring any digit — but I balk at saying it's 1/10. It's 0 or 1, I just don't know which, and I want to say that's different from case one even though I can't articulate why without handwaving. Case three: the weather service says 70% chance of rain tomorrow. I think this is a perfectly fine claim about tomorrow's actual weather, not just about the forecaster's betting behavior, and I also think it's *vindicated or discredited* by long-run track records — if on all the days they said 70% it only rained 40%, they were saying something false. Case four: a single particular radium atom, probability 1/2 it decays within its half-life. Here I'm confident the number is in the atom, not in us, and I'd keep believing it even if I could never assemble an ensemble of identical atoms. Case five: the surgeon tells my brother there's a 30% chance his specific operation succeeds. I think this is about my brother, not a reference class — but I also think that if you gathered 1000 patients who got this exact prognosis and 60% did fine, the 30% figure was just wrong. And case six, the one I keep flip-flopping on: the probability that I *would have* caught the train if I'd left five minutes earlier. It feels meaningful to me in conversation, but calling it a probability with a number feels like a category mistake.

So: is there a single account of probability that delivers all of these, or even five of six? The crux, I suspect, is cases one and two — whatever makes covered-coin ignorance legitimate but pi-digit ignorance illegitimate is doing the real work. Give me your best candidate principle (or two rivals, if you think it's genuinely underdetermined), stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case is motivating. Then tell me what it predicts about a case I didn't list: the probability, before we check, that there's microbial life in Europa's ocean. And be honest about which of my verdicts has to go — I've got a sinking feeling it's the surgery one, since I seem to be demanding single-case meaning with frequentist accountability, and maybe that's wanting to have it both ways.
