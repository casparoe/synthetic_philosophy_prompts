You’re a philosopher of action and formal epistemologist. Think carefully, brainstorm the tricky cases first, and only then organise the answer.

I’m trying to work out what an adequate theory of probabilities for one’s own intentional choices would have to do. By that I don’t mean predicting what some other agent will do, or retroactively explaining a choice already made. I mean the credences I can rationally have while I’m still deliberating: “there’s a 40% chance I’ll order the curry”, “I’m 80% likely to keep my promise”, and so on.

Write the desiderata before assessing any theories. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. In particular, I want the list to address:

- the difference between predicting my behaviour and settling what to do;
- how an intention can make an action likely without making the probability self-certifying;
- cases where I genuinely don’t know what I’ll do, including weakness of will, changing my mind, spontaneous action, and acting under pressure;
- why high confidence that I’ll do A shouldn’t automatically count as a reason to do A;
- what happens to the relevant probabilities after deliberation ends but before the action occurs;
- whether probabilities for my own choices can guide decision-making in the way probabilities about external events do;
- what such a theory may permissibly assume about free will, control, transparency of intention, and rational self-knowledge.

Please distinguish requirements that seem genuinely non-negotiable from those that look like the prejudices of a particular camp. Mark any desiderata that are difficult or impossible to satisfy jointly; if the specification contains an inconsistent triad, that is especially useful. I’d also like one requirement you think would eliminate the largest number of plausible approaches, with the reasoning spelt out.

Use a few concrete cases of your own, but include at least one where deliberating changes the probabilities, one where forming an intention makes failure less likely without guaranteeing success, and one where I can predict myself better by not deliberating. Seperate the descriptive question — how agents actually reason — from the normative one — how they should reason.

After the main list, briefly test three broad approaches against it: a simple self-prediction model, a model where intentions ground choice-probabilities, and a two-stage model that treats open deliberation and post-decision expectation differently. Don’t turn this into a bibliography or a survey of named positions; the criteria are the important part.

Also, push back if the request is confused. If there is a category mistake in assigning probabilities to choices still under one’s control, or if “probability of my own action” is ambiguous between several different quantities, say that plainly rather than politely forcing an answer.
