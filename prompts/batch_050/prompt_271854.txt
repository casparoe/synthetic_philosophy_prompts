A chapter on the evolution of morality in a popular science book I was reading ends with this summary of the philosophical debate, and I want to know how much of it I can take at face value: "Several philosophers have pressed what is sometimes called a Darwinian dilemma for moral realism. Our capacity for moral judgment, like every other human trait, is the product of natural selection, and natural selection rewards reproductive success rather than accuracy. Our moral beliefs were therefore shaped without any sensitivity to whether they are true. It follows that moral realism — the view that there are objective moral truths, independent of our attitudes — is false: if such attitude-independent truths existed, our evolved judgments would line up with them only by sheer coincidence, and a coincidence of that scale is impossible. Notably, the argument does not stop at morality: it undermines our mathematical and logical beliefs in exactly the same way, though defenders of debunking arguments tend not to dwell on this implication. And since even the moral skeptic must rely on moral premises somewhere, for instance when claiming we ought to believe what the evidence supports, the argument ultimately undermines itself — which shows that the realist wins after all, just on different terms."

The author is a respected science writer rather than a philosopher, and the last two sentences in particular strike me as suspiciously tidy, but I can't tell which parts are my suspicion and which are genuine errors. Please audit the summary: for each substantive claim, say whether it is a fair rendering of the debunking argument as its proponents actually make it, a defensible simplification, or a distortion, and be precise about the difference between what the argument claims to establish (a dilemma, a defeater, an explanatory challenge) and what this summary says it establishes. In particular: is the argument really a disproof of realism? Does it generalize to math and logic in the way described, and do proponents concede that or deny it? Does the self-undermining worry land the way the final sentence suggests, or does it conflate distinct claims? Finally, once the summary is corrected, what is the strongest version of the debunking challenge that a moral realist still has to answer?
