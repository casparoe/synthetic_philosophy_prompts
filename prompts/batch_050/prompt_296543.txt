So a couple months ago I wrote a student note arguing that certain highly autonomous AI systems -- the kind that operate for long stretches without meaningful human oversight, enter into transactions, and cause harms nobody specifically intended -- should get a narrow, stripped-down form of legal personhood modeled explicitly on the business corporation. My argument is not that these systems are morally like people (I actually think that's a distraction), but that corporate personhood was never about metaphysics either: it's a legal technology, a bundle of procedural conveniences -- the ability to hold assets in a segregated pool, to sue and be sued, to have liabilities stick to the entity -- and we could use the same technology to make AI-caused harms compensable, force the costs onto the people profiting from deployment, and create a single insurable, regulatable point of contact. Think of it as mandatory entity formation for autonomous systems, with a capitalized compensation fund standing where shareholder equity would be.

Somehow this got me invited to present at a faculty workshop at a law school next month, and I am definately out of my depth. The audience: mostly corporate law scholars, the nexus-of-contracts type, who I suspect think AI personhood is hype from people who've never read actual entity law; two torts professors who care about vicarious liability and judgment-proof defendants; one professor who spent years litigating habeas-style personhood petitions for nonhuman animals and lost, and who I imagine has complicated feelings about my framing; and one legal philosopher who keeps asking "but what IS a person" in the comments of everything. There will be no AI hype people in the room to balance this out.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them -- the corporate scholars' version of "who is the entity even for" is going to be very different from the animal law person's version. For each one: what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, plus a note on where the reply is soft. I especially want to know whether my core move -- corporate personhood as pure legal technology, no moral status implied -- actually survives contact with people who know how entity shielding and limited liability have historically been used to diffuse responsibility, since there's a version of that objection where my analogy does the opponents' work for them. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I have a bad feeling the torts people already have it written down.
