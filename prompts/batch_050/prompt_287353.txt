You are a professional philosopher with particular expertise in the ethics of communication — the literature on lying, deception, manipulation, and legitimate persuasion. Think carefully and methodically about the following question before settling on an answer.

Consider two features of the norms governing negotiation and sales in many contemporary market societies. First, bluffing about one's reservation price is widely treated as permissible, even expected: a seller who says she cannot possibly accept less than €50,000, when in fact she would gladly accept €40,000, has done nothing that the law or mainstream business ethics condemns. Yet misrepresenting an objective fact about the goods — telling a buyer that a car has never been in an accident when it has — is fraud, full stop. Second, these same societies regulate high-pressure sales tactics that involve no falsehoods at all: door-to-door and telemarketing sales are often subject to mandatory cooling-off periods during which the buyer may cancel without reason, and aggressive persistence that would be unremarkable in a bazaar can render a contract voidable. So the practice permits some outright falsehoods (about one's own bottom line) while restricting some entirely truthful influence (relentless but accurate persuasion aimed at a captive customer).

What distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion does this pattern of norms presuppose, if it is to be coherent? In answering, please proceed as follows:

1. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate theories that might underwrite the pattern — for example, a view on which the wrong of influence lies in deception about external facts but not about one's own intentions; a view on which the wrong lies in bypassing or subverting the target's capacity for rational deliberation rather than in falsehood as such; and a view on which certain contexts (negotiation, games) involve tacit consent to partial candor. Consider at least one further candidate of your own devising.

2. Reconstruct the implicit theory that best unifies both halves of the practice — the tolerance of reservation-price bluffing and the regulation of truthful high-pressure sales. Pay particular attention to whether any single theory explains both, or whether the practice is in fact stitched together from two independent principles.

3. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of manipulation would license exactly the same rules?

4. Finally, say what would concretely have to change in commercial norms if we replaced the implicit theory with (a) a strict Kantian prohibition on all deliberate misleading, and (b) a purely welfarist view on which influence is wrong only insofar as it predictably leaves the target worse off. Which parts of current practice would each of these force us to abandon, and which would each leave untouched?
