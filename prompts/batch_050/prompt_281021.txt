I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm sketching a long piece on what engineers owe their employers when the employer appears to be making a catastrophic mistake. The case I keep returning to is Roger Boisjoly and the Challenger disaster of January 1986, and I want to run the episode through Josiah Royce's theory of loyalty, set out in his 1908 book The Philosophy of Loyalty, before I commit to using it as the piece's frame. That is where I'd like your help, since my own reading of Royce may be shallow.

Let me stipulate the facts as I understand them, so we don't quarrel over the record. Boisjoly was a seal specialist at Morton Thiokol, the contractor that built the shuttle's solid rocket boosters. On the eve of the launch he and several colleagues argued, in a teleconference with NASA managers, that the cold forecast temperature put the booster O-rings at risk and that the launch should be postponed. Under pressure, senior Thiokol managers overruled the engineers and reversed the company's initial recommendation against launching; one manager was reportedly told to stop reasoning as an engineer and to reason as a manager instead. Challenger broke apart seventy-three seconds after lift-off, killing all seven crew members. Boisjoly then testified frankly to the presidential commission investigating the accident, was frozen out by colleagues and management, took extended sick leave, and spent the rest of his career lecturing on engineering ethics.

Now the theory. Royce, as I read him, holds that loyalty is a person's willing and thoroughgoing devotion to a cause, and that the moral life consists in choosing causes worthy of that devotion. His distinctive move is to judge causes by a further principle, loyalty to loyalty: a cause is good insofar as it sustains and enlarges the loyalty of others, and wicked insofar as it destroys or betrays it. So loyalty to a gang or a predatory firm fails the test not because the devotion is insincere but because the cause feeds on the wreck of other people's causes.

Here is what I want from you. First, map the episode onto Royce's scheme properly. Identify the competing causes each party was serving: the engineers' devotion to flight safety, the managers' devotion to the company's contract and schedule, NASA's devotion to the programme, and the astronauts' implicit reliance on all of them. Second, use Royce's test to say which of these loyalties should have yielded and to what, and in particular whether the instruction to think like a manager rather than an engineer was, on Royce's view, an invitation to change causes or a corruption of the original one. Third, take the hardest question for the theory: Boisjoly's testimony was experienced inside Thiokol as a betrayal, and Royce's framework seems to let both the whistleblower and the company describe the other as the betrayer of a cause. Does loyalty to loyalty actually adjudicate between them, or does it merely redescribe the stalemate in a loftier idiom? Fourth, tell me what the episode shows about the theory in return: whether the case confirms Royce, exposes a gap in him (say, his relative silence about divided loyalty within a single person, or about institutions that punish the loyal), or reveals that his account was never built for conflicts between an employer and a public. I would rather you said plainly where Royce has nothing to say than stretch him to cover every feature of the case.

Please write in British English, in around 1,200 to 1,500 words, and assume an intelligent reader who knows the Challenger story in outline but has never heard of Royce.
