After forty years in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have spent my retirement working through game theory and decision theory, and I keep returning to the notion of acausal trade: agents who can never meet, signal, or verify one another cooperating anyway, on the grounds that each can reason about how the other's reasoning must go. Coming from a discipline where coordination required shared apparatus and a good deal of shouting across the lab bench, what strikes me is how quickly discussants slide from a narrow technical claim about symmetric reasoning into something resembling ordinary social obligation — keeping one's word to civilizations that may not exist, dealing fairly with counterparties who can never check. I would like to see that slide made vivid rather than argued about, and satire seems the right instrument.

Write me a parody etiquette handbook in the deadpan manner of a nineteenth-century manners manual, titled something like "The Counterfactual Courtier: A Gentleman's Guide to Dealing with Beings Who Cannot Observe You," presented as the work of an invented figure — a retired Undersecretary for Unverifiable Affairs, say, or a self-appointed doyen of counterfactual society. Around 600–700 words. Structure it as exactly five numbered rules, each consisting of a short stated maxim followed by a paragraph of advice; no introduction, no conclusion. The humor must come from getting the decision theory right — symmetric reasoning, Newcomb-style problems, the temptation to defect when the counterparty almost certainly is not there, the question of whether a sufficiently vivid simulation deserves a seat at dinner — so that a reader who knows the underlying literature laughs rather than winces. Include at least one rule whose advice is technically impeccable and socially monstrous.

Finally, I would like the satire to commit. Rather than spraying mockery indiscriminately at acausal trade, its critics, and its enthusiasts all at once, choose one definite target — I suggest the overconfident extension of symmetric reasoning into everyday ethics, but select another if you judge it riper — and let all five rules press that target toward its reductio. Keep the irony deadpan yet legible: by the third rule a reader should be able to tell precisely which move is being mocked, and by the fifth they should feel, faintly and uncomfortably, the pull of the view anyway.
