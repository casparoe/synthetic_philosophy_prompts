After a long argument with a friend about whether constitutional monarchies (the UK, the Scandinavian countries, Japan) serve any real function in a democracy, my friend rested his case on an analogy he thought settled it: a constitutional monarch is like a backup generator in a hospital — inert and fuel-consuming in normal times, but the whole point is that it supplies power automatically during the rare failure of the main supply, and its mere existence disciplines everyone into keeping the primary system honest. I countered that the monarchy is better compared to an ornamental cannon on the town square: it impresses tourists and symbolizes defense, but it hasn't fired in living memory, nobody is sure it still works, and the day someone actually wheels it out in a political crisis it may blow up in the defenders' faces (think of the 1975 Australian dismissal as a warning shot, even though Australia isn't one of our countries).

Audit both analogies. State explicitly what each element of the source is supposed to map onto in the target (reserve power, automatic activation, upkeep costs, deterrent effect, and so on), identify where each mapping breaks — in particular, whether a hereditary monarch can "activate" without discretion of a kind a generator lacks, whether the monarchy's continuity and legitimacy functions have any analogue in either source object, and whether the cannon's "never fired" status is a bug or actually evidence the deterrent works — and say which features each analogy quietly imports to make the monarch look either safer or more useless than the institution warrants. Conclude with a verdict on whether either analogy can support the conclusions we drew from them, and propose a better one if neither survives.

Present your audit as a table with the following columns: Element (the feature of the source object), Generator mapping (what it corresponds to in a constitutional monarchy, or "no analogue"), Cannon mapping (same), Breaks where (the key disanalogy for that row, if any), and Matters for the argument (yes/no, with a one-sentence reason). Follow the table with a short paragraph giving your final verdict and, if applicable, your replacement analogy.
