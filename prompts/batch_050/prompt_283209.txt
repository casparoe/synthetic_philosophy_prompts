I'm one of five elected directors of the residents' management company for our block of flats (30 flats, a mix of owner-occupiers and rentals), and I need help drafting the written opinion I'll circulate before next month's board meeting. Some background: at this year's AGM, with quorum met and proper notice given, the members voted 19 to 8 to convert the ground-floor cycle store into a small gym, and separately to allocate the six basement parking spaces by length of ownership. Procedurally it's all valid, I checked the articles twice. The wrinkle is who wins and who loses: the cycle store is used almost entirely by people living in the eight rented flats, who aren't members of the company and so had no vote at all, and their landlords mostly didn't turn up or send proxies. Three of the cyclists have now written a fairly furious letter asking the board not to implement the resolution. The gym faction's position is basically: a properly passed resolution is binding, full stop, and if the board starts unpicking AGM votes whenever the losers complain loudly enough, no decision will ever stick and there's no point holding AGMs in the first place. Honestly I keep going back and forth on it.

Part of the wobble is that I was moaning about this to a friend who's doing a PhD in political theory, and she said our row is basically Pascal's old problem about justice and might, and pointed me to this fragment from the Pensées (no. 294 in the usual numbering, in W. F. Trotter's translation):

"Nothing, according to reason alone, is just itself; all changes with time. Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it."

and, a little further on in the same fragment:

"We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end."

I'd like you to act as a practical philosopher with some experience of advising committees and boards — think this through properly rather than reaching for the nearest platitude. First give me a quick brainstorm of the main considerations on each side (bullet points are fine), then write the opinion itself, roughly 600–800 words, in British English please. It needs a clear bottom-line recommendation (implement the resolution as passed / refer it back to an extraordinary general meeting / implement with some mitigation — your call, but commit to one), a concrete timetable, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, whichever way you come down. The reasoning should genuinely work through the Pascal passage rather than just gesturing at it: is "accepted, therefore authoritative" an adequate foundation for this particular decision, given that the people bearing the cost never got the chance to accept or reject anything? Or is this one of those cases where carrying a rule back to first priciples is worth the havoc Pascal warns it causes? Bear in mind the memo will be read by the other directors, who are not philosophy people at all, so keep the prose plain — Pascal should shape the argument without swamping it, though I'd like a line or two making the connection explicit so they can see where I'm coming from. The quoted passages don't count towards the word limit.
