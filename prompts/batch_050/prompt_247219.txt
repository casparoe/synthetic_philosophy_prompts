I serve on the ethics committee of a hospital system evaluating an intensive-care allocation platform. It estimates patients’ likely benefit from treatment, incorporates committee-approved triage rules, and can be deployed in three ways: as an advisory display, as a filter that removes some options before a physician chooses, or as an autonomous allocator that provisionally assigns beds subject to later human review. During our deliberations, one member said, “A machine should never decide who lives and dies,” and treated that sentence as a reason to reject all three deployment modes. Others thought it condemned only the autonomous mode; still others thought the problem was not computation itself but the absence of a human being who genuinely understands, endorses, and can be answerable for each consequential choice.

Treat the slogan as an ambiguous piece of language rather than as an argument. Lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making, including at least claims about computational selection, causal influence, delegated authority, effective human control, moral responsibility, expressive respect for patients, and the encoding of contested value judgments. For each reading, explain what feature of a machine-ethics implementation would violate it, distinguishing an advisory model from a filtering model and from autonomous allocation. Also indicate whether the reading is nearly trivial, substantive and defensible, or implausibly strong—for example, whether it would rule out ordinary prognostic calculators, human-in-the-loop systems whose approvals are predictable rubber stamps, or systems that merely make ethically relevant patterns easier to see. Finally, say which readings would actually support rejecting each of the three proposed deployments, and identify the strongest version of the underlying concern without pretending that the slogan alone settles the design question.
