I teach history and philosophy of science to undergraduates, and something about two examples my biology colleagues use with complete confidence has been bothering me. Periodical cicadas emerge from the ground on 13- and 17-year cycles, and the standard explanation is that because these numbers are prime, predators whose own populations cycle on shorter periods can rarely synchronize with an emergence; the primality of the numbers is itself said to explain the life cycle. Honeybees build hexagonal cells, and the standard explanation is that among the ways of partitioning a plane into equal cells, the hexagonal one provably requires the least material per unit of storage; the theorem is said to explain the architecture. In both cases, a fact of arithmetic or geometry appears to be doing explanatory work over and above any physical mechanism. My question is what the routine scientific acceptance of such explanations presupposes about mathematics. Does it presuppose that mathematical objects exist and genuinely figure in the explanation of physical events, or only that mathematical reasoning is a representational tool we currently cannot paraphrase away? I have seen the first reading presented as an argument for mathematical realism and the second as compatible with treating mathematics as a useful fiction, and I would like to know which, if either, the practice actually commits us to. If the practice is compatible with several positions, say which, and where it is genuinely underdetermined; and if mathematics is best understood nominalistically, would the cicada and honeycomb explanations have to be withdrawn or merely re-described? Finally, if my way of setting the question up already smuggles in a confused premise, about what an explanation is, say, I would rather be told that plainly than be given a tidy answer to a bad question. Please reply in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
