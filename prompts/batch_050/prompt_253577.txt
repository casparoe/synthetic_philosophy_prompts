So I'm a retired chemist (40 years, mostly materials science) and I've recently gotten way into philosophy, which has been alot of fun honestly. A group of us retirees meets at the library every other Tuesday -- about 12 people, mix of backgrounds, mostly engineers and one former accountant who loves arguing. We've been doing little activities I design because nobody wants to just sit and be lectured at, least of all by me.

Next session I want to tackle the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, which as I understand it are cousins: in a big lottery it's reasonable to believe of each ticket that it will lose, but not reasonable to believe they ALL lose; and an author can honestly believe every single claim in her book while also believing the book contains at least one mistake. What fascinates me as a lab person is that this feels like a precision problem -- each individual measurement is fine, the aggregate is where it breaks.

Here's what I want: design a 90-minute activity where the group runs headfirst into this themselves before anyone names it. Something where they each make a bunch of individual judgments they're genuinely confident in (could involve dice, cards, trivia about their own lives, whatever works), put some small stakes on them -- we have a running point system and the winner gets to pick next session's topic, so points actually matter to people -- and then the group as a whole ends up committed to something they can see is contradictory, with their own signatures on it. I want that moment of "wait, we each said this, but together we've said THAT."

Please include: the exact setup and rules, what materials I need to buy or make (budget is basically nothing, it's a library meeting room), a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the two paradoxes and explains how they differ from each other, because I keep half-confusing them. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with this particular crowd (someone WILL try to game the point system, I guarantee it) and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the game can't capture and I'll just have to explain in words.

One constraint: no lecture at the start. They should not hear the words "lottery paradox" until after the contradiction is on the table and they've felt it.
