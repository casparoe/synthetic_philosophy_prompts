Since retiring from a thirty-year career in population ecology, I've been working my way through the philosophical literature on measurement and social progress, and I've become entangled in an argument that grew out of a dinner-table dispute about whether the world is becoming less violent. My instinct as a scientist is that long-run declines in homicide, warfare deaths, and sanctioned cruelty are among the best-established trends in the human sciences; my interlocutor pressed an argument that seems to show the question itself is unanswerable, and I can't locate where it fails. Here it is, as I've reconstructed it. (1) A claim that violence has declined is meaningful only if there exists some measure of violence that can be applied uniformly to the eras being compared. (2) The extension of the term "violence" has grown over time precisely as tolerated levels of harm have fallen: conduct once unremarkable -- flogging schoolchildren, marital coercion, workplace bullying, the treatment of animals -- enters the category as a society becomes more sensitive, so the measuring stick lengthens as the thing measured shrinks. (3) A measure whose scope covaries with the level it is supposed to measure cannot deliver era-independent readings; any apparent decline (or stasis) under such a measure is an artifact of the shifting category rather than a fact about the world. (4) The only escape is to fix a single definition and apply it across all eras. (5) But any fixed definition either includes things now counted as violence that past societies never recorded (so the historical data do not exist) or excludes them (so it no longer measures what we now mean by violence). (6) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether violence has declined.

The conclusion strikes me as proving too much, because a parallel argument would seem to show that there is no fact of the matter about whether literacy, infant mortality, or poverty has declined either -- definitions of all three have shifted with rising expectations -- and yet I take those trends to be as real as anything in the social sciences. But I should say where my sympathies lie: I'm reluctant to abandon (2), since the expansion of the category seems to me genuinely well documented, and I'm equally reluctant to abandon the meaningfulness of claims about declining literacy or mortality. If forced, I would sooner accept some version of (6) than give up the idea that measurement-dependent concepts can still support objective trend claims. Which step should I reject, what did that step get right that made it seem plausible, and what exactly do I pay for rejecting it -- in particular, does the repair require me to say that "violence declined" is elliptical for a family of definition-relative claims, and if so, is that a retreat or just honesty?
