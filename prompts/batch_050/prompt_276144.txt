I am a novelist at work on my fifth book, and I have run into an accusation I cannot quite answer. My narrator conceals from the reader, for some two hundred pages, a fact the narrator has known all along; when the fact surfaces, the reader is meant to feel the same jolt of betrayal the protagonist feels. It works — an early reader wrote to say she put the book down and walked around the block — but she also called the device manipulative, and she did not mean it entirely as praise. I replied that steering a reader's feelings is simply what the craft is: pacing, withholding, timing, the placement of a single image so that it lands later like a blow. If that is manipulation, then every competent storyteller is a manipulator, and the word has lost its teeth. She replied that I had merely described my methods, not defended them.

In casting about for a way to think about this, I came back to Aristotle's Rhetoric, where he writes: "The Emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure. Such are anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites." He adds, a few lines earlier, that the orator's task includes putting his hearers "into the right frame of mind," and he then spends the better part of a book cataloguing how, in practice, to arouse anger, pity, and fear in an audience on purpose.

Please take this passage seriously as an account of what I do, and press on it. Three questions. First, on Aristotle's definition, the whole point of working on an audience's emotions is to change their judgements — so does his framework contain the resources to distinguish my storytelling from manipulation at all, or does it treat every successful appeal to feeling as simply persuasion done well? Second, if the distinction must come from somewhere Aristotle leaves implicit — say, from whether the judgement produced is warranted, whether the audience consented to the treatment, or whether the persuader's ends are ones the audience would share — which of these candidates best fits my case, where the reader has, after all, bought a ticket to precisely this ride, but has not consented to each particular turn? Third, does the novelist's situation differ in any morally relevant way from the orator's, given that my reader's judgements are changed about fictional people, while Aristotle's hearers are being steered toward real verdicts and votes? I would rather have a principled answer I dislike than a comfortable one that dissolves on inspection.
