A friend who reads a lot of population ethics cornered me with this argument and I can't find the step I actually want to reject, but the conclusion can't be right. Can you help?

(1) What makes a life or a world good is nothing over and above the absence of suffering and unfulfilled craving -- pleasure only matters because it removes or prevents a bad, never because it adds a good on its own.
(2) A state in which nobody exists contains no suffering and no unfulfilled craving.
(3) So by (1), a world with no sentient beings is not missing anything of value; nothing bad or deficient about it.
(4) Any world with sentient beings contains at least some suffering or frustrated craving that didn't need to exist.
(5) So every populated world is worse than the empty world, and creating new beings is always at least a little bit bad.
(6) So the morally best trajectory is the quiet extinction of all sentient life, and we should hope for it.

He calls this "tranquilism with the courage of its convictions." I want to keep (1)-ish intuitions -- the asymmetry between relieving pain and adding pleasure really does seem right to me -- but (6) feels like a reductio. My best guess is something fishy happens around (3), like "nothing is missing" sliding into "this is a good state," but I can't pin it down. Which step do I reject, what does rejecting it cost me, and is there a version of the view that keeps (1) and (2) but blocks (6)?

One thing I'd ask: be honest about what's actually settled here vs. what's live disagreement among people who work on this -- e.g., I have the impression some philosophers just accept (6), so don't present my reductio reaction as the consensus if it isn't.

Format-wise, could you structure the answer as: Diagnosis (the step you'd reject and why it looked fine, ~2 paragraphs), The costs (what I give up by rejecting it, ~1 short paragraph), Ways out (positions that keep my preferred premises, ~1-2 paragraphs), and Verdict (a few sentences on how strong the argument actually is).
