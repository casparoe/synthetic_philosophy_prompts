So this has been bugging me. On a recent thread (won't say which forum, but the usual suspects) someone dropped this summary of Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, in the middle of a discussion about introspection and calibration:

"Williamson showed that the KK principle — if you know something, you know that you know it — is false. His argument: knowledge requires a margin for error, meaning you only count as knowing p if you couldn't easily have been wrong in a very similar situation. But our powers of discrimination are limited, so the margin is always finite, and each time you iterate knowledge — knowing that you know that you know — you eat up more of the margin until it runs out. So basically nothing is luminous: you can be in a mental state, like feeling cold, without knowing you're in it. This kills the Cartesian idea that the mind is transparent to itself, and it means we should be generally skeptical of introspection — your sense of what you believe or feel is no more trustworthy than your perception of the outside world."

Something about that last part felt like a leap to me — it sounds suspiciously like it licenses the "introspection is basically useless, trust your priors not your feelings" move that people on these blogs love to make — but I can't tell whether the arugment actually goes there or whether the commenter is freelancing. So: which parts of this would an epistemologist sign off on, which are the usual pop-philosophy flattening, and which (if any) are just wrong? Specifically: does the skeptical conclusion about introspection actually follow from the anti-luminosity argument, or is that a separate claim being smuggled in under Williamson's authority? And is the "margin runs out" picture actually how the sorites-style reasoning works, or is that a cartoon version? I'm inclined to think the core argument is solid and the gloss on KK is fine, but I'd rather be told if that's the naive reading.
