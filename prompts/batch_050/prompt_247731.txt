So I've been reading rationalist blogs for years and I've internalized all the greatest hits -- Aumann agreement, prediction markets as epistemic machines, "if someone offers you a bet, update on the fact that they offered," the whole Milgrom-Stokey no-trade thing. Recently I tried to actually write out the argument as I understood it, and I think I've talked myself into a corner where it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. Here's the argument. (1) A rational, self-interested trader only offers a trade they expect to benefit from given their information. (2) So when someone offers to trade with me at price p, the offer itself is evidence that their information favors their side of the deal. (3) If I update on that evidence, my posterior moves toward the position implied by their offer, which eats away at whatever edge I thought I had. (4) If we're both rational and we both know it, this updating iterates -- my willingness to trade back is evidence for them, theirs for me -- until our beliefs converge and neither of us wants to trade at all. (5) So between rational agents with a common prior, the only possible motive for trade is information, and information-based trade always unwinds: no trade ever happens. (6) But that can't be right, because it would mean the only reason real markets have any volume is that traders are irrational or haven't thought about (1)-(4), which seems like a wildly implausible thing to say about, like, all of finance. (7) Therefore something in (1)-(5) is wrong.

The annoying part is every individual step feels like a theorem I read on the internet backed by an actual theorem somewhere. I suspect something goes wrong around (2) or (4) -- maybe "they expect to benefit" doesn't mean what the argument needs it to mean, or maybe the iteration doesn't actually force convergence the way I gestured at. But I've also had smart people tell me (5) is literally correct as stated and the mistake is in treating (6) as a reductio rather than as an approximately true description. Which step do you think I should reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give something up I'd rather sacrifice the clean common-prior assumption than the idea that offers are evidence, but I want to know whether that's actually where the pressure is. Bonus points if you can explain why insurance and hedging do or don't rescue the argument, since "people trade for reasons other than information" feels like it should be the obvious out and yet everyone assures me the theorem already priced that in.
