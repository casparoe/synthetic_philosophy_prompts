I live in a country whose government has just announced another year of record-low births. The fertility rate sits far below replacement level, and the evening news now alternates between segments on shuttered rural schools and segments on how to fund pensions with a shrinking workforce. Growing up, I was taught that Thomas Malthus was the canonical cautionary tale of a forecaster who got it wrong: population did not outrun food supply, and the misery he seemed to predict never arrived on schedule. Now I keep hearing commentators say the opposite fear — too few people — is the real demographic story of this century, while others insist that climate change, soil depletion, or some other constraint will vindicate the old Malthusian logic in the end.

I would like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can, how Malthus himself might respond to our situation. Specifically:

1. Fertility collapse as vindication? In later editions of his work he placed his hopes in what he termed moral restraint — people deliberately postponing marriage and limiting family size. Would he regard today's voluntary low fertility (driven by housing costs, careers, contraception, and shifting norms rather than by the prudence he envisioned) as his preventive check operating at unprecedented scale, or as something his framework simply has no room for?

2. "Right but early." Defenders of failed long-run predictions often say the theory identified a real mechanism and merely mistimed it. Would Malthus accept that defense of his own theory? And if he extended the same charity to today's forecasts of resource and climate limits, on what grounds could he distinguish a prediction that is right-but-early from one that has simply been rescued ad hoc every time its deadline passed?

3. The demographic transition as rescue or refutation. The modern account holds that rising incomes first raise and then depress fertility — a feedback loop his geometric-versus-arithmetic framing did not anticipate. Would he treat this as a friendly amendment to his principle of population, or as evidence that the principle itself operated at the wrong level of description?

Please reason from his actual arguments and intellectual temperament — including the differences between his early, harsher formulations and his later revisions — rather than from the cartoon version of Malthus that circulates in op-eds. Where you think he would surprise us, say so: for instance, if a man of his views might side with today's techno-optimists on some points, or dismiss the current underpopulation panic as a failure to think at his preferred timescale.
