So here's a position I keep circling back to for a paper, and I want the strongest case for it before I decide whether I actually believe it. The standard line in philosophy of physics is that symmetries explain: via Noether's theorem, conservation of momentum is explained by the translation invariance of the laws, energy conservation by time-translation invariance, and so on. Fine. But suppose — and plenty of people in quantum gravity take this seriously — that at the fundamental level the world isn't a smooth continuum at all but something discrete (a lattice, a causal set, spin networks, pick your poison; the details dont matter for the argument). In that world, continuous symmetries aren't fundamental features of reality. They're emergent approximations that only show up once you coarse-grain. But then at the fundamental level there's no continuous symmetry doing any explanatory work — yet the approximate conservation laws at emergent scales still hold, because the underlying discrete dynamics happen to be compatible with them. Please argue that in such a discrete-fundament world, the direction of explanation between symmetries and conservation laws reverses: the fundamental dynamics explain why the approximate symmetries show up at coarse-grained scales, rather than the symmetries explaining the conservation laws. In particular, argue that this makes symmetry explanation scale-relative and hostage to facts about the fundamental level — what looks like a deep modal fact (the laws couldn't be otherwise, given the symmetry) is really an artifact of where you're standing. And defend the inversion against the obvious objection that an emergent symmetry can still explain within an effective theory even if it isn't fundamental.
