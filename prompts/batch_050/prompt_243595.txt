I'm a philosophy PhD student and my office mate cornered me with this argument last week and it's been bugging me since. It starts from Carroll's tortoise (the 1895 "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" thing, where Achilles keeps adding the missing premise and the tortoise keeps refusing to draw the inference) and he thinks it proves way more than anyone admits:

(1) A rule of inference is justified only if there is a good argument whose conclusion is that the rule is truth-preserving.
(2) Any argument has to employ some rule of inference to get from premises to conclusion.
(3) An argument for rule R that employs R is circular, and an argument that employs some other rule R' just pushes the same demand onto R'.
(4) Circular justifications and infinite regresses justify nothing.
(5) So no rule of inference is justified, modus ponens included.
(6) He then concedes this sounds bad but says Bayesian epistemology escapes: we don't need to justify deductive rules, we just conditionalize on evidence, and there are Dutch book arguments that conditionalizing is the only coherent policy.
(7) But the Dutch book theorem is itself a deductive proof whose steps run on modus ponens, so the regress reappears one level up.
(8) Conclusion: literally nothing we count as reasoning is justified, and the tortoise wins.

What gets me is that (8) seems to prove too much -- by his lights his own argument for (8) isn't justified either, which feels self-defeating, but he says that's fine, he never claimed justification. If I have to give something up I'd rather it not be (4), since letting circular justifications count seems like it would vindicate anything. So: which step do I actually reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? And if you think the whole setup is confused somehow -- e.g. I've misdescribed what the tortoise argument is even about, or the question as posed can't be answered cleanly -- just say that instead of playing along.
