So there's this popular book on aesthetics I read (I won't name it, it's a general intro type thing) that summarizes Hume's essay on the standard of taste like this:

"Hume starts out agreeing with the skeptics: beauty is not a property of objects at all, it exists only in the feeling of the person perceiving them, the way warmth or sweetness isn't really 'in' the thing. But then he pulls a reversal. Since we obviously treat some critics as more reliable than others, he goes looking for the true standard of taste and finds it in the verdicts of ideal critics — people with delicate senses, long practice, wide experience of comparing works, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Once such critics agree, their verdict is the standard, and anyone who disagrees with it is simply mistaken about what is beautiful, the same way someone can be mistaken about a plain matter of fact. So Hume ends up defending aesthetic objectivity after all: the standard of taste is fixed by expert consensus, which is exactly why he expects Homer to keep pleasing every age and every nation."

My gut says the first half is basically fine and the second half is where the squeeze happens — like the book wants Hume to come out as an objectivist because that's a tidier story. But I don't know the essay well enough to be sure. Can you audit it properly? Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a Hume scholar would sign off on, which parts are a defensible simplification, and which parts misrepresent what Hume actually says or carefully avoids saying. I'm especially suspicious of two moves: the slide from "some critics are more reliable" to "disagreeing with them is being factually wrong," and the bit where expert consensus supposedly settles everything, since I vaguely remember Hume making allowances for differences he thought nobody could or should correct. Also: the book later uses this summary to argue that Hume gives us a method for ranking artworks by polling the best critics and publishing league tables. Does that follow from anything Hume actually committed to, once the summary is corrected?
