As a novelist, I keep constructing plots around characters faced with minuscule chances of enormous outcomes, and I find my sympathies toward them follow a pattern I cannot articulate; I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies it. Here are my verdicts. A man who spends a few dollars a week on a lottery ticket with a one-in-a-hundred-million chance of a fortune: foolish, but sane. A woman who devotes her career to guarding against a catastrophe she herself rates at one chance in a million per decade, because the death toll would be in the billions: admirable, even heroic. A traveler who refuses to hand twenty dollars to a stranger who claims, on no evidence, the power to inflict suffering on trillions of minds unless paid: clearly right to refuse, even though if the stranger's claim had any probability at all, the stakes at issue dwarf the twenty dollars. An heiress who signs away everything she owns for a one-in-a-billion chance at immortality: mad. What troubles me is that the second and third cases look structurally identical — tiny probability, vast stakes — yet my verdicts differ, and I cannot tell whether the difference lies in how the probability estimate was arrived at, in who stands to benefit, or in something else entirely. What principle separates the lottery player from the catastrophe-guardian, the traveler, and the heiress? Please state it precisely, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a general who accepts a one-in-ten-thousand risk of losing his entire army in exchange for a real chance of a decisive, war-ending victory.
