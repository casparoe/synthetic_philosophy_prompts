At a new years party a friend of ours ran the old two-envelope thing with real money: two envelopes, one containing twice as much cash as the other, you pick one and then get the option to swap before opening. Afterwards my friends Priya and Jonas got into a genuine arguement about it and I'd like a referee.

(1) Priya opened her envelope, saw $80, and reasoned: the other one has either $40 or $160, there's no reason to favor one over the other, so it's 50/50, expected value $100, wich beats the $80 in hand — swap. She added that since this works no matter what number you see, you should commit to swapping before even opening, a free 25% boost in expectation.

(2) Jonas said that last step has to be broken: the setup is symmetric, your envelope and the other are interchangeable, so nobody can gain on average by swapping — if Priya's free 25% worked, whoever ended up with the other envelope could run the same argument and also gain 25%, which is impossible. So the 50/50 assumption must be wrong somewhere.

(3) Priya granted that the pre-opening version is fishy but held her ground on the post-opening one: once you've actually seen $80 you have information you didn't have before, and conditional on that information the swap is worth +$20 in expectation, so a rational agent holding an open $80 envelope should still swap. The symmetry argument, she said, only kills the blind version.

(4) Jonas replied that seeing $80 changes nothing unless you know how the host chose the amounts. Whether $160 or $40 is more likely depends entirely on the host's distribution over envelope sizes, which Priya knows nothing about — for all she knows the host only ever makes pairs like ($20, $40) and ($160, $320), in wich case an $80 envelope couldn't even happen, or the host caps amounts at $100, in which case $160 is impossible. Without a prior there is no well-defined expected value, and once you do pick any proper prior, the unconditional expected gain from swapping is exactly zero, so her +$20 was never earned.

(5) Priya's closing move: fine, but then the paradox is just the generic point that expectations need priors — and yet there are perfectly proper probability distributions for the host where, conditional on every possible amount you might see, swapping has positive expected value, so something genuinely weird survives Jonas's fix, and it has something to do with the expected amount in each envelope being infinite. Jonas said that sounded like cheating with infinities and they dropped it.

Who's right here? Go move by move, rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short justification, and then tell me what both of them are missing, if anything.
