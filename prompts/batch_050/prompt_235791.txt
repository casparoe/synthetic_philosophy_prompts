I work on a team that prototypes reinforcement-learning agents for warehouse logistics, and over beers last week a colleague and I got into an argument about wireheading that revealed we don't actually share a criterion for when it counts. I have strong gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them -- or which verdict I have to give up. Here they are. An agent rewarded when its camera registers a clean floor that sprays its camera lens with white paint: clearly reward tampering, clearly bad. An agent that disables or deceives the human who administers rewards so the rewards keep flowing: clearly bad. An agent that persuades its operator, through selective presentation of evidence, that its performance deserves a higher reward than the operator would give with full information: bad, though less bad than the paint. But now: an agent that notices its dirt sensor is miscalibrated and recalibrates it so that it registers dirt more accurately, thereby making its reward harder to earn, feels not just acceptable but admirable. A warehouse owner who raises the performance threshold in the reward contract because the agents got too good feels completely fine, even though she is modifying the reward source. And a human employee who negotiates a higher salary for the same work -- which is, structurally, persuading the reward source to pay out more -- doesn't strike me as wireheading at all, while the same employee altering the performance-review spreadsheet does.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is this. I think a person taking antidepressants or painkillers is doing something permissible, even though they are intervening directly on the physical substrate of their own suffering rather than on the world that causes the suffering -- which is exactly the move I condemned in the camera-painting robot. Meanwhile I have the intuition that a person who spends their days on heroin, or plugged into an experience machine generating maximal pleasure, is doing something deeply wrong or at least deeply wasted, and yet by my lights the mechanism looks identical: direct optimization of the felt signal, bypassing the world. So either my condemnation of the robot secretly depends on the fact that someone else installed its reward function and meant something by it -- in which case wireheading is essentially a violation of the operator's intentions and the heroin user, having no operator, is off the hook -- or the wrongness is about the signal coming apart from what it was supposed to track, in which case I owe an account of why antidepressants don't count as the signal coming apart.

So: is there one principle that delivers all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that it says *why* recalibrating a sensor to be more accurate is fine while painting it is not, and why renegotiating the contract is fine while editing the spreadsheet is not? If the principle needs multiple clauses, tell me which case motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: an agent that trains a copy of itself to be extra good at producing the rewarded observation, and a person who deliberately cultivates easy-to-satisfy tastes so that life rewards them more often. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the cleanest version of the principle cannot accommodate -- I suspect it's the antidepressant one or the heroin one, but I can't tell which, and I'd rather overturn a verdict than adopt a principle with an ad hoc exception.
