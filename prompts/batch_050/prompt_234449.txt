I'm a PhD student in sociology (second year, mostly doing demography stuff) and two people in my cohort got into a long argument at the department happy hour last week about whether "generations" -- Millennial, Gen Z, Boomer, etc. -- refer to anything real. I kept out of it but I've been turning it over since and can't settle it. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Dani's opening move: generational labels are arbitrary bins. A "generation" is just a 15ish-year slice of birth years, and you could draw the boundaries anywhere else and get an equally defensible category. The labels are media folklore that we've reified, not things in the world.

(2) Marcus pushed back: the labels track shared formative conditions. People who came of age during the 2008 crash, or who got smartphones at 13 vs 25, genuinely have correlated traits and behaviors because of that. That's causal structure, not folklore -- similar to how "economic class" is real even though income is a continuum.

(3) Dani: even granting the correlations, the boundaries can't be carving anything. Someone born December 1994 and someone born January 1995 had nearly identical lives, but the labels put them in different generations. Real categories don't flip membership on a one-day difference. Generations are like being in the top 10% of height -- the percentile is real, but "the tall" isn't a kind of person.

(4) Marcus: lots of real kinds have fuzzy boundaries. Species boundaries are a mess (ring species, hybridization) and species are about as real as it gets. What matters is whether the category supports explanation and prediction, and generational cohorts do -- delayed homeownership, marriage timing, job-hopping all track cohort.

(5) Dani's counter, which I thought was his strongest: those patterns are confounded. Most "generational differences" are just age effects (25-year-olds always job-hop) or period effects (everyone's behavior changed in 2020, not just Gen Z). Once you try to statistically separate age, period, and cohort effects, the pure cohort term gets really weak, and there's an identification problem in doing the separation at all. So "generation" is mostly an age effect wearing a costume.

(6) Marcus's last move was sort of a retreat: fine, maybe generations aren't natural kinds, but money isn't a natural kind either and it's obviously real. A category can be socially constructed and still do real work -- institutions and markets respond to these labels, which itself creates cohort effects.

They both left thinking they'd won. Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or right-with-caveats, and why. Also: is there anything both of them are missing -- some distinction that would dissolve part of the disagreement, or some point where they're talking past each other? And if you had to say who's more right overall, who would it be?
