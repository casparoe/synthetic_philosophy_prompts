I serve on the ethics review board of a public health agency, and roughly once a month we must rule on proposals that raise what philosophers call the nonidentity problem: cases where the choice being evaluated does not merely affect how well some future people fare, but partly determines which future people will exist at all. Our board members are clinicians, economists, and community representatives, not philosophers, and our rulings keep collapsing into two bad habits: either someone declares that "you can't harm a person who would otherwise never have existed" and the complaint is dismissed outright, or someone insists that any avoidable suffering is decisive and the proposal dies regardless of its benefits. Neither habit survives contact with the actual docket.

Please write a decision procedure the board can actually run in a two-hour meeting. It should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions, with a statement of what counts as an adequate answer to each, an explicit indication of the points at which an inadequate answer ends the review with a rejection, and an explicit indication of the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the question to a values vote, because it is not the kind of question further factual investigation can settle. The procedure needs to handle at least the following distinctions, translated into language a non-philosopher can apply reliably: (1) choices that are bad for a determinate future person versus choices that merely lead to a worse-off person existing rather than a different better-off person; (2) cases where the very same individual could have existed better-off versus cases where any alternative would have produced a numerically different individual; (3) outcomes that are worth avoiding even when no existing or future identifiable person is wronged by them; and (4) the difference between a life so bad that its creation is objectionable on any view and a life that is flawed but clearly worth living, where the complaint is comparative rather than absolute. Where the philosophical literature is genuinely unsettled — for instance, whether one should reason in person-affecting terms or impersonal terms, or how to treat the intuition that some nonidentity choices are wrong even though no one is harmed — the procedure should say so plainly, tell us which way to default pending a values vote, and justify that default in terms of the costs of error rather than pretending the theory is settled.

Then run the procedure on three proposals of the kind we actually see, showing the answer it gives at each step:

1. A fertility clinic proposes offering prospective parents a choice between two embryos, where the selected embryo carries a gene predicting moderate chronic pain in adulthood, and the alternative is simply to implant the other embryo — no one is cured, but a different child is born.
2. An industrial policy will impose substantial costs on people alive today in exchange for preventing a slow environmental degradation that would otherwise, by changing migration and family formation over decades, result in an entirely different population existing a century from now — a population that, under the no-action branch, would still have lives worth living, just materially worse ones.
3. A public campaign proposes urging couples in a poor region to delay childbearing by two years, because children born later would, due to a scheduled infrastructure program, be born into significantly better conditions; the children who would have been born in the interim would, of course, never exist.

At least one of these runs should reach a conclusion the board will find uncomfortable, and the write-up should say explicitly that this is the procedure working as designed rather than a defect to be patched. Finally, include a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the recurring proposal shapes on which it will misfire or produce no determinate answer, and the kind of case in which the board should suspend the checklist and deliberate from scratch.
