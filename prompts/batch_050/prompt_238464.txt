I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and much of my recent work leans on a particular device. When I want readers to grasp the stakes of, say, autonomous vehicles or gene editing, I open with a carefully constructed hypothetical: a vivid, concrete scenario in which the reader must imagine themselves making a choice, followed by an invitation to examine what their reaction to that choice reveals. These openings reliably draw readers in, and I find that my own thinking clarifies when I build them. I have always assumed this is simply good expository craft.

A colleague whose judgment I respect recently told me I should abandon the device entirely. Her objection: these scenarios are engineered to produce a foreordained verdict. The writer selects every detail the reader sees — which facts are salient, which options feel available, whose suffering is described and whose is left abstract — so the reader's reaction reveals nothing about the world, only about the writer's narrative skill. Worse, she argues, the reaction feels like independent evidence because the reader arrives at it 'themselves', which makes the device more deceptive, not less, than an openly argued polemic. In her view, the scenarios are a way of laundering a conclusion the writer already holds through the reader's own moral machinery.

I pushed back with what seemed like an obvious point: some of the most productive moves in the history of physics and philosophy have come from imagined cases, not from new data. Her reply was that those famous successes are the exception that justifies a thousand bad arguments, and that for every imagined case that exposed a genuine inconsistency, a hundred merely exploited the quirks of how people react to stories — to proximity, to named individuals, to losses over gains — and told us about our biases rather than about the question at hand.

I am not asking whether my colleague or I am 'right' in the abstract. I am asking for something usable: first, a diagnosis of what, exactly, goes wrong when such a scenario misleads, and whether the failure mode is in the scenario itself or in how its conclusions are drawn; second, a set of concrete checks I could apply before publishing one — questions I should ask about my own construction to tell whether it is clarifying a genuine problem or smuggling in a verdict; and third, a judgment about whether her strongest point — that the reader's feeling of having reached the conclusion independently is itself part of the deception — should change how I present these scenarios even when they survive the checks.
