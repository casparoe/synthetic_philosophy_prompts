I am a professor of philosophy serving on my university's Senate Committee on Student Wellbeing, and at our next meeting we vote on an administration proposal that I have been asked to respond to in a written opinion circulated beforehand. The proposal is to adopt a single headline "student happiness score" as an official institutional performance indicator: specifically, a 0-to-10 life-evaluation ladder question of the kind used in the international happiness rankings, with the university committing publicly to raise its mean score by half a point within five years, to publish the figure annually, and to make a modest but real portion of each faculty's discretionary budget contingent on improvement in its students' scores. Our current annual survey already includes three distinct instruments, which the proposal would collapse into the ladder alone: the ladder itself, a set of questions about emotions experienced the previous day, and a set about whether students find their studies and lives worthwhile and meaningful. The provost's case is that what gets measured gets managed, that the ladder is the most validated and internationally comparable single measure we have, and that a university publicly committed to its students' happiness will attract students and donors. The skeptics on the committee, and I am one of them, have raised a tangle of worries we have never managed to formulate sharply: that the ladder question plausibly tracks a person's considered judgment about their life rather than how their days actually feel, and these can come apart; that students in genuinely bad circumstances sometimes report high satisfaction and comfortable students report low, because people calibrate their answers against what they think is reasonable to expect; that the act of making the number a target will change what the number means, since departments given a budget incentive will find ways to lift reported scores that leave students' lives untouched; and that aiming to climb a ranking of averages is a strange goal compared to, say, raising the floor by finding and helping the most miserable ten percent.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate, roughly 800 to 1,000 words. It must reach a clear, actionable bottom line the committee can vote on — adopt, adopt in some modified form you specify, or reject in favor of an alternative arrangement you describe concretely — and it must give the reasoning in terms my colleagues on the committee, who are mostly economists, psychologists, and administrators, will find rigorous rather than precious. Disentangle the different things the word happiness is doing in this dispute, say which of them a university can plausibly be responsible for, and address directly whether a measure can survive being turned into a target. Whatever you recommend, give the strongest argument on the losing side its full force before answering it, and acknowledge where your own recommendation is most exposed. Write it as continuous prose throughout, the way one would write a letter or a formal opinion: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
