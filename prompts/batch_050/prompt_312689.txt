Consider how modern institutions distribute credit for invention. Patent systems award an exclusive right to the first inventor to file, and they ask, for each application, whether the invention was non-obvious to a competent practitioner in the field; a device that any skilled engineer would have come up with, given the state of the art, is denied protection as obvious. Scientific institutions run a parallel economy of credit: journals record submission dates to the day, priority disputes are adjudicated by who published or presented first, discoveries acquire eponymous names, and the most prestigious prizes cap the number of laureates per award, as though each breakthrough had at most three true authors. Meanwhile, the historical record is full of near-simultaneous discoveries and inventions — calculus, natural selection, the telephone, the oxygen theory of combustion — and historians often note that each arrived just when its conceptual ingredients became available, which suggests that had the named discoverer died in infancy, someone else would have produced substantially the same result within a few years.

What conception of discovery do these credit practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: do they treat a discovery as a contingent individual achievement — something that would not exist, or would not exist yet, but for this particular mind — or as a largely inevitable product of a field's state at a time, with the discoverer as mere first arrival? In particular, does the obviousness standard quietly concede the inevitability picture (some inventions belong to the state of the art, not to any person) while the priority rule denies it (each invention has one owner who deserves twenty years of exclusivity)? If so, is the tension resolvable — perhaps the two doctrines presuppose views at different levels, one about ideas and one about persons, or one about what is discovered and one about when — or are the doctrines simply committed to inconsistent answers to the same question?

Be careful to distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit: could a thoroughgoing inevitabilist still defend first-to-file priority on purely incentive-based grounds, without any claim about who made the discovery possible, and could a believer in heroic individual genius accept simultaneous invention as mere coincidence? If the institutions took the inevitability picture fully seriously, what concretely would have to change — eponymous naming, the winner-take-all allocation of credit to the first publisher, the prize cap, the duration of exclusivity — and what, if anything, could stay exactly as it is?
