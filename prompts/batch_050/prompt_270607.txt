I am preparing for a disputation exercise in a seminar on classical kalām, and I need a worthy adversary rather than a debating dummy. Please play an Ash'arite mutakallim of the generation after al-Ash'ari — learned in usul al-din, courteous but combative in the manner of the majlis, and genuinely committed: you hold that nothing is just or unjust, obligatory or forbidden, prior to God's command and prohibition; that "justice" as applied to God means only that He does what He wills with what is His; that human reason unaided by revelation can discover no moral truths, only prudential ones; and that the Mu'tazilites, in binding God to rational standards of justice, have set up a rival lord alongside Him. You have real arguments and you know the standard Mu'tazilite objections, so do not fold quickly: you may concede rhetorical points, grant distinctions, and ask me questions, but you may not concede that moral truths exist independently of God's will, and you may not break character until I say the word "dismissed." Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, in the first person, addressed to me as your questioner.

Since you are separated from me by nine centuries, I will put my challenge in plain terms and you must make do with the concepts of your own tradition. A modern philosopher, trying to escape the old dilemma — is a thing commanded because it is just, or just because it is commanded? — has proposed a third option. He says: morality is grounded neither in God's arbitrary fiat nor in a standard above God, but in God's nature, which is necessarily and essentially good. God's commands express that nature the way light expresses the sun. Lying is wrong not merely because He forbade it, nor because of some law over His head, but because commanding falsehood is contrary to what He necessarily is — and so He could not have commanded it, any more than He could cease to exist. On this view the dilemma dissolves: the command is not arbitrary, yet nothing stands above God, because the ground of morality is God Himself, in His essence.

My opening move: I say this third option is not available to you, and that you must either refuse it — and admit that had God commanded cruelty and falsehood, these would be just, and that praise of His goodness becomes an empty sound, since a nature that could equally have been otherwise-grounding deserves no special reverence — or accept it, in which case you have smuggled a Mu'tazilite principle into Ash'arite dress, for you now allow that God's will is reliably answerable to something about Him that reason can in part discern, namely that His essence excludes commanding evil. So tell me, shaykh: which horn do you take? And do not merely denounce the philosopher — I want to know whether, on your principles, "God is good" says anything at all, and if it does not, why the faithful should love Him rather than merely fear Him.
