Got caught in the middle of a long arguement between two friends last night and I want a verdict, because they're both smarter than me and both sounded right at different points. Topic: given that numbers are supposedly just positions in a structure, is "2 is an element of 4" true, false, or nonsense?

(1) Maya: the question has no answer. On the von Neumann construction 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4; on Zermelo's construction it isn't; arithmetic comes out identical either way. Asking which construction the "real" numbers are is like asking what the chess king is made of -- depends on the copy, not the game. So the question isn't false, it's a category mistake.

(2) Dan: no -- structuralism doesn't make the question senseless, it answers it with a no. If 2 is just a position in the natural number structure, its only properties are structural ones: being even, being prime, coming after 1. Membership is not among those, so 2 determinately fails to be an element of 4. "No answer" and "answer: no" are different claims, and the second one is right.

(3) Maya: but that answer costs you. If positions have only structural properties, then structurally indiscernible positions should be identical -- and some structures have those, like i and −i in the complex numbers, which get swapped by conjugation. So his view needs objects with no non-relational nature whatsoever, and that collapses exactly where two positions can't be told apart. A view that fails for the complexes shouldn't get to legislate for the naturals.

(4) Dan: the complex case is special. The natural number structure is rigid -- no nontrivial automorphisms -- so every position there is pinned down uniquely by its relations, and "2 ∉ 4" stays determinately no. At worst Maya's point shows structuralism needs primitive identity of positions for some structures, not that it's wrong about arithmetic.
