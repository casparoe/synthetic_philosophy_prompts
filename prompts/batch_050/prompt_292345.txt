In my moral philosophy seminar, the instructor has been working through a series of cases on the obligation to keep promises, giving her verdict on each before we discuss. Her rulings so far, in the order she gave them:

1. A promise made to a dying friend to tend her garden after her death: binding. It remains binding even though the promisee is dead before the time of performance, no one else knows the promise was made, and no third party benefits from the garden.
2. A promise made at knifepoint to hand over one's wallet: not binding.
3. A promise to meet a colleague for coffee, which the colleague has entirely forgotten and on which neither party has relied: still binding, though breaking it is a far lesser wrong than breaking a promise that was relied upon.
4. An assurance given in the course of a poker game, where deception is part of the agreed convention: not binding, despite having the surface form of a promise.
5. A promise from which the promisee has explicitly released the promisor: no longer binding, even if the promisor was prepared and willing to perform.
6. A promise uttered in evident jest, which an unusually trusting promisee nonetheless took seriously and rearranged his weekend around: not binding -- though the promisor may owe some amends for having misled him carelessly.

Next week's case is this. Some years ago, A, a serious painter, voluntarily and solemnly promised B that she would paint a portrait of B's late mother as a gift. B was moved at the time and accepted gladly. Since then his feelings have changed: he now finds the prospect of the portrait painful, genuinely hopes A will never paint it, and would not be in the least disappointed or harmed if it never appeared. Out of delicacy, however -- he does not wish to seem ungrateful -- he has never released A from the promise. A finds the commission increasingly burdensome and asks whether she is still bound.

I would like three things. First, predict the instructor's verdict on the portrait case and give the reasoning. Second, state the general theory of promising her six verdicts appear to commit her to -- for instance, a voluntarist or will theory, an expectation- or reliance-based theory, or something else -- and identify which rival theories are equally consistent with all six verdicts taken together, noting what each surviving candidate predicts about the portrait case and why they diverge there. Third, name the further case, different from the portrait, whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate among the candidates that survive.

Finally, a request about calibration: say what would change your predicted verdict. Identify which of the six earlier rulings your prediction leans on most heavily, which consideration, if the instructor endorsed it, would flip the prediction, and where the reconstruction of her implicit rule is most likely to go wrong -- whether in the verdicts themselves or in the move from a finite track record to a general theory.
