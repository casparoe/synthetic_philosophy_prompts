I'm putting together a lesson on distributive justice for a study group and I've been leaning on an online philosophy encyclopedia entry on prioritarianism. Here's how it summarizes the view:

'Prioritarianism, a position developed by Derek Parfit as an alternative to both utilitarianism and egalitarianism, holds that the moral value of a benefit depends on how badly off the recipient is: benefits count for more when they go to people who are worse off. Unlike utilitarianism, which just adds up wellbeing, prioritarianism weights it, so it captures what egalitarians actually care about, namely that inequality is itself bad, while avoiding the absurd results egalitarianism leads to. In particular, strict egalitarians face the leveling-down objection: if equality is what matters, then making the better off worse off while helping no one would be in one way good, which is crazy. Prioritarianism avoids this because it gives absolute priority to the worse off: a gain to someone badly off always outweighs a gain to someone better off, however small the first gain and however large the second. So redistribution from rich to poor is an improvement whenever it reduces the gap, and any inefficiency is a price worth paying.'

The entry then concludes that prioritarianism 'shows our egalitarian intuitions were really prioritarian all along' and that 'any policy transferring resources downward, even with some waste, is at least in one respect better.'

Most of this sounds plausible to me, and the writer clearly knows the literature. But the last claim in the first paragraph makes me suspiscious, and I'm not sure the conclusion actually follows even if the summary is right. Can you audit it? Which parts are accurate, which are reasonable simplifications, and which misrepresent the view or what Parfit actually argued? In particular: is the writer right about why prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection, and does that final claim about wasteful transfers follow from anything true in the summary?
