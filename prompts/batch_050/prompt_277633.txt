I've been running a small forecasting group for a few years now — the usual story, we started as people who met in the comment sections of rationalist blogs, and now we put numbers on questions, track calibration, and argue about whose judgment to trust when our numbers disagree. The arguments keep stalling at the same place: not the evidence itself, but what to do with evidence *about* our own reasoning. When Alice and I look at the same data and I say 70% and she says 30%, or when I learn that I formed my estimate at 2 a.m. after a fight with my landlord, or when a trusted colleague tells me that my argument sounds airtight but that I always sound like that when I'm wrong — these feel like they ought to move me, but nobody in the group can agree on *how*, and every informal rule we try seems to either collapse into stubbornness or dissolve into an infinite regress of distrusting our own distrust.

So before we adopt any particular theory of higher-order evidence, I want the desiderata written out: what any adequate account of how a rational agent should respond to evidence about her own cognitive processes, her own reliability, and the disagreement of her peers must deliver. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and indicate whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. At minimum, the list should address:

- Which canonical cases the account has to get right, including cases where conciliating toward a peer seems obviously mandatory, cases where standing firm seems obviously right, and cases where the evidence suggests one's own reasoning was compromised (fatigue, motivated reasoning, a known blind spot) even though one cannot find the error in it.
- What it must say about epistemic akrasia: whether a state like "p, but the evidence says I shouldn't believe p" can be rational, and if not, which horn the account takes and what it costs.
- Whether the account handles *iterated* higher-order evidence — evidence about one's ability to process higher-order evidence — without either regress or an arbitrary stopping point.
- Whether it explains why discounting your own judgment on the basis of a general fact about your unreliability feels self-undermining in a way that discounting it on the basis of a specific flaw does not, or else shows that this felt asymmetry is an illusion.
- Whether it treats higher-order evidence as just more first-order evidence to be pooled, and whether that treatment survives contact with cases where the two seem to pull apart.

The part I care most about: if some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and say which desideratum you think should be dropped — that would itself be a substantive result, and I'd rather have the list show the impossibility than paper over it. Then, briefly and without a detailed scorecard, note how the main families of views do against the list — conciliationist or equal-weight views, steadfast views, total-evidence views, and the level-splitting picture — since I mainly need to know which desiderata are doing the excluding. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; I can adjudicate the contenders myself once I know what I'm actually asking of them.
