I am co-teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Classical Chinese Philosophy and the Philosophy of Action," and I am responsible for the final exam's long-essay section. Students have worked through the Daodejing in James Legge's 1891 translation (the Sacred Books of the East version), and the liveliest seminar of the term grew out of a student's objection to chapter 3. In that chapter Laozi writes:

Therefore the sage, in the exercise of his government, empties their minds, fills their bellies, weakens their wills, and strengthens their bones. He constantly (tries to) keep them without knowledge and without desire, and where there are those who have knowledge, to keep them from presuming to act (on it). When there is this abstinence from action, good order is universal.

My student's worry, roughly, was that the sage is here trying very hard indeed to bring about a state of not-trying, and that this looks self-defeating in the way that trying to fall asleep, or trying to be spontaneous on a first date, is self-defeating. We never resolved it to my satisfaction. So please act as a specialist in classical Chinese philosophy who also has substantial experience designing university assessments, and write one final-exam essay question built around this passage, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The exam is closed-book, students have 45 minutes for this question, and it is worth 25 marks out of 100.

Before writing anything, brainstorm the distinct ways the apparent paradox in the passage could be sharpened and the distinct moves available for dissolving or surviving it (for instance: competing readings of what wu-wei actually excludes — action as such, deliberate action, self-conscious or striving action, action guided by conventional desires; the difference between first-order and second-order trying, and whether deliberate cultivation of spontaneity is coherent at one level even if self-undermining at another; and the fact that the passage is addressed to a ruler engineering non-action in others rather than pursuing it in himself, which may relocate rather than solve the problem). Then write the three deliverables. The exam question itself should be unanswerable by reciting a summary of wu-wei: it should force students to reconstruct the strongest version of the paradox and take a position on whether it defeats the sage's project. The model answer should be about 600 words and reflect what a genuinely excellent 45-minute answer could accomplish, not an idealized term paper. The marking scheme should allocate the 25 marks across specific moves a top answer must make (e.g., a precise formulation of the paradox rather than a vague gesture at it; explicit disambiguation of at least two readings of wu-wei with textual motivation; some account of why the political, governing-others framing of the passage matters to the paradox) and should name the specific mistakes that must cost marks (e.g., treating wu-wei as literal inactivity or laziness, dismissing the paradox as a mere verbal quibble without argument, or ignoring Legge's parenthetical "tries to" and what the translator's discomfort signals).
