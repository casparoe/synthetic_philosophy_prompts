So I'm in this informal philosophy reading group (mostly grad students but a couple of us are hangers-on like me) and we just spent two sessions on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. I got annoyed that our discussion kept circling the same three or four stock objections, so I started drafting a short essay I might post on the group's blog. Below is the draft. It's meant to be accurate about Strawson but the middle section is my own attempt at a new angle, and that's the part I'm least sure about.

---draft---

Strawson's Basic Argument goes roughly like this. Whatever you do, you do because of the way you are — your character, temperament, values, the whole package. If you're going to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you have to be truly responsible for the way you are. But to be responsible for the way you are, you'd have had to intentionally make yourself that way, and any act of self-making is itself something you do because of the way you already are at that time — which you'd then also have to be responsible for. Regress. The only way out would be to be somehow responsible for your own original self, causa sui, which is impossible. So true moral responsibility is impossible, full stop, determined or not.

Most pushback I've heard accepts the regress but denies you need "ultimate" responsibility: responsibility just needs the action to flow from your values in the right way, whether or not you chose those values. I want to try a different move, which I'll call the apprentice objection.

Imagine Mara. At twenty, Mara notices she's cowardly in conflict and hates it. She can't just decide to be brave — that kind of choice doesn't work like that. But she can do something subtler: she can sign up for things that predictably reshape people. She joins a volunteer fire crew, knowing it will terrify her and knowing, roughly, what repeated terror-plus-showing-up does to a person. Five years later she is, in fact, braver, and her new bravery issues in brave acts she didn't foresee in any detail. Now run Strawson's regress on her. Her act of joining the crew flowed from her twenty-year-old self, which she didn't choose. Fine. But notice what she did: she didn't choose her values, she chose a process she correctly believed would rewrite them. She exploited the causal structure of self-change the way an apprentice exploits a craft they didn't invent.

Here's my claim: the Basic Argument assumes responsibility for self would have to look like authorship — deliberate creation according to a plan you already endorse. But apprentice-style self-change is neither authorship nor mere happening-to-you. It's a third thing: steering. And if steering is enough for a interesting kind of responsibility for the way you are, then the regress never gets started for people like Mara, because the link between her old self and her new self passes through her foresight and her choice, not just through her given character.

The obvious Strawsonian reply: her foresight and her desire to change were themselves just features of her given character, so the regress bites one level up. My counter: the reply proves too much, because by that standard no causal contribution of an unchosen factor can ever be laundered by anything, and Strawson owes us a reason why laundering-by-steering in particular doesn't count, rather than just repeating that the inputs were unchosen. I admit I haven't fully made this stick.

---end draft---
