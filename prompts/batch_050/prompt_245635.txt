I want to know what an adequate account of the restitution of cultural objects would actually have to deliver before I listen to any more arguments about particular cases. The public debate oscillates between two specimens — the Benin Bronzes, taken in a punitive expedition in 1897, and the Parthenon Marbles, removed under contested Ottoman-era permission — and partisans on each side seem to treat their preferred case as the paradigm the whole theory must be built around. I would like the specification first.

Please give me the requirements list: the cases such an account must be able to handle (unambiguous wartime looting; removal that was arguably legal at the time but under conditions of grossly unequal power; objects acquired in good faith on a market that was itself corrupt; human remains; objects whose source community no longer exists in any continuous form; objects the source community is not asking to have back, or would rather have as replicas; and cases where rival claimants within the source country — a national government, a traditional authority, a regional museum — disagree about where a returned object belongs). For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and state the distinctions the account cannot be allowed to collapse: between legal title and moral claim, between return and restitution, between an object's origin and the strength of the present connection to it, between wrongful acquisition and wrongful retention, and between what a museum owes and what it may permissibly value in keeping.

I also want your judgement, not a menu. Tell me which of the commonly demanded criteria you take to be genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of one camp dressed up as neutrality (I suspect arguments from the universal or encyclopaedic value of museums, and from preservation record, may fall on different sides of that line, but commit yourself either way). Say whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable and, if so, treat that as a result about the debate rather than a defect of the list. Identify the single requirement that excludes the most positions currently defended in public. Only then, briefly, score the obvious contenders against the list — retention on stewardship grounds, automatic return to the state of origin, and source-community title — since I want the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them, and I want a definite verdict on which requirement does the real work.
