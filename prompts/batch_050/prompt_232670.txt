I'm designing a college syllabus on historiography, and in the rationale I've leaned on the old line "history is written by the victors" to justify centering peasants, workers, and household life rather than monarchs, ministers, and battles. A colleague objected that the slogan does too much work and that my remedy may not even follow from it. Please separate the distinct claims the one-liner could be making -- for instance: surviving sources disproportionately preserve the victors' perspective; official historiography is written to legitimate the winning regime; the very criteria for what counts as historically significant are set by the powerful; or, in the strongest version, the experience of the defeated is simply unrecoverable. For each, say whether it's a truism about archives, a substantive but contested methodological thesis, or actually false. Then address the complication that bothers me: the great microhistories of ordinary people -- millers, heretics, accused witches -- are typically reconstructed from the records of the victors' own institutions, above all inquisitorial and judicial archives. Does that show the slogan is wrong, or does it refine it? Finally, tell me which reading, if any, actually supports my syllabus design, and whether the slogan is the right thing to put in the rationale at all.
