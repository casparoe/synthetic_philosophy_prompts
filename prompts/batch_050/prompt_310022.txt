okay so background: last night I spent like three hours arguing with my friend Danny about the Death in Damascus story and we got nowhere, which is infuriating because I am right. The twist is that neither of us was working from the usual cocktail-party version — Danny dug up what seems to be the ancestor of the whole thing, from the Babylonian Talmud, Tractate Sukkah 53a. Here it is:

"One day Solomon saw that the Angel of Death was sad. He said to him: Why are you sad? He said to him: They are asking me to take the lives of these two Cushites who are sitting here. Solomon handed them to the demons in his service, and sent them to the district of Luz, where the Angel of Death has no dominion. When they arrived at the district of Luz, they died. The following day, Solomon saw that the Angel of Death was happy. He said to him: Why are you happy? He replied: In the place that they asked me to take them, there you sent them. [...] Immediately, Solomon began to speak and said: The feet of a person are responsible for him; to the place where he is in demand, there they lead him."

The argument: Danny says this is a straightforward fate story — the servants were marked, running was useless, done, and the modern decision-theory version is just the same point wearing a tie. I say no way. Notice that the servants don't even flee — Solomon, the informed rational agent, does the fleeing *for* them, and his cleverness is precisely the mechanism that delivers them. And that last line doesn't sound like fate to me, it sounds like something creepier: the ledger doesn't chase you, your own evasive maneuvres are the delivery service. Also the detail that Luz is a place "where the Angel of Death has no dominion" and they die there anyway — the story is grinning at you.

So here's what I want you to write, to settle this: a FAQ page from Death's Office of Appointments, written for customers trying to reschedule, relocate, or dispute their appointments. Deadpan civil-service voice — the tone of a tax authority that has seen every possible scheme and is no longer even annoyed. About 650-700 words, formatted as numbered questions with official answers. The Talmud quote above doesn't count toward the length.

Requirements, so the jokes come from the actual philosophy and not just from "death is spooky":

- The running joke should be the office's distinction between predicting you and merely recording you. Someone asks "did you know I'd try to flee?" and the answer is along the lines of: the office does not predict; it notes where the appointment is; how the customer came to be there is outside its remit. That's the decision-theory bite — perfect foresight versus your own action being the evidence, the evidential-vs-causal mess, the way fleeing feels rational and yet can't help. A reader who knows the Death in Damascus setup should laugh; a reader who doesn't should still find the bureaucracy funny.
- There must be a "Is this fate?" question, and the office must politely decline the metaphysical upgrade (fate is not a service we provide, etc.). This is where Danny's reading gets ironized: the flat-footed fatalist reading should be the least sophisticated thing on the page, with the office almost embarrassed on behalf of the people who ask.
- But be fair to Danny too — land one or two hits on my side. Include a complaint letter from a customer who carefully reasoned his way to Aleppo, and have him come off sounding exactly like a man submitting a change-of-address form for a fixed appointment. The office's response should deny that anyone was "waiting" for him anywhere.
- Do something with the Luz detail: a question about districts outside the office's dominion, answered via coverage maps not being available to the public, or a complaint that the dominion boundaries were misleadingly advertised.
- The final item should quote Solomon's line — "The feet of a person are responsible for him" — as official policy or the office motto, and the answer has to actually interpret it: not as fate-talk, but as the office's position that customers deliver themselves. That's where the piece takes a stand in my argument, so make it land.

Keep it deadpan the whole way through, no winking. And don't make Death a character with a personality — the office is the voice.
