I am a journalist who covers education policy and the science of learning for a general-audience ideas magazine. For the past year I have hosted a running exchange between two people who keep talking past each other, and my editor wants me to close the series with the document they would produce if forced to agree on something. I need you to draft that document.

The two parties: first, a philosopher of education in the tradition of Alfred North Whitehead, who takes her bearings from the essays in The Aims of Education and Other Essays (1929), especially his insistence that the opposition between a technical and a liberal education is fallacious -- that any technical training worth the name must also impart intellectual vision, and any liberal education worth the name must also impart technique -- together with his diagnosis of inert ideas, knowledge absorbed but never used, tested, or recombined. Second, an education economist who designs and evaluates competency-based, career-connected programs and holds that secondary and postsecondary schooling should be organized around demonstrable, labor-market-valued capabilities, with public money following measured outcomes.

Their shared assignment, which both have accepted in principle, is to produce a joint statement on what schooling is for. Both must take a position on Whitehead's fusion claim: what the world would have to be like for it to be true, what evidence since 1929 confirms or embarrasses it, and whether his diagnosis of inert ideas indicts the economist's metrics, the philosopher's seminar room, or both.

Draft the statement in this format: a numbered section of agreed claims (nothing vacuous; each must be a claim at least one of them found costly to sign); a disagreements section giving each residual dispute in a single sentence phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position; a list of three or four cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party -- and for each crux, a named body of evidence or kind of study both accept as bearing on it (for instance, earnings returns to career-technical education, the transfer-of-training literature, long-run follow-ups of liberal arts graduates, or the signaling-versus-human-capital debate), with each party's prediction about that evidence recorded in advance. Flag any disagreement that, on your reconstruction, dissolves into a difference over words. Close with one paragraph each side could sign describing what would change their own mind.

Constraints: neutral wording throughout; no strawmen, and where you are unsure of a position attribute the weaker-looking version to no one; about 1,000 words, formatted so the agreed claims and the disagreements can be read at a glance.
