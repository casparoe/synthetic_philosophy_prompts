I'm putting together a handout for my undergrad phil of law seminar and I want a "where do you stand on the blackmail paradox?" decision tree / quiz. For context: the paradox is that it's generally legal to reveal someone's embarrassing secret, and legal to ask them for money in plenty of contexts, but illegal to say "pay me or I reveal it" -- two rights supposedly making a wrong.

Here's what I want the instrument to do. It should have 8-10 diagnostic nodes, and each node has to be a concrete case the student reacts to, not an abstract principle. Some cases I definitely want used, because the positions genuinely split on them:

- the classic: X knows Y is having an affair, offers silence for $10k
- the contrast case that makes the paradox actually bite: a union threatening to strike unless wages rise, or a customer threatening a scathing online review unless they get a refund -- legal, but structurally the same shape as blackmail
- a prosecutor offering a plea deal ("plead guilty or we throw the book at you at trial")
- a variant where the secret isn't a wrongdoing but just private (a medical condition, a sexuality), because some views change their answer there
- a variant where a third party arguably deserves to know the secret (the cheated-on spouse), because one family of views says the wrong of blackmail is done to *them*, not to Y
- a variant where the blackmailer never initiates -- Y offers the money first ("please don't tell, I'll pay you")

Each node should be phrased so a student can answer it without knowing any of the literature -- no "do you accept a coercion-based account?", instead "is this person's complaint that they were threatened, or that they had to pay?"

The leaves should be actual named positions on the paradox, roughly: the libertarian dissolve-it view (no real paradox, criminalizing blackmail is a mistake), coercion/wrongful-threat accounts, information-as-property or privacy accounts, the view that selling silence wrongs a third party entitled to the information, and indirect/practice-level consequentialist accounts (it's the incentive effects of permitting blackmail that justify the ban). If your tree naturally generates other leaves, add them, and flag any pair of positions your cases can't actually tell apart rather than pretending they can.

Also, build it from the logical space first: list the possible answer-combinations your questions allow, then locate the real positions in that space, and if some combination is unoccupied say briefly why you think that is (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?). Don't just arrange five named views in a row and back-fit questions to them.

Keep the whole thing casual in tone, it's for 20-year-olds, and it should fit on two printed pages. Oh and give me the scoring/reading key as a separate section at the end so I can detach it.
