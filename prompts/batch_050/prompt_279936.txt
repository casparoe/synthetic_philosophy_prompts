My reading group just spent a semester on Davidson — radical interpretation, the principle of charity, the claim that massive error in another person's beliefs is unintelligible because interpretation requires assuming most of what they say is true — and the running joke all term was about what would happen if someone genuinely lived this way. I'd like you to write the joke properly. Take on the role of a satirist with a real background in philosophy of language: the humor has to come from the theory being applied correctly, not from a cartoon version of it, so that a specialist laughs in recognition rather than wincing at a misreading.

Write a mock newspaper advice column — an agony-aunt relationship column, roughly 550–700 words — written by an invented columnist who is a sincere, fully committed Davidsonian and answers every reader's interpersonal problem purely as a problem of radical interpretation. Give him a name and a column title. He should field two or three letters (a marriage on the rocks, something from a workplace, whatever you like), and in each case his remedy is hermeneutic: there was never a real disagreement, only a failure of interpretation, and the way to save the relationship is to settle on the interpretation of the other person's words under which they come out mostly true and mostly reasonable. He should lean, deadpan, on the actual machinery — charity, the idea that disagreement presupposes a vast shared background of true belief, the indeterminacy that lets rival interpretations fit the same evidence equally well — and the centerpiece of at least one reply should be his serene handling of insults, malapropisms, and slips: a columnist who simply cannot be offended, because he can always find the passing theory on which "I can't stand you" was a perfectly sensible, even touching, thing to say.

Keep him internally consistent from first line to last — the piece fails if he ever concedes that his correspondents' spouses might just be wrong, or angry, or cruel — and let the reductio land without him ever noticing it: a man who cannot be disagreed with also cannot be reached, and his readers keep writing back more distressed than before. It should be legible and funny to someone who has never heard of Davidson but has argued with a partner, and sharper still for someone who has. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm which Davidsonian commitments generate the best material and how to sequence the letters so the irony builds; then give me only the finished column.
