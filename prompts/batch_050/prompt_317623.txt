Every thanksgiving my uncle finds a way to bring up CEO pay, and this year he dropped the conversation-ender: "whatever you think about the system, people should get what they deserve." His point being that the market pays you what you're worth, so complaining about inequality is really just complaining about desert. I pushed back and he just repeated the sentence slower, like the problem was my listening.

Thing is, I can't tell anymore what that sentence is even saying. Sometimes it sounds like a moral claim (effort ought to be rewarded), sometimes like an empirical one (the market actually does track merit), sometimes just a tautology (you deserve whatever the system hands you, because that's what deserve means). I've also heard the exact same sentence used for the opposite conclusion -- that rich people don't deserve their luck with talent or upbringing, so redistribution is what desert actually demands.

Can you untangle this for me? Lay out the distinct precise theses "people should get what they deserve" could be making -- I'm guessing they differ on things like: what the basis of desert is supposed to be (effort? contribution? raw talent? moral character?), whether desert is backward-looking or just a forward-looking incentive structure, whether it applies to market outcomes at all or only to things like praise and blame, and whether "should" is doing moral work or just describing how incentives have to be set up. For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive claim that's at least defensible, or actually false? And crucially -- which reading does my uncle's argument need to be true for his point about CEO pay to go through? My sense is that the sentence survives by sliding between a boring true reading and a spicy false one, but I want the menu laid out properly before I accuse him of that at christmas.
