You are a philosopher with expertise in cognitive science and the psychology of concepts. Think carefully before writing; it may help to begin by privately brainstorming the strongest points on each side before composing the essay itself.

Consider the following situation. A competitive programming league must decide whether a contestant has cheated. The contestant used an AI coding assistant that generated large portions of her solution, which she then edited and submitted as her own work. The league's rules prohibit "cheating" but were written before such tools existed and do not mention them. The judges' deliberation has stalled because they keep talking past each other in a revealing way:

- Judge A argues by resemblance to paradigm cases: copying from another contestant's screen, smuggling in notes, paying someone to write your code. Using the assistant, she says, feels like the same kind of thing, and the felt similarity should settle it.
- Judge B argues from particular past rulings: in 2019 the league tolerated contestants reusing their own old code; in 2021 it punished a contestant who had a friend dictate solutions over the phone. She claims the right verdict is whichever one best matches the closest of these remembered precedents, and she distrusts any attempt to abstract away from the actual cases.
- Judge C argues that both of the others are missing the point: what matters is the purpose of the competition -- to test a contestant's skill at decomposing problems -- and whether AI-generated code undermines that purpose. On her view, the case should be decided by reasoning about what cheating is and why it is prohibited, not by matching cases.

Write an essay analyzing this dispute through the lens of the three main psychological theories of concepts: prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory. Your essay should address at least the following questions. First, which theory best describes what each judge is actually doing, and where does each judge's reasoning expose a known weakness of the corresponding theory (for instance, the prototype theory's troubles with compositionality and with concepts that have no clear family-resemblance structure, the exemplar theory's difficulty explaining how we generalize beyond remembered cases, and the theory theory's vagueness about what a "theory" is and its trouble with casual, unreflective classification)? Second, does the persistence of this three-way disagreement itself count as evidence for or against any of the theories -- for example, does it support hybrid or pluralist views on which different theories capture different concepts or different tasks? Third, and most importantly: which theory, or combination of theories, gives the best normative guidance for actually deciding hard cases like this one, where a rule must be extended to genuinely novel circumstances?

Reach a definite conclusion on that third question and justify it. Do not end with a shrug to the effect that all three theories have something to be said for them; if you think one approach should dominate in rule-extension cases even if others better describe everyday categorization, say so and explain why.

Throughout the essay, be careful to distinguish what is reasonably settled among researchers who work on concepts (for example, that simple feature-list and necessary-and-sufficient-condition views face severe empirical problems) from what remains genuinely contested (for example, whether the three theories are rivals for a single psychological kind or describe different processes that coexist). Do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus, and flag explicitly where you are taking sides in an unresolved debate.
