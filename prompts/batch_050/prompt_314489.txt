Last week at my office's "green team" meeting I made what I thought was a pretty obvious point: me skipping one flight or eating one less burger changes literally nothing about total emissions, so the commitee should stop guilting individuals and focus on pushing the company to change its procurement policies instead. A coworker just shrugged and said "every little bit helps" like that settled it, and a bunch of people nodded along. It's been bugging me for days because it feels like a total non-answer, but I also can't articulate exactly WHY it's a non-answer, which is annoying.

So: what are the different precise claims that slogan could actually be making? I figure some readings are trivially true but useless (my one burger adds some tiny nonzero bit of demand), some are empirical claims that could go either way (individual actions shift social norms, snowball into bigger commitments, get noticed by politicians), some are moral claims rather than causal ones (you shouldn't free-ride on a collective effort even when your own contribution is negligible), and maybe some readings are just plain false. What I really want to know is: which of these readings would actually work as a reply to my inefficacy point, and which ones only "work" by quietly changing the subject from outcomes to something else, like character or fairness?

Lay the readings out as a menu I can bring to the next meeting, running from the most defensible to the least, and for each one say whether it genuinely answers the objection or just dodges it. And if the best version of the slogan still loses to my point, tell me that too -- I'd rather know than win.
