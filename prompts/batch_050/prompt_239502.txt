I'm preparing a two-page proposal for a summer research fellowship in philosophy of science (I'm between my third and fourth year of a philosophy PhD, and the fellowship funds one focused project over about ten weeks). The project I have in mind concerns Hempel's raven paradox, but from an angle I haven't seen developed in the standard discussions, and I need help sharpening it into something a committee would take seriously. My half-formed idea is this: nearly every response to the paradox accepts the Bayesian verdict that observing a white shoe does confirm that all ravens are black, only by a vanishingly small amount, and then treats the lingering discomfort as a psychological quirk to be explained away. I suspect the discomfort is tracking something real, but something the confirmation-theoretic framework can't express: the difference between evidence that confirms a hypothesis and evidence that would have been capable of disconfirming it. Gathering white shoes feels like cheating not because it confirms less, but because the procedure could never have produced a counterexample no matter what the world was like, whereas going out and examining ravens at least exposes the hypothesis to risk. My hunch is that the paradox is really two paradoxes run together — one about degrees of confirmation, which Bayes settles, and one about the evidential value of non-risky tests — and that separating them requires a two-factor account on which confirmation and what I'd provisionally call "test exposure" are independent dimensions along which evidence can be good or bad.
