I'm an associate editor at a mid-tier animal behavior journal, and about half of what crosses my desk attributes some kind of cognition to an animal -- planning in scrub-jays, grief in elephants, spite in cleaner fish. My job at triage is to decide whether the cognitive interpretation is warranted or whether the authors have to walk it back to something leaner before the paper even goes out for review. Right now I basically eyeball it and my calls are inconsistent, so I want to operationalize Morgan's canon into a triage procedure I can run on a manuscript in about 30 minutes.

To fix terms: by Morgan's canon I mean the old methodological rule that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a "higher" psychological faculty if it can be adequately explained by a "lower" one. Two things I'm NOT asking for. First, don't just collapse it into Occam's razor -- the standard complaint is that parsimony counts entities or assumptions, not rungs on a psychological ladder, and a cognitively rich explanation can be the simpler one overall (say, given evolutionary continuity with us). Second, don't treat the canon as an infallible truth-finder: it plainly courts false negatives, systematically under-attributing cognition. I want a usable heuristic with those two failure modes built in rather than waved away.

The procedure should include:

- The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each. E.g., step zero should probably check whether the behavior is described without smuggling mentalistic vocabulary into the description itself ("the jay re-hid the food because she knew the observer would pilfer it" already assumes the conclusion).
- An explicit working scale of "lower" to "higher" for the procedure to use, since the historical canon leaned on a psychological ladder nobody actually possesses. Something like: fixed action pattern/reflex; stimulus-bound associative learning; flexable goal-directed behavior without representing the goal; representation-based inference; metacognition. Tell me where the scale is doing real work and where its just a placeholder, because that's where the whole thing is most likely to break.
- Which lower explanations the authors must have actively ruled out -- and what kind of evidence would count as ruling them out -- before a higher attribution survives triage, versus cases where ruling out is impossible with current methods and I should just send it to review with a flag.
- A step asking whether the lean explanation is merely available or actually adequate, and a step asking whether the cognitive explanation might be simpler in the overall sense. This is where the two failure modes above get handled.
- The points where the procedure hands off to a human judgment call or to field norms, marked as such, so I don't mistake my discretion for a rule.

Then run the finished procedure on three (hypothetical) manuscripts:

1. Scrub-jays re-cache food when a competitor observed the original caching but not when observed by their mate; the authors claim the jays attribute knowledge states to specific observers.
2. Dogs display the classic "guilty look" when scolded regardless of whether they actually broke the rule; the authors claim dogs experience guilt. I suspect the procedure spits out an answer our more enthusiastic readers won't like -- good, I want to see it do that.
3. Cleaner fish pass a version of the mirror mark test; the authors claim self-recognition.

Finish by telling me honestly what the procedure can't do, and where it will systematically mislead me even when followed correctly.

Please write the answer in American English, and format it so I could more or less paste it into our editorial handbook -- concrete steps and tests, not an essay about anthropomorphism.
