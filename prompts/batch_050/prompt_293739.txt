You are a professional philosopher of mind and cognitive science with a specialization in the epistemology of artificial intelligence. I would like you to think carefully and slowly through the following problem, beginning with some open brainstorming about candidate principles before committing to one. I am a philosophy professor putting together a seminar on machine understanding, and I have realized I should first get my own untutored verdicts in order before assigning anything. Here they are. (1) An undergraduate who has memorized the textbook word for word, reproduces every definition flawlessly, but collapses when given a slightly novel counterexample: I say she does not understand the material, or at best understands it shallowly. (2) A chess engine that finds brilliant moves in positions no human has ever seen, but could not in principle tell you anything about why a move works: I say it does understand chess, in some real if alien sense. (3) A person in a room mechanically following a rulebook that maps incoming Chinese characters to outgoing ones, producing fluent conversation: my gut says no understanding of Chinese, though I admit my confidence wavers when I imagine the rulebook getting large enough. (4) A current large language model that answers questions across dozens of domains, generates novel arguments, catches and corrects its own mistakes when prompted, and generalizes to problems unlike anything in its training data: my gut says this one does understand, at least somewhat. (5) And yet that same model will occasionally fail at something trivial — miscounting the letters in a word it can define perfectly — and when it does, my gut says it never really understood that thing at all, despite its flawless verbal performance.

Here is what troubles me. Verdicts (1) and (4) seem to rest on the same test — generalization to the novel — but verdicts (2) and (3) pull against each other: the engine passes no verbal test and I grant it understanding, while the room passes every behavioral test and I deny it. And (4) and (5) together force me to say that a single system understands and does not understand, apparently depending on which dispositions I happen to probe, which sits uneasily with my instinct that understanding is a matter of what is going on inside, not of which questions I thought to ask. So: is there a single coherent principle — about the causal structure of internal representations, or about the full dispositional profile of a system, or something else — that delivers all five verdicts, or comes close? Please state your best candidate precisely, indicate which clause of it each verdict motivates, and then be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and why that abandonment is the right price to pay rather than a refutation of the principle.

Finally, apply your principle to two cases I have not listed, and tell me what it predicts: first, a hypothetical future model whose internal representations have been verified by interpretability research to encode the relevant abstractions, but which occasionally hallucinates confidently; second, a human savant who can factor large numbers instantly but cannot explain or even comprehend the concept of a factor. If your principle gives a verdict on either case that strikes you as clearly wrong, say so, and say whether you would patch the principle or accept the cost.
