Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about advance directives, and I want to check whether his characterization of the other side was fair. The case we kept circling back to: a woman in her forties, after watching her mother's decline, signs a directive stating that if she ever becomes severely demented, no life-prolonging treatment is to be given, and she adds a clause anticipating that her future demented self may well be content in a way that no longer reflects her real values, so that any later expression of a wish to live is to be discounted in advance. Thirty years later she is severely demented, apparently cheerful, and develops treatable pneumonia. I argued that the person now in the nursing home — the one who enjoys her afternoons and resists being taken to the hospital — is the one whose interests should count. My friend replied by summarizing what he called the standard philosophical defense of the directive, which he attributed to Ronald Dworkin:

"On this view, autonomy is the foundational value in medical ethics, and an advance directive is simply autonomy extended over time. Overriding it is therefore exactly as wrong as overriding a competent patient's contemporaneous refusal of treatment. And since the demented patient is no longer competent, there is no one left with the standing to override the directive. Her current contentment is merely experiential pleasure, and experiential interests carry no real weight against a considered judgment about how one's life as a whole should go. So the pneumonia should not be treated."

Something about this summary felt slippery to me, but I could not put my finger on it in the moment. Which parts would a careful defender of the authority of the earlier self actually accept, and which parts overstate or distort the position? In particular: is the analogy to contemporaneous refusal one that defenders of the view themselves rely on, or does it smuggle in the conclusion? Does the friend's final verdict follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate? And does the woman's self-prediction clause — her explicit advance discounting of her future contentment — change any of that?
