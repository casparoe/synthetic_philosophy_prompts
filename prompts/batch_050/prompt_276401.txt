In the third lecture of Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity, there is a stretch of argument about theoretical identifications that I keep getting wrong. Paraphrasing as best I can: Kripke considers the scientific identification of heat with molecular motion. We originally picked out heat by a contingent property of it, namely that it produces a certain characteristic sensation in us. That fact, he says, misleads us into thinking the identity "heat is molecular motion" could have turned out false: we imagine a situation in which we have that very sensation without any molecular motion going on, and we take ourselves to be imagining heat without molecular motion. But according to Kripke, what we are actually imagining is merely a situation in which creatures like us are affected in the heat-ish way by something that is not heat — just as we can imagine finding out that the sensation was produced by some other mechanism. The identity, once established empirically, is necessary; its apparent contingency is an illusion generated by confusing the contingent reference-fixing description with the rigidly designated phenomenon itself.
