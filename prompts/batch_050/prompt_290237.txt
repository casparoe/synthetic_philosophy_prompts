"David Chalmers distinguishes the 'hard problem' of consciousness from what he calls the 'meta-problem' of consciousness. Explain both problems and the difference between them. And here's the student's answer:

"The hard problem is the question of why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience — why it feels like something to see red or feel pain, rather than all that processing going on 'in the dark.' The easy problems, like explaining attention, reportability, and the integration of information, are in principle solvable by standard cognitive science, but the hard problem seems to resist that kind of explanation.

The meta-problem, by contrast, is the problem of explaining why we think and say there is a hard problem — why we make judgments like 'my experience of red is ineffable' or report that conciousness seems deeply mysterious. Since these judgments and reports are themselves behaviors and behavioral dispositions, the meta-problem is really one of the easy problems: tractable in principle, even if we don't have the full explanation yet.

Illusionists like Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett hold that consciousness does not exist — that it is an illusion produced by introspective mechanisms that misrepresent our own brain states. On their view, once we have explained why we judge ourselves to have phenomenal consciousness (the meta-problem), there is nothing left over to explain, and the hard problem dissolves.

Chalmers, however, argues that solving the meta-problem would amount to solving the hard problem, since the hard problem ultimately consists in a set of intuitions and reports, and explaining those is precisely the meta-problem's job. So on his view a completed science of the meta-problem leaves no explanatory remainder.

My own view is that the illusionists have the better of the exchange: since the meta-problem concerns purely physical facts about reports and judgments, it must have a physical solution, and therefore consciousness itself must be physical too."
