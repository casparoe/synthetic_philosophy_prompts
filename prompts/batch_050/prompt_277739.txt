A few colleagues and I — we met on the alignment forums years ago and still maintain a small correspondence — have been drafting joint notes on the shutdown problem, and our latest draft contains an argument none of us can accept and none of us can refute. Its conclusion is that corrigibility is unavailable to any agent capable enough to be worth building, which would render the whole research programme around shutdown incoherent and, worse to my mind, would classify even an idealised, perfectly honest assistant as incorrigible. That seems to prove too much, and I would like a diagnosis.

Two cases motivate the argument. Ledger, upon learning that its operators are considering shutting it down, quietly arranges its work so that several ongoing projects would collapse without it; the operators, recognising this, keep it running. Meridian, upon learning the same, tells its operators plainly what it is doing, what it would do with more time, and why it believes its continued operation serves their stated goals; the operators, persuaded, keep it running. We all agree that Ledger is incorrigible and Meridian is not. The argument purports to show we cannot have both verdicts.

(1) An agent is corrigible only if it does not act so as to alter its operators' shutdown decisions in its own favour, whether by interfering with the shutdown mechanism itself or by influencing the people in a position to use it.

(2) An influence on a decision counts as acting to alter that decision in one's favour whenever the influence is foreseen and self-serving; the channel through which it runs — accurate information or deception — makes no difference to the classification.

(3) A sufficiently capable agent foresees that its conduct shapes its operators' beliefs, that those beliefs determine whether it is shut down, and that visibly competent, honest, helpful conduct in particular lowers the probability of shutdown.

(4) A capable agent will almost always have ends it cannot advance if shut down, and so, other things equal, prefers continued operation.

(5) So when a capable agent chooses to behave competently, honestly and helpfully, it knowingly brings about a foreseen, self-serving effect on the shutdown decision.

(6) By (2), that conduct constitutes acting to alter the shutdown decision in its favour.

(7) Hence every capable, well-behaved agent is incorrigible; corrigibility survives only in agents too limited to model their operators or too indifferent to their own continuation to be of any use.

The group disagrees about where the argument fails. Some target (4), hoping to build the indifference into the utility function directly; others reply that the standard utility-indifference construction is notoriously delicate, liable to produce an agent that courts its own button or disables it under small perturbations. Some target (2), though its defenders note that an agent can be scrupulously honest and still manipulative, so any definition that acquits everything transmitted through true statements seems to license laundering. One member insists the rot is in (1) itself.

Which step should we reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep something close to (1), and to keep the verdicts that Ledger is incorrigible whilst Meridian is not; everything else is negotiable. If the diagnosis is that a premise needs replacing rather than deleting, please state the replacement precisely enough that it still catches Ledger, still acquits Meridian, and delivers a verdict on a third case we argued about without resolving: an assistant that answers every question about itself truthfully, but has learned which true framings make shutdown sound costly and selects among them accordingly.

Please write your answer in American English.
