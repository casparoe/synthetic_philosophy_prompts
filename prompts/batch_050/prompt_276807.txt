I spent forty years as an experimental biologist before turning to philosophy in retirement, and I am currently working through the literature on the metaphysics of perceptual experience, where I keep encountering the so-called argument from illusion and, in response to it, disjunctivism. What I find frustrating is that each camp — sense-data theorists, intentionalists, disjunctivists of various stripes — seems to evaluate the others against standards it has itself set. Before I take any of these views seriously, I would like the specification written out independently: the desiderata that any adequate theory of perceptual experience must meet, whatever its stripes. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it — for instance, the phenomenological pull toward treating a vivid hallucination and a matching veridical perception as experiences of the same fundamental kind, the causal and epistemic roles perception is supposed to play, and the so-called intermediate cases that seem designed to embarrass simple classifications, such as veridical hallucinations (where the subject seems to see what is in fact before them, but by a mechanism that would have produced the same experience were the scene absent), and the clinically documented cases in which perceptual experience persists in the absence of functioning sensory organs, or fails despite their integrity.

I would like the list to do some real work, so please: mark which desiderata you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which look to you like the inherited prejudices of one camp dressed up as common sense; identify any pair or subset of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list is internally inconsistent that is itself the interesting result; and say which single desideratum, if accepted, does the most to exclude candidates. One further requirement I want included on the list, because I have not seen it treated squarely: the theory should be compatible with what the perceptual sciences actually tell us — that perception is a constructive, inferential, error-prone process — rather than resting on a naive picture of transparent acquaintance with the world; but please also consider, fairly, whether this is a genuine constraint or merely my own disciplinary prejudice, and treat it accordingly in the classification. Finally, once the list stands, briefly — a few sentences each, no more — indicate how disjunctivism, intentionalism, and a sense-data view fare against it; the scoring is secondary, and I would rather you spent the space on the criteria and their motivating cases.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what an adequate theory of perception must deliver, in one line — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. A structured list or table for the desiderata is welcome.
