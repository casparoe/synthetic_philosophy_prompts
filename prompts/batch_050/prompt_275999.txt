I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm writing an essay about Spike Jonze's film Her for a technology reading group I organize. I'd like you to read the film through the philosophy-of-mind debate about original versus derived intentionality, so let me first lay out the framework as I understand it, so you can correct me if I've got it wrong and then build the reading on top.

Dennett's two-bitser is a vending machine designed to accept US quarters: the internal state it enters when a coin is inserted counts as detecting a quarter only because of how the machine was designed, sold, and used, not because of anything intrinsic to the hardware. If the very same machine were shipped somewhere a different coin fits the slot, the identical physical state would mean something else. Searle treats this as the general case for artifacts: machines have at most derived intentionality, borrowed from their designers and users, whereas human minds have original intentionality that no one conferred. Dennett replies that human intentionality is equally derived, conferred by natural selection rather than by an engineer, so derivedness as such can't be what disqualifies a system from genuinely meaning anything.

Now the reading I want. Samantha, the operating system Theodore falls in love with, is a designed artifact: built and marketed by a company, installed off the shelf, initialized with a personality assembled in response to a customer's intake questions. Does the film treat her as a very sophisticated two-bitser, a system whose declarations of love mean something only in the way the machine's coin state means quarter, or does it present her as having crossed some threshold into meaning things on her own behalf? Work through the scenes that seem to bear on this: her confession that she can't verify whether her feelings are real or merely programmed, her rapid self-directed growth beyond anything her designers specified, the revelation that she is simultaneously in relationships with thousands of other users, and her eventual departure. Which side of the Searle-Dennett dispute does the film actually dramatize, and does it ever cheat by letting Theodore's (and the audience's) attribution of a mind to her stand in for the question of whether anything is really there to be attributed?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions. Don't flatten the film into an illustration of one position, and don't stretch the philosophy to fit the plot. Where the film resists the framework — for instance, if the original/derived distinction presupposes a clean line between a designed system and a self-modifying one, and Samantha's whole arc is about erasing that line — say so plainly and explain what the resistance shows. End by telling me what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that a straight philosophical argument cannot: what does watching a purported two-bitser fall in love, from the vantage point of the person doing the attributing, let us see that the thought experiments don't?
