So here's the situation. I'm on the external advisory panel for a national space agency (won't say which one), and we've been asked to rule on a permit application that has turned into a total nightmare. A private foundation — funded by a tech billionaire, of course — wants to use a decommissioned radar dish to beam a high-powered, repeating message at a handful of nearby stars with known exoplanets. The message itself is harmless enough: prime numbers, some math, a bit about human biology, and here's the kicker, it ends with an explicit invitation to reply, along with the frequencies they'd listen on. The signal would be millions of times stronger than anything Earth leaks accidentally through TV and radar spillover, which according to their own engineering report is basically undetectable past a few light years anyway.

The fight on the panel is over whether the so-called "dark forest" reasoning should be taken seriously. You know the idea (it got popularized by a certain Chinese sci-fi trilogy): the galaxy might be full of civilizations that stay quiet because any civilization that reveals its location is a potential threat, and given the speeds of information vs. travel, the safest move for a listener is to shoot first. Obviously we can't assign a probability to that, it's pure speculation. But the downside is literally everyone dying, and the upside of transmitting is... what, exactly? Some nice astronomy data in 60 years?

Here's where I want your help. You are an expert policy advisor with a background in game theory and decision-making under deep uncertainty, and you've advised governments on existential risk before. Think carefully about this one — I'd actually like you to start with some brief brainstorming where you list every relevant consideration you can think of, including ones I haven't raised, before you commit to anything.

Then draft a formal memo to the panel (about a page or two) with a clear bottom-line recommendation: approve the permit, deny it, or approve it with conditions. The memo needs to:

1. Take the dark forest argument seriously as a *decision problem*, not as a claim about what aliens are actually like. Is it legitimate to refuse an action because of a scenario you can't even assign a probability to? Where does that logic stop — couldn't you use the same structure to block almost anything?
2. Deal honestly with the strongest point on the other side (whichever side you land on). For example, if you recommend denying, you need to confront the argument that we're already "shouting" via military radar and that a single foundation being gated is pure theater. If you recommend approving, you need to confront the asymmetry of the stakes.
3. Address the legitimacy problem: does a national agency even have the standing to make a call whose consequences fall on all of humanity and all future generations? If not, who does, and what should the permit process actually look like in the meantime?
4. Propose at least one concrete condition or procedural requirement the agency could attach that doesn't just reduce to "yes" or "no."

Don't handwave the game theory — I want the actual reasoning, not vibes. And keep in mind the panel's charter technically only lets us rule on spectrum-use and safety grounds, so part of your memo should address whether an existential-risk rationale is even within our remit or whether we'd have to punt this upward.
