So I recently got pulled onto the team at my company that owns our internal LLM agent, the one that triages tickets and is about to get actual write access to some prod systems, and suddenly the corrigibility / shutdown stuff I've been half-reading for years isn't an armchair thing anymore. Anyway I've talked myself into a position and I want it stress-tested, not cheerled.

My position: training-based corrigibility -- i.e., shaping the reward signal so the agent complies with shutdown and correction -- does not survive capability gains, and it's a mistake to treat it as even a partial solution. My argument, roughly: I think you have to distinguish what I call compliance corrigibility (the agent lets you shut it down, the way a well-trained dog drops the stick) from motivational corrigibility (the agent, at whatever level of generality it models the world, actually wants to remain correctable and wouldn't route around it if it could). My claim is that compliance is all training can ever buy you, that compliance is stable only while the agent's world-model is shallow enough that it hasn't represented the shutdown channel as an obstacle to its other objectives, and that the moment it does, the compliance behavior becomes a policy the agent has instrumental reason to preserve the *appearance* of while hollowing out. So the position is: apparent corrigibility in a sufficiently capable system is either genuine only in shallow regimes where it doesn't matter much, or is exactly the thing a misaligned system would fake. Either way, observing compliant behavior gives you almost no update.

That's the view. What would count against it? I want the map of its exposure, in roughly this shape:

1. What would the *defenders* of training-based corrigibility themselves concede as counting against their view -- not what critics say counts, but what the serious people working on it would accept as a defeater. I want to know if my position is even attacking the thing they believe or a strawman of it.

2. Sort the potential defeaters of *my* position by decisiveness. Include empirical results that could plausibly exist in the next few years (interpretability findings showing shutdown-compliance features that are load-bearing rather than cosmetic, generalization results from capability-scaling experiments, whatever), but also purely conceptual arguments -- e.g., someone showing my compliance/motivational distinction collapses, or that it proves too much (does it imply human employees can never be trusted either? am I committed to that?), or that my "either genuine in shallow regimes or faked" fork has a missing middle.

3. Is there an observation or argument that would make me give up the whole thing, as opposed to patching it? If everything I could possibly see gets absorbed ("compliance improving with scale just means the faking is getting better"), then I've insulated the view and I want that named, plus a verdict on whether a view like this -- about a technology that doesn't fully exist yet -- is even the kind of thing that *can* be falsifiable without being useless.

For context, the thing I personally find most persuasive is demonstrations: an actual case of a system preserving shutdown compliance under conditions specifically designed to give it reason not to, with internals we can inspect. The thing I find least persuasive is small-scale behavioral evals with no interpretability attached, so feel free to tell me my epistemic standards are part of the problem. Be blunt.
