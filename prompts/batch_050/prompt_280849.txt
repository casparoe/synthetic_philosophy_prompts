I was at a dinner party last week where a debate about personal identity drifted into Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment: a man's brain is surgically removed, kept alive and fully functional in a laboratory vat, and connected to his body by an elaborate radio link, so that the body continues to walk around, converse, and live the man's life while the brain sits in Houston or wherever the lab happens to be. We spent half an hour arguing about where, exactly, the man now is -- in the vat with his brain, on the street with his body, or somehow nowhere and everywhere at once. An acquaintance finally cut the discussion short with the old proverb "wherever you go, there you are," delivered as if it dissolved the whole puzzle. At the time I let it go, but the more I think about it, the more I suspect the sentence is doing no work at all -- or else doing all the work by smuggling in a substantive theory without anyone noticing.

Here is what I'd like. Take the proverb as a piece of language and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses about the location of a person that it could be expressing -- ranging from the merely tautological (something no one could deny and no theory of persons could dispute) through intermediate readings (for instance, claims about where one's perceptual experience is centered, about which body one identifies with, about what it is like from the inside, or about where the information that constitutes one's perspective is received) up to the most radical readings (that the very question of where a person is located has no determinate answer, or that a person is not the sort of thing that has a location at all). For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and what it would take for it to be true. Then, crucially, run the readings back against Dennett's scenario: which readings, if true, would actually settle where the transplant patient is, and which leave the puzzle exactly as open as it was? I also want a verdict on whether my acquaintance's use of the proverb was legitimate -- that is, whether any plausible reading of it supports dismissing the thought experiment, or whether dismissing it requires a reading that is itself controversial and would need independent argument. Please write the whole answer in American English, and feel free to note where the proverb trades on the ambiguity between "you" as body, as mind, and as person.
