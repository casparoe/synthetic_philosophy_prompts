I'm writing a long magazine feature on the hidden premises inside game theory — the idea that rational players who all know they're rational, and know that they know it, will end up playing the textbook solutions. Before I start interviewing, I want the specification in hand. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of the epistemic foundations of game-theoretic solution concepts, where "adequate" means: strong enough to tell economists when they're entitled to invoke a solution concept in applied work. Your list should cover which results the account must recover or explain (why common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in finite perfect-information games; why finite mutual knowledge of some fixed depth suffices in some settings but not others), which puzzles it must dissolve or at least diagnose (the agreement and no-trade results; the tension that common knowledge of rationality appears to be refuted the moment play reaches a node that rationality itself says is unreachable), and which distinctions it cannot collapse (knowledge versus belief, mutual knowledge of depth n versus genuine common knowledge, rationality of players versus rationality of choices off the path of play). Give the motivating case or consideration behind each desideratum, mark which are non-negotiable and which are the hobbyhorse of one camp, and flag any subset you believe is jointly unsatisfiable — that part is what my piece will hang on. Close with one short paragraph on which single requirement excludes the most existing accounts, and one line each on how the standard Aumann-style framework and the extensive-form belief-revision approaches fare against the list.
