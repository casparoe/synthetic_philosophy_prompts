In J. B. Priestley's An Inspector Calls, the Birling family and Gerald Croft each commit a single, individually defensible act -- a dismissal after a strike, a complaint to a manager, an affair ended, a drunken encounter, a refusal of charity -- and no one of them intends or foresees Eva Smith's suicide, yet the chain of their separate decisions ends in her death. Read the play through the literature on corporate and collective moral agency. Does the family function as a group agent in anything like List and Pettit's sense, with a collective responsibility over and above the members' individual culpability, or does the play instead vindicate the individualist view (associated with critics of Peter French's account of corporate personhood) that so-called collective responsibility always dissolves into a sum of personal failings? Pay particular attention to the play's structure as an instance of the "problem of many hands": each contribution is small, causal responsibility is distributed, and no single act suffices for the outcome -- which is exactly the condition under which assigning blame to corporations is thought to be hardest. Consider also the final twist: once the elder Birlings and Gerald persuade themselves that Goole was no real inspector and that no girl has died, they relax, while Sheila and Eric insist nothing has changed in what they did. Does the play thereby suggest that collective responsibility is hostage to outcomes and to external investigation -- that a firm, like this family, feels responsible only when caught -- and is that a critique of corporate moral agency or a diagnostic observation about it? Where does the play resist the corporate reading (a family has no constitution, no decision procedure, no authorized spokesperson), and where does the resistance itself illuminate the theory? Finally: does Priestley's dramatization capture something about distributed complicity that the philosophical debate, conducted largely in terms of organizational structure and rational agency, tends to miss? I'm preparing a seminar session pairing the play with the corporate responsibility literature and want a reading that is faithful in both directions rather than a forced allegory.
