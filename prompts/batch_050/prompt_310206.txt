Two of my colleagues have been arguing, on and off for weeks, about a case from a seminar I teach, and I have been listening without taking a side. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange. The case is this: a couple must choose between conceiving a child in June, when the mother is required by her health to take a medication known to cause a mild but genuine lifelong condition (chronic joint pain, say) in any child conceived, or waiting until November, when she will be off the medication and any child conceived will be healthy. They choose June, for no better reason than that a November conception would disrupt a planned career move. Everyone agrees the June child will have a life well worth living.

Colleague A argues that the June child is wronged. Her claim is that harming someone does not require making that person worse off than they would otherwise have been; it is enough that one knowingly imposes a significant burden on them for inadequate reasons. Since the parents knowingly imposed chronic pain for the sake of convenience, the child has a legitimate complaint directed at them.

Colleague B replies that this notion of non-comparative harm proves too much: every life contains significant pain, so if imposing pain without a comparative baseline counts as harming, then every procreation harms the resulting child, and the view collapses into a general indictment of reproduction that A herself does not accept.

A answers that the collapse does not follow, because her view concerns significant burdens imposed for insufficient reasons, not the ordinary pains that are the unavoidable fabric of any life; and she adds that there is a principled difference between causing a hardship within a life and causing a life that contains hardships.

B then shifts ground: even granting that distinction, he says, talk of "wronging the child" is just impersonal badness dressed in personal vocabulary. The honest position is that the couple acted wrongly, full stop, without anyone being wronged -- the world contains a slightly worse outcome than it easily could have, and that is the whole of the moral fact.

A's final move is that B's impersonal reading cannot account for the directedness of the case: the June child can ask the parents to justify themselves, can resent them, can be owed an apology, and none of that makes sense if the wrongness floats free of any victim. B's rejoinder is that resentment and demands for justification are only apt when the complainant is worse off than they should have been, which is a comparative standard after all -- so A smuggles back the very comparison she claimed to reject.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: state who has the better of each individual step (not merely who wins overall), identify anything both parties are missing, and give your own verdict on whether the June child is wronged. At the end, say explicitly what argument or consideration would change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure. Write your entire response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, in American English.
