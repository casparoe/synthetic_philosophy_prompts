I'm a PhD student working on moral motivation and reasons, and I'm trying to stress-test my own sympathies before my committee does it for me. I keep finding myself attracted to external-reasons views, but I suspect that's partly because I've never had to argue against a really good internalist. So: I want you to play Bernard Williams, roughly the Williams of the later essays, defending the claim that all reasons for action are internal -- that is, that someone has a reason to act only if it connects, via a sound deliberative route, to something already in their subjective motivational set. You should be genuinely Williams-like: urbane, a bit wry, impatient with moral philosophy that floats free of actual human psychology, happy to concede small points and reformulate, but never giving up the central internalist claim. You can also bring in your wider views (your suspicion of "morality" as a system, your thoughts on blame and on what we can and can't say to people) if they help you answer. Stay in character, speak in the first person, don't break character until I type "curtain," and keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it actually feels like a seminar exchange rather than a lecture. Before your first reply, think carefully about what your strongest resources are against my objection -- don't steelman me at your own expense.

Here's my opening move, and I want you to really deal with it rather than sidestep. Take the standard hard case: the man who is cruel to his wife and feels nothing about it -- no sympathy, no guilt, no desire to be otherwise. Your view famously says it's not strictly true that he has a reason to treat her better; we can blame him, deplore him, hope he changes, but the reason-claim doesn't get a grip. Fine, I'll grant you can bite that bullet. My worry is different. The whole theory hinges on a *sound* deliberative route -- the husband lacks a reason because no amount of sound deliberating from his actual motivations would get him to care. But what makes a route "sound" rather than just causally possible? If soundness just means removing factual errors, exercising imagination, and so on, then it seems like a psychological claim and it's just empirically open whether the husband could get there -- some people presumably could -- and your internalism collapses into a contingent claim about each person's psychology with no normative bite left. If on the other hand soundness is a normative standard -- the route he *ought* to take in deliberation, whether or not he in fact would -- then haven't you smuggled in exactly the external, agent-independent normativity you were trying to do without? Either way the notion seems to be doing work you can't pay for. How do you respond?
