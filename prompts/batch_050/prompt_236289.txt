(b) Explain the objection raised independently by Tichý and Miller against that definition. (c) Say what the objection does and does not show about the idea that science progresses toward the truth. Students were told to answer in no more than 400 words, and the mark scheme allocates 4 points to (a), 10 to (b), and 6 to (c), out of 20.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

Popper defines verisimilitude in terms of a theory's content. The truth content of a theory is the set of its true consequences, and its falsity content is the set of its false consequences. Theory B has greater verisimilitude than theory A if and only if the truth content of A is a subset of the truth content of B and the falsity content of B is a subset of the falsity content of A, with at least one of the two inclusions strict. Popper's idea was that science can then be seen as progress toward the truth even though our theories are false, since each theory can be closer to the truth than its predecessor.

Tichý's objection runs as follows. Suppose A and B are both false and B has greater verisimilitude than A. Because B is false, it has at least one false consequence. Tichý showed that the two subset conditions then force A and B to have exactly the same consequences overall, which contradicts the assumption that one of the inclusions is strict. So on Popper's definition, a false theory can never be closer to the truth than a true theory. Tichý illustrated this with his weather example: take the false claim that it is hot, rainy, and windy; whether it counts as closer to the truth than a rival false claim flips when we re-express everything in a vocabulary built from different primitives, which shows the comparisons are unstable.

The objection is damaging but not fatal. It shows that Popper's particular definition cannot rank false theories, which matters because virtually every interesting scientific theory is strictly false. It does not show that the very notion of approaching the truth is incoherent: later writers such as Niiniluoto have proposed measuring truthlikeness by a theory's distance from the complete true description of the world in the relevant language, and on such accounts one false theory can be nearer the truth than another. Still, because the later proposals abandon Popper's purely logical definition, we should conclude that realism about scientific progress is in serious trouble unless truthlikeness can be made precise, and Laudan's pessimism about convergence remains the default position.
