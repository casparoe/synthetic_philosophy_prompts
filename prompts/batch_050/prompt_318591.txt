I am a fourth-year student in a seminar on the philosophy of death, and for my final paper I want to test the Epicurean tradition against Tolstoy's novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich. Our professor argues that the Epicurean position is usually attacked at its strongest formulation and defended at its weakest, and she pointed us to a simile in Book III of Lucretius's On the Nature of Things that she says carries more of the argument than the famous slogan about death being nothing to us (William Ellery Leonard's 1916 translation):

"For just as children tremble and fear all
In the viewless dark, so even we at times
Dread in the light so many things that be
No whit more fearsome than what children feign,
Shuddering, will be upon them in the dark.
This terror, then, this darkness of the mind,
Not sunrise with its flaring spokes of light,
Nor glittering arrows of morning can disperse,
But only nature's aspect and her law."

Please write a critical reading of the novella built around this passage. (The passage does not count toward the length requested below.)

First, interpret the passage on its own terms. What exactly does the simile claim about the structure of our fear of death — that the fear is objectless, or that its object is fabricated, the way a child fabricates monsters? And what work is done by the closing claim that only nature's aspect and her law, not sunlight or morning, can dispel the darkness: is Lucretius saying the fear is immune to ordinary comforts and curable only by understanding what death actually is?

Second, test the diagnosis against Ivan Ilyich. On the side of confirmation: Ivan's pre-illness existence — the careerism, the propriety, the screened-off pleasantness — could be read as precisely the restless, fear-driven life that Lucretius describes elsewhere in Book III as festering from an unacknowledged terror of death. And Ivan's final hour, in which he stops struggling against the force pressing him into the black sack and at last sees his life plainly, ends with the terror dissolving into light, as if lucidity itself were the cure Lucretius promises. On the side of resistance: Ivan's anguish appears to have genuine objects that no child-in-the-dark diagnosis touches — the pain of dying as a process, the goods of which he is about to be deprived, and above all the dawning judgment that his life was not the right life. Notice also that Ivan grieves his irrecoverable childhood as bitterly as his lost future; does that symmetry of grief undermine Lucretius's claim (developed just before this simile) that we should regard our post-mortem nonexistence with the same indifference we feel toward the eternity before our birth?

Third, settle the ending. Read the final scene two ways: as an Epicurean vindication (once death arrives there is no subject left to suffer it, and Ivan's release confirms that the fear, not the thing, was the evil) or as a religious consolation that natural philosophy cannot deliver (grace, not nature's aspect and her law, is what scatters Ivan's dark). Say which reading the text better supports, and do not force a tidy answer if the scene is genuinely undecidable.

Finally, say what the novella contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — for instance, whether it shows that the question "is death bad for you?" cannot be separated from the question "was this life lived rightly?", a connection the standard journal literature treats as a distraction.
