The homework question was: "State the dogmatism paradox as an explicit argument. Identify which epistemic principle does the heavy lifting in generating the puzzle, and briefly assess one possible response to it." It's out of 10 points.

Here's the student's answer:

"The dogmatism paradox goes like this. Suppose I know that p, say that my flight leaves at 6pm. Since p is true, any evidence that appears to tell against p must be misleading evidence -- that's just what misleading means. But I know that p, and I know that if p is true then any counterevidence is misleading, so by closure I know that any counterevidence is misleading. And if I know some piece of evidence is misleading, I'm rational to simply dismiss it. So knowledge licenses me to ignore any evidence against what I know, which seems absurdly closed-minded. The principle doing the work is closure: the argument derives the dogmatic conclusion precisely by closing knowledge under known implication, so the puzzle is that an attractive principle yields an unattractive result. This is closely related to the surprise examination paradox, where similar reasoning about knowledge generates a bizarre conclusion. One response is to note that the reasoning at most covers evidence I haven't yet encountered; the moment misleading counterevidence actually arrives and I hold it in my hands, the paradox shows I'm still fully rational to disregard it, since I already know it's misleading. Incidentally, this whole puzzle is basically an objection to Pryor-style dogmatism about perception, since that view says seemings give justification without needing independent support, which is exactly the closed-mindedness at issue."
