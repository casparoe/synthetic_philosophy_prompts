I produce a long-form philosophy podcast, and next month I'm recording with a metaphysician of mind whose new book restates and defends the conceivability argument against physicalism: because a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me lacking phenomenal consciousness is ideally conceivable, such a zombie is metaphysically possible, and therefore physicalism is false. She is well read and quick on her feet, and my previous interviews on this topic have tended to stall out at polished restatements of the standard dialectic — conceivability, possibility, conclusion, repeat. I want eight to ten questions that push her onto genuinely contested ground, and for each one, a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give (plus, where useful, a second follow-up for her fallback answer).

Concretely, I need at least the following ground covered. First, one question pressing the inference from ideal conceivability to metaphysical possibility, given that she grants conceivability is a fallible guide in familiar a posteriori cases like water and H2O — and a follow-up for when she insists the zombie case is different because both of the relevant concepts pick out their referents directly, with no contingent modes of presentation in between. Second, one question on the phenomenal concept strategy, the type-B materialist's claim that our special first-personal way of thinking about experience generates the appearance of an explanatory gap without any gap in reality — with a follow-up for her predictable reply that phenomenal concepts are themselves physical states that zombies would share by stipulation, so the strategy only relocates the problem. Third, one question about the cost of the conclusion: if zombies are possible and phenomenal properties are non-physical, what secures their causal relevance to the physical world, and is she really comfortable with epiphenomenalism about the very thing she says matters most — and a follow-up for when she gestures at overdetermination or at revising the causal closure of the physical. Fourth, one question engaging illusionism, the claim that we don't instantiate phenomenal consciousness but merely represent ourselves as doing so, so the zombie intuition misdescribes us from the start — with a follow-up for her likely answer that the illusionist has simply changed the subject, since the illusion itself would have to seem like something.

Finally, I'd like a closing question asking what it would take for her to abandon the argument — what result, objection, or theoretical development would actually move her — because she has a reputation for treating the case as near-unassailable, and I want to see whether she can state conditions under which she'd concede. Please keep the questions pointed enough that she can't answer them by re-delivering material from the book's first three chapters.
