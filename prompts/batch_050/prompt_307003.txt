I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and a few years back I got pulled into the recurring threads there and on LessWrong about how moral norms actually change — the ones arguing over Kwame Anthony Appiah's The Honor Code and whether abolition, dueling, and footbinding ended because of moral arguments, material interests, or shifts in honor. I now help run an online reading seminar in that orbit, and I'm grading participant essays from our session on moral revolutions. Below is the essay prompt I set and one participant's answer. **Essay prompt I set:** Appiah contends that moral revolutions are not brought about by moral arguments. Drawing on at least two of his three case studies (the end of the Atlantic slave trade and slavery in the British Empire, the disappearance of dueling among English gentlemen, and the end of footbinding in China), explain the mechanism he proposes and assess whether the Williams thesis about the decline of slavery is a rival to his account or compatible with it.

**The participant's answer:**

Appiah's central claim is that the moral arguments against practices like slavery, dueling, and footbinding were all available, and indeed widely known, long before the practices ended. Abolition therefore cannot be explained by the discovery or spread of new arguments. What changed, on his account, was honor: each practice came to be seen as dishonorable, and honor — the desire to be entitled to respect — did the motivational work that arguments alone could not.

In the case of slavery, Appiah argues that the moral case against it matured through the eighteenth century and finally persuaded Parliament in 1807 and 1833. The new element was that slavery came to be seen as a stain on British national honor, and the growing dignity attached to free wage labor made holding slaves dishonorable for those who aspired to respectability. The case of dueling is more straightforward. Dueling was suppressed once it was finally criminalized and the prohibition was seriously enforced: gentlemen could no longer afford the legal risk, and the practice withered within a few decades.

Footbinding offers Appiah's cleanest case. The practice persisted for a millennium despite known harms, then collapsed within roughly a generation. The mechanism was the anti-footbinding societies, whose members pledged neither to bind their daughters' feet nor to let their sons marry women with bound feet, combined with the Chinese literati's reframing of footbinding as a source of national shame in the eyes of foreign powers. Here honor clearly did the work: once a family's marriageability and a nation's standing were at stake, the practice unraveled with remarkable speed.

As for the Williams thesis — that slavery was abolished because it had ceased to be economically valuable to British capitalism, with humanitarian sentiment serving as rationalization — this is a direct rival to Appiah's account rather than a complement to it. Williams locates the cause in material interests, Appiah in an essentially moral motive, honor. Since the two explanations assign causal priority to fundamentally different kinds of thing, they cannot both be correct; the historical evidence will vindicate at most one of them.
