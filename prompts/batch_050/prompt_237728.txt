I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on a contested letter, and I keep circling a question that is as much craft as philosophy: where does the meaning of a written thing actually live? I have found it useful to borrow, loosely, a three-part scheme associated with Douglas Hofstadter's discussion of messages: a frame message, which announces that something is a message and invites decoding; an outer message, which carries or points to the rules by which the decoding is to proceed; and an inner message, the content the author meant to convey. Do not treat that scheme as sacred — it is my scaffold, not the point.

Write a dialogue, in American English, between two characters I may adapt for the novel. The first, Anselm, holds that a text's meaning is fixed by its inner message, by what the author succeeded in encoding, and that a reader who misses it has simply failed. The second, Blythe, holds that meaning is completed, perhaps even created, at the moment of decoding, so that a reader who finds a coherent reading the author never intended has discovered a genuine meaning of the text rather than made an error.

The dialogue should not be a recital of positions. I want them to argue over a single hard case, because cases discipline disagreement better than abstractions do. The case: a manuscript surfaces whose frame message is in order — it plainly presents itself as a message — but whose outer message is contested. One plausible decoding renders it a heartfelt confession; another, equally coherent decoding renders it a savage parody of confessions; and the historical evidence about the author underdetermines which decoding the author actually employed. Anselm and Blythe should each have to say where the meaning of this manuscript resides, and each should press the other on the costs of their answer: Anselm should have to confront that his view makes the manuscript's meaning possibly unknowable even to a perfect reader, and Blythe should have to confront that her view seems to license readers to make any text mean anything. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments, concede where concession is forced, and use the frame/outer/inner vocabulary only where it genuinely sharpens a point rather than as decoration.

Two requests about register and honesty. First, keep both characters equally intelligent and equally well-informed; I have no use for a scene in which one of them is a foil, and neither should score points the other couldn't plausibly answer. Second, if you think my setup rests on a confusion — for instance, if the three-message scheme cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, or if the disagreement as I have framed it collapses into a merely verbal one — say so plainly and explain why, rather than writing a polished scene around a muddle. A brief note after the dialogue, flagging where it leans on contested assumptions about meaning, would also be welcome.
