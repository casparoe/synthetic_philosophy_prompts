I help teach a research ethics seminar, and before next term I want to nail down what I actually believe about when it's permissible to randomize patients in a clinical trial, because my reactions to cases are messier than the textbook slogan about equipoise suggests. Here are my verdicts, going by gut. Randomizing patients between two chemotherapy regimens that are both in routine use and thought to be roughly comparable: clearly fine, even if the enrolling physician would privately pick one for her own mother. A placebo-controlled trial of a new drug for mild, self-limiting insomnia, even though effective treatments already exist: fine, because no one is denied anything that matters much over a few weeks. A placebo-controlled trial of a new drug for a serious infection in a low-income country where the existing effective treatment is simply unavailable: not fine, and I notice this sits uneasily with the insomnia verdict, since in both cases participants get nothing they would otherwise have had. Sham-surgery controls, where half the participants get anesthesia and an incision but no operation: not fine, full stop, even with consent, though I grant the sham is in some sense "just a placebo." A trial that should keep going on my own logic, but where interim results strongly favor one arm: I feel the investigators are obligated to stop early, even if the trend isn't yet statistically conclusive. Randomizing emergency-room patients between two standard resuscitation strategies under deferred consent, when neither is known to be better: fine. A cluster-randomized trial that assigns whole villages to a sanitation intervention versus nothing, without individual consent: fine, and the consent part bothers me less than it probably should.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a trial comparing the standard of care against a cheaper, somewhat less burdensome treatment that is genuinely expected by most experts to be slightly less effective, where the sponsor's real motivation is cost savings for the health system rather than any hope the new option is better. No one believes the two arms are equivalent, which is what seems to license randomization in my first case; but the direction of the expected difference is known, and it's against the patients getting the new treatment. Yet if the cheaper option really is nearly as good, finding out would benefit many future patients.

Is there a principle -- or a small family of principles -- that captures most of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates? I'm especially interested in whether one principle can handle both the insomnia/placebo and the infection/placebo pair without simply citing geography, and whether my acceptance of inert pills but rejection of sham scalpels can be made coherent or is just squeamishness about cutting. Please tell me which of my verdicts the best candidate principle would force me to give up, what it predicts for the cost-saving non-inferiority case, and what it would say about one further case I haven't listed. Finally, indicate how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the specific point in your answer where you feel least sure of yourself.
