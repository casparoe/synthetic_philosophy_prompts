I am a second-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the ethics of climate engineering, and I keep running into an asymmetry in how the assigned literature and policy documents treat two ways of deliberately altering the global atmosphere. A state that emits greenhouse gases at a level foreseeably changing the climate of every other state is treated as exercising something like ordinary sovereign discretion: it may be criticized, but the default assumption is that emissions policy is a domestic matter, constrained only by voluntary international pledges. A state that injected aerosols into the stratosphere to cool the planet, by contrast — even at a level merely offsetting its own warming contribution — is treated in nearly every governance proposal we have read as categorically different: research is grudgingly permitted, but deployment is said to require something close to global consent, and even small outdoor experiments are expected to seek public approval in a way that commissioning a new coal fleet never is. The seminar has also spent a week on the termination problem: once aerosol injection operates at scale, stopping abruptly would produce very rapid warming, so a decision to deploy is not a single act but an open-ended commitment binding successor governments and generations who never consented to it.

My essay asks what normative theory this pattern presupposes, and I would like your help reconstructing it. First: on what distinction does the asymmetry actually rest? A doing-versus-allowing distinction, under which emitting counts as the side effect of permitted activity while injecting counts as intervening on others? An intending-versus-foreseeing distinction? A natural-versus-artificial distinction, under which warming the planet is merely using a shared sink while cooling it is acting upon it? Or a view about where legitimacy comes from? Which of these candidates can bear the weight the practice puts on it, given that the climate harm from emissions is by now clearly foreseen rather than merely foreseen-in-principle? Second, is the practice internally consistent? If the consent requirement for deployment presupposes that legitimacy is procedural rather than outcome-based, does that sit coherently with tolerating an emissions status quo whose outcomes are, by the practice's own lights, catastrophic? Third, what does treating the termination problem as a decisive objection presuppose about our authority to impose obligations on future generations — and is that presupposition consistent with how the same institutions treat constitutions, sovereign debt, and nuclear waste repositories, all of which bind successors without their consent? Fourth, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — which parts of it could be licensed by several rival theories at once, so that no single implicit theory can be read off them? Finally, a classmate proposed that deployment be permitted to any coalition willing to post a bond sized to worst-case termination damages, with no consent threshold at all. What does that proposal presuppose about the wrong of unilateral deployment — that it consists in the uncompensated imposition of risk rather than in the absence of consent — and what would the rest of the practice have to look like if that presupposition were correct?

For each part of your answer, please state how confident you are in the reconstruction you are offering, and close by singling out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, with a sentence on why it is the weak point.
