Last night a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument after rewatching The Green Mile. Her position was that the film is ultimately a sentimental anti-death-penalty parable that cheats: it condemns execution by giving us John Coffey, a man we know to be innocent and even miraculous, and so it argues from the easiest possible case while dodging the hard question of what we owe the guilty. My position was that the film is more philosophically ambitious than that -- that the botched execution of Eduard Delacroix, Percy Wetmore's sadism, Paul Edgecombe's quiet complicity, and Coffey's own weariness amount to an indictment of the institution itself, not merely of its mistakes. Neither of us convinced the other, and I want a reading that settles it more carefully than we managed.

So: read the film through the standard philosophical triad on capital punishment -- retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing the innocent -- and assess which of these the film actually engages and which it evades. Does the Delacroix scene do retributivist work (if even the guilty cannot be executed without cruelty, does retribution survive?), or is it merely a visceral appeal? Does the film ever test deterrence, given that the crimes happen off-screen and punishment never seems to prevent anything? Is Coffey's innocence the load-bearing argument or a distraction from the harder case of Wild Bill Wharton, the actual murderer, whom the film disposes of without trial or ceremony? And what should we make of Edgecombe's supernatural punishment -- a life prolonged past everyone he loves -- as the film's own comment on what just deserts would even look like? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to a position paper, and it should not pretend the philosophy is tidier than it is. Where the film resists the retribution-deterrence-innocence framework entirely, say so and say what it is doing instead.

Please structure your response as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two of substantive analysis, with no introduction and no conclusion.
