After forty years as an atmospheric chemist, I have in retirement been working my way through the philosophical literature on collective action and rational choice, and I keep returning to two concepts that seem to me related in a way I cannot quite pin down. I would like your help thinking them through.

The first is the tragedy of the commons, which any scientist knows: where a resource is shared and each person's use of it imposes costs on everyone, individually rational behaviour aggregates into collective ruin, and the remedy, if there is one, lies in binding each person to restraint they would not choose alone.

The second is a less familiar idea I have encountered in my reading, sometimes called the unilateralist's curse. Suppose a group of agents each has the power to undertake some initiative single-handedly, and suppose the initiative goes ahead if even one of them judges it worthwhile. Then the effective decision rule is not the average or the median judgment of the group but the most optimistic one. Since people tend to underestimate their own fallibility, and since the most enthusiastic member of a large group is very likely to be over-optimistic, initiatives that most members correctly regard as bad ideas will nonetheless be launched. The curse, I take it, is that this happens even when everyone involved is individually rational, well informed by ordinary standards, and acting in good faith. The proposed remedy is a kind of deliberate epistemic humility: each agent should adjust their private judgment downward to account for the fact that they might be the most optimistic member of the group, and should hesitate precisely when others are hesitating.

Here is the case that brought these two ideas together for me. Stratospheric aerosol injection -- the deliberate release of reflective particles into the upper atmosphere to cool the planet -- is, on the technical estimates I trust, cheap enough that a single mid-sized state, or conceivably a consortium of private actors, could undertake it alone. The climate it would alter, however, belongs to everyone, as do the risks: altered rainfall patterns, termination shocks, ozone effects. When I describe this scenario to my old colleagues, half of them immediately reach for commons language -- the atmosphere as a shared resource being unilaterally exploited -- and the other half describe a unilateralist dynamic -- the most reckless or most convinced actor setting policy for seven billion people who were never consulted.

Please compare the tragedy of the commons and the unilateralist's curse as accounts of what goes wrong in cases of this kind. In particular: are these two names for a single underlying structure of collective failure, or genuinely distinct mechanisms that merely happen to converge on the same scenario? Consider where the two diagnoses pull apart -- for instance, whether the problem lies in aggregated self-interest or in the statistical logic of who gets to decide, and whether the failure persists if every actor is public-spirited. Then say which diagnosis you think better fits the geoengineering case, and what follows for institutional design: I am especially interested in whether the remedies each concept suggests (enforced collective restraint on the one hand, deliberation, veto rights, and threshold-raising on the other) are compatible, or whether designing institutions against one of these failure modes could leave us more exposed to the other.
