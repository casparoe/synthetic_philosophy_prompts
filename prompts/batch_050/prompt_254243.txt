So I'm in this philosophy of mind seminar and we're doing the unit on consciousness and time, and I've gotten kind of obsessed with the color phi phenomenon. Quick recap in case it matters: you flash a red dot on the left side of a screen and then a green dot on the right, and people don't report two stationary dots, they see one dot moving across and switching from red to green partway along the path. The spooky part is that the color change appears to happen at a spot that gets reached before the green dot has even flashed, so the experiance somehow seems to anticipate what's coming next.

In the Dennett chapter we read, he lays out two rival theories of what the brain is doing here. The Stalinesque theory says the whole little movie (dot glides over, changes color halfway) gets edited together backstage before it ever reaches consciousness, like evidence doctored before the trial. The Orwellian theory says you genuinely experience the two stationary dots first, and then memory gets rewritten a moment later so that all you can ever report or remember is the smooth moving version. I have to write my term paper arguing for one of these, and honestly the Orwellian option seems obviously false to me -- if the unedited two-dots version really was conscious, even for a split second, wouldn't there have to be some moment where it was "on stage" where you could in principle catch it? The Stalinesque story just says the brain holds off presenting anything for a couple hundred milliseconds until it knows the full sequence, which strikes me as way more parsimonious.

So my paper plan is: argue the Stalinesque model is the empirically better-supported theory, and claim the debate could in principle be settled with fine enough neural timing measurements, e.g. if we could pinpoint the exact moment the moving-dot representation gets assembled and compare it against the exact moment of conscious awareness, whichever comes first tells you which theory is true.
