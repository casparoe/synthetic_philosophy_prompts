So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been interviewing people about how panels and committess actually reach decisions, and I keep running into a weird structural thing I want to write about but can't quite get my head around. Here's the cleanest version of it, from appellate courts (though I've seen basically the same thing in hiring committees and grant panels): a three-judge panel has to decide whether a defendant's conviction gets overturned, and the law is clear that you overturn only if two things are both true — say, that the police search was unlawful AND that the remaining evidence wouldn't have convicted him anyway. The court votes on each legal question separately, because that's how they're supposed to reason transparently, and a majority of judges says the search was unlawful, and a (different) majority says the remaining evidence wouldn't have convicted. So the court, as a court, has now "held" both things, and by its own stated rule it has to overturn the conviction. But here's the kicker: only one of the three judges actually thinks the conviction should be overturned. Each judge's own vote on the outcome was against overturning, twice over. The court's conclusion is one that two-thirds of its members reject.

My editors think this is just a glitch. I'm not so sure, and this is where I need help from someone philosophically literate. What does a court's practice of voting issue-by-issue — rather than just voting on the final outcome — actually presuppose about what a "court decision" even is? Does it presuppose that the court is some kind of agent in its own right, with beliefs that have to hang together logically, even when no individual member holds them all? Or does it presuppose something more modest, like that courts are really just in the business of answering questions, and outcomes are downstream? And on the flip side: if a court instead voted directly on the outcome (overturn or not), what would that practice presuppose — that majority rule is about aggregating people's all-things-considered judgments rather than their reasons, or what?

I especially want to know whether the two procedures rest on *rival* theories of group decision-making or whether they're both defensible under one theory that just hasn't been spelled out. And practically: if you took seriously the idea that the court-as-agent view is wrong, what would have to change about how courts publish opinions, since right now the "holding" of the court is supposed to be binding precedent even in cases like mine where most judges privately dissent from where it leads? Feel free to tell me if I'm conflating two different problems here — I suspect I might be.
