As a PhD student working on categorization and perceptual representation, I keep running into a question I can't settle: what makes a shape a token of the letter 'a'? I'd like you to take on the role of an expert philosopher of mind and language, think carefully, and work through my cases systematically before proposing anything. Here are my verdicts, going by gut. A Times New Roman 'a' and a single-story handwriting-style 'ɑ' are both 'a's, despite sharing almost no shape. A child's wobbly, lopsided 'a' is still an 'a'. A perfectly rendered 'a' inside a heavily distorted CAPTCHA is an 'a'. A mirror-reversed 'a' is not an 'a', though a slightly rotated or slanted one is. A Greek alpha that happens to be pixel-identical to a Latin 'a' is not an 'a'. The exact same curve counts as 'a' in one word of a handwritten note but as a sloppy 'o' in the next, if that's how the writer meant it. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a shape in the sand, identical to an 'a', produced purely by wind and footsteps — I'm inclined to say it is not an 'a', but I worry this verdict sits badly with my claim that a printed 'a' is an 'a' regardless of what any particular viewer recognizes, and with the fact that I let the child's wobbly token in on shape-adjacent grounds. Is there a single principle — presumably some mix of shape similarity, causal-historical origin, and producer intention — that captures all of these, or close? Please state the principle precisely, flag which clause each case motivates, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you'd advise me to drop, and say what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a blackletter 'a' that most modern readers can't recognize, and a font designer's deliberate 'a' that is shaped exactly like a 'd' missing its ascender.
