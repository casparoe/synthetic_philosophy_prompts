I've been keeping a list of situations where my gut tells me whether one person's small contribution matters morally, and I can't figure out whether the gut is tracking anything coherent. Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. One: buying conventionally farmed chicken is a bit wrong, even though I know full well that no supermarket will ever order one fewer bird because of my shop. Two: voting in a general election is worth doing even in a safe seat, and I'd think less of a friend who couldn't be bothered. Three: chanting louder at a football match when the crowd is already deafening is completely optional -- nobody could sensibly be obliged to add their voice. Four: dropping litter on a beach that is already filthy is still wrong, and it wouldn't excuse me to point out that my crisp packet changes nothing. Five: if a firing squad of ten will kill the prisoner regardless, volunteering to be the eleventh shooter is monstrous, not morally neutral. Six: when a community project only gets funded if ten thousand people each chip in £20, my £20 genuinely matters, and if it falls short by one pledge I'd feel responsible in a way I never do about the chant.

Now, cases one, two, five and six all feel like the contribution matters; three and four pull in opposite directions despite looking structurally identical -- in both, the outcome (a roaring crowd, a dirty beach) is guaranteed with or without me, yet I excuse the quiet fan and condemn the litterer. And case five baffles me, because the eleventh shot is the paradigm of a wasted contribution, and still it seems the worst thing on the list. My friends offered me a tidy principle -- an act contributing to a collective outcome is wrong exactly when it raises the expected chance of the outcome occurring -- but that seems to acquit the litterer and the eleventh shooter alike, and I'm not willing to follow it there. Another friend said the principle is about what would happen if everyone did likewise, but surely whether everyone else actually does it must matter too: littering on a pristine beach and a filthy one can't be the same act.

What I want is a principle -- or two or three candidate principles, if one won't do -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which verdict each clause is there to save. Where my verdicts can't all be kept, tell me which one you'd sacrifice and why. And then apply the winning account to the two cases I keep circling without settling: buying fast fashion that I have good reason to think was made in sweatshop conditions, and tipping in a country where everyone tips and my withholding one gratuity will never change the wage structure. Please answer in British English.
