Setup: the standard case. I glimpse a jelly bean under bad lighting and end up about 0.7 that it's red, 0.3 that it's green, without becoming certain of any color proposition. Jeffrey's rule says: keep your conditional credences fixed on the {red, green} partition and shift the unconditional ones to the new distribution. Everyone treats this as the go-to rule for uncertain evidence. Here's the argument that it's actually never needed:

(1) For any Jeffrey shift from prior P to posterior Q on a partition, there exists some proposition A (in a suitably enriched probability space) such that plain conditionalization of P on A lands exactly on Q. This is just a representation fact -- any Jeffrey update can be mimicked by ordinary conditioning once you enrich the space enough.

(2) So an agent who performs the Jeffrey shift could instead have conditionalized on A and arrived at the very same posterior.

(3) Plain conditionalization is the gold standard of updating; wherever it's available to the agent, it's at least as rationally justified as any rival rule.

(4) So wherever Jeffrey's rule delivers a verdict, conditionalization was available and delivers the identical verdict.

(5) The only remaining cases are ones where the Jeffrey shift outruns what conditionalizing on the actual experience would give you -- i.e. cases where rigidity of the conditional credences fails. But there the new distribution over the partition alone doesn't determine anything, so Jeffrey's rule is just arbitrary about the rest.

(6) Therefore Jeffrey conditionalization is either redundant (it coincides with conditionalization) or unjustified (it goes beyond the evidence). Either way, no one is ever rationally required to Jeffrey-conditionalize.
