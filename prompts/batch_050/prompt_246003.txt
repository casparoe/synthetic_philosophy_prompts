Ok this is going to sound unhinged but bear with me. My family has had a running argument for years about whether my late grandad actually said "never trust a man who doesn't like gravy." My aunt swears he said it at Christmas 2014, and it's now carved on his memorial bench, which she paid for, so she is EXTREMELY invested. My dad says grandad hated gravy and would never have said anything of the sort. Three of my cousins claim to "independently remember" it but I'm fairly sure they all just heard it from my aunt at the funeral.

Anyway I recently went down a wikipedia rabbit hole about the quest for the historical Jesus and the "criteria of authenticity" thing -- multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, coherence -- and it occured to me this is literally the same epistemic situation as my family's gravy dispute.

So: write a parody of a historical-Jesus journal article, around 700-800 words, that applies the full criteria-of-authenticity apparatus to the question of whether my grandad really said the gravy line. Dry academic voice, an abstract, footnote-ish asides, invented scholars are fine but don't cite real ones (I don't want fake bibliography entries pretending to be real). The humor should come from the method itself: the criteria pulling in opposite directions, the criterion of embarrassment doing a suspicious amount of argumentative work (was the saying embarrassing enough that nobody would invent it?), the cousins' "independent attestation" collapsing once you trace the sources, rival scholarly camps forming over whether the original word was "gravy" or something else, and a concluding section that reconstructs a confident "portrait" of my grandad that contradicts the portrait in a hypothetical rival article. Somebody who knows the quest literature should laugh and feel mildly called out, but my family should still be able to follow it.

Start with the piece itself -- title and abstract first -- no preface, no commentary, no "here's your parody".
