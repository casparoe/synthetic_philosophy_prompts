I'm preparing a discussion session for an interdisciplinary reading group (a mix of literature students and biologists), and our next pairing is Golding's Lord of the Flies with Chapter IV of Darwin's The Descent of Man, the chapter on the moral sense. In that chapter Darwin advances what he calls a fundamental proposition: roughly, that any animal endowed with well-marked social instincts, including sympathy and a desire for the approval of its fellows, would inevitably develop a conscience once its intellectual powers became sufficiently developed. Morality, on Darwin's picture, is not a thin cultural coating over a selfish animal; it grows directly out of the social instincts themselves, which are older and deeper than reason. This seems to put Darwin directly at odds with the popular reading of Golding's novel, in which civilization is a fragile veneer and the boys' descent under Jack merely peels it back to reveal an innate savagery underneath.

Here is where I'd like the reading to do some real work. On the one hand, an evolutionary psychologist might push back on the veneer reading and argue the novel actually vindicates Darwin: the boys never stop being moral creatures, they just redirect their social instincts — Jack's tribe runs on loyalty, reciprocity, punishment of defectors, and terror of exclusion, and even Roger needs the disinhibiting cover of the group before he can kill. On the other hand, I worry about the just-so story problem running in both directions: the Gould-and-Lewontin-style critique says adaptationist explanations of behavior are often unfalsifiable narratives, but isn't the veneer reading of Golding itself just a just-so story in reverse — a literary narrative treated as if it were evidence about human nature, when Golding simply built his island to show what he wanted to show? A fictional breakdown cannot bear evidential weight, yet novels like this one clearly shaped what generations of readers think "human nature" is.

So my question: read the novel through this triangle — Darwin's proposition, the veneer theory, and the just-so stories critique — and tell me which reading survives. Where does the Darwinian reading illuminate specific moments (I'm thinking of Simon's fate, Piggy's attachment to the conch and to rules, the littluns' beast, and above all the naval officer's arrival, which restores "civilization" in an instant and so seems to cut against both readings), and where does the novel genuinely resist being recruited into the evolutionary psychology debate? Please don't quote the book at length; work from the well-known scenes. I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions — that doesn't distort Darwin to fit Golding or flatten Golding into an illustration — and that ends by saying what, if anything, a novel can legitimately contribute to the science-versus-just-so-stories dispute that the empirical literature cannot.
