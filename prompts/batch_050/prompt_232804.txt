So I'm drafting a novel where the main character, Mirella, checks herself into a voluntary rehab program where you sign a contract saying they won't discharge you for six months no matter how much you beg. In my critique group this week two of the other writers got into a real fight about the scene where her brother shows up in month four and smuggles her out, and honestly I can't tell who's right, which is annoying because it changes the ending. Please act as a philosopher who works on agency and self-governance, think it through carefully, and adjudicate their argument move by move.

Here's how it went:

1. Tomas said the brother is the hero of the scene. Mirella's earlier self signed away her later self's freedom, and by month four she sincerely, articulately wants to leave. A present, competent, clearly stated preference beats a months-old signature. The contract is basically her past self holding her future self hostage.

2. Nina said Tomas has it backwards. Month-four Mirella is in the grip of cravings and withdrawal; the Mirella who signed was the clear-eyed one, so the contract protects her *real* agency rather than violating it. It's the Ulysses-and-the-mast setup: you bind yourself in advance precisely because you know your future judgment will be impaired, and the people who refuse to untie you are doing you a kindness.

3. Tomas replied that the Ulysses case only works because the sirens' effect is temporary and known in advance -- Ulysses is back to normal an hour later. A six-month program isn't an episode, it's a chunk of a life, and by month four Mirella might not be *impaired* so much as genuinely changed: she's reflected and decided sobriety-at-this-cost isn't what she values anymore. Honoring the old contract anyway means using her present self as a tool of her past self's plans. He said it reminded him of some old philosophy case about a young aristocrat who inherits a fortune and signs papers to donate it later, then changes his politics -- and at some point the signature just stops being authoritative.

4. Nina's comeback: Tomas is just calling every inconvenient commitment a "genuine value change" and every one he likes a "temporary impairment," and he has no principled way to tell them apart from the outside -- especially since rationalization under craving *feels exactly like* reflection from the inside. And if present preference always defeats prior commitment, nobody could ever commit to anything: marriages, mortgages, treaties, constitutions -- the whole point of a commitment is that it survives a future change of heart.

Who's right about which specific points? Rate each of the four moves, tell me what (if anything) both of them are missing, and don't just split the difference to be nice. Keep the whole thing under 400 words -- I mostly need it to decide the ending, not to write a dissertation.
