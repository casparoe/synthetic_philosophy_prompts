Two students in my philosophy of mind reading group got into an extended argument over the causal exclusion problem for mental causation, and I couldn't tell who, if anyone, came out ahead. Here's the exchange as best I remember it. (1) Anya stated the problem: if every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, and we refuse to accept systematic causal overdetermination, then a mental property that is not identical to any physical property can never cause anything — so the non-reductive physicalist is forced to choose between identity and epiphenomenalism. (2) Ben replied that this assumes the mental cause and the physical cause are competitors, but the mental cause is realized by the physical cause; realization is not identity, yet it also isn't the kind of independence the exclusion principle needs, so there is no competition and no overdetermination. (3) Anya said this just relocates the problem: if the mental property's causal powers are wholly inherited from its physical realizer, then the property qua mental makes no difference, and inherited efficacy is epiphenomenalism in friendlier vocabulary. (4) Ben countered that her argument generalizes disastrously — by the same logic, a cell's being a cell does nothing over and above its molecular arrangement, so nothing biological, geological, or economic ever causes anything; a principle that abolishes all higher-level causation proves too much, and the right lesson is that the appropriate cause is the one proportional to the effect — your pain explains why you withdrew your hand better than the exact firing pattern of your C-fibers does. (5) Anya answered that proportionality buys explanatory relevance, not causal relevance: he has quietly switched from asking what causes the withdrawal to asking which description best explains it, thereby conceding causal closure and changing the subject. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or concedes more than its speaker notices, and then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing about the overall structure of the dispute.
