Last night I got into a long and surprisingly heated argument with a friend about whether the dispute between Mozi's impartial care and Confucian graded love is a real practical disagreement or merely a disagreement about how to justify conduct everyone already endorses. My friend claimed that in any realistic dilemma the two views will recommend the same action, with the Mohist simply running the verdict through a mutual-benefit calculation and the Confucian through the gradation of love from family outward. I suspect that's too quick, but I couldn't produce a clean test case on the spot, and I'd like one built to order.

Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications. First, the scenario must involve a genuine conflict of interest between parties near to the agent and parties distant from them -- something like a clinic director allocating scarce treatment slots, or an official distributing disaster relief -- not a case (such as pulling one's own child from a fire) where every plausible view agrees for trivial reasons. Second, the scenario must be one in which Mozi's impartial care and Confucian graded love deliver the *same* verdict about what to do, but where the agreement is visibly contingent rather than principled: there must be a single identifiable parameter of the case such that, if that parameter is removed, the Mohist verdict flips while the Confucian verdict stays fixed. The point is to show that the Mohist's conclusion was reached only because a feature of the situation made impartial conduct reciprocally advantageous, not because impartiality as such was doing the work. Constraints: the scenario should be realistic and set in an ordinary institutional context, describable in one paragraph; no stipulating that one theory is right; and the agreement between the two views must be generated from the theories themselves, not baked into the setup.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following: walk through the reasoning by which each view arrives at the shared verdict, making clear where the two justifications diverge even though the conclusions coincide; name the load-bearing parameter and show exactly how removing it reverses the Mohist recommendation while leaving the Confucian one intact; and then give the strongest Mohist counterattack -- presumably the response that impartial care was always meant to track conditions of mutual benefit, so that the contingency I've engineered is a feature of the doctrine rather than an embarrassment to it. Finally, tell me honestly whether you think that counterattack succeeds, or whether a case of this form genuinely shows the two traditions agree in practice only by accident. This is meant to settle, or at least sharpen, the argument with my friend, so I'd rather have a verdict with reasons than an even-handed shrug.
