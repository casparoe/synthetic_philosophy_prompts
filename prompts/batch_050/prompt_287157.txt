So I'm a novelist, working on a book about a woman who runs a small forecasting outfit — she's uncannily good at putting numbers on things, geopolitics, her friends' marriages, all of it. The scene I keep circling is this: she has to decide whether to take a job in another country, and she sits down to do what she always does, assign a probability to the outcome — except the outcome is her own decision. She tries to write down "70% I take the job" and it feels like it short-circuits something. If she already knows she'll take it, what is there to decide? If she doesn't know, where does the number come from? I mentioned this to my critique partner and he shrugged and said that's just a quirk of her psychology, not a real philosophical problem — a person can obviously predict their own behavior, we do it constantly. I said there's a genuine tension: while you're honestly deliberating, treating your future action as just another event to be predicted seems to collide with treating it as up to you. He put his credence that there's a real incoherence here at like 0.05. I'm around 0.7, but honestly I might just be attached to my scene.

Give me your credence that the tension is genuine — specifically, that a rational agent in the middle of sincere, unresolved deliberation cannot coherently assign a subjective probability to which option she'll end up choosing (or that doing so undermines the deliberation itself) — but decomposed, not a bare number.
