I'm a PhD student working at the boundary of normative ethics and decision theory, and I've become convinced of a position I want stress-tested. The position is this: when people hold genuinely different moral views, there often exist exchanges in which each party does something their own view counts as somewhat costly or mildly regrettable but which the other's view values greatly, in return for the other doing likewise — and such exchanges can be genuine moral improvements by the lights of each party's own theory, not merely pragmatic accommodations. Think of a deontologist and a consequentialist who each divert some of their charitable giving to the other's preferred cause in exchange for the same; each theory gets more of what it values than either could secure unilaterally. I take the analogy to economic gains from trade to run deep: diversity of moral views, like diversity of preferences, creates room for mutually improving exchange, and pursuing it is often the morally right response to disagreement rather than a surrender of one's convictions. I also take this to be stronger than ordinary moral compromise, where parties settle on an outcome nobody ranks first, since in the cases I care about each party acts so as to produce what they regard as a net moral gain.

What would count against this position? I'd like a map of its exposure, ordered from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like it to go beyond the two objections I already know — that intertheoretic value comparison is impossible, so there is no fact of the matter about whether a trade improves things by each party's lights, and that the complicity worry is decisive, since intentionally enabling what your own view regards as serious wrongdoing cannot be laundered by reciprocation. For each defeater, please indicate whether a committed defender of moral trade would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as a misdescription, and on what grounds. I'd also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, what it recommends differently from simply standing fast on one's own theory, from second-order norms of fair compromise, or from treating disagreement as evidence that should shrink one's confidence. Finally, I'm worried the position is quietly unfalsifiable: defenders can always exclude a purported trade as impure, misdescribed, or not truly valued by one of the theories. If that's right, tell me whether it's a defect in a view of this kind or just the normal texture of normative theorizing. What would persuade me fastest is a case where all the trading conditions are clearly met and the exchange is still plainly wrong, together with an explanation of which premise that case forces me to drop.
