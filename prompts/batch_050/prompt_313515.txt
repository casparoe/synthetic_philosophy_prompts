I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, working mostly on causal inference in the behavioral sciences, and I'm putting together a proposal for a small internal grant my department offers for six months of research time. I want to work on heritability, but on a narrower and, I think, less trodden question than the usual ones. The standard story I keep encountering goes like this: within-group heritability estimates are routinely invoked in public and academic debate to draw conclusions about whether a trait is "malleable," whether interventions are worthwhile, or whether group differences could be environmentally produced; and philosophers and statisticians have spent decades replying that heritability is population- and environment-relative, that high heritability is compatible with large environmental effects, and that nothing about intervention efficacy follows from variance decomposition alone. My sense is that both sides of this exchange are now so rehearsed that they talk past each other, and that the interesting live question has shifted: does a high within-group heritability estimate, conditional on plausible assumptions about the *kinds* of interventions we could actually deploy (e.g., interventions that move existing environmental variables within their observed range, versus interventions that introduce entirely novel environmental features), carry *any* defeasible information about the expected effect size of those interventions, or is the skeptics' "no implications whatsoever" conclusion strictly correct?

Write the one-page grant proposal for this project. State the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than dissolving into the familiar dispute. Explain why the existing dialectic — the standard examples (heritable traits transformed by environmental shifts, low-heritability traits that are hard to change) and the standard rejoinders — leaves this particular question open, since those examples typically target crude inferential moves rather than the conditional, assumption-explicit version I want to examine. Describe the approach I'd take: I expect it to involve a careful analysis of what variance decomposition licenses under explicit causal-model assumptions, worked through a small number of stylized cases, rather than new empirical work; explain why this formal/conceptual route is better suited to the question than, say, a review of intervention studies or a survey of how scientists actually use heritability. Say what a successful result would look like (e.g., a precise statement of the conditions under which heritability bounds or informs intervention effects, or a proof that no such conditions exist outside trivial cases) and what would count as the project failing.
