Need help with a best man speech. My college roommate is getting married in a few weeks, he's a financial economist, and for about a decade his favorite party trick has been the no-trade theorem: the idea that between rational agents with common priors, a willing counterparty is itself bad news -- if someone smart is happy to take the other side of your bet, that's evidence you're the sucker, and once everyone knows everyone is rational, and knows that everyone knows it, the trade just never happens. Roughly half the wedding guests are his old PhD cohort who will heckle me mercilessly if I mangle any of this. The other half are the bride's relatives, mostly nurses and public school teachers.

Here's the hook I want the speech built on. His standard line about the engagement, which he has said at least fifty times, is that both of them are convinced they got the better end of the deal. Adorable -- and, by his own favorite theorem, impossible. Two rational people can't both walk away from the same transaction sure they won it; at least one of them has to be wrong. So I want to open by formally charging him, in front of witnesses, with violating the no-trade theorem at his own wedding, then spend the middle of the speech finding the actual way out, and it has to be an exit the economists in the room will grant, not wordplay. Does the theorem not apply because marriage isn't a bet about some common fact but gains from trade between people with different endowments? Does it fail because love breaks the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption? Something about the priors? I want the resolution to feel earned, and I'd like the bride to come out of it looking like the person who understood the economics better than he did all along.

About five minutes. Warm, but with teeth. And definately no "marriage is an efficient market" jokes, he's made them all himself.
