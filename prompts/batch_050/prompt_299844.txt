Some background: I'm a retired experimental physicist (optics, mostly) who has spent the last couple of years reading philosophy of mind for fun, and I've gotten stuck on something and want to see the strongest case for a position I'm instinctively against.

The setup, as I understand it: in the color phi experiment, a subject sees a dot flashed at one spot, then a different-colored dot flashed at a nearby spot a fraction of a second later, and what they report experiencing is a single dot that moves across the screen and changes color somewhere in the middle. The weird part is the timing. The subject seems to experience the color switch at a point in space and time *before* the second dot has even appeared -- so how did the experience "know" what color to switch to?

Philosophers apparently use this to motivate two rival stories. On one (Dennett calls it Stalinesque, like the show trials), the brain fabricates the final narrative after both dots are in: there was never a moment when the subject experienced the first dot just sitting there, the finished experience is cooked up once all the evidence arrives. On the other (Orwellian, like the rewritten newspapers), the subject really did experience the first dot alone, but the memory of that experience is retroactively overwritten so fast that the subject can never report it, and sincerely misremembers what happened.

Here's what I want: please argue that no experiment, not even in principle, could ever decide between these two stories. As an experimentalist this strikes me as almost certainly wrong -- give me reaction times, masking paradigms, neural recordings with millisecond resolution, *something* -- but the people who defend undecidability here seem to think the two stories are engineered to make exactly the same predictions for any possible probe, including the subject's own reports and memories. Make that argument as strongly as you can. Address specifically (a) why appeals to "what the subject would have said if asked at the right microsecond" don't break the tie, (b) why neural data about when representations form don't break the tie either, and (c) whether the argument generalizes: does it show something deep about the timing of consciousness, or does it just show we chose two cleverly unfalsifiable descriptions?

One more thing: if you think the undecidability claim actually *fails* -- that some conceivable evidence would favor one story -- I'd rather you say so and tell me where the argument breaks than paper over it. I can handle being wrong, I've had forty years of practice.
