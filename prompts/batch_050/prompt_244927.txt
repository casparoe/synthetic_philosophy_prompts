A guy in my law school reading group cornered me with this argument and I can't find the mistake, but the conclusion can't be right. (1) In a civil case the plaintiff wins iff his story is more probable than not -- that's just what "preponderance of the evidence" means. (2) Take the gatecrasher case: 1000 people attend a rodeo, only 499 paid, and the organizers sue a randomly picked attendee with no other evidence against him. It's more likely than not (501/1000) that this defendant didn't pay. (3) So by (1) the organizers should win against him, and indeed against every attendee one by one. (4) But obviously no court should find for the organizers on that alone, so (1) must be wrong: bare statistical probability can't discharge the burden, you need something individualized or causal on top. (5) But DNA match evidence is also bare statistics -- it just says the random-match probability is one in a billion -- and courts happily convict on DNA alone. (6) So either courts are being inconsistent about statistics, or the whole idea that gatecrasher cases show something wrong with statistical proof is confused, since by the same logic DNA convictions are illegitimate too. He says the reasonable conclusion is that the preponderance standard is incoherent. I suspect the move from (4) to (5) hides something, since one in a billion feels different from 501/1000, but I can't say what without hand-waving. Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs for actual legal practice. End with \answer{step N} naming the step you'd give up.
