I had a long argument with a friend last night about distributive justice, and I'd like help reconstructing what principle she was actually defending. She insists she follows no theory and simply judges case by case, but I'm convinced there's a rule under her verdicts. Here they are, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them:

1. On Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example — a basketball star signs a contract giving him twenty-five cents from every ticket sold, a million fans happily pay, and he ends the season far richer than everyone else in a society whose initial distribution was, by hypothesis, just — she said the resulting inequality is entirely just and nobody has a legitimate complaint.
2. Taxing the star's earnings at forty percent to fund a public library: unjust, she said, a taking of what is his, however worthy the library.
3. A talented worker who freely chooses part-time hours and a modest income while her peers grow rich through long hours: no injustice, no matter how wide the gap becomes.
4. A government that confiscates inherited wealth purely to make holdings more equal: unjust.
5. Seizing a watch from a thief and returning it to the person he stole it from: just, and in fact required.
6. A billionaire who acquired every cent through honest, voluntary, uncoerced transactions: his fortune is untouchable, she said, even while others in the same society struggle to afford small comforts.

The new case: On an island whose initial distribution everyone agrees was just, a long series of wholly voluntary, fully informed, uncoerced trades — no theft, no fraud, no deception — gradually leaves one person owning every source of fresh water. His neighbors must now pay whatever price he sets, or go thirsty. He did nothing but trade shrewdly with willing partners.

Here's what I want from you. First, predict her verdict on the island case, with your reasoning. Second, state the rule you take her to be implicitly running, and identify at least one rival rule that fits verdicts 1–6 just as well but diverges on the island; say what each rule predicts there. Third, describe the further case whose verdict would best discriminate among the surviving rules. Finally, tell me what would change your prediction: if she responded to some further case in a particular way, or if I have misdescribed one of her verdicts, which part of your analysis would be the first to fall?
