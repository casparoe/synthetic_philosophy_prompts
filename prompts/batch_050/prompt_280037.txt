So my book club finished Brave New World last week and we got into a two-hour argument that basically came down to whether it's secretly a novel about wireheading, and I want help thinking it through before next meeting. My friend Mark's position: Huxley basically invented the wireheading thought experiment decades early, twice over. Once with soma -- a drug that just hands you the reward, no accomplishment required, and everyone's fine with that -- and once, more sneakily, with the conditioning and decanting, which doesn't hijack anyone's reward signal but engineers the reward function itself so that every caste genuinely loves exactly the life it's stuck with. An Epsilon isn't a frustrated person being pacified; there was never a frustrated person to begin with. Mark says that's actually scarier than wireheading, because wireheading at least leaves a gap between what you feel and what your life is like, and Huxley's move closes the gap.

I pushed back that this can't be the whole story, because the standard philosophical objection to wireheading (and to Nozick's experience machine, which obviously comes later) is about losing contact with reality -- and Huxley's citizens mostly aren't deceived about their reality. They know they're bottled and conditioned, they know what soma does, and they don't care. So if the novel still horrifies us, the horror can't just be "they're not in touch with the truth." Which makes me think the Savage's actual objection in the big showdown with Mustapha Mond is something different -- something about needing the possibility of suffering, danger, tragedy, God, all the stuff Mond openly concedes they've traded away for stability and happiness. But then I get confused about what exactly the claim is: that pleasure without those things is worthless, or that it isn't even really pleasure, or that a person engineered to want only that isn't really a person?

So what I'd love: a reading of the novel through wireheading and reward tampering that figures out which scenes cash out which critique. Like, is Lenina's whole arc a case of someone whose reward system works perfectly and that's precisely the tragedy? Is John's self-punishment at the lighthouse a doomed attempt to un-wirehead himself, and is the crowd treating it as entertainment Huxley's way of saying even revolt gets absorbed into the pleasure economy? And where does the wireheading reading break down -- I'm sure it does somewhere, because Mond is a more thoughtful defender of the system than any wireheading skeptic's strawman, and he seems to win the argument on the page. If the novel ends up saying something against engineering contentment that neither soma-as-wireheading nor the experience machine objection quite captures, I want to know what it is, because that's what I'm going to argue at book club.
