I am running a graduate reading group on personal identity this term, and next week I am supposed to defend Locke's account against the standard objections. In preparing, I tried to write out the strongest version of the objection myself, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove far more than Reid ever intended — it appears to show not that Locke's criterion needs repair, but that there is no such thing as personal identity over time at all, and hence that nobody is ever justly punished or held to a promise. I find that conclusion absurd, but I cannot locate the step where the argument goes wrong, and I am now worried it is actually valid and I simply have to pay a price somewhere. Here it is. (1) A person existing at one time is the same person as a person existing at an earlier time if and only if the later person can remember, from the inside, some experience of the earlier person — this is the memory criterion, and it is the best candidate we have for a non-circular, non-physical criterion of personal identity. (2) Identity is transitive: if the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, then the general is the boy. (3) Memory across a whole life is not transitive: take Reid's case — the old general remembers his bravery as a young officer, the young officer remembered being flogged as a schoolboy, but the general no longer remembers the flogging at all. (4) By (1) and (3), the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, but the general is not the boy — contradicting (2). (5) The only way to avoid the contradiction without ad hoc patchwork is to deny that any relation like memory could constitute identity, since any genuine criterion must respect the logic of identity, and memory demonstrably does not. (6) But (5) generalizes: every proposed psychological criterion will rest on relations that fade, overlap, and fail of transitivity across a long life, and bodily criteria fare no better over total gradual replacement. (7) Therefore there is no criterion of personal identity over time, talk of the same person at different times is a convenient fiction, and practices like punishing the general for the officer's crimes are without foundation.

What I would most like to keep are (2) — giving up the transitivity of identity feels like giving up on logic itself — and the conclusion that the general really is answerable for what the officer did, since our entire practice of responsibility seems to presuppose it. I am more willing to sacrifice (1) if I must, but I want to know exactly what I lose by doing so. Please tell me which premise or step you would reject, whether the argument even needs the biconditional strength I built into (1) or whether a weaker one-way reading of the memory criterion already breaks at some later step, and why the faulty step looked innocent. Then price the main rescue strategies: treating memory as merely evidence of identity rather than constitutive of it; replacing direct memory with overlapping chains of connected memories so that transitivity is restored by construction; and insisting that 'remembers' is factive and already presupposes identity, so the criterion was circular from the start. For each, say what new problem it inherits — I am especially suspicious that the overlapping-chains repair buys transitivity at the cost of collapse in branching cases, where one earlier person is psychologically continuous with two later ones — and which rescue, if any, leaves me able to tell the reading group that punishing the general is just.
