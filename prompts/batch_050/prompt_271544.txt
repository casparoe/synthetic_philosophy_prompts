So I'm TAing a phil of mind seminar this semester (long story, the prof owes me) and one of my students handed in an answer on the Dennett unit that I need to grade by Friday. Regular readers of the Sequences will know this territory, but I've been staring at it too long and want a second pair of eyes before I commit to a grade.

Here's the exam question I set:

"Explain Dennett's Chase and Sanborn cases from Quining Qualia. What are the two competing hypotheses each character faces about what has happened to him, and why can't either character settle the question from the inside? What does Dennett take the cases to show about qualia — and be careful to distinguish what he thinks the cases show from what he thinks they do not show."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Dennett describes two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who have both tasted the same brand of coffee for years and both now complain that something is off. Chase concludes that his memory of how the coffee used to taste must have become distorted, while Sanborn concludes that his taste receptors have changed so the coffee literally tastes different to him now. Dennett's point is that neither man can introspectively determine which of these two hypotheses — altered current reactions vs. altered memory of past reactions — is the true one, because any appeal to memory just re-invokes the very faculty under suspicion, and no amount of careful attending to one's own experience can break the tie. Outside observers can't settle it either, since both hypotheses produce identical behavioral and physiological predictions.

Dennett then draws his famous skeptical conclusion: the facts about our qualia exist, but they are permanently hidden from us. There is a real answer to whether Chase's qualia inverted, but neither introspection nor science can ever find it. In this way Dennett undermines the picture of qualia as intrinsic, ineffable, private properties directly apprehensible by their owner — because if such properties existed, the owner would presumably be in a position to know about them, and the cases show she is not. The lesson is one of epistemic humility about our own minds."

The surface structure looks competent to me, but the question explicitly asked students to separate Dennett's actual conclusion from nearby misreadings, and I have a nagging feeling something in here is off in a way a skim would miss.
