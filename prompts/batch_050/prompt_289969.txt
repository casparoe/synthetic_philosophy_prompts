Write a satirical internal memorandum, roughly 600 words, from the newly appointed Chief Epistemology Officer of a quantitative trading firm, addressed to all staff, announcing that the firm will cease all trading forthwith. The officer's reasoning should state the no-trade theorem (Milgrom–Stokey) correctly before parodying its application: under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, a counterparty's willingness to trade is itself evidence about the value of the position, and updating on that evidence dissolves both sides' willingness to trade. The officer then extends the logic across the whole firm, and every joke should be a genuine result pushed past its domain rather than a misunderstanding of one: the weekly analysts' meeting is abolished because rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree (Aumann) and should therefore converge on the spot; the research division is declared at once indispensable and self-undermining, since if prices impound all available information nobody is compensated for gathering any, while if nobody gathers any there is nothing for prices to impound (Grossman–Stiglitz); the officer regretfully declines his own salary, the firm's willingness to pay it constituting decisive evidence that he would have accepted less; and the memo itself is to be re-sent indefinitely, on the grounds that a single emailed announcement yields at best an endless regress of acknowledgments and never the common knowledge that the policy is in force. Append one marginal annotation from the head of trading — the only reply the memo receives, and pointedly unanswered — observing, accurately, that the firm's profits exist precisely because the theorem's assumptions fail. Keep the register deadpan corporate memo-speak throughout: subject line, numbered action items, no winking at the reader. The satire's target is the habit of reading equilibrium theorems as operating instructions, and it should land as fair: a specialist should recognize each absurdity as a real feature of the literature exaggerated rather than invented, while a reader unfamiliar with it should still be able to locate the joke.
