Homework question: "Two Bayesian agents with different priors observe the same growing sequence of data drawn from a fixed statistical model. Results in the theory of Bayesian convergence are often summarized by saying that accumulating evidence 'washes out' the prior. State precisely one theorem in this vicinity (Doob's posterior consistency theorem or the Blackwell–Dubins merging-of-opinions theorem), including its hypotheses and the mode of convergence it asserts. Then explain in what sense the theorem vindicates the claim that priors wash out, and identify at least one respect in which the washing-out claim is weaker than the informal slogan suggests."

Student's answer:

"Take the Blackwell–Dubins merging theorem. Suppose two agents have priors P and Q over a sequence of future observations, and suppose each prior is non-dogmatic in the sense that it assigns positive probability to every open neighborhood of the truth. Then as both agents condition on the same observations, the distance between their predictive distributions for the remaining observations goes to zero almost surely, so their forecasts about the future eventually become indistinguishable even though their priors differed. This is the precise content of the claim that priors wash out: whatever you believe at the start, enough shared evidence brings you into agreement with anyone else who started out open-minded.

Doob's consistency theorem makes the same point from the other direction. It says that if the prior has full support over the parameter space, then as the number of observations grows, the posterior concentrates around the true parameter value, for every possible value the true parameter could take. The measure-zero qualification that sometimes appears in statements of the theorem is a technicality that can be set aside whenever the prior assigns positive density everywhere, since a full-support prior rules out the pathological cases.

Two qualifications are worth noting. First, both results are asymptotic: they say nothing about how much data is needed before agreement or concentration kicks in, so for any finite amount of evidence the priors can still be doing substantial work. Second, the merging result is a statement about agreement between agents, not about agreement with the truth; two priors can merge onto a shared false conclusion if the data are uninformative about the question at issue. But subject to those caveats, the slogan that evidence swamps priors is essentially correct."
