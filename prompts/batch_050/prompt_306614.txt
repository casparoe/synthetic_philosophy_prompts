A friend of mine sits on the priority-setting committee of a large regional health system, and over the years I've kept an informal record of how she votes when the committee must choose between funding programs that help very few people a great deal and programs that help enormous numbers of people trivially. Four of her votes stick with me. First, given the choice between fixing one person's fractured wrist and sparing twenty people an afternoon of mild headache, she funded the wrist without hesitation. Second, when the same wrist was set against sparing ten million people that same mild headache, she flipped and funded the headaches, saying she'd have to be a monster not to. Third, when a program that would save one person's life was set against a program that would spare some astronomically large number of people — 10^25, say — the minor irritation of getting a single dust speck in the eye, she funded the life and said no number of dust specks changes that. Fourth, when one life was set against curing five hundred thousand fractured wrists, she funded the wrists, insisting that at some point the numbers simply win.

Now the committee faces a new decision, and she has asked me what I think before she votes. The choice is between a program that will save one identified person's life and a program that will spare 10^18 people an afternoon of mild headache — the same headache as before, the same life as before, and a budget that stretches to only one of the two. The committee members are predictably split, and the chair has asked each member to defend a vote that coheres with their own past record rather than merely expressing a gut reaction.

Predict her vote, and defend the prediction. Which of her four earlier votes is the closest analog to the new case, and which does the most work in constraining it? More interestingly, I suspect her record is consistent with at least two genuinely different decision rules: something like a flat total-aggregation view on which dust specks carry literally zero weight but headaches and wrists add up without limit, and something like a tiered or capped view on which harms far below a seriousness threshold aggregate only against harms near their own tier, so that headaches can outweigh a wrist but never a life. These rules agree on all four of her past votes but diverge on the new case. Lay out the strongest candidate rules that fit her record, say what each predicts here and why, give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running (and why), and name the further hypothetical case — the one her record doesn't yet cover — that would most cleanly distinguish your favored rule from its nearest rival.
