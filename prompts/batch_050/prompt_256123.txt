I run a small philosophy reading group (we meet in the back room of a pub, which probably explains a lot) and for the past few months we've been stuck on pragmatist theories of truth. Two members in particular -- I'll call them Dan and Rosa -- have basically become the house Peircean and the house Jamesian, and every session ends with them talking past each other about whether truth is what inquiry would settle on at the end of the road or whether it's whatever proves itself useful in the life of the believer. Last week our moderator got fed up and told them to stop arguing and instead co-write a joint statement, the way feuding scientists sometimes do. They've now admitted they can't do it themselves, so it's fallen to me to draft it, and I'm passing the buck to you.

Here's where I think they stand, so you can get the wording right. Dan's side: truth is bound up with what a sufficiently prolonged investigation would converge on, and any belief that wouldn't survive that process isn't true no matter how comforting or practical it is. Rosa's side: truth is something that happens to an idea, an idea becomes true insofar as it helps us cope, and a belief that works -- gets someone through grief, organises their experience, pays its way -- has as much claim to truth as any scientific result. The flashpoint every single week is a case Rosa loves: an elderly woman who firmly believes her late husband forgave her for something, which nothing could ever confirm or refute, and whose belief clearly makes her remaining years livable. Rosa says the belief is true in the only sense that matters for beliefs of that kind; Dan says it's at best a beneficial fiction and calling it true just confuses two questions. Meanwhile they both keep insisting they agree more than they disagree, but neither can say exactly on what.

So: write the joint statement. I want (a) a numbered list of claims both would sign -- presumably something about truth not being a bare copy-relation to a mind-independent world, about beliefs being rules for action, about fallibilism -- phrased carefully enough that neither can later say "that's not what I meant"; (b) a short table of their remaining disagreements, each stated in neutral wording both would accept as a fair description of their own position; (c) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that answering them would force at least one of them to budge -- and for each crux, a concrete case or consideration both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how the case should be analysed recorded in advance. If some of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal (I have a strong hunch the word "useful" is doing at least three different jobs in Rosa's mouth), say which part and why, and rewrite that bit so the real disagreement underneath, if any, comes out cleanly. Keep it under 1200 words so they can actually print it for the group.
