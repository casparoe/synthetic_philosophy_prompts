I run product at a mid-size sleep and meditation app, and once a week my team decides what to build next based on behavorial metrics our data team hands us. I've noticed we keep making the same mistake: treating whatever users do as evidence of what they want. I want a reusable decision procedure we can run in a thirty-minute meeting that takes any behavior pattern and tells us whether it plausibly reveals an actual preference, or is better explained by constraints, defaults, or a self-control problem -- basically turning the stuff inverse reinforcement learning people worry about (inferring a reward function from behavior, behavior as choice-under-constraints, irrational agents) into steps a non-specialist can follow. One step has to test for what I call "shadow preferences": patterns that look like revealed preference but are really just the path of least resistance (nobody ever changed their notification settings; did they choose the defaults, or did the defaults choose them?). For each step say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure has to stop and hand things to an actual user study instead, and where the answer is a judgment call no checklist can settle. Then run it on the two cases we're arguing about right now: users binge-playing our sleep stories at 2am (engagement numbers great, exit surveys full of regret), and free-tier users never touching the paid tier (a revealed preference for free, or just a default nobody questions?).

Format: exactly five numbered items, a few sentences each, no intro or wrap-up -- the first three items the procedure's steps in order, the fourth the judgment-call points, the fifth applying the procedure to both of my cases.
