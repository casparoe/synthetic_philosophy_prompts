I help run a fortnightly philosophy society at my uni (mix of second and third years, usually 12-16 people show up, we get a seminar room for 90 minutes) and next month it's my turn to lead. We've been circling around rational choice theory all term and I want to do a session on Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle from his paper The Puzzle of the Self-Torturer. Quick version in case it helps: Quinn imagines someone with a device fitted to them with a dial that has a huge number of settings, where each click up delivers a very slightly more painful electric shock -- so slight that you genuinely can't feel the difference between one setting and the next -- and every week you're offered a decent sum of money to nudge the dial up by one click and leave it there. Each individual step looks obviously worth taking, but if you keep saying yes you end up in constant agony, which you could have avoided for free by never starting. The puzzle is about whether a sequence of apparently rational choices can land you somewhere irrational, and what that says about whether preferences have to be transitive.

Design me a runnable in-person activity for the session that makes the group feel the force of this rather than just hearing about it. I want them to actually make a chain of small, individually sensible decisions with real (small) stakes and then discover, looking back, that they've collectively or individually walked somewhere none of them would have chosen to go in one step. No lecture up front and no mentioning Quinn or the puzzle until the debrief. Please give me: the full rules and any materials I need to prep; a payoff scheme that works without real money (we can do chocolate, or points that convert to not having to stack chairs, or something better you come up with); how to structure it across roughly 60 minutes so the rest of the session is debrief; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the philosophical machinery -- imperceptible differences, the sorites-like structure, and the question of whether each choice was rational even though the outcome wasn't.

Also tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g. someone gaming the payoff scheme, or people refusing to take any steps because they smell a trap, which I suspect will be a big one with this crowd), and which feature of Quinn's puzzle the activity structurally can't reproduce -- I'm guessing the genuine imperceptibility of each increment is hard to fake in a room, and maybe the fact that the self-torturer can't reverse the dial. One thing to keep in mind: a couple of the regulars are economics students who will immediately shout "just don't be a money pump!" so the activity needs to survive that objection rather than collapse into it. Reply in British English please.
