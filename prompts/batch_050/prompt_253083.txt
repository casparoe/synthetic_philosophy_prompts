My reading group has spent the term on the philosophy of deliberation, and we keep circling the question Isaac Levi pressed with his slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction: while you are genuinely deciding what to do, can you coherently assign probabilities to what you will do? Two of us have settled into stable camps. One defends the crowding-out view: to treat your own forthcoming choice as an event with a credence is already to have stepped out of the deliberative stance, since deliberating just is treating the options as open in a way that is incompatible with regarding the outcome as settled-enough-to-bet-on; the moment you find yourself confident you will take an option, the deliberation is over, whether you have admitted it or not. The other defends what I'll call the compatibility view: agents not only can but routinely must predict their own choices mid-deliberation -- when coordinating with others, when planning around their own future conduct, when deciding how much evidence to gather before acting -- and a person who is 95% confident she will decline the job offer while still genuinely weighing it is not confused but ordinary.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims -- for instance, what both accept about the difference between credence in one's future actions based on evidence of character and credence based on having already decided, about whether the debate turns on a substantive or merely perspectival notion of 'openness,' and about which everyday cases (promise-keeping, dieting, voting) are too contaminated to be data; (2) a disagreements section where each residual dispute is stated in one sentence each side would accept as a fair description of its own position; and (3) a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move one side -- I suspect these include whether credences about one's own actions are action-guiding in a way that makes them self-defeating or self-confirming during deliberation, and whether the existence of agents who must model themselves (a chess engine searching its own future moves, a person taking a drug that will make her impulsive) tells us anything about human deliberation or only about prediction simpliciter. For each crux, specify a case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the correct treatment of that case would show.

Format the output as the three sections above, with the disagreements as a short table if that is cleaner. The formulations matter more than the verdicts: nothing either side signs should be vacuous (both sides 'agreeing that agents are free' helps no one) or slanted toward one camp's vocabulary. Where a dispute that occupied my reading group turns out to be verbal -- about what 'open' or 'deliberation' means rather than about the world -- say so explicitly and explain what non-verbal residue, if any, survives the disambiguation. Finally, append a brief note on how confident you are that your agreed-claims list is genuinely agreeable to both camps rather than stitched together from ambiguous wording, and single out the one point in the statement where you are least sure you have represented a position fairly.
