I run a small philosophy podcast with no institutional backing and no relevant qualifications of my own -- I came to all this through a long-running argument with a friend about Newton's spinning bucket, of all things, and I have been worrying at it ever since. Next month I am interviewing a guest, a philosopher of physics who defends the relationist view that there is no such thing as space itself, only spatial relations between bodies. She is careful and well prepared, and I expect her to reach for Ernst Mach's answer to the bucket (the water climbs the sides because it rotates relative to the distant stars) within the first ten minutes, and then to claim that general relativity vindicates that answer. I want questions she cannot glide past.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, in a sensible running order, with a follow-up appended to each that is keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. I would like the set to include, at minimum: a question pressing the empty-universe version of the bucket -- if the bucket were the only thing in existence, would the water stay flat, and if so, has rotation relative to nothing ceased to mean anything; a question about Newton's two globes joined by a cord in otherwise empty space, where the tension in the cord seems to detect rotation without any external reference bodies; a question on whether general relativity really does settle the matter in her favour, given that the theory appears to permit a rotating universe containing nothing else at all, which suggests spacetime is doing work no arrangement of bodies could; and a question about whether any conceivable observation could ever distinguish her view from the substantivalist's, and if not, why we should prefer hers.

At the end, tell me honestly where this line of questioning is weakest: which reply from her would leave me without a follow-up worth asking, and what she could say that should genuinely change my mind about leading with the bucket at all.
