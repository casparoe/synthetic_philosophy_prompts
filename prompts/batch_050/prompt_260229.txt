Two colleagues of mine — an operations manager and a data scientist — recently fell into a prolonged disagreement about how the manager had filled an open position on her team, and it escalated past the particulars into something I could not settle. I will reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) The data scientist's opening complaint was that the manager had interviewed candidates only until she found one who cleared a bar she had fixed in advance, and then stopped and hired him. On his view, this deliberately leaves value on the table: the best candidate in the pool might have been scheduled for the following week, and interviewing everyone and selecting the strongest is the only procedure that could justify calling the outcome the best available.

(2) The manager replied that every additional week of interviews is a week the position sits empty, the remaining team covers the workload, and candidates in hand accept other offers. A procedure that reliably produces a good hire in three weeks, she said, beats a procedure that produces a marginally better hire in ten, and any model of the decision that ignores the cost of continued searching is a model of a different problem than the one she faced.

(3) The data scientist conceded that search is costly but pressed a different point: the bar itself had been set by feel, before any candidate was seen. Without knowing what the applicant pool actually looks like, a fixed threshold could be far too low — hiring someone mediocre out of impatience — or far too high — rejecting people the manager would have been delighted with. He suggested there are known results for this kind of situation: if you must decide on each candidate before seeing the next, you should observe an initial portion of the pool without hiring anyone, purely to calibrate, and then take the first subsequent candidate who beats everyone you have seen. That procedure, he said, treats early candidates as information rather than committing blind to a number.

(4) The manager answered that this observe-then-leap scheme rests on assumptions that did not hold: that candidates arrive in random order, that you know in advance how many there will be, that no declined candidate can be recalled, and that your offer will be accepted. In her case none of those was true. Moreover, she argued, even the data scientist's preferred exhaustive search would have to stop somewhere — the pool is theoretically endless — so everyone who hires anyone is running some stopping rule; the honest question is which one, and a stated threshold at least makes the compromise visible rather than hiding it inside an idealized model.

(5) His final move was to grant all of that but insist she should then describe her procedure as a practical compromise rather than as a rational way to hire. Her final move was to reply that calling full optimization the rational standard is a fiction, since no actual decision-maker has the information or time to carry it out, and a standard of rationality that no one could ever meet cannot be used to fault anyone.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right about some things and wrong about others, with a brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, what both parties are missing, and whether there is any principled way to set a threshold like the manager's rather than choosing it by feel.
