So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and I re-watched The Big Short last weekend and something's been bugging me. I went down a wikipedia hole and found this thing called the no-trade theorem (and Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result), which as I understand it says roughly: if everyone is rational, everyone knows everyone is rational, and everyone shares the same prior beliefs, then the mere fact that someone wants to trade with you is itself information, and rational people should keep updating until nobody trades. Ok. But then like... the whole movie is the puzzle. Burry walks into Goldman and Deutsche and they are *thrilled* to sell him credit default swaps on the housing market. Both sides think they're fleecing the other guy. So the film is basically a dramatization of the no-trade theorem failing, right? Which assumption is failing, and does the movie know which one?

Here's what I'd like. First, explain the no-trade theorem to me properly -- what the actual assumptions are (common priors, common knowledge of rationality, trades being common knowledge, whatever else), because I've only got the pop version and I suspect the pop version is stronger than the real theorem. Then read the film through it: where in the movie do you see each assumption cracking? Like, is the story "different priors" (the shorts literally pulled the loan tapes and read them, the banks didn't)? Is it "rationality isn't common knowledge" (everyone assumes the other side is an idiot, and half of them are right)? Or is it something dumber and more structural -- the CDO desks and the ratings agencies aren't trying to be right at all, they just get paid on volume, so they're not even playing the epistemic game the theorem assumes? There's that scene where the rating agency lady basically admits they rate things to keep the business. Does that break the theorem or is it outside the model entirely?

Also flip it around: the Hayek-flavored story about markets is that prices aggregate scattered information that no single person has. The Big Short seems like a giant counterexample -- the information was *there*, in the loan documents, and the price of MBSs reflected nothing for years. But then again the shorts did find it, and arguably the market "worked" in the very slow sense that the guys who did the reading got paid. So does the film vindicate the epistemic case for markets or demolish it? And is the answer different for the stock market vs. the market for these bespoke illiquid securities where prices barely exist?

Last thing: what does the film show about all this that the theorem and the econ papers don't? My sense is the movie keeps insisting the answer isn't cognitive, it's institutional -- the incentives were arranged so that being wrong cost you nothing and being right early cost you your investors (Burry's clients trying to sue him to get their money back *while he's winning* is the best scene in the movie imo). If that's right, is the lesson that no-trade-style results are fine epistemology but useless once agents get paid for not updating?
