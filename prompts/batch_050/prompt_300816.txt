So I've been lurking on a philosophy of law reading group that spun off from my usual LW-adjacent crowd, and we got stuck on the naked statistical evidence stuff -- the blue bus case, the gatecrasher at the rodeo, that whole cluster. My inner Bayesian keeps screaming at me that these cases shouldn't be puzzling at all, and my gut keeps disagreeing, and I want to figure out which one is confused. So let me just dump my verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, is generating them.

Case 1: A bus sideswipes a car and drives off. No witnesses, no camera. We know that 80% of the buses operating on that road that day belonged to the Blue Bus Company. My verdict: the company shouldn't be found liable. Case 2: same accident, but now a single eyewitness testifies it was a blue bus, and we know from testing that this witness correctly identifies bus colors only 70% of the time. My verdict, and I hate this: it feels *more* legitimate to find against the company here, even though the witness is less reliable than the base rate in case 1. Case 3: the rodeo gatecrasher -- 1000 people in the stands, only 499 bought tickets, organizers pick one attendee at random and sue them for the admission price. Verdict: obviously they lose, this is basically the blue bus. Case 4: cold-case DNA. Police run a sample through a database of 3 million profiles, get exactly one hit, and the lab says the random match probability is about 1 in 10 million. Verdict: arrest the guy, and honestly if nothing else turns up I still think a jury could convict -- which seems statistically not so different from the blue bus, and yet. Case 5: pure lottery. A raffle with 1000 tickets, and on that basis alone I judge that ticket #347 lost. Verdict: I'm not justified in believing that, even though it's 99.9% likely. Case 6: a fraud-detection model flags the defendent, and the model's developers show it has a 95% precision rate on historical data, but nobody can articulate what features drove this particular flag. Verdict: feels like the blue bus again, not evidence anyone should go to jail on.

Now, the naive Bayesian move is to say cases 1 and 3 are just cases where the posterior is 80% and 50.1% respectively, and if that's not enough for a verdict then nothing below 95% should be, and the whole doctrine is just legal discomfort with explicit probability. But that doesn't touch case 5, and it makes a hash of case 2 vs case 1. The alternative I keep gesturing at is something like: the evidence has to be *about this particular case* in some causal or explanatory sense -- the DNA hit is sensitive to whether this guy is actually guilty in a way the base rate isn't -- but I can't state it precisely enough to tell whether it's a real principle or just a relabeling of my intuitions. So: is there a principle that captures all six verdicts, or close to it? State it precisely enough that it gives verdicts on cases I haven't listed (like: what if the eyewitness in case 2 is 95% reliable? what if the DNA database contained everyone in the country?). And be honest about which of my verdicts you'd overturn if forced to choose -- my money is on case 2 being the one that has to go, but maybe you can save it.
