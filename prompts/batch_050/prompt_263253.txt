I've been teaching a grad seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and we just spent two weeks on the result that rational agents with a common prior who share their credences back and forth can't end up disagreeing forever -- at some point further exchange stops moving anyone, and the posteriors coincide. Most of my students took this in stride as a nice little theorem, but one of them has become genuinely obsessed with it and is now arguing that it has teeth for real life. Her proposal: when two smart, honest people persistently disagree about something (she keeps using the example of her and her boyfriend disagreeing about whether their mutual friend is happy in his marriage), they should just sit down and take turns announcing their probabilities, updating on each other's announcements, round after round, and the theorem supposedly guarantees they'll converge. She calls this her "disagreement dissolution protocol" and wants to actually try it at Thanksgiving, which sounds like a disaster, but I want to engage with the idea on its merits.

Write me an essay assessing whether the protocol would work and, more importantly, what its success or failure would even show. Some things I'd want the essay to grapple with: whether the theorem's assumptions (common priors, common knowledge of each other's rationality, credences that fully capture what each party knows) are merely idealizations or whether they fail in ways that break the conclusion entirely; whether convergence, if it happened, would be evidence of truth-tracking or just evidence of mutual deference; and whether there's something epistemically fishy about treating your conversation partner's announced credence as just another piece of evidence on par with, say, looking at the marriage yourself. I'm also curious about the reverse direction of her argument -- she's implicitly committed to the claim that if two people *don't* converge after honest exchange, at least one of them is being irrational or dishonest about their reasoning, and I'm not sure the theorem licenses that accusation against actual humans.

The essay should take a position. Mine, loosely, is that the theorem is real but the protocol is somewhere between useless and misleading in practice, because the interesting disagreements are precisely the ones where you can't cleanly separate "what I saw" from "how I weight it," and trading numbers obscures that rather than resolving it. But I'm genuinely not certain, and I'd rather read an essay that argues me out of this than one that flatters it. Either way, don't end with "it depends on the situation" -- pick a verdict about the protocol and defend it, even if the verdict is conditional (e.g. works for domain X but not Y, as long as you say which and why).
