I serve on the five-member classification board of an international food-security agency, and I need to put a decision memo before the board at the end of the month. The case concerns the Republic of Karavia, a fictional but representative state whose northern provinces have now had two consecutive failed rainy seasons, with rainfall roughly forty percent below the seasonal average. On the meteorological evidence alone, this looks like a drought-driven emergency. But our field staff have assembled a second body of evidence: the Karavian government requisitioned a large share of last year's harvest to provision the capital and the army, closed the provincial borders to grain traders, expelled independent monitors eight months ago, and has refused to allow mortality surveys in the worst-affected districts. What data we do have — hospital records, testimonies from displaced families, satellite imagery of abandoned farmland — suggests excess child deaths well above emergency thresholds, but nothing meets the formal statistical standards our charter requires for a famine determination, precisely because the government will not let us collect it.

A famine declaration is not a neutral label in our system. It triggers mandatory donor commitments, unlocks access protocols that let relief convoys cross borders without the host government's consent, and — under a recent treaty several of our member states have ratified — makes the deliberate obstruction of relief during a declared famine a prosecutable international crime. The Karavian government insists the crisis is an act of God and that a declaration would be a political weapon dressed as humanitarianism. Within the board, our hydrologist argues that we classify hazards, not crimes: every famine has mixed causes, she says, so demanding a verdict on whether this one is natural or political is a distraction from the operational question of whether people are starving. Our field director counters that the drought alone would not have produced mass death absent the requisitions and the information blackout, and that a declaration that says drought while meaning policy is a lie of omission. I keep thinking about what I have read of Amartya Sen's work on the Bengal famine of 1943 — his argument that the food was there and the rural poor were simply priced and pushed out of access to it — and about how food continued to leave Ireland during the famine of the 1840s and how procurement quotas did the lethal work during China's Great Leap Forward. If even the canonical natural famines turn out, on inspection, to be failures of entitlements and policy rather than of food supply, I am no longer sure the natural-versus-political axis is one our declaration should even use.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action — declare now, decline to declare, or declare under a narrower category with a defined review date — and it must take a position on how the declaration's public language should characterize causation, if at all. I want the strongest version of the losing side's argument stated fairly enough that its proponent on the board would recognize it, an honest treatment of what we can and cannot claim given the incomplete data, and a direct answer to the hydrologist's challenge: is the natural-versus-political question doing any real work in a decision like ours, or is it a question our institutional mandate can and should refuse to answer?
