I serve on a national commission that hears claims for reparation arising from historical injustices committed by the state. We have no consistent framework: some panels compensate only living victims, others entertain claims by descendants, and the reasoning rarely survives appeal. Please write a decision procedure a panel of non-philosophers could actually run on any claim that reaches us: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a denial, and where it must hand the question to political judgment because it is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure has to take seriously three difficulties the philosophical literature on reparations raises, and you should say explicitly which of them each step is meant to handle. First, the non-identity problem: where the historical wrong shaped who exists today, the counterfactual that the descendants would have been better off without the wrong appears to fail, since they would not have existed at all. Second, supersession: the suggestion that changed circumstances -- the flourishing of the victims' descendants, the passage of the property through many innocent hands, the disappearance of the original institutions -- can erode or extinguish even a once-valid claim. Third, the question of who holds the claim: an individual with a traceable loss, or a group whose persisting disadvantage is real but cannot be cleanly attributed to the original act.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show its working: (1) living, identifiable survivors of a forced relocation forty years ago whose confiscated land has since been resold several times to good-faith purchasers; (2) descendants of enslaved people claiming against the state as such, where the group remains measurably disadvantaged; (3) a cultural community whose language was deliberately suppressed two generations ago, with no quantifiable economic loss but a near-total loss of transmission. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer the commission will not want to give, and say what the procedure cannot settle at all. Finally, indicate how confident you are in the procedure's handling of each of the three difficulties, and single out the one point in your answer where you are least sure you have it right.
