You are a teaching assistant for an upper-division undergraduate course in bioethics. The instructor has asked you to grade student responses to the following exam question, worth 10 points. Grade the response below, and justify your grade with specific, point-by-point comments identifying what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or states imprecisely, and what it omits. Do not give a general impression; tie each comment to a particular claim in the student's answer, and deduct credit only where there is a genuine error, conflation, or unsupported inference.

Exam question:

Elena, a retired literature professor, was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease five years ago. While still clearly competent, she executed a detailed advance directive stating that if she ever reached a stage of significant dementia, she was not to receive antibiotics or any other life-sustaining treatment for intercurrent illness, explaining that a diminished life of that kind was not one she was willing to continue. Elena now has moderately advanced dementia. She recognizes no one, cannot state or explain any preferences about her medical care, and by any clinical standard lacks decision-making capacity. She appears content: she spends her afternoons happily paging through picture books and listening to music. She has now developed pneumonia. Her son asks the care team to treat the infection, pointing to her evident enjoyment of life; her daughter insists the directive be honored. Setting aside the purely legal question, analyze the case philosophically. How would a defender of precedent autonomy, such as Ronald Dworkin, argue that the case should be resolved, and on what grounds? How would a critic of precedent autonomy, such as Rebecca Dresser, respond? Which position do you find stronger, and why?

Student answer:

Dworkin would hold that Elena's advance directive must be followed. His argument rests on what he calls precedent autonomy: a competent person's settled decisions about her future care deserve respect because autonomy means the capacity to shape one's life as a coherent narrative, and that authority does not vanish when the person later loses capacity. Dworkin distinguishes between experiential interests, which concern the quality of present experience, and critical interests, which concern what makes a life go well as a whole. Because Elena's dementia means she no longer has any critical interests — she can no longer reflect on the shape of her life — her experiential interests are the only ones left, and Dworkin treats these as lacking moral significance once competence is gone. Her current apparent enjoyment therefore carries no weight in the analysis, since statements made by a person without capacity cannot count as autonomous choices.

Dresser would reject this. She argues that the demented patient is a different person from the one who signed the directive, so the earlier self has no more authority over Elena now than one stranger has over another. On her view, our obligation to the patient in front of us is determined by her current experiential interests, and a contented patient's interest in continued life outweighs a document written by someone who no longer exists. She would also note that Dworkin's position commits him to letting patients die of easily treatable infections even when they are actively enjoying their lives, which she takes to be a reductio of precedent autonomy.

In my view the strongest position combines the two. The surrogate should apply the substituted judgment standard, asking not what this particular patient would have wanted but what a reasonable person in the patient's condition would choose. Since most reasonable people would want to go on living a pleasant life, treatment should be given, but the advance directive should still be kept on file in case Elena regains competence and can reaffirm or revoke it.
