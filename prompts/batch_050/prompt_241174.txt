I'm putting together a unit on philosophy of language for an undergrad seminar I TA, and my students have hit a wall with definite descriptions. They can parrot the analysis fine, but the minute I ask them to apply it to a fresh case they just redo the king of France example or some variant on it, which tells me they don't actually get it. My office-mate suggested I make them design their own cases instead of mine, and I want to show them what a good one looks like first.

So: construct a thought experiment in which Russell's theory of descriptions and a Strawsonian presupposition account come apart on a sentence containing a definite description that picks out nothing -- but with an extra twist. The scenario must also include a moment where a speaker uses a description that fits nobody (or the wrong thing entirely) and yet successfully communicates about a particular person in the room, Donnellan-style, so that the case tests referential versus attributive use at the same time. Constraints: the setting must be utterly mundane (a bakery, a PTA fundraiser, a dentist's waiting room -- not royalty, not gods, not anything involving France), the whole scenario should be one paragraph, and you can't stipulate anyone's intuitions; the verdicts have to fall out of the theories. After the scenario, walk through what Russell says about the empty-description sentence, what Strawson says about the same sentence, and what Donnellan's distinction says about the misdescribing-but-communicating moment, and be explicit about which single feature of the case is making the two big theories diverge. Then tell me how a Strawsonian would push back on the case -- what they'd say it illicitly assumes -- and how a Russellian would respond to that.

One thing I care about alot for teaching purposes: when you give the verdicts, flag which parts of this are basically settled ground among philosophers of language (as much as anything is) and which parts are still genuinely contested, because my students tend to walk away thinking whichever analysis I presented last is the official answer, and I don't want the demo case to reinforce that. Finally, after the prose, give me a table I can drop into my slides with these columns: scenario_element (which part of the story), theory (Russell, Strawson, or Donnellan), verdict (what that theory says about it), mechanism (one line on why), and contested? (yes/no plus a few words on what the live dispute is, if yes).
