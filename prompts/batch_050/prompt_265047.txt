I've been reading about the archaeology of Hisarlik and got into an argument with a colleague about what it would even mean for the Trojan War to be "historical." She says the excavations settled it; I think the question is ill-posed until you say exactly which proposition you're assigning a probability to. So let me fix a precise one and ask for your credence with the reasoning decomposed. The proposition: there occurred, in the Late Bronze Age, an armed conflict in northwestern Anatolia involving a coalition with meaningful ties to the Mycenaean Greek world, such that the plot-skeleton of the Iliadic tradition -- a Greek expedition against a fortified city near the Hellespont, ending in that city's destruction -- descends by continuous transmission from memory of that conflict, rather than being a later invention retrospectively attached to impressive ruins. Note what this excludes: it doesn't require a Helen, a decade-long siege, a wooden horse, or any individual named in the poems; it requires a real event standing in the right causal ancestry to the story's core.

Please give me your credence in that proposition, and derive it rather than stating it. Here's the decomposition I'd suggest, though you should amend it if you think it's wrong: (a) the probability that some conflict matching that rough description occurred at all, given what we know of Late Bronze Age Anatolian and Aegean geopolitics; (b) the probability, conditional on its occurrence, that it was encoded into an oral tradition that survived the collapse of literate palace culture -- i.e., that transmission across the post-Bronze-Age gap is even possible for event-level content as opposed to names, places, and vague "there was once a great war" motifs; (c) the probability, conditional on (a) and (b), that the surviving tradition preserves the skeleton I specified rather than distorting it past recognition -- that centuries of formulaic recomposition don't scramble which side attacked, where, and how it ended. Give a number and one or two lines of justification for each term, show how they combine, and state your final credence. Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and which single archaeological or textual discovery -- something that could plausibly still be found -- would move it the most, and in which direction.

One further wrinkle I'd like addressed, because it came up in the argument and neither of us could resolve it: my colleague claims that for traditions of this kind, the posterior on "historical kernel exists" should be pulled upward by the mere fact that multiple independent traditions (Greek epic, and others) converge on the same region and era, whereas I suspect convergence is near-evidence-free here because later poets cluster their stories around famous ruins whether or not the fame is warranted. In your decomposition, treat this as a separate factor: say how much weight, if any, convergent legendary geography deserves, and whether your answer changes if the convergence is among traditions composed centuries after the putative event rather than contemporary with it.
