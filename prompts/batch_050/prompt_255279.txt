I am a systems administrator and sometime woodworker with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking for a while about what happens when tools fail. My working intuition is this: while a tool is functioning, I do not really experience it as a thing at all — I experience the task through it — and it is only when something breaks or misbehaves that the tool steps forward and becomes an object I inspect, diagnose, and theorize about. When the drill bit snaps, suddenly the drill has properties I never noticed; when the server times out, the network, which had been invisible, becomes all I can see.

I recently learned that Heidegger apparently developed a detailed account along these lines, distinguishing something like a tool's readiness-to-hand while it is in use from its presence-at-hand once it is broken, conspicuous, or in the way, and treating the breakdown not as a mere annoyance but as something that reveals the web of tasks, other tools, and purposes the equipment was quietly embedded in all along.

Here is what I would like. First, restate Heidegger's position about readiness-to-hand and the breakdown of equipment in the vocabulary I actually work with — system monitoring, failure modes, error messages, dependency trees, debugging, logs that only get read after an outage, and the difference between running on autopilot and reading documentation mid-incident. Translate his claims as precisely as you can into that idiom rather than loosely gesturing at it.

Second, and more important, give me a residue report: which parts of the position map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which parts you could only translate by distorting them, and which parts — if any — have no real counterpart in the language of failures and debugging at all. I suspect the claim that the breakdown reveals something about the whole network of purposes, not just the broken item, may resist translation, since in my world an alert names a specific failing component, not a totality of assignments. Tell me whether I am right about that, or whether something in my framework actually does capture it.

Third, where the translation fails, tell me your honest judgment about which side is at fault: does the residue expose a genuine blindness in the engineering way of seeing tools as components that fail individually, or does it expose a vagueness in Heidegger's account that my vocabulary forces into the open? I would rather be told that my framework is missing something than be flattered.

Finally, please be explicit throughout about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — for instance, which features of the readiness-to-hand/presence-at-hand distinction are common ground among interpreters, and which readings of the breakdown's revelatory role are disputed — rather than presenting any single interpretation as the consensus view. I have no way to check the scholarship myself, so I need you to flag your confidence honestly.
