I am not a philosopher, but I enjoy inventing probability puzzles, and one of mine has grown into an argument that seems to prove something absurd. The puzzle first. A walker is parachuted onto an island divided into two regions of equal size, East and West; she has no idea where she has landed, so she gives each region a confidence of one half. She knows the geography: in each region, a quarter of the land is marsh and three quarters heath. Over the radio she receives exactly one message: "If you are in the West, your chance of standing in marsh is three quarters." The question: should her confidence that she is in the West stay at one half, move up, or move down?

A friend argues as follows. (1) Confidence should change only in response to something learnt. (2) The only principled way to change it is conditionalisation: your new confidence in anything becomes your old confidence in it given what you learnt. (3) Conditionalisation requires a proposition you have come to know for certain. (4) The message makes nothing about her location certain — it could truthfully have been sent wherever she landed. (5) So there is nothing to conditionalise on, and her confidence in the West must stay at one half. (6) Generalising: a message consistent with every possibility can never rationally move any confidence. (7) But that is absurd — a weather forecast, a medical statistic, or "if the bridge is closed, take the ferry" shifts our confidence constantly without making anything certain.

I would like to keep (1) and the verdict in (7). So: which step do you reject, why did it look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? And if there are two principled repairs that give different answers to my puzzle — one keeping the West at one half, one nudging it up — is that a genuine disagreement about rationality or merely two conventions? Give your answer as exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
