A friend hit me with this arguement over drinks last week and I still can't find the exit. Background: I'd been complaining that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons — it trades one person's misery off against another person's gain as if there were one giant experiencer who gets both. He said fine, but watch what happens when you actually spell out why that's supposed to be wrong:

(1) A burden on one person can only be made acceptable by a compensating benefit to that same person — that is the entire content of the separateness objection.
(2) The reason intrapersonal trade-offs (suffering through a root canal now for healthy teeth later) are unproblematic is that the sufferer and the beneficiary are numerically the same person.
(3) But the reductionists are right about personal identity: what holds a life together over time is just overlapping chains of psychological connectedness, nothing deeper, and identity is not what matters.
(4) So there is no deep fact that makes the intrapersonal case different from the interpersonal one — the only difference is the degree and routing of the psychological connections.
(5) So if aggregating across persons is illegitimate, then ordinary prudence is illegitimate too: my present self sacrificing for my future self is also one experiencer being used as a means for a loosely connected other.
(6) But prudence is obviously fine, so by modus tollens it's the separateness objection that has to go, and utilitarian aggregation was never the real problem.

He cheerfully admitted he'd rather conclude that prudence is irrational than give up utilitarianism, which feels like a tell, but I can't actually locate the mistake — each step looks okay-ish on its own. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is valid as it stands, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up instead.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no intro or wrap-up: 1) the step or premise you reject, 2) why it looked fine in the first place, 3) what rejecting it costs me, 4) what my friend should say in response if he's being honest, and 5) whether ordinary prudence survives your fix.
