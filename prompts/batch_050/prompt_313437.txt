I work as a distributed systems engineer, and my working vocabulary consists of things like state machines and their transition functions, message passing and causal ordering of events, deterministic replay versus live execution, shadow deployments and traffic mirroring, observability and monitoring, fault injection, and trust boundaries that define what counts as part of a system. I have no formal philosophy background, but I keep running into a debate I want to understand on my own terms.

Take the philosophical dispute about which physical systems genuinely implement a given computation, and therefore — on computationalist views of mind — which systems could be sentient and morally considerable. Please restate the following positions in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, treating each separately:

First, liberal computationalism: the view that any system implementing the right abstract state-transition organization has the same mentality, whatever it is made of, including the thought experiment in which a biological brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, by functionally equivalent silicon, and the argument that consciousness could not fade away mid-replacement without producing an absurdity.

Second, the triviality challenge: the claim that, absent further constraints, any sufficiently complex physical system — a wall, a rock — can be redescribed as implementing any finite state machine you like, so that implementation talk is cheap and the liberal view either collapses or implies sentience is everywhere.

Third, the counterfactual or causal constraint responses: attempts to rescue computationalism by requiring that the physical system not merely match the computation's trace but actually support the right counterfactual transitions — that it would have gone to the appropriate next state had the inputs differed.

Fourth, the position that mere behavioral or functional equivalence is insufficient because what matters is the intrinsic causal structure of the system — that, for instance, a feed-forward replay of the right outputs differs fundamentally from a system whose internal states genuinely cause one another in the right pattern, even if the two are indistinguishable from outside.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly — for example, is the counterfactual constraint just a requirement that behavior hold up under fault injection rather than only on the happy path, and is multiple realizability just implementation independence? Which claims you had to distort to fit, and where exactly the distortion entered. And which claims have no expression at all in my vocabulary: I suspect the entire phenomenal dimension — that there is something it is like to be the system — and the moral valence of the whole question fall into this category, since my observability tools are all third-person and my field has no notion of a computation mattering, but tell me whether I am right about that.

Finally, address the boundary question in both vocabularies. In my world, what counts as part of the system is a design decision — does the message broker belong to the service, does the logging pipeline participate in the computation or merely record it? State what the philosophers' boundary question is (which portions of a causal nexus constitute the experiencing subject, and where attribution of sentience should begin and end), say whether my trust-boundary intuitions are a useful guide or a misleading one, and close with your judgment: is the residue a defect of the engineering vocabulary, or a sign that some of the philosophical claims were less clear than they appeared?
