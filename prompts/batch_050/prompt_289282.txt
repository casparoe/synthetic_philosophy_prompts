I am a doctoral student in political theory, and a chapter of my thesis defends what I call a neo-Humean position on national character. In his essay Of National Characters, Hume distinguishes what he terms moral causes — government, laws, customs, occupation, the forms of collective life — from physical causes such as climate and terrain, and argues that the characteristic temper of a people is produced almost entirely by the former: the same stock changes its manners within a generation when its institutions change, while peoples sharing a climate differ markedly. My modernised version of the thesis holds that (i) national character is a coherent notion — there exist stable, non-trivial differences in the distribution of behavioural dispositions between national populations; (ii) these differences are causally explained by institutions and transmitted customs rather than by anything innate or climatic; and (iii) generalisations about the character of a people can therefore be both epistemically respectable and morally permissible, provided they are statistical rather than exceptionless. I treat Hume's essay as the position's founding text, and my defence of (iii) leans heavily on his insistence that the relevant causes are acquired and alterable.

Before I commit the chapter to print, I want an honest audit of the position's exposure rather than another defence of it. What would have to be true for it to be false? Which findings — from personality geography, stereotype-accuracy research, the study of migrants and their descendants, or the speed with which national self-descriptions have historically reversed — would actually bear on each of (i) to (iii), and which only appear to? Which conceptual objections (that between-group variance is dwarfed by within-group variance, that national populations are reified artefacts of borders and censuses, that any such generalisation is self-fulfilling) strike at the thesis itself, and which strike only at vulgar versions I already disown? Please distinguish what a committed defender would concede as a defeater from what only critics would count as one, rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude by telling me whether the position, suitably qualified, still risks anything at all — and if it does not, whether that should worry me.
