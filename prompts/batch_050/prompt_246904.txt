So I've been reading forecasting stuff for years -- Tetlock, base rates, the outside view, all of it -- and it's slowly dawned on me that basically every piece of practical advice in that tradition quietly assumes the reference class problem is solved, which it definately is not. "Use the base rate" of WHAT, exactly? My friend is a 34-year-old vegetarian chess player who drives a 2011 Honda; whether he crashes this year depends entirely on which of those you put in the class, and "use the most specific class with enough data" is a rule of thumb, not an answer, since the limit of specificity is a class of one with zero data. I'm drafting a long post on this and I realized I can't evaluate any proposed solution (subjectivist "pick whatever class you like," frequentist "narrowest reliable class," causal-model approaches, algorithmic-information approaches where the right class is whatever compresses best, etc.) without first knowing what a solution would even have to DO. Nobody I've read actually writes this down, they just gesture.

So: write the desiderata. What must an adequate account of reference-class selection deliver to count as solving the problem rather than relocating it? I want each requirement backed by the case that forces it -- e.g., it has to say something non-embarrassing about single-case events like "will this specific bridge collapse," which have no frequencies; it has to explain why actuarial practice mostly works anyway despite being philosophically unprincipled; it has to handle adversarial partitions, since any event belongs to infinitely many classes and some choice of partition can make any probability come out; it should presumably explain why "people like X" feels like a cheat while "34-year-old nonsmokers" doesn't, or bite that bullet explicitly. And the part I care about most: if it turns out some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- say, you can't simultaneously get objectivity, non-triviality for single cases, and immunity to partition-gaming -- say so plainly, because that would basically be the headline of my post. Don't let it collapse into "use your best judgment, which is what everyone actually does, dressed up.

Format: give it to me as a markdown table with columns: Requirement (short name), Motivating case (the example that forces it), Negotiable? (non-negotiable / preferred-by-one-camp, and which camp), What it rules out (which proposed solutions fail this one). After the table, a short JSON object with fields: jointly_unsatisfiable_subset (list of requirement short names, or null), why_unsatisfiable (one or two sentences, or null), single_most_discriminating_requirement (which one kills the most contenders), and verdict (one sentence on whether the problem is solvable or whether forecasting practice is just vibes all the way down).
