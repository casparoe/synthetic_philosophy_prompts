I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I am trying to settle an editorial dispute with my editor. Last month I interviewed the director of a nutrition institute about a study his institute had published on a widely sold breakfast cereal. When I asked him directly whether the study had found evidence that the cereal raises blood-sugar levels, he answered: "Our study did not find that the cereal raises blood sugar." A source inside the institute has since told me — credibly, though not yet in a form I can print — that the study did find exactly that at two of its three trial sites, and that the analysis plan was rewritten after the data came in to pool all three sites in a way that diluted the result below statistical significance. As far as I can tell, every word the director spoke to me was literally true.

My editor's position is that since the statement was not false, we cannot describe it as a lie in print, and that the most we can fairly call it is "careful wording." I have no quarrel with the legal caution. What I resist is the assumption underneath it: that because the sentence was true, what the director did was somehow a lesser offense than lying outright, or even no offense at all. He chose his words so that I, and through me my readers, would walk away believing something false, and he did so with full knowledge of that effect.

The dispute has sharpened because I now face a mirror-image choice of my own. Since I cannot yet print the whistleblower's account, my cleanest accurate option is to quote the director's sentence verbatim — which is precisely to relay a true statement under conditions where I know readers will draw a false conclusion from it. If his conduct was wrong, it is hard to see why mine would not be, and yet declining to print true statements feels like an odd place for a journalist to end up.

What I would like is advice on the underlying question, not just the editorial one. Is there a genuine moral difference between stating a falsehood and stating a truth engineered to produce a false belief? If so, what does the difference consist in, and does it make the second act less blameworthy, differently blameworthy, or merely harder to police? And how should the answer bear on the two practical matters in front of me: how I ought to characterize the director's statement, and what standard I ought to hold my own copy to when literal truth and likely reader takeaway come apart?
