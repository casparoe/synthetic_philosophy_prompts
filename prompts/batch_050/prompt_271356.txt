Ok, background: I moved from the US (Ohio) to Germany for a job about two years ago, and I keep ending up in the same argument — with coworkers, with my landlord, with my girlfriend's family. It usually starts with the jaywalking-at-a-red-light thing but it's really about something bigger. The short version: German folk morality seems to treat following the rules as itself the moral thing to do — order, reliability, everyone doing their part so the whole thing works — while American folk morality treats individual conscience as the real source of moral authority and gets suspicious of anyone hiding behind rules. "Just following orders" is basically an insult in the US, wheras I've heard Germans say something close to it almost approvingly, like taking your assigned role seriously is a virtue.

Both sides talk past each other constantly, me included. So: draft the joint statement that a thoughtful defender of each folk morality could actually sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. The German rules-camp and the American conscience-camp, each at its strongest — no carricatures.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about what rules are actually for, what conscience is actually for, and what each side's nightmare scenario looks like (bureaucratic inhumanity on one side, self-righteous chaos on the other, presumably).
2. The exact remaining disagreements, each phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording of its own position.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where, if you settled them, at least one side would have to budge. I suspect one is whether rule-following is itself a kind of integrity or just outsourced judgment, and another is whether individual discretion mostly buys you compassion or mostly buys you favoritism and bias.
4. For each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Everyday cases are fine — the red light at 3am with no cars anywhere, the cashier who gave you too much change, the neighbor who reports a minor violation instead of knocking on your door, the official who quietly bends an absurd rule to help someone.

Two requests. First, keep these as folk moralities, i.e. ideal types — real Germans and real Americans are all over the map and I don't need a paragraph reminding me of that. But second, wherever you touch on actual social-science claims about the two countries — rule compliance, trust in institutions, how people in fact behave — separate what's reasonably established from what's genuinely contested among people who study this, and don't present a national-character stereotype as a finding. If you conclude that part of the disagreement is purely verbal (both sides using "responsibility" or "integrity" to mean different things), say which part and why.
