I chair the diagnostic classification committee of my national neurological society, and next month we must rule on a proposal concerning Merrow's disease, which is currently defined by a cluster of motor and cognitive symptoms. A research consortium has identified a blood biomarker that divides diagnosed patients into two groups: biomarker-positive patients, roughly 60%, share a uniform brain pathology and respond reliably to a new drug that failed its clinical trials when tested on the undivided patient population; biomarker-negative patients show heterogeneous underlying pathology and derive no benefit from the drug. The consortium proposes that we redefine Merrow's disease as the biomarker-positive condition, which would leave the remaining patients — who are genuinely ill and currently receive care under this diagnosis — classified as having something else, or nothing named at all. Please draft the committee's decision memo: recommend for or against the redefinition, with reasoning that addresses whether the biomarker boundary marks a real division in nature rather than merely a convenient one for trial design, and what the answer to that question should settle, given that diagnoses also serve insurers, patients, and clinicians rather than scientists alone. The memo should state a concrete recommendation the committee can vote on and give an honest account of the strongest argument on the losing side, including what becomes of the excluded patients under whichever policy we adopt.
