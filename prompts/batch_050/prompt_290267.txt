I'm a PhD student in criminal law and I've been circling deontological constraints for a chapter on why the law refuses to allow torture even when it seems like it would prevent more torture. Before I commit to anything in writing I want to know whether the views I've been assuming actually fit together. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) It's impermissible to intentionally kill one innocent person even when doing so is the only way to prevent five other innocent people being intentionally killed by someone else.

(2) The reason the constraint in (1) exists is about the victim: persons are inviolable, and what makes the killing wrong is what you'd be doing to her -- using her, disregarding that she isn't a resource for others' projects. It's not mainly about the agent keeping her own hands clean.

(3) Standard trolley verdicts: flipping the switch to redirect the trolley away from five onto one is permissible, pushing the large person off the bridge to stop it is not, and the difference that matters is means versus side-effect, not contact or anything physical like that.

(4) Constraints have thresholds: if violating the constraint were the only way to prevent, say, a million comparable murders, it would become permissible to kill the one.

(5) When no constraint is triggered, numbers just count: save the larger group, and more harm prevented is straightforwardly better.

Is this package consistent? In particular, walk me through which pairs of these are in real tension and which only look like they are. For each genuine tension, tell me how deep it goes -- whether it's a surface muddle or whether the motivation for one commitment actively undercuts another -- and what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would do the most work. I'd rather you rank things by severity than flag everything as equally problematic, and please don't just tell me I should read more Kant.
