A close friend and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about the endowment effect, and he ended by walking me into an argument I cannot accept but cannot yet escape. I would like a diagnosis: tell me which step to reject, why that step looked perfectly reasonable at the time, and what rejecting it commits me to.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, ran as follows:

(1) Among the ways we value things, some are genuine preferences and some are processing errors. Anchoring on a number you were just shown is the standard example of the second kind; your taste for coffee over tea is the standard example of the first. The distinction is real and it matters, because errors are properly corrected while preferences are properly respected.

(2) The way to tell which kind a valuation is: ask whether acting on it advances the agent's own ends by the agent's own lights. A taste does; a glitch reliably frustrates what the agent is after. That is precisely how we know anchoring is an error -- it steers people away from outcomes they themselves were aiming at.

(3) The well-documented gap between what owners demand to give up an item and what non-owners will pay for the identical item -- owners typically want roughly double -- is a candidate either way. If it is a genuine preference, then refusing to sell at any price inside the gap is simply declining a bad deal, and nobody is harmed.

(4) But whether you own a given thing at a given moment is overwhelmingly the product of accident: gifts, inheritances, employer default options, which stall you were assigned, which mug the experimenter happened to hand you. Almost never did you choose to own it on the basis of an antecedent evaluation.

(5) So if the gap is a genuine preference and you act on it consistently, the composition of your life -- what you keep, what you hold out for -- is settled by the coin flips of history rather than by your own evaluative outlook. A different run of accidents, with the same underlying tastes, would have left you treasuring entirely different things.

(6) A valuation whose content is fixed by accidents you did not choose, and whose effect is to make you hold whatever those accidents delivered, does not advance your ends by your own lights; it manufactures ends to fit whatever position you happen to occupy. By the test in (2), the endowment gap is therefore a bias, and institutions are right to treat the sell-price/buy-price gap as noise to be designed around rather than a preference to be honored.

(7) And here is where he pressed his advantage: the same reasoning shows that my attachment to my late father's watch -- which I would not sell for the price of an identical watch, nor pay to replace with an identical one if I lost it -- is likewise a bias, since that attachment is nothing but an accident-indexed valuation that fails the test in (2). So is a parent's preferring their own child's welfare to a stranger's, genetic accident being the original unchosen assignment.

I refuse (7). But I also cannot see how to keep (6) for the mugs while denying it for the watch and the child, and my friend fairly pointed out that anyone who wants to debunk the endowment effect needs *some* criterion that does the sorting -- simply declaring attachment sacred and mugs pathological is not a criterion. He is right about that much.

So: which step goes wrong, and why did it look fine? I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (2) or (6), in what it takes for a valuation to serve one's ends "by one's own lights," but I cannot make the suspicion precise. And if I reject that step, what am I paying? In particular, what replacement criterion can I offer that separates loss aversion worth respecting from loss aversion worth correcting -- or am I stuck conceding that the distinction cannot be drawn cleanly and that debunking is a matter of degree and judgment rather than principle? I would rather concede that the mug experiments reveal a genuine bias than accept that loving one's own child more than a stranger's is one, but I want to know what that preference of mine costs in theoretical terms.
