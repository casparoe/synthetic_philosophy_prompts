Ok so context: I just got out of a two hour argument with my friend Marcus over beers and I lost, badly. His position: it's just obvious that we harm future people when we trash the climate or leave them holding the bag, and philosophers who tie themselves in knots over "who exactly was harmed" are doing sophistry. I tried the standard line — different policies mean different people get born, so the person in the flooded world can't claim they'd have been better off under the other policy, because under the other policy they wouldn't exist. He just stared at me and said "the grandkids suffer either way, this is exactly why normal people hate philosophers." The problem is I could only state the puzzle at him, I couldn't make him feel it.

So here's what I want. I'm a TA for a second-year applied ethics course and I've got a 75-minute section with about 18 students next month. Design me a classroom activity (game, simulation, whatever works) that makes participants run headfirst into the nonidentity problem before anyone names it.

Requirements:

- Nothing up front. No lecture, no "today we're discussing population ethics." Students should make actual choices in the game and only afterwards realize the structure of what they did.
- The core mechanic has to be that choices made by one group of players determine which later players exist at all — not just how well off they are. So if round 1 players pick the selfish option, the round 2 "population" is a different set of people than if they'd picked the generous option, and the ones who got the raw deal can't honestly say "I'd have existed and been better off if you'd chosen otherwise." Figure out a concrete way to implement this — cards, envelopes, name draws, whatever — I want something runnable, not vibes.
- I want some kind of grievance mechanic, like the disadvantaged round 2 players get to write formal complaints to the round 1 players, and then in the debrief we read them out loud and notice they don't quite... parse. That's the moment I wanted Marcus to have at dinner.
- Small stakes, no real money (chocolate or points that convert into something trivial is fine).
- A debrief plan that maps each beat of the game onto the actual problem: the choice that changed WHO exists, the complaint with no valid addressee, and the two broad ways out (person-affecting vs impersonal), including what it costs to take each one, so the debrief isn't just "weird, huh."
- What will predictably go wrong. I'm guessing at least: one student shrugs "total happiness is what matters, who cares" and deflates the whole thing; someone invents a workaround like "it's the same soul in a different body"; and some logistical disaster with the envelopes. Tell me how to handle each.
- Be honest about what the game can't reproduce. I suspect the card mechanic smuggles in something real future people don't have — the players get to contemplate their own non-existence counterfactually, for one — and real identity-affecting choices probably feel different from card-drawn ones. Tell me where the analogy breaks so I can flag it in class myself.
- Bonus: a stripped-down version I can run on Marcus at a party with like 4 people and a deck of cards, since he's the actual target audience here.

Don't just explain the nonidentity problem back at me, I know it, I want the activity. And don't build it around the classic case of the teenager deciding whether to conceive now or wait — half the class will definately have seen it or some cousin of it and it'll tip them off before the game can do its work.
