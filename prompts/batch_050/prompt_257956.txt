I'm a graduate student in a philosophy program, and last semester I sat in on a joint philosophy-economics reading group on welfare economics. What struck me -- and what I've since confirmed by reading older surveys of the area -- is that the debate over interpersonal comparisons of utility seems to have been in essentially the same place for decades. Lionel Robbins' objection that such comparisons are unscientific value judgments rather than statements of fact still gets cited approvingly by some economists; meanwhile, other economists and philosophers reply that we plainly make such comparisons constantly, in QALY-based health allocation, cost-benefit analysis, optimal tax theory, and even everyday conversation, so the comparisons must at least sometimes be legitimate. There are formal proposals (Harsanyi-style extended sympathy, where one imaginatively occupies another person's position; impartial observer constructions), empirical proposals (happiness surveys, neural or behavioral proxies for welfare), and a quieter conventionalist strand holding that interpersonal comparisons are not discoveries at all but decisions -- social conventions we adopt and could adopt otherwise. Everyone seems to have answered everyone else's arguments long ago, and yet nobody has moved, and the positions seem to me to be talking past each other more than they contradict each other.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but several stacked on top of each other: a dispute about what mental states actually are and whether they have measurable intensity (a question in philosophy of mind and possibly an empirical one), a dispute about what economics as a discipline is supposed to deliver (a methodological or even political question about the scope of science), and a genuinely verbal dispute about what the word utility refers to, with ordinalists and cardinalists using the same word for different objects. I want this hypothesis tested, not adopted. Please diagnose the debate: identify which of the positions actually conflict and which are answering different questions under one heading; say whether the disagreement bottoms out in unargued starting intuitions (about other minds, about the nature of welfare, about what science is) or whether there is a hidden empirical or formal question -- say, about the measurement of hedonic states or about what social choice functions require -- that is doing the real work. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that would dissolve part of the debate, an empirical result that would settle part of it, a theorem, or simply a more honest relabeling of the questions. If your honest assessment is that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed, argue for that instead of offering false hope. I want a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually claims, not a neutral survey of the literature. Please answer in American English.
