I co-own a small specialty food company with three partners, and we have to decide by June whether to sign a ten-year exclusive supply contract with a regional distributor. Signing would roughly double our volume but locks in prices near today's levels; declining keeps us independent but leaves us exposed to the slow decline of our current sales channels. A consulting firm has offered us a market study, at $55,000, that would give a much sharper picture of where demand in our category is heading over exactly the period the contract would cover.

I keep returning to an argument I've worked out for myself, and I can't find the hole in it. Information you're free to ignore can only help you: whatever I would have decided without the study, I can still decide after reading it, so the study adds options without removing any, and its value can't be less than zero. On that view the only real question is whether $55,000 is more than the improvement we'd get from making a better-informed choice, and the answer seems to turn on one thing -- whether there is some finding the study could deliver that would actually change what we do. If no possible result would flip our decision, the study is worthless at any price; if some result would, it's worth something, and the only fight is over the arithmetic.

But I have three nagging doubts I can't fold into that argument. First, I've watched us overreact to fresh information before -- two years ago a single scary industry report pushed us out of a product line that is now thriving for a competitor, and I don't trust us to weigh a glossy $55,000 report calmly. Second, my brother-in-law refused a medical test that was free, on the grounds that some knowledge makes your life worse even if it changes nothing you do, and while a company isn't a person, I wonder if something similar holds for us. Third, the distributor knows we're considering the study, and our lead negotiator thinks commissioning it signals hesitation that will cost us at the table, while declining it signals confidence.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the four partners before our vote. It needs a clear bottom line -- commission the study or decline it -- with reasoning the partners can actually argue against. In particular, I want it to state precisely the condition under which paying for information makes sense, to test whether our situation meets that condition given the numbers above, and to answer head-on the question of whether the study could leave us worse off in some way beyond its price tag. Whichever way the recommendation goes, give the strongest version of the case for the other side, including whichever of my three doubts has real substance, rather than dismissing them.

Length: roughly one page. No footnotes or outside sources -- this should stand on the reasoning alone.
