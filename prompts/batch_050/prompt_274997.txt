I'm putting together a problem set for a graduate seminar on vagueness, and I want a constructed case rather than another discussion question. Here's the assignment I'd like you to complete. Build a thought experiment in which epistemicism and supervaluationism come apart downstream of the borderline case itself: not a disagreement about whether some vague sentence is true (both sides have their stock answers there), but a disagreement about what follows for action -- whether a rule was violated, a promise kept, an entitlement triggered. The scenario should be bureaucratic or legal in flavor rather than science-fictional: think contracts, eligibility rules, referees, committee votes. A hard constraint: no heaps, no sorites series, no bald men. The vague predicate should be one where a borderline instance is genuinely borderline by both theories' lights, and the practical stakes of the case must turn specifically on the difference between the sentence's having a truth value we cannot know (epistemicism) and its lacking a truth value at all (supervaluationism) -- not on some incidental feature that either theorist could redescribe away.

Engineer the case in the harder direction: the supervaluationist should be the one who comes out looking awkward. The embarrassment I have in mind is structural -- the supervaluationist accepts the relevant instance of excluded middle as true while denying truth to each disjunct, so design the scenario so that someone in it (a judge, a claims adjuster, an election official) is required to act on one disjunct or the other and cannot, on the supervaluationist's account, act truly. The epistemicist should be able to say something clean and even comforting about the same situation. Then do the verification work: state each theory's verdict on the case and derive it from the theory rather than stipulating it; identify the single feature of the scenario that generates the divergence and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; and give the supervaluationist's best counterattack -- presumably something involving supertruth, or a denial that the official's predicament is a semantic rather than a practical one -- along with an assessment of whether it succeeds. If the case can be defused by the parties simply stipulating a precisification of the predicate, say so honestly and explain what, if anything, the thought experiment still shows.
