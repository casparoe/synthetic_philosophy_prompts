A colleague and I have co-taught a third-year seminar on paternalism for about five years now, and the departmental joke is that we still haven't resolved our own dispute. I'm the one who thinks nudging is mostly benign: choice architecture is unavoidable, somebody has to set the defaults, so it may as well be set in the chooser's interests, and opt-out rights preserve everything that matters. She's the liberal holdout who thinks even welfare-motivated nudges are a form of manipulation that routes around people's rational capacities rather than engaging them, and that the honest alternative is either outright regulation or open persuasion. The immediate occasion is that our university's new "behavioural insights team" has proposed three interventions for staff: automatic enrolment in a supplementary pension contribution (opt-out at any time, but the default is in), vegetarian-as-default catering at conferences with meat available on request, and relocating sugary drinks to the least convenient shelf of the canteen fridge. We've been asked to comment jointly, so we've decided to write the thing up as a proper adversarial collaboration, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement we should be able to produce.

Here's what I want in it. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: the things both of us can sign without wincing. I'd guess these include that there is no neutral architecture, that opt-outs are never literally costless, that commercial "dark patterns" exist and that we'd both condemn at least some of them, and that some interventions (mandatory calorie labels, say) fall in a different moral category from defaults — but work out what the real common ground is rather than taking my guesses on trust. Second, a disagreements table in which each row states a residual disagreement in wording that each of us would accept as a fair description of our own side. Some candidates: whether a nudge that exploits a known cognitive bias is worse than one that merely exploits inattention; whether full transparency (the pension email openly announcing that you've been defaulted in to help you save) dissolves the manipulation worry or just adds insult to it; and whether it matters who is doing the nudging — the state, your employer, or a supermarket — given that I object to Tesco's shelf layouts far less than she objects to Whitehall's.

Third, and this is the bit I'm most keen on, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of us. For each crux, give a case or a piece of evidence we would both accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each of us predicts about it — so, for instance, if one crux is whether people's post-hoc approval of having been nudged tracks anything morally significant, specify what survey result would embarrass which of us. Also please include one thing adversarial statements rarely do: an honest note on whether part of our dispute is verbal, e.g. whether we're just using "rational persuasion" and "respect for autonomy" differently, and which part that is. Finally, one of our exam papers floated the idea that "boosts" (interventions that train people's competences rather than exploiting their biases) dissolve the whole debate; say whether we'd both accept that, or whether it's a false truce. Casual tone is fine, but the formulations need to be precise enough that neither of us can later claim we were misrepresented.
