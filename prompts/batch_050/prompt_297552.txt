I write a science-and-ideas column for a British magazine, and for the Christmas issue I want to do the humour slot at the back. Here's my idea, tell me what you can do with it.

Write a parody of product-recall notices, issued by an imaginary "Office for Conceptual Standards", announcing the recall of four everyday concepts that have drifted out of specification through overuse and imitation: I'd go with "gaslighting", "curated", "artisanal" and "community", but swap one if you think there's a funnier candidate. Each notice should be deadpan regulatory prose: a recall number, the affected batch (e.g. all uses of the term on social media since a certain year), the nature of the defect (a "gaslighting" unit that now fires on ordinary disagreement; a "curated" that has come to mean merely "selected"; an "artisanal" whose small-batch certification cannot be verified), the risk to the user, and instructions for safe disposal or continued use under licence.

The joke should work on two levels and I'd like it to be fair on both: the recall notices mock the prescriptivist fantasy that words could be recalled and repaired at all, but the defect reports themselves have to accurately describe real kinds of semantic drift -- widening, bleaching, and what I think of as copycat drift, where people adopt a word for its prestige rather than because anything in their situation actually fits it. So someone who knows the concept-creep literature should recognise that the author gets it, and ideally feel the satire cuts both ways rather than being a grumpy "words mean things" column. It should also be readable and funny to someone who's never heard the term "concept creep" in their life.

About 700 words, formatted as numbered notices. Stay completely deadpan -- no winking, no punchlines flagged. At the end, add a short author's note (not part of the parody) flagging where the piece takes liberties: e.g. whether it quietly treats drift as loss when plenty of linguists would say semantic change is just what words do, and whether any of my four targets is actually a contested case rather than a clear one.

One more thing: if you think the conceit is muddled somewhere -- say, if treating drift as a manufacturing defect smuggles in a premise that ruins the joke, or one of the four concepts doesn't actually fit the brief -- say so plainly and suggest a fix rather than politely writing round the problem. I'd rather have the argument now than a dud column.
