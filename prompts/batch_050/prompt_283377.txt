I think the regress does show something is broken, but I kept failing to make the worry land without using technical vocabulary (universals, instantiation, unsaturatedness) that he waved away as question-begging.

Construct a thought experiment I could use on someone like him, built to these specifications:

1. The scenario itself must be stated entirely in everyday terms — no metaphysical vocabulary, nothing about universals, tropes, properties, or instantiation. Something like a workplace, a machine, or a bureaucracy.
2. It must generate an obvious regress of connectors-each-requiring-a-further-connector, and the regress must be visibly *bad* in the story (something concretely fails, or the explanation clearly never gets started), not merely infinite — because my friend's whole position is that a bare infinity of further facts is harmless.
3. It must not rig the case: there must be a move available within the scenario corresponding to treating one connector as self-connecting (the analogue of the relating-relation or unsaturated-concept response), so that my friend can honestly try it and we can see what it costs.

Keep the scenario to one paragraph. Then do the bookkeeping: map each element of the scenario onto the corresponding element of the unity-of-proposition problem, state what the regress-skeptic (my friend) and the regress-worrier (me) each say the scenario shows, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work of making the regress seem vicious, and say what happens to the lesson of the case if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest objection my friend could press against the thought experiment and whether you think it succeeds.
