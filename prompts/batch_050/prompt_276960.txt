I sit on the governance committee of a century-old charitable foundation, and I keep hitting the same interpretive situation. Our charter's adopted text is silent or even contrary on some point, and a trustee will produce founders' letters or draft minutes showing what the founders privately wanted, arguing that this settles the question. I notice I keep resisting in a particular way: not by denying the founders wanted it, but by denying that an unexpressed want ever became part of the rule we all joined.

I recently found Frederick Douglass making a strikingly similar move. In his 1860 Glasgow speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", answering abolitionists who read the Constitution as pro-slavery partly on the strength of what its framers privately intended, he writes:

"It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

My question is whether this stance has an established name and literature. It seems to be neither plain textualism nor ordinary originalism: Douglass does not argue that the framers' intentions are unknowable (he concedes them), nor that the words must bear their 1787 public meaning (his reading is arguably strained by that standard). He excludes the intentions on the ground that they were never enacted. Is that a recognized position in its own right? How exactly does it differ from original-public-meaning originalism, from purposivism, and from the lawyer's rule against using drafting history? And where are its known pressure points — for instance, when the adopted text is genuinely ambiguous and the only tiebreaker is intent, or when the "secret" intentions actually shaped which words were chosen? A bare label won't satisfy me; I want to know where the position's edges are and whether Douglass himself stays consistently on one side of them.
