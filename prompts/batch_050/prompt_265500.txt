So here's a position I keep defending in arguments and I want to stress-test it properly. I'll call it the non-negligibility thesis. The claim: for deciding where animal-welfare effort should go, we don't need to settle whether insects are sentient. We only need the probability of insect sentience to not be effectively zero. There are something like 10^18 insects alive at any moment, so even at a credence of 0.1% or 1%, their expected suffering dwarfs that of every farmed mammal and wild vertebrate combined, and the rational move is to prioritise insect welfare over basically everything else in near-term animal ethics. The whole point of the view is that it's supposed to be robust to uncertainty -- it concedes the science is unsettled and claims to win anyway, on the aggregation.

What would count against it? And I mean against *this* thesis, not against insect sentience itself. "Insects probably aren't sentient" is not a defeater here; the view is built to survive exactly that. So I want things that still bite if we grant a small-but-real credence. For instance: formal worries about expected-value reasoning with tiny probabilities and huge stakes (does this collapse into a Pascal's mugging structure, and is that actually an objection or just a restatement?). Worries about whether a credence like 0.1% is even meaningful -- whether below some threshold our probability assignments are noise and shouldn't be allowed to drive decisions. Worries located in the aggregation step rather than the sentience step (maybe vast-number arguments prove too much in other domains, and if I modus tollens those I have to give this one up too). Worries about moral weight *conditional* on sentience -- maybe the view smuggles in an assumption that an insect-moment of suffering is commensurable with a mammal-moment, and maybe that's where it actually lives or dies.

A few specific things I'd like:

1. The defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with a sentence on why each lands where it does.
2. For each one, an honest call on whether a committed defender -- the sort of person who actually works on invertebrate welfare -- would concede it counts against the view, or would have a standard move to deflect it, and whether that deflection works.
3. Does the thesis predict or recommend anything its rivals don't, such that some future evidence (say, particular findings about insect nociception, or particular results in the ethics of aggregation) would discriminate between it and a view that just says "wait for the science"? If it never sticks its neck out, is that a defect for a view of this kind or fine?
4. Tell me straight whether the non-negligibility framing has insulated the view into unfalsifiability, because that's my own worry about it and I can't tell if I'm being paranoid.

The defeater I'd personally find most persuasive is one showing the argument generalises to something I'd reject, so weight those heavily if you've got one.
