I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology and I'm writing a paper on Judy Benjamin-type cases (the soldier who learns a conditional about where she is, instead of learning a plain fact). The literature strikes me as two camps mostly talking past each other, so I want to draft what an adversarial collaberation between them would produce.

Camp one, the protocolists: the puzzle is just underdescribed. Once you model how the conditional information was actually generated and transmitted (what the radio message would have been in each quadrant, say), ordinary conditionalization gives a definite answer, so we don't need any new update rule. Camp two, the revisionists: conditional information is a genuinely new kind of evidence, and we need rules beyond conditionalization (imaging, minimizing relative entropy, Adams-style conditioning on subjunctive conditionals) that can deliver answers no protocol reconstruction reproduces.

Draft their joint statement: (a) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, e.g. about what van Fraassen's original formulation leaves underdetermined, about whether different message-generating protocols yield different posteriors, and about what the disagreement is *not* about; (b) the precise statements of where they still differ, worded so each side would accept the formulation of its own view; (c) the cruxes — sub-questions whose resolution would force at least one camp to move, like whether relative-entropy updating ever conflicts with conditionalization in cases with a fully specified protocol, or whether there are realistic scenarios where an agent learns a conditional with no generative story at all; and (d) any test cases both agree would bear on those cruxes, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. If some of the dispute turns out to be verbal — say, about what "learning a conditional" even means — identify which part and say why.
