So for the philosophy of causation module I TA, two grad students have been going at it all semester and my prof suggested they try an "adversarial collaboration" instead of arguing in circles. Here's the dispute: one of them defends the common-sense realist view that omissions can be genuine, literal causes -- the classic case being that your failure to water my plant while I was away *caused* its death. The other defends the austere view that absences can't cause anything, since causation is a relation between actual events involving some transfer of energy or influence, so omission-causation talk is at best loose speech: omissions show up in causal *explanations*, and norms decide whose omission we single out (we blame the gardener I hired, not my neighbor, and certainly not the Queen), but nothing absent does any causing. They both think the other side is conflating something, funnily enough.

Please act as an expert metaphysician who's refereed this kind of debate before, and think carefully rather than just picking a winner. Start by briefly brainstorming the strongest version of each side -- what each view is really committed to, and what the best (not strawman) arguments are. Then draft the joint statement they should come out with:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. I'm guessing both can sign things like: omission-talk is everywhere in ordinary life and the law; norm-governed selection explains why we cite the gardener's omission and not the Queen's; cases of double prevention are real and need to be handled by any theory; moral responsiblity for omissions doesn't by itself settle the metaphysics. Push back on my guesses if some of them aren't actually common ground, and add what I'm missing.

2. A disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept (no slanted paraphrases!), with columns for what the realist says and what the austere theorist says.

3. A cruxes section: for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or verdict written down in advance. Candidates I'd consider: preemption scenarios where the would-be preempted cause is itself an omission; whether causation and causal explanation can come apart (and which side needs them to); whether "the omission caused it" sentences can be literally true rather than useful fictions; and whether a theory of causation has to match our intuitions about cases or is allowed to be revisionary.

Finally, if part of their fight turns out to be verbal -- e.g. they're using "cause" differently and could both be right in their own dialect -- say exactly which part and why. That alone would save my seminars alot of pain.
