I have written novels for twenty years, and no work about fiction troubles my working assumptions as Pirandello's Six Characters in Search of an Author does, because it stages, with embarrassing fidelity, an experience I have had at my own desk: figures who arrive with their own consistency and refuse the story I had planned for them. I am preparing an essay on this for a literary quarterly, and I want a reading of the play through the philosophy of empty names and truth in fiction — the debate over whether a name such as Sherlock Holmes refers to anything at all, and over what makes a claim true in a story as opposed to true of it.

The questions I need pressed, in order. First, the six claim to exist, and to possess fixed natures, although their author declined to write them: does the play vindicate the creationist picture on which characters are abstract artifacts dependent on an author — the six, after all, are literally searching for one — or does it embarrass that picture by reversing the dependency, showing the author answerable to material that precedes him? Second, the Father argues that a character is stable and determinate in a way no living person is; yet the play's ending leaves it unresolved whether the Boy's revolver shot was real death or stage trick, and the Manager despairs of telling which. What do the standard accounts — Russellian negative existentials, Meinongian objects, Walton's make-believe, the artifactualism associated with Searle and Thomasson — say about whether there is a fact of the matter here, and does the play's nested structure, fictional characters who within the fiction are real, generate a difficulty for any of them that ordinary fiction does not? Third, a fairness check: where does the play resist conscription into ontology altogether — the melodrama, the Stepdaughter's shame, the Manager's commercial impatience — and what should an honest reader concede the philosophical reading misses? Finally, I keep telling my students that characters only feel autonomous, and Pirandello keeps proving me a liar. Does any account of empty names explain why the experience of authorship is as it is, and what does the play give someone who makes characters that the arguments alone cannot?
