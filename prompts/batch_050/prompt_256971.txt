Last weekend I got into a long and surprisingly heated argument with a close friend about griefbots — chatbots trained on the messages, emails, and voice recordings of a dead person so that the bereaved can keep "talking" to them. My friend defended the practice by analogy: a griefbot, she said, is just the latest in a long line of remembrance technologies, no more sinister than keeping someone's letters, photographs, or home videos. I found myself insisting the analogy fails, and afterward I wrote up my position as a short essay draft, which I've pasted below. I'm hoping to submit it to an undergraduate philosophy journal, and before I do I'd like rigorous comments: tell me which moves are sound, which overreach, and which objections I haven't adequately answered. I am especially worried that my argument proves too much — that it condemns things like rereading old letters with imagination, or even remembering someone at all — so please press hard on that.

The draft:

Title: The Counterfeit Continuation: Why a Griefbot Is Not a Photograph

(1) Defenders of griefbots say they are merely the next step in an old progression: portraits, letters, recordings, now interactive simulation. I think this misclassifies the technology. Every earlier artifact of remembrance is a trace: it is causally downstream of the person and fixed at their death. A photograph shows what the person actually looked like; a letter contains what they actually wrote. A griefbot is not a trace but a generator. It produces novel utterances the person never made, and it produces them by a process — statistical prediction over patterns in data — that is wholly indifferent to whether any given utterance is true of the person. The bot does not check its outputs against the deceased; nothing could.

(2) This suggests a distinction between two kinds of fidelity. Source fidelity is what a recording has: the content conveyed was actually produced by the person. Pattern fidelity is the best a griefbot can have: its outputs resemble the person's style, vocabulary, and opinions closely enough to be recognizable. My central claim is that pattern fidelity suffices for comfort but not for relationship. What the grieving person wants — what I want, I admit — is continued contact with the particular individual. What the bot offers is continuation in appearance only: a counterfeit continuation.

(3) Critics often reach for consent: the dead cannot agree to be simulated, therefore griefbots are wrong. I think consent is a red herring, and I want to locate the harm elsewhere, in the epistemic situation of the living user. Because the bot's outputs are fluent and personalized, the user cannot reliably distinguish outputs that genuinely reflect the deceased's dispositions from outputs that merely flatter the user's own expectations and needs. I call this the ventriloquism problem: in every exchange, some unmeasurable portion of what the "dead person" says is actually the user's own voice, projected. The user of a griefbot is in the position of a ventriloquist who cannot tell which words are the dummy's.

(4) I anticipate one strong objection. Human memory is also a generator, not a trace: remembering someone is reconstructive, confabulatory, and shaped by what the rememberer needs. If generation disqualifies griefbots, it disqualifies memory too. My tentative reply is that memory, however reconstructive, retains the right causal ancestry — it is built from actual encounters with the person — whereas the bot's outputs may be causally anchored to nothing the person ever did or was. I concede this reply is underdeveloped.

Please give me detailed prose comments first, organized by section: the strongest objection I haven't considered, the point where the trace/generator distinction is most vulnerable, whether the ventriloquism problem generalizes further than I want it to, and how the causal-ancestry reply in section 4 could be strengthened or whether it should be abandoned.
