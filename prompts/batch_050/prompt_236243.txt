I've been trying to pin down what I actually believe about humanitarian military intervention, so I went by my gut on the cases everyone argues about and wrote down my verdicts before thinking too hard. Here's what I came up with. NATO bombing Serbia over Kosovo in 1999: justified, even though the Security Council never authorized it, because the alternative was letting the ethnic cleansing run its course. The Iraq invasion in 2003: not justified, and I don't think it becomes justified even if you grant (what turned out false) that Saddam had WMDs, because the humanitarian case there was mostly a retrospective add-on. The 2011 Libya intervention: justified when it started -- protecting Benghazi from a threatened massacre -- but unjustified by the end, when it had clearly become about getting rid of Gaddafi, even though I also can't say Gaddafi staying in power was a good outcome. Not intervening in Rwanda in 1994: a catastrophic failure, we'd have been right to send troops. Not intervening in Syria after 2013: I genuinely can't land anywhere -- it feels like Rwanda but also like Iraq, depending on which hour you ask me. And one hypothetical I keep testing myself with: if some future Kosovo-type situation arises but the only country willing to intervene happens to border the region and obviously stands to gain influence there, I still think the intervention is fine, though I'd trust the intervening government's stated reasons less.

What principle, if any, captures all that? I have a rough candidate of my own -- something like: intervention is justified when there's an imminent mass atrocity, a reasonable prospect of stopping it, and proportionality between the means used and the harm prevented, with the interveners' motives irrelevant to the justification even if relevant to our trust -- but it seems to handle Iraq and Rwanda fine while choking on Libya, where the same operation slid from justified to unjustified without the underlying facts on the ground changing that much, and on my hypothetical, where I want motives not to matter yet clearly let them color how much evidence I'd demand. So: is there a cleaner principle that gets my verdicts, or does getting them require something clunky with lots of clauses? If a simpler principle means dropping one of my verdicts, tell me which one should go and why. And say what your principle predicts for the Syria case I'm stuck on, and for one case nobody ever brings up: intervention that would work, but only by bombing the capital of a country whose government is committing atrocities in a far-off province, with large civilian casualties in a city full of people who never supported the regime.
