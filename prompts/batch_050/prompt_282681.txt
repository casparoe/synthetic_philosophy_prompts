A friend and I argued late into the night over whether "generations" -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z -- pick out anything real, and I came away suspecting my own verdicts don't cohere. I'd like to know what principle, if any, underlies them. Here they are:

1. The Baby Boomers are a genuine kind: the label tracks the postwar birth-rate spike, a real demographic discontinuity, so the boundaries are not arbitrary.
2. "Gen Z" is not a real kind: the cutoffs were fixed by pollsters and marketers, and other boundaries would have served just as well.
3. People who spent their late teens and early twenties during the Second World War form a real kind, even though this grouping cuts across the standard labels: a shared formative event at the same life stage produces genuinely shared dispositions.
4. Millennials are a real kind, because their formative years coincided with 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, and mass internet adoption.
5. Micro-cohorts like "Xennials" (born around the turn of the 1980s) are not real: at that granularity the exercise becomes astrology.
6. "Generation" in the genealogical sense -- position in a lineage -- is obviously real but obviously not what the sociological labels mean.

Verdicts 3 and 4 seem to commit me to counting Gen Z (formative years shaped by COVID and smartphones) as real too, contradicting 2; and 1 and 4 rest on entirely different criteria -- demographic discontinuity versus formative events -- which makes me suspect I just reach for whichever criterion validates a label I already like. Is there a single principled account of when a birth cohort constitutes a real social kind, as opposed to a convenient fiction, that captures most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, say which verdict it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it implies for cases I haven't listed -- a cohort defined by a one-off event (the fall of the Berlin Wall) versus one defined by a slow trend (rising university attendance). Finally, please distinguish what is genuinely settled here -- the demographic facts, the evidence for and against measurable cohort effects -- from what remains contested among sociologists and philosophers of social science, rather than presenting one side of that dispute as the consensus.
