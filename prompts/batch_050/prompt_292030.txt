I'm a PhD student in machine learning, working on statistical methods for high-stakes decision systems, and I recently organized a reading group session on fairness in algorithmic lending. We discussed a stylized case: a municipal bank deploys a model that scores small-business loan applicants by predicted default risk, and an audit reveals that the underlying default rates genuinely differ between two demographic groups in the applicant pool. Two postdocs, both of whom I respect, got into an argument I could not settle, and I'd like an outside ruling. The exchange, as best I reconstructed it afterward, ran as follows. (1) Priya opened by saying the model is fair because it is calibrated within each group: among applicants assigned the same risk score, the actual default rate is the same regardless of group, so a score means exactly the same thing for everyone, and that is what fairness in prediction amounts to. (2) Marcus replied that calibration is compatible with badly asymmetric errors: among applicants who would in fact have repaid, members of one group are flagged as high-risk at roughly twice the rate, so the group with the lower base rate sees far more of its qualified members wrongly denied; fairness requires equalized error rates, and calibration merely guarantees that the bank's mistakes are well measured, not fairly distributed. (3) Priya countered that if you adjust the model to equalize false positive rates, you destroy calibration, at which point identical scores correspond to different real risks for different groups — the scores stop meaning the same thing for everyone — which is itself a straightforward kind of unfairness, and possibly an actionable one. (4) Marcus concluded that the impossibility results in this literature show fairness cannot be fully achieved whenever base rates differ, which proves fairness is a political choice about whose errors matter, not a mathematical property a model can simply have; Priya's final word was that the impossibility theorems presuppose imperfect prediction, so the honest engineering response is better features and better prediction to shrink the gap, not choosing a side in error distribution, to which Marcus said she was changing the subject, since no realistic model is perfect and the theorem binds everywhere that matters.

Please adjudicate this move by move — for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with a qualification, and justify the verdict. I have two specific worries I'd like addressed. First, is Marcus's inference in (4) valid: does the joint unsatisfiability of the standard fairness metrics actually entail that fairness is a political choice, or is he smuggling in a stronger claim than the mathematics supports (and note that he moves rather quickly from "these particular metrics conflict" to "fairness cannot be achieved")? Second, does Priya's better-features response in (4) genuinely engage the impossibility result — given that the theorems require imperfect prediction as an antecedent — or is she, as Marcus charged, evading it by appealing to a limiting case no deployed system will ever reach? Finally, if there is something both of them are missing — for instance, whether calibration and equalized error rates are even answers to the same question, or whether the whole framing treats fairness as purely a property of the model rather than of the decision policy built on it — please identify it.
