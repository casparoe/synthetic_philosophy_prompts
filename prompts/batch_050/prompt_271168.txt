I write a column for a general-audience science-and-ideas magazine, and we've just started doing short Q&As with philosophers on topics where the stakes feel genuinely puzzling to smart non-specialists. Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends the "one thing" view of material constitution: when a lump of clay is shaped into a statue, she says, there aren't two things occupying the pedestal — the statue just is the lump of clay, full stop, and the apparent two-ness is an illusion created by how we sort and count things. I've read enough to know the standard pressure points, but I'm worried about the interview going in circles: I put the modal objection to her (the lump could survive being squashed, the statue couldn't), she gives the stock reply about how modal talk is ambiguous or how "being a statue" is a temporary, extrinsic matter of how something is shaped and regarded, and then we're stuck restating our priors for twenty minutes.

Draft me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that actually moves things along. I want at least one question pressing Leibniz's law directly (if the statue and the lump are one thing, how can one have a property the other lacks?), one on the counting problem (how many things are on the pedestal right now, and if the answer is "one," why does it feel like we can count two ways?), one on whether her view generalizes to messier cases — a person and their body, a river and its water — and one closing question about what, if anything, could convince her she's wrong. Crucially, for each question I need a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give: e.g., when she says "being a statue is just a phase the lump goes through," I want to ask whether that means statues don't really go out of existence when squashed, and when she says identity is what matters for counting, I want to press whether she's smuggling in two sortals after all. The questions should sound like a curious journalist, not a grad student showing off, but they shouldn't let her coast on the usual deflections either.

Answer in American English, and start the response with the first interview question itself — no preface, no restating my request, no framing paragraph before the list.
