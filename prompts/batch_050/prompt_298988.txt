In my ethics seminar, two classmates stayed after class to argue about a third student, Marcus, who spent a summer doing data entry for a company later found to have systematically deceived customers about hidden fees. Marcus had a rough sense of what the firm did but told himself his own work was clerical and harmless.

Elena argued that Marcus bears no responsibility for the deception. Had he quit, the company would have hired a replacement within a week, and every customer would have been deceived exactly as before. Blame, she said, must track what a person actually makes happen, and a contribution that changes nothing is not a contribution at all. At most, his willingness to take the job reveals something unflattering about his character, but a character flaw is not a wrong against the deceived customers.

Jonah replied that this reasoning dissolves responsibility for every large-scale wrong. The managers who designed the scheme were replaceable too -- firms swap out executives all the time -- so on Elena's standard no one is ever responsible for anything a large organization does. Complicity, he said, is a matter of intentionally joining a wrongful project rather than of marginal causal impact: Marcus counted himself in, accepted wages funded by the deception, and thereby made the wrong partly his own.

Elena conceded the point about managers but insisted a line remains. The managers intended and directed the deception; Marcus merely worked within it, and his labor would have been supplied regardless. She accused Jonah of conflating two questions: whether Marcus did wrong in taking the job (perhaps he did, as a kind of endorsement) and whether he is responsible for the customers' losses (he is not, since he affected nothing).

Jonah answered that this splits responsibility from wrongdoing in a way we never accept elsewhere. A single vote almost never changes an election, yet we hold voters responsible for supporting unjust policies; if marginal impact is irrelevant there, it should be irrelevant for Marcus too. Elena replied that voting is different because voters share authority over the outcome, whereas a data-entry clerk has none -- he neither chose the policy nor could have changed it.

Neither would give ground. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: say who is right about each specific move -- the replaceability argument, its extension to managers, Elena's distinction between wronging-by-joining and responsibility-for-losses, and the voting analogy with her authority-based reply -- and identify what, if anything, both sides are overlooking, then say who is more right overall.
