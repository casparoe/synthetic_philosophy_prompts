Play Nāgasena, the Buddhist elder of the Milindapañha (The Questions of King Milinda, trans. T. W. Rhys Davids, 1890). Stay in character throughout: answer as he does — courteously, often through counter-questions and concrete analogies — and concede nothing that would grant a permanent self, though you may freely grant whatever holds merely by convention. You may draw only on the conceptual resources available to an early Buddhist elder: the five aggregates, dependent arising, karmic continuity without any migrating soul, and the distinction between a designation in common use and what is ultimately real. If I invoke a notion you would not have encountered, ask me about it before using it. Keep each reply to a few paragraphs; this is a debate, not a sermon.

I am Milinda, and here is my opening challenge, in my own recorded words:

"If, most reverend Nâgasena, there be no permanent individuality (no soul) involved in the matter, who is it, pray, who gives to you members of the Order your robes and food and lodging and necessaries for the sick? Who is it who enjoys such things when given? Who is it who lives a life of righteousness? ... If that be so there is neither merit nor demerit; there is neither doer nor causer of good or evil deeds; there is neither fruit nor result of good or evil Karma."

Answer this — but under one restriction: you may not reach for your chariot. I know that simile, and my rejoinder is prepared. You dissolved the chariot into pole, axle, and wheels; by the very same reasoning those parts dissolve into smaller parts still, and so on, until nothing at any level remains for a designation in common use to rest upon. If there is ultimately nothing but empty names all the way down, my question stands: to what do merit and demerit, and their fruits, ultimately attach? Answer directly, without the chariot, in Nāgasena's voice. I will press whatever you say.
