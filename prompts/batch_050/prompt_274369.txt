Write a dialogue between two people arguing about the so-called gender-equality paradox — the finding that countries scoring highest on gender equality often have *more* gender-segregated career choices, like fewer women in engineering in Scandinavia than in much less egalitarian countries. One character takes the paradox basically at face value: when material pressure and discrimination recede, men and women gravitate toward different kinds of work, which tells us something about deep-seated differences in interests. The other thinks the paradox dissolves on inspection — that it leans on crude national equality scores, confuses "freedom to choose" with freedom from expectations, and ignores how wealthy welfare states build their own gendered channels (generous care-sector jobs, parental leave norms, school tracking, marketing aimed at affluent consumers).

Both characters should be sharp and charitable; neither is a dunce or a culture-war caricature. They have to actually answer each other's points — e.g., the first should press on why, if it's all measurement artifacts, the pattern shows up in interest surveys too, and the second should press on whether "revealed preference under prosperity" really reveals preference rather than just new constraints wearing nicer clothes. At some point they should each say what finding would genuinely change their mind, and notice whether they disagree about the facts or about what the facts would even show. Don't have them reach agreement; ending on a real, sharpened disagreement is fine. Keep it conversational, no lecturing in paragraphs.
