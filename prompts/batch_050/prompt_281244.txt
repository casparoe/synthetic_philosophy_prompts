I chair the governance committee of a community land trust — about 140 households on land the trust owns collectively and leases to residents on 99-year terms. I've never studied political theory, but I've spent two years on this committee and have thought hard about one question we now have to answer formally. The trust must hold a referendum on whether to accept a county buyout that would dissolve the trust and relocate everyone within a decade. Before we can hold it, we must define who votes, and our bylaws are useless: they say only that "members" vote, one vote per leasehold, and membership tracks the original property lines drawn in the 1970s.

Here is the problem in full. The categories under dispute: long-term renters in trust-built homes who have never held a leasehold; absentee leaseholders who rent their units out and live elsewhere; thirty residents of a housing cooperative that holds a single leasehold (one vote, under current rules); households just outside the trust boundary whose wells and drainage depend on trust land and who would lose their water table if the land is sold to developers instead of to the county; and, on my own argument, the children currently growing up here, since a 99-year lease decision binds them far longer than it binds me. My own leaning, after much thought, is that there are really two tests pulling apart — who must live with the consequences, and who is legally bound by the decision — and they give different answers, and I can't get them to line up.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend a specific franchise rule, including whether all votes count equally and what to do about the cooperative and the minors, give reasons the committee can act on, and treat honestly the strongest case for keeping things as they are — roughly, that a defined, stable, property-anchored electorate is what makes the trust governable at all.
