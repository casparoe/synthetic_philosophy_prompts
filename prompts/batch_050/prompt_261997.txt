I am a high school student preparing for a philosophy seminar on Abelard’s claim that sin consists in consent rather than in the outward act. Our group reconstructed his view into the argument below, but it appears to prove more than we can accept. Assume throughout that both agents have the same beliefs, motives, and degree of resolve, and that the court somehow knows their inward consent with certainty.

1. A mere desire or temptation is not sinful; one becomes sinful only by inwardly consenting to do what one takes to be wrong.
2. The outward performance of the act is not itself an additional sin; the moral wrong is located in the consent.
3. Whether an agent’s consent produces the intended outcome often depends on circumstances outside the agent’s control.
4. Circumstances outside an agent’s control cannot make that agent more or less morally guilty.
5. Therefore, a person who fully consents to commit murder but is accidentally thwarted is exactly as morally guilty as a person with the same consent whose victim dies.
6. People who are exactly equally guilty deserve exactly the same punitive response.
7. Punishment should be proportional to what people deserve, not to outcomes that happen to result from their choices.
8. Therefore, if the court knows that the two agents consented in exactly the same way, justice requires it to impose the same punishment on the thwarted attempt and the completed murder.

We cannot accept the conclusion that the completed act adds nothing whatsoever to what the law may justly punish. Which premise or inference should be rejected? Please identify the exact point where the argument fails, or, if it is valid, explain which initially plausible premise is least costly to abandon.

Your answer should distinguish at least three claims: what makes something a sin, what makes a person blameworthy, and what makes a punishment legally justified. Explain why premise 6 or the move from moral guilt to punishment may have seemed obvious, and compare the costs of rejecting that move with the costs of denying premise 2, premise 4, or premise 7. Also consider whether consequences, deterrence, public reassurance, or respect for victims can justify different punishments without implying that the successful murderer is more inwardly sinful. Finally, state whether an Abelardian can retain the important distinction between temptation and consent while still resisting the conclusion, or whether accepting Abelard’s starting point commits one to punishing attempts and completed crimes equally.
