I spent forty years doing experimental physics and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of mind, most recently Schrödinger's later writings where he flirts with Vedanta -- the idea that there's really only one consciousness and the many individual minds are facets or appearances of it. In that whole corner of the literature people keep leaning on the same analogy: the brain doesn't produce consciousness, it receives or filters it, like a radio picking up a broadcast. A damaged radio garbles the music but the broadcast itself is untouched -- so (the argument goes) brain damage, split brains, and anaesthesia are no evidence at all that consciousness is made by the brain.

Please audit this analogy for me. Lay out what is supposed to map onto what, then tell me where it breaks. A few things that strike me as a layman: a radio is a designed decoder of a signal we can detect independently with other instruments, and there's no analogous independent detection of the "broadcast" here; also brains show an extremely fine-grained dependence of content -- specific memories, grammar, personality, perceptual quirks -- that a mere reciever or filter doesn't naturally explain, since receivers don't store the songs. What else does the analogy quietly smuggle in? In particular it seems like a non sequitur that even if the receiver picture were right, the one-consciousness conclusion would follow: my radio and your radio play different stations, after all, so plurality of receivers seems to argue against unity, not for it. Finally: can the analogy actually support Schrödinger's Vedanta-flavored conclusion, or does it at most push back on materialism while leaving the unity thesis untouched? And if it can't carry that weight, is there a better analogy for whatever real puzzle is left over?
