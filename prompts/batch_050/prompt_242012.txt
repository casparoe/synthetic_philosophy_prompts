I sit on the model release board of a mid-sized AI lab (seven of us: two researchers, a security person, a lawyer, an ethicist, and two engineers) and we have to decide what to do with our newest model, which I'll call Kestrel. Kestrel is clearly the strongest open-weights-adjacent model anyone outside the top three labs has, it's maybe 6-12 months behind the actual frontier, and our internal evals are annoyingly ambiguous: on the dangerous-capability evals it sometimes flirts with the thresholds we said would trigger restricted release, sometimes not, depending on scaffolding and how generous the prompting is. Nobody thinks it's existentially dangerous on its own. The fight is about precedent and trajectory. We've narrowed it to three options: release the weights openly like we always have; keep it API-only with monitored fine-tuning; or do a staged release, closed now, open in a year or when a comparably capable model is already public, whichever comes first.

Here's the wrinkle that's been eating our meetings. One camp argues that open release is basically free safety research: thousands of outsiders red-team it, fine-tune it, interpret it, and the whole field learns what these models can do, whereas a closed release just means the capabilities exist with less scrutiny. The other camp says this conflates scrutiny with proliferation — the scrutiny argument works for vulnerabilities you can patch, but you can't patch weights once they're out, and "someone else will release this capability in a year anyway" is a rationalization that would justify releasing literally anything. A junior engineer on the board tried to broker peace by proposing that we publish a detailed capability report without the weights, and both camps hated it for opposite reasons, which either means it's the right answer or the worst one. Our last meeting ended with the ethicist saying the whole debate is actually about whether the board thinks of itself as governing a product or governing a scientific contribution, and I'm not sure shes wrong but I also have to write the memo.

So: draft the board's decision memo. Pick one of the three options (or the engineer's report-only variant, but only if you actually believe it) and commit to it — I don't want a memo that lays out the considerations and leaves the call to us, because leaving the call to us is the entire problem. The memo should give the recommendation up front, the reasoning, an honest rendering of the strongest argument for the option you're rejecting that I'd have to defend against at the next meeting, and a set of concrete conditions under which the board should revisit the decision (what new evidence about Kestrel or the ecosystem would flip it). Keep it the length a real board memo would be, not a treatise.
