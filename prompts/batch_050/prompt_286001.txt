In a seminar discussion of my thesis chapter on twentieth-century warfare, a colleague waved off my worry that industrialized killing undermines progress narratives by declaring that "we live in the most peaceful era in human history," and several others nodded as if this settled something. It clearly did not settle the point I was making, but when I tried to say why, I found I could not articulate which of several possible claims was being asserted and which my objection actually touched. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing? For each reading, please address:

1. The measure of violence it presupposes -- deaths in war only, or also homicide, sexual violence, domestic abuse, corporal and capital punishment, and structural harms -- and whether expanding or narrowing that measure changes the verdict;
2. Whether the claim is per capita (a person's chance of dying violently) or absolute (total violent deaths), and whether these can diverge so sharply that one is true and the other false;
3. The relevant comparison class and time horizon -- prehistoric hunter-gatherer societies, the Thirty Years' War, the World Wars, or only the last few decades -- and whether the slogan survives on all of them or only some;
4. Whether the claim is about realized deaths or about the underlying propensity for violence, and what difference it makes that a single nuclear exchange could retroactively overturn the realized-deaths version while leaving the propensity version untouched.

For each precisification, give a verdict: is it near-trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and likely false, or not a factual claim at all but a disguised value judgment about which harms matter? Then say which reading my colleague most plausibly intended, whether that reading would actually rebut the worry that modern states can kill on an unprecedented scale, and whether there is any version of the slogan strong enough to support the rhetorical work it was doing in our seminar -- namely, foreclosing pessimism about the moral trajectory of modernity. If some of the readings are incommensurable with one another because there is no theory-neutral way to aggregate different kinds of harm, say so explicitly rather than forcing a ranking.
