Write a satirical piece of roughly 800 words, in the form of a keynote address by an invented self-help guru — call him whatever you like — whose entire philosophy is built on Gödel's incompleteness theorems. He has a book, a seminar, and a slide deck, and he is going to explain to his audience why mathematics proves that they can never fully know themselves, that no relationship can ever be complete, that science will never reach "the deeper truths," and that the human mind transcends any machine. The address should be deadpan throughout: he never winks, never hedges, and the comedy must come entirely from the content, not from the narrator mocking him.

The hard constraint is this: every claim he makes must be a slight exaggeration of an error that is actually made in the popular misuse of Gödel, so that a reader who knows the logic recognizes each specific mistake, while a reader who doesn't still enjoys the speech. His repertoire should include at least the following moves, woven naturally into the speech rather than listed:

1. Sliding from "any consistent formal system capable of expressing arithmetic contains true sentences it cannot prove" to "there are truths that no one can ever know," without noticing that the unprovable sentence is unprovable *in that system*, not unprovable simpliciter.
2. Treating the human mind (or a marriage, or a legal code, or the self) as though it were a recursively axiomatized formal system rich enough to do arithmetic, so that incompleteness applies to it directly.
3. Reading the second incompleteness theorem as showing that mathematics might be inconsistent, or that we can never really trust arithmetic, rather than that a sufficiently strong consistent system cannot prove its own consistency by its own methods.
4. A version of the familiar argument that minds outrun machines, delivered with total confidence that it is a theorem rather than a contentious philosophical inference with a well-known gap in it.
5. Conflating incompleteness with incomputability, undecidability, and quantum indeterminacy, as though these were all one insight.

The speech should build to a grandiose conclusion in which the guru's logic, taken at face value, undermines the very book and seminar he is selling — and he should arrive there without noticing.

After the address, append a "translator's notes" section of about 150 words, written in a dry editorial voice, that identifies each of the garbled claims and states, in one or two sentences each, what the theorems actually do show. The notes are where the accuracy lives: the speech may be nonsense, but the notes must be correct.
