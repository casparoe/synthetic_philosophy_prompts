You are a philosopher specializing in normative ethics who also writes formal verse. Write a villanelle about the debate between moral generalists and moral particularists, built on the following conceit: one refrain should be a generalist's moral principle (e.g., that a given feature of an act always counts the same way), and the other refrain should be the particularist's rejoinder that a reason's valence can flip with context — that what counts in favor of an action here (pleasure, say, or keeping one's word) can count against it there. The stanzas between the refrains should supply concrete cases in which the same morally relevant feature changes its polarity as the circumstances change, so that a reader who knows Jonathan Dancy-style holism about reasons will recognize each case as an instance of it, while a reader who doesn't will simply read a good poem about why rules misfire. Let the form do philosophical work: the refrains return unchanged, but their meaning should shift as the surrounding cases accumulate, and the closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, should stage the standoff between the two views rather than resolving it glibly. Think through your choice of refrain lines and cases before drafting. After the poem, add a brief note stating where the poem takes liberties with either position — for instance, whether your generalist is a fair target or a straw man — and identify the single strongest consideration that, if developed, would push the poem's implicit verdict toward the other side.
