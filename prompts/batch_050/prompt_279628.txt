I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm commissioning a long-form essay for our ideas section. In 2024, after roughly fifteen years of deliberation, the governing bodies of the geologic timescale -- the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy, and then the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences -- voted down the proposal to make the Anthropocene a formal epoch. The proposal had envisioned a "golden spike" marker in the sediments of Crawford Lake in Canada, with a start date around 1950, keyed to the plutonium fallout from hydrogen bomb tests and the mid-century surge in population, industry, fertilizer use, and resource extraction often called the Great Acceleration. Yet the term itself, popularized by the atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen around 2000, is thriving everywhere outside stratigraphy: in climate science, history, philosophy, and ordinary journalism like mine. Rival start dates have their champions -- the dawn of farming thousands of years ago, the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 following the Columbian exchange, the coal-fired Industrial Revolution of the late eighteenth century -- and some researchers argue that the whole enterprise of pinning a single global date on human influence is a mistake, because human impacts spread across the planet unevenly over millennia, more like the slow, drawn-out transformation of the Great Oxidation Event than the clean worldwide layer left by an asteroid impact.

For the essay, I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of science with deep expertise in historical geology and the sociology of scientific classification. Write the essay itself, addressed to a general educated readership, and reach a firm conclusion on two connected questions: when, if ever, the Anthropocene should be said to have begun, and who should get to decide. Please do the following:

1. Begin with a short brainstorming pass (clearly marked, before the essay proper) in which you sketch the strongest case for at least three candidate start dates -- including the mid-twentieth-century golden spike and at least one much earlier candidate -- and the strongest case for refusing a start date altogether.

2. In the essay, take a position on the core conceptual dispute: is the Anthropocene best understood as an epoch, which by stratigraphic convention demands a single, globally synchronous marker, or as a diachronous "event" that unfolds at different times in different places? Explain what turns on this distinction, and don't treat it as merely terminological.

3. Address the authority question head-on. Stratigraphers voted, and they said no. Is that the final word, one legitimate verdict among several, or arguably the wrong jury altogether? Consider whether the Anthropocene is fundamentally a geological unit, a historical period, a political diagnosis, or some hybrid -- and what follows for who is entitled to define it.

4. Steelman the losing side before dismissing it, avoid hedging your way to a non-answer, and end with a clear, defended verdict: a specific start date (or a principled refusal to assign one) and a specific answer to who decides.

Think carefully before committing, and aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words for the essay itself.
