In clinical pain medicine, two norms sit side by side. On the ward, self-report is sovereign: nurses are trained that the patient's word settles the matter, pain intensity is recorded on a 0–10 numerical scale exactly as the patient states it, and analgesic dosing protocols key morphine or oxycodone to that number. But when the same patient appears before an insurance tribunal or a disability assessment, the very same report is treated with suspicion: adjudicators look for inconsistency, exaggeration, malingering, and may discount the testimony entirely if it outruns the imaging findings. Meanwhile, proposals to replace or check the verbal scale with objective measures — functional neuroimaging, pupillometry, autonomic markers — are repeatedly rejected as grounds for overriding the patient: a scan suggesting low pain activity is not, at present, permitted to contradict a reported 9.

What view of consciousness and of first-person authority does this set of practices presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the ward norm presuppose that patients have incorrigible access to a private phenomenal state, or only the weaker claim that their reports are the best available evidence for it? Is the practice compatible with a heterophenomenological stance, on which reports are treated as a text to be interpreted — authoritative as data about what the subject believes and experiences, without committing the investigator to the existence of the phenomenal items the text describes? Or does dosing potent drugs on the strength of a number require something stronger than that neutrality allows?

Consider also whether the two settings presuppose consistent things: can one coherently hold that self-report is incorrigible in the clinic yet defeasible at the tribunal, or does the combined practice tacitly endorse a single view — perhaps that reports are evidence rather than verdicts — which the ward's rhetoric merely overstates? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with: would a behaviourist, an eliminativist about qualia, or a naive Cartesian each be able to license the same protocols, and if so, where is the practice genuinely philosophically underdetermined? Finally, say what would have to change — in the scale, the dosing algorithms, and the tribunal — if the institution adopted a thoroughgoing third-person methodology in which neural markers outrank testimony, and whether anything of clinical or moral importance would be lost that the current practice, whatever its muddled foundations, manages to protect.
