I work in the accounts department of a mid-sized firm. Three months ago I discovered that a senior colleague — widely loved, close to retirement, and the person who trained me — had been quietly approving inflated invoices from a supplier run by his brother-in-law. The sums were modest relative to our turnover, but the practice had gone on for years. I reported it through the proper channel. He was dismissed, and because the investigation was confidential, almost nobody in the office knows what I actually found. What has circulated instead is a story in which a disloyal junior destroyed a good man's final years over a technicality. Several colleagues no longer speak to me; my manager has hinted that my "judgement" will be remembered at review time. I could substantially repair my standing by letting a softened version of events circulate — one in which I merely passed on a query raised by someone else — and part of me wants to, not out of fear, but because the unanimous disapproval of people I respect makes me wonder whether I have misjudged the matter. Yet when I rehearse the full facts to myself, I keep concluding that anyone who knew everything I know, and had no stake in it, would have done what I did. My question is this: when the verdict of everyone actually around me conflicts with the verdict I expect from a fully informed and disinterested onlooker, which should carry more weight in deciding what I do next — and does it matter whether my motive for wanting my reputation back is a wish to be thought well of, or a wish to deserve being thought well of? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
