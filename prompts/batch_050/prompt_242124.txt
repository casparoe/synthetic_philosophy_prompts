I'm in the middle of a conversion course in psychology, having come over from linguistics, and I've just finished the module on the architecture of cognition. Early on we read Fodor's case for a modular mind -- fast, automatic, informationally encapsulated systems handling perception and language, feeding a slow, general-purpose central processor that does the actual thinking. Then, in the back half of the term, we hit the evolutionary psychology literature, where the picture changes completely: the mind, we were told, is like a Swiss army knife. There is no general-purpose blade at all; there is only a large collection of specialised tools, each designed by natural selection for a specific recurrent problem our ancestors faced -- detecting cheaters, recognising faces, acquiring grammar, tracking contaminants, navigating kinship. Our seminar leader leans on this analogy constantly, and it does real argumentative work for her: because you would never use one blade of the knife for every job, she argues, we should be deeply suspicious of domain-general theories of learning, of g as an explanation of much, and of the idea that children come equipped with anything like a blank central processor. The knife, on her telling, is the argument for massive modularity in miniature.

I'd like you to audit the analogy properly, because I suspect it's doing more work than it can bear and I have an essay due on exactly this. First, give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the individual blades and tools, what plays the role of the handle they share, what corresponds to the designer who built the knife for foreseeable tasks, and what corresponds to the knife's owner deciding which tool to pull out -- if anything does. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and be discriminating about it: I want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions being drawn, not just a list of ways minds and penknives differ. For instance, the blades of a real knife don't interact, don't develop, and don't get modified by their own use, whereas cognitive systems plainly influence each other and are built over the course of development -- but does that undermine the specific lesson about domain-general learning, or is it beside the point? Third, the knife analogy seems to quietly import a sharp distinction between the tool and its user: who, in the massively modular mind, is supposed to be choosing which module to deploy, and if the answer is just "other modules," has the analogy smuggled in a homunculus it then disowns?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusions survive the audit. If the Swiss army knife picture, taken strictly, licenses only the weak claim that minds contain some specialised machinery, but is being stretched to license the strong claim that the mind is nothing but specialised machinery, I'd like that stated plainly -- Fodor himself, after all, accepted modularity and rejected the massive version, so the analogy apparently can't be compulsory even for a modularist. And if there's a better comparison for whatever the truth of the matter turns out to be -- a toolbox in use by a crew rather than a knife in one pocket, an ecosystem of competing specialists, a smartphone with apps sharing an operating system -- I'd like to know what it is and what it captures that the knife cannot.
