I am a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of emergency powers, and I would like a disciplined case analysis rather than a survey of the literature. Please analyze the following episode through the lens of the dirty-hands problem in politics — the framework associated above all with Michael Walzer's essay on political action and dirty hands, on which a political actor can face a situation in which the morally right thing to do is also a genuine moral wrong, so that doing one's duty leaves a residue of guilt that must be acknowledged and borne rather than dissolved into the justification.

The episode: in the wake of the September 11 attacks, Germany enacted an Aviation Security Act (the Luftsicherheitsgesetz), which came into force in January 2005. One provision would have authorized the defense minister to order the air force to shoot down a civilian passenger aircraft once it had become apparent that hijackers intended to use it as a weapon against people on the ground. The scenario the legislature had in mind was one in which killing everyone aboard, including wholly innocent passengers and crew, would foreseeably save a larger number of people in the target zone. In February 2006, the Federal Constitutional Court declared the provision void, holding that authorizing the state to sacrifice the innocent passengers for the benefit of others treats them as mere objects of a state decision and is therefore incompatible with the inviolable human dignity guaranteed by Article 1 of the German constitution, together with the right to life.

I know the standard dignity-versus-consequences framing of this decision, and I am not asking you to relitigate it. What I want is the dirty-hands analysis, which the Court conspicuously did not give. In particular, please address the following, and note that the first and last are the parts I care most about:

1. A precise mapping. In a dirty-hands case there is an agent who must act, a wrong that is done, a good that the wrong secures, and a residue. Identify each here — and take seriously that there are at least three candidate agents (the minister who would issue the order, the pilot who would execute it, and the parliament that wrote the authorization in advance) and say whether the dirt distributes across them or lands somewhere specific.

2. What the lens captures that the Court's reasoning does not. The Court's holding has the consequence that in an actual 9/11-style scenario the state must simply let the aircraft reach its target. Does the dirty-hands framework give a more honest description of what that consequence costs, and of what a minister who disobeyed the constitution in the actual emergency would have done and owed afterward?

3. Where the lens strains. Dirty-hands cases are usually imagined as secret, urgent, and personal — a leader alone with a terrible choice. This episode was public, deliberate, and institutional: a legislature tried to launder the decision in advance, and a court refused to let it. Does the framework even apply to pre-authorized, codified wrongs, or does codification destroy the very structure the theory describes?

4. An institutional variant I have not seen discussed. Imagine the statute had instead authorized the shootdown while requiring, by law, the minister's immediate resignation, a public confession of wrongdoing before parliament, and a formal act of national mourning for the passengers. Does the dirty-hands framework rank this design above the German court's outright prohibition, below it, or does it reveal that the framework has no stable preference between codified wrong with ritual acknowledgment and clean law with foreseeable catastrophe? Please commit to an answer.

5. What the episode shows about the theory. Is this a confirmation of the dirty-hands framework, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated? I am especially interested in whether the Court's refusal can itself be read as a dirty-hands act — the judges keeping the law's hands clean by pushing the dirt downward onto a hypothetical future official.
