I am a retired experimental physicist — four decades in condensed matter, comfortable with classical and statistical mechanics, passable on field theory — who has taken up philosophy in earnest only since retiring. I have been reading about al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: his claim, in the course of arguing against the philosophers of his day, that fire does not really cause cotton to burn, that God is the only genuine agent and produces the burning directly on the occasion of contact, and that what we call causal necessity in nature is nothing more than a divinely sustained habit of conjunction that could, in principle, be interrupted. I would like you to restate this position in the vocabulary I actually think in — initial and boundary conditions, dynamical laws, conserved quantities, symmetries, counterfactual dependence under interventions, and the ongoing debate about whether laws of nature are anything more than exceptionally reliable regularities — as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me a residue report. Tell me which parts of the doctrine translate cleanly (I suspect his denial of necessary connection has a near-twin in the regularity view of laws, but I may be flattering the resemblance), which parts survive only under distortion, and which parts have no expression at all in that vocabulary — I expect the divine agent and his habits to be candidates, but I want this argued, not assumed. Then give me your verdict on the residue: does it mark something the physical vocabulary is genuinely missing, or does it show that the doctrine was trading on notions that only appear substantive when left untranslated? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
