At my sister's birthday dinner last weekend I watched two of her oldest friends, Dan and Priya, get properly stuck into an argument about inheritance tax, and I've been turning it over ever since. Neither is an economist — Dan's a structural engineer, Priya teaches politics at a sixth-form college — but both argue well and neither would give an inch. Here's the exchange as best I remember it. (1) Dan opened with the double-taxation line: the money was taxed when it was earned, so taking another slice at death is just the state helping itself because the owner is no longer around to vote about it. Worse, he said, the tax punishes virtue — the frugal couple who skip holidays for twenty years to build something for their kids get clobbered, while the couple who spend everything on cruises pay nothing, so it's really a tax on savers. (2) Priya replied that nobody is taxed twice, because the person paying isn't the person who earned it: the dead pay nothing, and for the child an inheritance is simply income — unearned income, the purest windfall there is — so if anything it should be taxed more heavily than wages, not less. Her real target, she said, wasn't one generation looking after its kids, which she thinks is perfectly fine, but dynasties; and she sketched on a napkin a scheme of her own where the tax rate on a bequest depends on how long the wealth has been in the family — freshly earned money passing from parent to child at a low rate, money already inherited once at a middling rate, third-generation trust wealth at a steep one. Her reasoning: equal opportunity is offended by accumulated ancestral advantage, not by your mum helping you out, so the rate should climb with each hand the money passes through. (3) Dan came back with three objections. First, the scheme taxes intergenerational patience: a family that stewards its assets well gets taxed harder than one where each generation blows the lot, so it rewards dissipation. Second, avoidance: every wealthy family would just gift money decades early, skip a generation, or park it in trusts, so the scheme would collect little whilst creating full employment for accountants. Third, if the birth lottery is the target, money is the least of it — parents hand down genes, accents, postcodes, confidence and contacts, and nobody proposes levelling any of that.

(4) Priya said the avoidance point proves too much: every tax is avoidable at the margin and we don't conclude income tax is therefore unjust — evasion is an engineering problem, not a verdict on fairness. On the other inherited advantages, she said the fact that some luck can't be redistributed is no reason not to redistribute the sort that can; you might as well refuse to treat curable diseases on the grounds that incurable ones exist. On patience she half-conceded, but said stewardship could be recognised through reliefs for family firms and farms where the children actually work the asset, as opposed to trust funds nobody lifts a finger for. (5) Dan's last move was to say that this concession gives the game away: the moment you carve out exceptions for 'deserving' wealth, some committee of officials has to decide which families used their money well, and he wouldn't trust any committee with that judgement — better a simple flat rule, even a crude one, than a scheme whose rates depend on civil servants' opinions of your family. Then dessert arrived and it fizzled out unresolved.

I honestly can't tell who won, and it's been nagging at me all week. Could you adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, then say who is more right overall. And say what, if anything, both of them are missing — I've a nagging suspicion they never properly seperated the question of whether inheritance tax is fair to the giver from the question of whether it's good for the recipients, and that a lot of the argument was two different complaints talking past each other, but I may be wrong about that.
