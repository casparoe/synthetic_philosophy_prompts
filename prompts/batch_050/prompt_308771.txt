I've come to accept the view that epistemic akrasia is always irrational: one cannot rationally believe that p while simultaneously believing that one's total evidence does not support believing that p. The position strikes me as close to a requirement of coherence in what one takes one's own reasons to be -- believing p amounts to taking the world to be a certain way, and believing that your evidence doesn't support p amounts to taking your grounds for that belief to be inadequate, and holding both at once seems like indicting your own belief in the very act of holding it. I want to know what would count against this position, and I want the answer to be more disciplined than the usual skirmish in the disagreement literature.

Some structure for the response. First, the cases. Consider the following scenario, which I'll stipulate rather than borrow: a mathematician has just completed a proof, checked it line by line, and finds it sound. She then runs it through a verification service with a long, well-documented track record; the service reports an error somewhere in the proof but cannot say where. She rechecks honestly and still finds nothing. Suppose further that the service's historical error rate is known to her and is neither negligible nor overwhelming -- say it has been wrong about one flag in five. Can she rationally retain full belief in the theorem while believing that her total evidence, service report included, no longer supports it? If she can, is that a counterexample to my position, or only a case where 'total evidence' is doing equivocal work? I want this case and any others you think matter analyzed as potential defeaters -- including the famous family of cases in which an agent is told, by a source she trusts, that she is cognitively impaired right now (drugged, sleep-deprived, prone to a specific bias) while the first-order reasoning in front of her remains as transparent as it ever was.

Second, the diagnosis. For each candidate defeater, say whether it would count against the position on the position's own terms -- something a committed anti-akrasia theorist would concede as trouble -- or only on assumptions the position's defenders already reject. In particular: does the position stand or fall with a particular view about how higher-order evidence combines with first-order evidence, such that showing that combination rule to be wrong would bring the position down with it? Does it covertly assume that an agent can always compare her first-order reasoning against the higher-order verdict, and what happens to it in cases where she can't? And is there a reading on which the position is true but trivial -- irrational by definition of 'rational' -- and if so, does that render it unfalsifiable, and is unfalsifiability actually a defect for a normative thesis of this kind rather than an empirical one?

Third, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say what the strongest rival position would have to look like to absorb whatever survives.

Finally, please be careful about the state of the literature here. Where there is genuine, ongoing dispute among people who work on this -- as there plainly is about conciliationism, steadfastness, and how higher-order evidence should move credences -- present it as disputed, with the live options laid out, rather than reporting one side as the settled consensus. Where something really is broadly agreed, say that too. I'd rather have an honest map of what is unsettled than a confident verdict the field hasn't actually reached.
