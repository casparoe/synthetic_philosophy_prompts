Two colleagues of mine have been arguing about Kripke's rule-following paradox, and I've stayed out of it because I genuinely can't tell who's winning. It started when Maya defended a sophisticated dispositionalism: what I mean by '+' is fixed by what I would answer under idealized conditions -- unlimited time, no fatigue, no memory lapses. Ben replied that this just relocates the problem: any specification of "ideal conditions" that rules out systematic error must already presuppose which answers are correct, so the account smuggles in the very notion of meaning it was supposed to reduce. Maya answered that this circularity charge proves too much, since it equally defeats Kripke's own skeptical solution: saying I use '+' correctly when I conform to the community requires a standard for which community responses count, and picking that standard is no less circular. Ben said no -- the skeptical solution never needed correctness facts at all, only conditions under which assertions are licensed, so it escapes the dilemma dispositionalism faces. Maya's final move was that this concession is fatal to the solution: if there are only assertibility conditions and no facts about correctness, then there's no distinction between seeming right to me (or to us) and being right, and without that distinction we haven't explained meaning, we've eliminated it. Ben's last word was that wanting more than this is precisely the misunderstanding the paradox exposes.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Does it carry over to the communitarian reading of the skeptical solution?
