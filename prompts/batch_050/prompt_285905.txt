I'm running a graduate-student colloquium series this semester, and our next session is a Q&A with a philosopher whose new paper defends the felt-quality solution to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: her claim is that all pleasures, from the taste of ripe mango to the quiet satisfaction of finishing a proof, share a common phenomenal property, a distinctive hedonic tone that is literally felt and that is what makes each of them a pleasure. She's sharp, and she has heard the obvious objections before, so I need questions that do real work rather than let her run her standard responses. Please draft a sequence of six to eight interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I'd like the sequence to cover at least the following pressure points: first, the introspective challenge that the pleasures of eating, sex, intellectual achievement, and relief from pain simply don't seem to share any felt quality, and whether her appeal to a subtle common tone risks becoming unfalsifiable; second, the status of pleasures we don't seem to feel at all, such as the background contentment of a good day or pleasures had while asleep or distracted, and whether positing unfelt hedonic tone undermines the view's introspective motivation; third, why her account should be preferred to attitudinal rivals on which pleasures are unified not by how they feel but by the subject's desire or liking directed at them; and fourth, what she says about cases of pleasure taken in things the subject believes are bad, where a common tone seems to float free of the pleasure's object entirely. The final question should press her on what evidence, introspective or otherwise, could actually settle the dispute between her view and the attitudinal account. Present the questions and their follow-ups as continuous prose, the way you'd write them in an email to me before the session: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
