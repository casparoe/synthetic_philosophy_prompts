I serve on a regional emergency-preparedness committee that is revising our disaster-response manual, and I keep getting stuck on whether the document's rules for choosing whom to save hang together philosophically. Three clauses matter here. The first says that when a rescue team can reach only one of two sites before conditions deteriorate, it must go to the site where more people are trapped; lives are to be counted, and the greater number wins. The second, added a few years ago after public complaints about rigid arithmetic, says that when the difference in expected lives saved between two options is small — the manual says two or fewer — the team leader may instead flip a coin, on the stated ground that everyone in peril deserves a real chance of rescue. The third clause governs a different situation entirely, a hospital allocating a single ventilator among eligible patients, and it forbids counting altogether: every eligible patient gets one ticket in a lottery, regardless of how many other patients are competing or what would happen elsewhere in the ward. A philosopher friend tells me this whole territory is contested — that there is a serious view, associated with John Taurek, on which saving five strangers instead of one is not a better outcome, since there is no one for whom the larger loss is suffered, and that choosing by lot is not a second-best fudge but a way of respecting each person. I am not sure how far that thought reaches or whether our manual is secretly committed to it in some places and to its denial in others.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the manual's implicit moral theory. Take each clause in turn: what view of whether, and how, numbers count does it presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Then assess the document as a whole. Is there any single coherent position under which counting fully in clause one, flipping coins for small differences in clause two, and refusing to count at all in clause three are all licensed — perhaps a view on which the numbers matter only once the stakes pass a threshold, or on which institutional role changes what fairness requires? Or is the manual simply stitched together from incompatible intuitions? I am especially interested in whether clause two is a defensible compromise or an unstable midpoint that gets the worst of both views, and in what the coin flip is really doing: is it distributing chances as a kind of good in their own right, and does that sit coherently with the outcome-focused reasoning of clause one?

Finally, tell me what consistency would cost in each direction. If we rewrote the manual on a thoroughly Taurekian basis, where every conflict is settled by giving each person an equal or proportional chance rather than by counting, what concretely changes and what stays the same? If we rewrote it on a thoroughly aggregative basis, does clause three's lottery survive at all, or only as a tie-breaker? And where is the manual philosophically underdetermined — compatible with several theories, so that we need a policy argument rather than a conceptual one to settle it? I am not after references or a survey of the literature; I want the implicit theory excavated and the two consistent rewrites sketched, so the committee can see what it is actually choosing between.
