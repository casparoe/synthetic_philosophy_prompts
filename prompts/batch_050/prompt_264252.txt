The claim that evolution selects for fitness rather than truth gets repeated constantly in popular philosophy of biology and in metaethics, usually as if it were a single settled result. In a recent argument I had, someone used it to dismiss my trust in moral intuitions about suffering, treating it as obvious that beliefs shaped by selection have no reliable connection to how things morally are. Unpack the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be expressing: a bare claim about what selection pressures respond to; a claim about the reliability of perceptual or cognitive faculties generally; a claim specifically about evaluative beliefs, where the link between usefulness and correctness is allegedly absent; and a global skeptical claim that would undermine the evolutionary argument itself. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical or philosophical thesis, or straightforwardly false; note where the fitness-versus-truth contrast actually has traction and where the two coincide; and identify which reading the slogan needs to be given to support debunking moral intuitions without also debunking the scientific reasoning the debunker relied on to get there.
