I am a PhD student in political theory preparing for a field examination on theories of rights, and I want a decision tree that will serve both as a map of the literature and as an instrument for sorting my own commitments. The tree's organizing problem should come from the following passage in Jeremy Bentham's Anarchical Fallacies, his examination of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, composed in the 1790s but not published in English in his lifetime:

"To know the several laws which the exigencies of mankind call for, a view of all these several exigencies must be obtained. But to obtain this view, there is but one possible means, which is, to take a view of the laws that have already been framed, and of the exigencies which have given birth to them."

Bentham's target in this section is the idea that a declaration of fundamental rights can be framed in advance of a complete body of law: he appears to be arguing that the content of any purported right can only be fixed by working through the detailed rules a society actually needs, so that rights talk floats free unless it is anchored in positive law. I say "appears" because I am genuinely unsure how to read the passage, and part of what I want from the tree is a sharper grip on it.

The deliverable is a decision tree with six to nine branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Each branch node should be phrased as a question a respondent could answer from a concrete case without knowing which position the answer commits them to, and each leaf should be a named position on what gives a claim the status of a right. The required leaves are: Benthamite legal positivism (rights as creatures of actual law), Lockean natural-rights theory, Dworkin's view of rights as constraints on the pursuit of collective goals, Hohfeldian analytic deflationism, the will theory of rights associated with Hart, the interest theory associated with Raz, and the Marxian critique of rights as the rights of egoistic man. If one of these does not fit naturally at a leaf, explain why rather than forcing it.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first identify the questions on which theories of rights genuinely come apart (for instance, whether rights can exist where no legal or social rule recognizes them; whether a right's content can be specified independently of the duties correlating with it; whether an unmet need can by itself ground a right), then work out which combinations of answers are possible and which are occupied. The passage should function as the root node or the first cut: whether a right can have determinate content antecedent to the detailed positive rules of a legal order. For each leaf, state in one or two sentences how that position would read the passage — as a sound constitutive claim about what rights are, as a merely epistemic point about how we could come to know them, or as a confusion to be rebutted — and where the position instead locates the anchor Bentham says rights talk needs.

Flag two kinds of trouble explicitly: pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate with any diagnostic question (my suspicion is that the interest theory and at least one natural-rights view will coincide on every answer — confirm or disconfirm this), and unoccupied combinations of answers, with a brief diagnosis of whether each empty region is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Conclude with a short note, not part of the tree, on the strongest objection to my reading: whether Bentham's argument, if sound, threatens his own utilitarian standard as much as it threatens natural rights, given that the principle of utility is not itself a creature of positive law.

The quoted passage does not count toward the length; the tree plus commentary should come to roughly 1,200–1,500 words.
