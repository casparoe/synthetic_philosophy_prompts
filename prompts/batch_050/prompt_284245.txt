I'm TAing an intro political theory class this semester and we just finished the unit on war, where we read some Walzer and then a chunk of McMahan pushing back on him. The students got the debate fine, but what I keep getting stuck on is that the actual laws of war seem to have already picked a side, and I want to pin down which side and whether it can even afford to. Here's the practice as I understand it: a soldier who fights in a blatantly unjust war of aggression -- say his country invades a neighbor to seize territory -- has committed no crime under international law, as long as he fights clean (targets only combatants, wears a uniform, doesn't torture prisoners, and so on). When captured he gets POW status and cannot be prosecuted for the enemy soldiers he killed. His political leaders, meanwhile, can be tried for the crime of aggression, so *somebody* is on the hook for the war being wrong, just never the people who actually did the killing. And a civilian who, on his own initiative and out of uniform, shoots those same invading soldiers can be prosecuted as a murderer rather than treated as a combatant. On top of all that, the rules are perfectly symmetric: a soldier fighting a just war of self-defense enjoys no more legal protection or permission than the soldier invading his country.

So: what theory of individual moral responsibility does this whole arrangement presuppose? Work it out concretely. Does the practice assume soldiers can't reasonably be expected to judge the justice of their own cause? That the decision to wage war belongs to the state in a way that absorbs the subordinate's responsibility? That killing in war is just a different moral category from killing in civilian life? Also tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out -- I'm thinking especially of the seemingly obvious idea that you're responsible for what you deliberately and knowingly do, regardless of who told you to do it. And is the package internally consistent? We hold the leaders criminally responsible for choosing the war while immunizing everyone who carried out the choice, and that pairing strikes me as unstable, but maybe there's a way to defend it I haven't seen.

Then do the counterfactual part. Suppose we rebuilt the laws of armed conflict on the revisionist picture, where unjust combatants are morally liable to be killed and just combatants do nothing wrong by killing them, so the legal symmetry between the two sides tracks no real moral symmetry. What would concretely have to change -- prosecutions of ordinary soldiers after wars? Asymmetric privileges depending on which cause you serve? Something else? Finally, be honest about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a pure consequentialist, who thinks the rules exist just to limit the brutality of war and make peace possible afterward, endorse the exact same symmetric package without committing to any thesis about what individual soldiers deserve? If so, spell out what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely happens to fit well with.
