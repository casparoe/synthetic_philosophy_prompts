So at a dinner last week two friends of mine got into a huge argument about the so-called meta-problem of consciousness (the problem of explaining why we *think* there's a hard problem of consciousness in the first place) and I got asked to referee, which I regret agreeing to. Here's roughly how it went:

Maya's view: the meta-problem is actually the only problem there is. Her line was that the "hard problem" is nothing over and above our judgements, intuitions, and reports that consciousness seems mysterious — so once we've explained, in cognitive/functional terms, why creatures like us produce those judgements and find the zombie idea conceivable and so on, there's nothing left to explain. Explain the seeming, and the seeming was the whole thing. She said anyone who thinks otherwise is just refusing to accept that their intuitions could have a mundane psychological explanation.

Daniel's view: that's a bait-and-switch. Explaining why people *believe* there's a hard problem doesn't explain consciousness itself, any more than explaining why people believe in God settles whether God exists. Even a perfect psychology of our mystery-intuitions leaves the actual explanandum — phenomenal experience — untouched.

Maya said the God analogy fails, because consciousness is essentially a matter of appearance: if it seems like there's experience, there is. God existing isn't like that. So in this one special case, explaining the seeming really does explain the thing.

Daniel said that move is question-begging: talk of "seeming" or "appearance" already smuggles in the very phenomenal states realists are asking about — a seeming has to seem *to someone*, in some way. Maya answered that seeming can be cashed out purely functionally, as internal states that dispose the system to report and reason certain ways, no phenomenal anything required. Daniel's last move was: fine, but then what you've explained is just dispositions to report, and the realist's question was never about reports in the first place, so you've changed the subject rather than dissolved anything.

They've both texted me since asking who's right. Point by point — who's right about what? Does the God analogy actually fail the way Maya says? Does "seeming" really smuggle in phenomenality, or is Daniel just asserting the functional story can't work? And who is more right *overall*? Also curious what you think both of them are missing.
