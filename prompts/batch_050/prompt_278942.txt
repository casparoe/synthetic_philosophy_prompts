In my seminar on contractualist ethics we ran into something I cannot locate in the literature, and I want to know whether it already has a name. Consider the standard person-by-person method: compare the strongest individual complaint under each option and choose the option whose strongest complaint is weakest. With only two options this is well-behaved. But with three, my students produced cases like this: option one leaves A with thirty years of chronic moderate pain, option two leaves B with five years of severe depressive illness, option three leaves C with the loss of a limb and of their vocation. Pairwise, the class judged chronic pain the worse complaint against depression, depression the worse complaint against limb loss, and limb loss the worse complaint against chronic pain. So whichever option you protect first, a third option beats it, and the verdict of the whole procedure depends on the order in which the comparisons are run. This is not the familiar problem of whether smaller complaints may be aggregated against a larger one -- numbers play no role here -- and it is not ordinary intransitivity of overall betterness, since the objector can concede a transitive ranking of outcomes while denying that pairwise complaint comparisons inherit it. Is there an established concept or literature for order-dependent or cyclical complaint comparison of this sort? If so, identify it and distinguish it from the aggregation problem and from Temkin-style intransitivity. If not, coin a term, define it precisely, and give its boundary conditions: under what assumptions about the commensurability of complaints does the phenomenon arise, and does it threaten the contractualist method itself or only particular ways of operationalizing it?
