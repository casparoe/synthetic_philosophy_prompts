Okay so I'm a philosophy PhD student and I'm TAing an upper-level undergrad seminar on agency and decision theory this semester, ~16 students, and I get to run one 80-minute session myself. I want to use it on the "deliberation crowds out prediction" idea -- roughly the Levi-style claim that you can't treat your own still-open choice as an object of prediction the way you can treat everyone else's, because while you're genuinely deliberating, the outcome isn't a fact about the world you can estimate, it's the thing you're in the middle of producing. The students read the stuff and nod along and clearly do not feel it at all. It reads like a verbal puzzle to them.

So: design me an activity or game that makes them feel the asymmetry before anyone names it. My rough thought is something where they spend a while predicting each other's choices for small real stakes (I'm happy to bring chocolate or do points-for-prize, and a proper scoring rule setup is fine, they can handle the math), so that predicting other people feels natural and easy -- and then the structure forces them to assign probabilities to their own upcoming move while it's still live, and the whole thing falls apart or gets weird in instructive ways. Like, I want at least one student to announce "70% I'll do X" and then either feel weirdly bound by their own forecast, or do the 30% thing out of spite, and I want the room to notice that both reactions are strange in a way that predicting each other never was.

What I need from you: concrete rules with actual payoffs and incentives (not vibes), any roles, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and instructions for what the facilitator does at each stage. Crucially, tell me how to handle the failure modes I can already see coming: the student who says "I'm 100% doing X" and makes their self-prediction trivially true by just deciding early; the student who flips a coin to make their probabilities accurate (which is clever but sidesteps deliberation entirely, so I need to know whether to ban randomization or let it happen and use it in the debrief); and the student who insists there's no problem at all because they predicted themself fine. Also: should self-predictions be private or announced? I suspect announced is better because then the prediction becomes a move in the game and changes everyone else's incentives, which is itself the phenomenon, but you tell me.

Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the idea -- including the distinction between predicting your own settled habits (fine) and forecasting a choice you're still making (the weird case), and the point that your own self-prediction, once made, becomes just another consideration you deliberate about, which is maybe the deepest version of the problem. And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce: my worry is it only ever demonstrates instability (self-predictions keep getting upset by the deliberation they feed into), not the stronger claim that self-prediction of a live choice is somehow impossible or incoherent, and a smart student will absolutely press on that gap. Tell me how to handle that student.
