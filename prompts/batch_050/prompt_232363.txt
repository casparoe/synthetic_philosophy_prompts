I help run a community land trust, and every year we have to divide one pool of money among tenant projects — repairs, shared gardens, accessibility work, security upgrades, that kind of thing. There’s no market clearing price and we don’t want to just split by headcount or by whoever shouts loudest. Someone suggested using the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, but I need it turned into something my staff can actually operate, not a description of the theorem.

Assume the basic version: identify each project group’s disagreement outcome if no grant is awarded, identify the best outcome that group could feasibly get from the available pool, and then look for the largest common fraction of everyone’s possible gain that can be delivered simultaneously. Please turn that into a written procedure we could reuse every funding cycle: what information to collect, in what order; how to make different groups’ self-reported “outcomes” comparable enough to calculate with; what to do when a group has no meaningful disagreement point, gives an impossible ideal point, or can benefit from another group’s project; and when staff should stop calculating and send the case to the board because the problem is really about values or rights rather than arithmetic.

The procedure needs checks against the obvious gaming strategies. For example, a group might exaggerate how bad things will be without funding, claim an absurdly large maximum benefit, break one project into several applicants, or make itself look worse off by refusing an available fallback. For each manipulation, say what evidence we should ask for, what correction is legitimate, and what correction would quietly smuggle in our own preferences. Also explain which properties of Kalai–Smorodinsky survive once we replace abstract utility with survey scores, cost estimates, and staff judgment — and which assumptions we should write on the form in plain English so applicants know what is being done to them.

Please make the whole thing usable in a two-hour meeting by people who can do arithmetic and weighted averages but are not game theorists. If a full calculation is impossible, give a defensible fallback rather than just saying “elicitate utilities better.” I’d also like a short audit trail template: the numbers recorded, the judgment calls recorded, and the point at which the chair has to sign off.

Then run the procedure on three fictional cases: (1) roof repairs benefiting a large number of residents versus an elevator benefiting a much smaller disabled group; (2) two competing garden proposals where one group submits a suspiciously catastrophic no-funding outcome; and (3) a security-camera proposal where some residents count the cameras as a benefit and others count them as a harm even if their reported safety improves. At least one application should produce an allocation our board probably won’t like, and you should explain whether that means the procedure failed or means the board needs to override it openly. End with what this procedure cannot decide, even in principle.
