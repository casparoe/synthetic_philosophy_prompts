I'm preparing a magazine essay on how historians frame their big explanatory questions, and I keep running into two famous puzzles that are usually discussed separately: the Needham question — why did modern science not arise indigenously in China, despite centuries of technological sophistication — and the Great Divergence question — why did northwest Europe, rather than the Yangtze Delta or another advanced region, industrialize first. Please compare the two as contrastive questions. For each, ask whether its contrast is well posed: is the foil well chosen, is the explanandum a real event or a retrospective construction, and does the question presuppose a theory of historical development (stages, inevitable trajectories, a European "norm") that smuggles in the answer? Then the harder part: my working hypothesis is that one of these questions survives scrutiny much better than the other — that they differ not just in subject matter but in kind, because one asks why something failed to happen while the other asks why something happened where it did. Tell me whether that asymmetry (explaining an absence versus explaining an occurrence) actually does philosophical work here, drawing on what makes contrastive why-questions legitimate or defective in general, and which of the two questions a responsible historian should keep asking — and in what reformulated shape.
