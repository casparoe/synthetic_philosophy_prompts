I am drafting the concluding chapter of a monograph in which I state my considered positions on population ethics and the far future, and before it goes to press I want the set audited for internal consistency rather than praised. Here is what I currently hold:

1. Axiology: total hedonic utilitarianism. The value of a world history is the sum of welfare across everyone who ever lives in it, with no pure time preference: a unit of welfare counts the same whether it occurs this year or in ten million years.

2. The procreation asymmetry: we have weighty moral reason not to bring into existence lives of net suffering, but no reason of the same kind to bring into existence lives of net happiness. A happy person who is never created is no loss; a miserable person who is created is one.

3. Strong longtermism: for the most consequential decisions we currently face, the effects on the very far future are the primary determinant of what we ought to do, swamping considerations confined to the next few generations.

4. The badness of extinction: human extinction would be an astronomical catastrophe, and the chief reason is the foregone future — the vast number of worthwhile lives that would never be lived — rather than the deaths of those alive at the time, terrible as those would be. This, and not the welfare of the present generation, is my main ground for treating extinction-risk reduction as the overriding moral priority.

5. Decision theory: strict expected-value maximization under risk. Where probabilities are known or well estimated, the right act is the one with the highest expected value, with no adjustment for the shape of the distribution.

6. Anti-fanaticism: rationality permits declining arbitrarily long-shot wagers on arbitrarily large stakes. The correct response to a Pascalian mugger is refusal, and I would regard any normative theory that counsels paying as thereby refuted.

7. Practice: in cost-benefit contexts I am comfortable applying a modest positive discount rate to future benefits, and I take this to be fully compatible with commitment (1).

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which pairs are in merely apparent tension that dissolves on inspection? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, state what would have to be given up on either horn and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the chapter. Where a tension propagates — where fixing one pair shifts pressure onto another commitment — trace that too. If any tension turns on an ambiguity in my formulations, say so and run the analysis both ways. Finally, identify the single revision that would restore the most coherence at the lowest price, and note any place where a repair that looks cheap in one part of the list quietly undermines a premise I rely on in another.
