I'm a graduate student in mathematics who has recently been reading about intuitionism and constructive mathematics, and it's made me notice something odd about the everyday norms of my own field, which I'd like you to help me think through. Consider three things that are all true of mainstream research mathematics. First, a proof that a mathematical object exists is considered complete and publishable even when it exhibits no such object: proofs by contradiction, counting arguments, and the probabilistic method (where one shows that a randomly chosen combinatorial object has the desired property with positive probability, without producing any specific instance) are fully accepted as settling existence. Second, there is nonetheless a persistent informal norm that constructive proofs are *better*: a nonconstructive existence theorem is routinely followed by work that explicitly constructs the object, and that later work is treated as a genuine advance, not as redundant. Third, in adjacent areas of computer science, an existence proof without an accompanying algorithm or explicit bound is often described in referee reports and talks as somehow incomplete -- as if the real theorem hadn't been proved yet.

What philosophical commitments does this bundle of practices presuppose? On the one hand, accepting nonconstructive existence proofs seems to presuppose something like realism about mathematical objects: that "there exists an x such that..." is a matter of mathematical fact, independent of whether anyone can exhibit or compute such an x, which is exactly what intuitionists like Brouwer and constructivists like Bishop denied when they read existence as "we can construct." On the other hand, the second and third norms seem to presuppose that construction confers something a bare existence proof lacks -- but what? If existence is already a settled fact, what is left for the constructive proof to add, and is "added knowledge" a coherent answer if the constructive proof establishes a strictly weaker-looking claim? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mathematics behind these norms, and in particular to distinguish what they strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with: could a thoroughgoing Platonist justify the preference for constructive proofs (say, on purely epistemic or practical grounds), and could a constructivist accept classical proofs as legitimate within a restricted reading, so that the practices might be philosophically underdetermined? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in mathematical practice -- in refereeing, in what counts as a solved problem, in the status of results like nonconstructive existence theorems -- if the community explicitly adopted either a strict constructivism or an unapologetic classical realism, and whether the current compromise position is actually coherent or just an unexamined truce.
