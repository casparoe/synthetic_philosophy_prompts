Write a poem in seven stanzas that enacts the Jain doctrine of conditional predication, syādvāda — the logical expression of anekāntavāda, the view that reality is many-sided and that every claim about a thing holds only from some standpoint. Under this doctrine, every predication is implicitly prefixed by syāt ("from a certain standpoint," "in some respect"), and the complete account of any thing unfolds in exactly seven modes: (1) from some standpoint, it is; (2) from some standpoint, it is not; (3) from some standpoint, it is and it is not — affirmed successively, each from its own aspect; (4) from some standpoint, it is inexpressible — because being and non-being affirmed simultaneously, of the same aspect, defeats speech; (5) it is, and it is inexpressible; (6) it is not, and it is inexpressible; (7) it is, it is not, and it is inexpressible.

The poem should observe the following constraints:

- Take as its subject a single ordinary object, held fixed across all seven stanzas. Do not retell the parable of the blind men and the elephant — it is too familiar, and the poem should make the doctrine work harder than that.
- Let the stanzas correspond, in order, to the seven modes. For the first two or three stanzas, ground the standpoints in the classical parameters of substance, place, time, and condition: a thing is, relative to its own material, location, moment, and state; it is not, relative to the material, location, moment, and state of what it is not. The standpoint should never disappear from view — no stanza may assert anything outright.
- Make the form do the philosophical work in stanzas four through seven. The successive affirmation of the third stanza should be grammatically comfortable; the simultaneous affirmation of the fourth should be formally strained — a sentence that revises itself mid-line, a claim whose syntax will not settle, an utterance that says and unsays within the same breath — so that a reader can feel why simultaneity, unlike succession, escapes language. Stanzas five through seven should carry that strain forward in combination rather than relieving it.
- The seventh stanza must hold being, non-being, and inexpressibility together without resolving them into a higher synthesis: the doctrine does not treat the seventh mode as a reconciliation, only as the honest joint report of the standpoints.

Write in free verse of roughly four to eight lines per stanza, in American English, with some loose recurring device — a repeated opening gesture, a returning image — to mark that each stanza concerns the same object. The test of success is double: a reader who knows syādvāda should be able to say, without being told, which stanza enacts which mode and why the fourth had to break; a reader who does not should still find it a good poem about one thing seen seven ways.
