You are a political philosopher and environmental ethicist with long experience advising technology-governance bodies on climate policy. I want you to approach what follows carefully and systematically. Before answering, spend some time brainstorming candidate explanations, including ones you expect to discard later, and only then work through the analysis rule by rule. I am not looking for citations or a literature survey; reason entirely from the material I give you.

Some context about me: I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. For the past five years I have served as a technical advisor to a (fictional, but realistic) national Climate Intervention Review Board that rules on proposed geoengineering projects. I now have to predict how the board will rule on two upcoming proposals, and I think about this the way I would think about reverse-engineering a classifier from labeled examples -- except the labels are moral verdicts and the features are things like reversibility and legitimacy. I need your help inferring the decision boundary.

Quick definitions, in case any are unfamiliar. Solar radiation management means reflecting a small fraction of sunlight to cool the planet without removing any carbon dioxide; carbon dioxide removal means pulling CO2 out of the air. Termination shock is the problem that if a solar-management system is masking warming and is suddenly switched off, all the masked warming arrives within a few years -- essentially a rollback-plan question. Moral hazard is the worry that the mere availability of a techno-fix reduces the pressure to cut emissions -- a principal-agent problem. And there is the question of who has commit access to the shared atmosphere when an intervention's effects cross borders.

Here are the board's five past rulings:

1. Stratospheric aerosol injection, full global deployment by a coalition of willing governments, modeled to offset about 1°C of warming, with effects decaying within two years of stopping. Rejected.
2. Commercial ocean iron fertilization in international waters by a private firm selling carbon offsets, with poorly characterized effects on marine food webs. Rejected.
3. A small cirrus-cloud-thinning field trial by one country over its own territory, with effects lasting days to weeks. Approved.
4. A small marine-cloud-brightening field trial in one country's coastal waters, reversible within days. Approved.
5. A large direct-air-capture build-out. Approved.

The two proposals now before the board:

A. Operational (not experimental) marine cloud brightening over one country's territorial sea to shield a coral reef during marine heatwaves, run by that country's government. Effects are well-characterized at this scale and reversible within days of stopping; there is no meaningful termination shock. Instruments would detect a roughly 1-2% change in rainfall downwind in a neighboring country. The government is behind on its emissions targets, and senior officials have publicly described the program as buying time so the country does not have to rush its fossil-fuel phase-out.

B. A permanently maintained stratospheric aerosol capability sized to offset about 1°C of warming, owned not by any state but by a new treaty body with near-universal membership, deployable only by a two-thirds vote of members, and otherwise kept idle. Its physical risk profile is essentially that of case 1, termination shock included; only the governance structure differs.

Here is what I want from you:

First, state at least three distinct decision rules, each crisply formulated, that are each consistent with all five past verdicts. I am fairly sure the track record underdetermines the rule, and I want the underdetermination made explicit rather than smoothed over.

Second, for each candidate rule, give its verdict on proposals A and B, with the reasoning. Note where a rule's verdict depends on how strictly some clause is interpreted.

Third, tell me which rule you think best captures what this board is actually doing, and why. Identify the closest single precedent for each new proposal, give me your predicted verdicts with rough confidence levels, and flag where the honest answer is that the record genuinely cannot settle it.

Fourth, design the one hypothetical future case whose ruling would most efficiently discriminate among the rules you still take to be live -- the maximally informative test case, in my idiom.

Finally, a recommendation: if I were advising the reef agency behind proposal A, should I tell them the precedent-consistent outcome is approval or rejection? Whatever the answer, state plainly what general principle the board would be ratifying by ruling that way, and what it would be committing itself to in future cases it may not have foreseen.
