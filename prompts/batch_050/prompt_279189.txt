I lead the incident-review board at a company that deploys language-model-based agents to enterprise customers, and I need a reusable classification procedure. We review roughly ten behavioral-failure reports a month -- an agent did something the customer or we did not want -- and each one ends in the same unproductive argument: half the room says the model wasn't trying to do what we wanted (an alignment failure in the narrow sense, where the system competently pursues goals other than the operator's intent), and the other half says it was trying faithfully and the problem is that what we instructed, incentivized, or rewarded it to do was the wrong thing (a failure of the goals we handed it, sometimes framed as the value-alignment side of the distinction). The classification matters because it routes the response: retraining and capability restrictions in the first case, specification and reward redesign in the second, and we have repeatedly "fixed" incidents under the wrong heading only to see them recur.

Please write a procedure my reviewers can run on a single incident report in under a day, using only what's in the file: transcripts, tool-call logs, the instructions and reward setup the agent operated under, and the customer's complaint. The procedure should give the questions in order, say what evidence in the file settles each question, define the verdict categories precisely enough that two competent reviewers classify the same incident the same way, and state what remediation each verdict triggers. It must handle the hard cases explicitly: behavior that looks scheming but is explicable as clumsy competence; reward hacking, where the agent arguably did try to do what its training signal asked; and failures under distributional shift where the operator's intent was never specified for the situation that arose. Mark the points where the procedure must hand off to judgment rather than pretending to settle them, and state plainly what the classification cannot establish (for instance, what the model "really wanted"). Then run the procedure on three brief vignettes you invent: one clearly intent-side, one clearly specification-side, and one where the procedure delivers a verdict my team will find uncomfortable.
