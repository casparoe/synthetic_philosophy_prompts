I'm applying for a one-semester graduate research grant (about fifteen weeks, deliverable is a single 8,000-word paper) and need a proposal of roughly 800 words. The domain is the metaphysics of dispositions, but with an angle I haven't seen worked out, so the proposal has to sell it.

The half-formed idea. The literature on finks and masks treats the interfering factor as external to the object: a device attached to the wire that would cut the current the instant it is touched, protective wrapping that keeps the glass from breaking even when struck. The standard repairs to the conditional analysis -- for instance, tying the disposition to the object's retention of its causal basis through the stimulus -- are built for that picture. My hunch is that the interesting cases are systems that fink or mask themselves. A fuse works by destroying its own causal basis at the very moment of manifestation. An engine governor removes the engine's disposition to over-rev as soon as over-revving begins; the finkish mechanism is the entire point of the design, not an exotic accident. And most uncomfortably: any system that can detect that it is being tested, and alter its behavior or its internal basis in response, is a self-masking system. Call these endogenous finks and masks. My question: do endogenous cases break the standard accounts in a way exogenous ones do not, and what follows for a very mundane inference -- from how a thing behaves under test conditions to what it would do in deployment -- that every crash test, stress test, materials certification, and behavioral evaluation depends on?

Turn this into a proposal with the following parts:

1. A precise statement of the question, so framed that it has a determinate answer: whether the standard machinery for handling finks and masks survives once the interfering factor is the object's own response to the stimulus conditions, and if not, what minimal revision handles the endogenous cases without collapsing the analysis.
2. Why existing positions leave it open: why treating finks as attached devices makes the self-finking cases either invisible or trivially handled, and why that is too quick.
3. A working hypothesis I commit to. I do not want a proposal that surveys the options and lets the committee choose; I want one that bets. Pick the most defensible specific thesis about endogenous finks (for instance, that they collapse the distinction between losing a disposition and never having manifested it, so the basis-retention clause must be replaced with something counterfactual about unmonitored conditions -- or a different thesis if you think that one is wrong), state it as the project's thesis, give the strongest reason to believe it, and name the strongest objection it must survive.
4. The method, concretely: which cases get analyzed in which order, which two or three standard positions serve as foils (refer to them by description, e.g. the reformed conditional analysis or the appeal to intrinsic causal bases; I will add citations myself), and a week-by-week sequence for the semester.
5. Success and failure conditions: what the paper concludes if the hypothesis holds, what it concludes if the strongest objection wins instead, and what would count as the project collapsing entirely -- for instance, if the endogenous/exogenous distinction itself turns out to be unstable.
6. An honest scope assessment: what must be bracketed (dispositional essentialism, the metaphysics of causal bases, fragility-style counterexamples to the very notion of a disposition) for this to be finishable in fifteen weeks, and which single sub-question I should cut first if the project proves too big.

Wherever the proposal has to choose between framings, choose one and defend the choice; do not present the options and defer.
