Next month I'm giving a talk in our department's ethics colloquium defending a paper with a deliberately annoying thesis: the supererogatory isn't a real moral category, it's an IOU a theory writes when it can't afford its own conclusions. Roughly my argument: if your theory can identify which acts are good and explain why they're good, but then labels some of them "optional," it's either smuggling in a second unacknowledged standard of obligation, or admitting it never actually derived its obligations in the first place. A theory that genuinely knew what morality required wouldn't need the escape hatch — demandingness worries should be answered by fixing the obligations, not by fencing off a zone of heroic optional goodness.

The audience: a Kant scholar who works on imperfect duties and "latitude," a committed consequentialist with EA sympathies who I think agrees with my conclusion for the opposite reasons and will hate my argument anyway, and some grad students who mostly know supererogation through the demandingness literature. Q&A is twenty minutes.

Prepare me. I want the objections roughly in the order they'll come, who they'll come from, phrased the way each person would actually put it (the Kantian will talk wide duties and imperfect obligation, the consequentialist something about scalar vs binary morality, etc). For each: how damaging is it, what should I concede up front, what do I hold, and a reply I can get out in under a minuet. Also tell me where my argument overreaches. And give me the question I'm praying nobody asks — I suspect it's something like "doesn't this prove too much? by your logic the merely permissible is also a patch, so you've argued yourself into maximalism where everything is obligatory or forbidden" — but if there's a worse one I'm not seeing I want to know now.
