I've been chewing on this for months and keep going in circles, so I'd love some help. Background: I grew up in a country I won't name where "connections" are just how life works, and now I live in northern Europe where people act like corruption is this clear bright line. I don't buy it. Not an academic btw, I just read alot.

Recently I stumbled on this old Indian text, the Arthashastra by Kautilya, some advisor to a king from a couple centuries BC, in an old public domain translation on Wikisource. I don't have it infront of me so I'm paraphrasing, but there's a passage where he says roughly: a government official taking a little of the king's revenue is as unavoidable as tasting honey that's already sitting on the tip of your tongue, and figuring out whether officials are skimming is like trying to tell whether a fish under water is drinking or not. So his picture seems to be: everybody skims, you can't stop it, only manage it. The book then goes on to list like forty different tricks officials use to embezzle.

Anyway, my problem. I have all these gut verdicts about what counts as corruption and they don't add up:

1. My uncle back home is a district official and got my cousin a clerk job without ever advertising it. Totally standard practice. My verdict: corrupt, even though I love them both.
2. At weddings back home you hand cash in an envelope to the important guests, including officials. When my neighbor gave the mayor an envelope at his son's wedding: fine, that's just respect. When a building contractor gave the same mayor an envelope the week before a bid: corrupt. Same cash, same man, same envelope. My verdicts differ and I can't fully say why.
3. Where I live now, companies legally donate to politicians' campaigns, fully disclosed, then get meetings and sometimes contracts. Legal, transparent, and it stinks. My verdict: corrupt anyway, maybe worse for being laundered.
4. I once slipped a hospital administrator an envelope so my grandmother would get a decent room faster. I still feel gross but I'd do it again, the system left me no choice. Verdict: corrupt of me but forgivable? Or maybe not corruption at all, just survival.
5. A shopkeeper I know gives the local tax inspector a bottle of whiskey every Christmas, small value, going on twenty years. My verdict: harmless, not corruption.
6. My dad hired me at his hardware store over other applicants. Nobody, including me, calls that corrupt. But my uncle doing the parallel thing in a district office (case 1) is. I'm confident in both verdicts.

So: what principle, if any, captures this? Things I've toyed with: corruption = breaking a rule (but case 3 breaks no rule and feels corrupt, case 4 breaks one and feels forgivable). Corruption = abuse of public power for private gain (but then why does the contractor's envelope differ from the neighbor's — the mayor's gain is identical). Corruption = betraying a trust (but the whiskey bottle doesn't feel like betrayal, and maybe it is one?). Corruption = whatever wrecks equal treatment (but then case 4 is as bad as case 1 and my gut refuses that).

What I want: a precise principle, or two candidates if one won't do, saying which cases motivate each clause. Be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I suspect at least one has to go, I just can't see which. And give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: tipping a maître d' for a better table, a country where paying officials is the only way anything functions at all, a politician who hires a brilliant loyal aide over a slightly more qualified stranger.

Then the Kautilya bit, which is what's really bugging me. Does his everyone-skims-like-fish-in-water fatalism excuse anyone — my uncle, me at the hospital? His picture also seems entirely about officials dipping into revenue, which wouldn't even cover case 3 where arguably nobody pockets anything. If your principle and Kautilya's disagree about which cases are the core of corruption, who do you think is closer to right? And if the right principle ends up making half of daily life back home corrupt, is the principle just Western standards dressed up as universal, or do I bite that bullet?
