In the seminar I teach on science communication, the standard move whenever we reach cultural evolution is the replicator analogy: an idea is to a mind what a gene is to an organism, so culture evolves by differential copying of memes just as biology evolves by differential copying of genes. In the textbook we use, this analogy is then put to argumentative work in two directions. First, it is taken to show that cultural change is fundamentally blind and therefore largely unsteerable: since selection operates on replicators and not on the good of their vehicles, societies can no more rationally plan their own cultures than organisms can will their own adaptations, and reformers who think otherwise are compared to breeders who haven't heard of natural selection. Second, it is taken to justify a certain cynicism about belief: a belief that spreads widely does so because it is good at spreading, not because it is true or good for its hosts, so popular ideas should be treated the way epidemiologists treat a successful pathogen, with suspicion rather than deference.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply endorse or reject it. Work through the correspondence explicitly: identify what plays the role of the replicator, the vehicle, reproduction, copying fidelity, mutation, drift, selection pressure, and fitness, and note which of these has a clean cultural counterpart and which has to be forced. Then examine the disanalogies that seem to me most threatening: cultural transmission is horizontal as well as vertical and often involves deliberate reconstruction rather than blind copying; there is nothing obviously corresponding to the genotype-phenotype distinction, since the idea in my head and the idea I express seem inseparable; and cultural units appear to lack the discrete, countable identity that makes population genetics a quantitative science rather than a metaphor. For each of these, say whether it undermines the analogy as such or merely complicates it. Finally, assess whether the two conclusions above survive once the disanalogies are on the table: does the analogy really establish that cultural steering is futile, and does it really license the epidemiological suspicion of popular belief, or were those conclusions imported from elsewhere and merely decorated with Darwinian vocabulary? If you think a different comparison (for instance, to epidemiology proper, to domestication, or to market competition) would carry the argument more honestly, say so and explain why.

Please give your answer as continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, even where a table of correspondences might seem natural.
