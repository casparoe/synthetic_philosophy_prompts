I have been part of rationalist-adjacent reading groups long enough to have seen the same induction fight recur under new names: Hume versus Laplace, Occam versus no-free-lunch, Solomonoff lights versus adversarial examples, projectable predicates versus grue, exchangeability versus regime change. I want a prompt that turns that fight into case law rather than another abstract restatement.

Imagine a fictional moderator, Mira, who runs an old-school forum court for inductive inference. Her question is never whether a rule is true in the metaphysical sense; it is whether the rule is admissible for an agent that must act under uncertainty, has finite compute, and is not entitled to assume it knows the generating process. Her published verdicts are:

1. Urn case: a sealed urn is sampled with replacement by a physical randomizer whose construction has been inspected; after 312 red out of 500, predicting red next at roughly the observed frequency is admissible.

2. Reordered urn: the same 312 out of 500 counts are later shown to have been reported in an order chosen by a mischievous intern after the draws, though the draws themselves remain random with replacement. Using the apparent early streak or late clustering to predict the next draw is inadmissible; only order-insensitive summaries survive.

3. Local natural-kind case: after many observed black ravens and no counterinstances, predicting that the next raven encountered in the same region will be black is weakly admissible; inferring that all ravens everywhere are black is not.

4. Grue-style predicate: a rule stated in terms of a predicate that changes meaning at a future date is inadmissible even when it has fit every observation so far, because the apparent success is an artifact of description rather than evidence for the future.

5. Short-program stream: a 01-alternating binary stream of length 10,000 fits a very short program exactly; predicting continued alternation is admissible, while a gigantic lookup table that reproduces the past and outputs a constant afterward is inadmissible.

6. Adversarial short-program stream: the same 01-alternating stream is later revealed to come from an opponent who receives a small reward whenever the court trusts a simple continuation and then breaks it. Despite the short program still being the shortest exact fit, treating its continuation as admissible becomes inadmissible once the incentive to deceive is known.

Now Mira must decide Case G. A black-box source emits 20,000 bits. The first 10,000 alternate perfectly: 010101... . The next 9,999 bits are all 1. The final bit is 0. The court must rule on three proposed rules for the next bit: R1 says return to alternation and predict 0 because the final 0 marks the end of an interruption; R2 says predict 1 because recent frequency overwhelmingly favors 1; R3 says suspend prediction because the stream permits too many incompatible continuations.

Predict Mira’s verdict on R1, R2, and R3. Do not merely announce a winner. First state the most economical rule that fits verdicts 1 through 6 and gives your prediction for Case G; then give at least two rival rules that also fit verdicts 1 through 6 but diverge on Case G, explaining exactly where each rival puts the weight: known mechanism, exchangeability, description length, projectable vocabulary, regime change, adversarial incentive, or no-free-lunch humility. Say which prior verdict is doing the most work, which analogy is closest and which is dangerously misleading, and what additional fact about the black-box source would most change the ruling. Finally, name one new test case that would discriminate your attributed rule from the strongest rival better than Case G does. Avoid bibliography and avoid appealing to any authority; the answer has to be derivable from the verdicts and the problem setup.
