A colleague of mine teaches Kant's ethics and has a habit of issuing brisk verdicts on whether everyday maxims can survive universalisation. I have been keeping a record of her rulings, and I want to predict how she would rule on a new case, and to understand what she is actually doing when she rules.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Borrowing money on a promise one does not intend to keep, when one is in need — fails universalisation.
2. Declining vaccination against a contagious disease while relying on everyone else's vaccination to keep one safe — fails.
3. Slipping ahead of others in a queue whenever the queue is long and one is unlikely to be challenged — fails.
4. Refusing ever to lie, even to someone who announces murderous intent and asks where his intended victim is hiding — passes.
5. Buying the cheaper of two fungible tins of tomatoes — passes.
6. Keeping a promise even when breaking it would be undetectable and mildly profitable — passes.

The new case: taking a single apple from the vast orchard of a commercial grower, in the knowledge that this particular apple would otherwise have fallen and rotted, that the grower's plans take no account of it, and that one's taking it would never be discovered.

Please do the following:

First, reconstruct at least two distinct principles under which verdicts 1–6 come out exactly as she ruled, and note which familiar readings of Kant's universal-law test the track record already excludes — for instance, whether she could be running a strict contradiction-in-conception test, a contradiction-in-willing test keyed to the ends a rational agent must have, a parasitism or publicity test on which a maxim fails if the practice it exploits would collapse were the maxim universally adopted and known, or a cruder test that asks simply whether universal adoption would have bad effects.

Second, state what each surviving principle predicts for the apple case, making explicit where they diverge. In doing so, attend to how finely the maxim is specified — "taking what belongs to another when unobserved" versus "taking only what would otherwise perish unused" — since her earlier verdicts may reveal how she individuates maxims, and since the rival principles may disagree precisely at that point.

Third, give me your best prediction of her verdict, with reasons drawn from the pattern of the earlier rulings rather than from what you take the correct Kantian view to be, and say honestly how confident the prediction is.

Fourth, specify the single further case, with her verdict on it, that would most efficiently discriminate between the principles still in contention.

Please write in British English, and end your response with a final line of the form \answer{permissible} or \answer{impermissible}, giving your predicted verdict on the apple case.
