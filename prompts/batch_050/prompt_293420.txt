I'm in a seminar this term on death, meaning, and future generations, and we've just spent two weeks on Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife. Somewhere along the way I talked myself into basically accepting his "afterlife conjecture" — the idea that our unexamined confidence that humanity will carry on after we personally die is doing way more work in holding our values in place than any hope of personal immortality ever did. The passage that hooked me comes near the end (p. 73 of the OUP edition): "there is a very specific sense in which our own survival is less important to us than the survival of the human race." And his prediction is that if we learned, say, that an asteroid would end everything thirty years after our own deaths, the result would be "a pervasive loss of conviction about the value or point of many activities." I find this weirdly convincing and it has lowkey rewired how I think about why I care about anything.

Here's my problem though. I have a term paper due where I'm supposed to defend the conjecture, and my professor keeps pushing me on what would actually prove me wrong, and honestly I'm flailing. So: what would count against this view? Don't just re-run the doomsday thought experiment at me — I want the defeaters. For instance, would it sink the conjecture if it turned out that people facing real civilizational collapse or real imminent endings (war zones, hospice patients still starting projects, millenarian communities whose prophecies fail) mostly kept caring about their stuff anyway? Does it matter that Scheffler is making an armchair prediction about mass human psychology that we can basically never test — i.e., is there anything at all that could disconfirm it, and if not, is that disqualifying for a claim of this type or just how this kind of philosophy works? And what about the objection (I think Susan Wolf presses something like this in the commentaries at the back of the book) that plenty of things — relieving the suffering of people alive right now, basic decency — seem to matter regardless of whether humanity continues, and that Scheffler himself seems to half-grant this?

Sort the possible counterarguments by how much damage they'd actually do: which ones a defender of the conjecture should concede genuinely threaten it, which are survivable with minor qualifications, and which miss the point entirely. And if the honest verdict is that the view is specified so loosely that nothing could really count against it, tell me that too, and whether you think that's fatal for a conjecture about human valuing or actually fine. I don't need a reading list — just argue with me.
