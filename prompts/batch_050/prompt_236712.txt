I rewatched The Martian last week and the throwaway joke where Watney basically claims he's colonised Mars got me thinking. To survive, the man mixes his own labour — and, er, his own bodily waste — into Martian soil to grow potatoes, which is about as literal a performance of Locke's labour theory of acquisition from the Second Treatise as you could stage. And around him there's an entire empty planet, so the Lockean proviso (the requirement that enough and as good be left for others) looks trivially satisfied. Which itself seems like a problem worth poking at: if the proviso does no work wherever resources are genuinely superabundant, what is it actually for, and is the Mars case a vindication of Locke or a reductio?

So: read The Martian (film or novel — the beats that matter are in both, and there's the space-pirate bit about commandeering the MAV which I think is only in the book) through Locke on property. Does Watney own the potato crop? The farm plot? The rover he's modified? Mars itself? I'm especially interested in where the story strains Locke rather than just illustrating him: the spoilage limit (potatoes rot — does that cap what he could rightfully stockpile?), abandonment (once he's rescued and never coming back, does the claim lapse, and does Locke's own view of abandonment say so?), and the awkward fact that all his equipment is already NASA's property, so his labour gets mixed into someone else's stuff from day one — which is most people's actual situation back on Earth, come to think of it, and maybe where the film is more honest than the treatise. Also: does the film itself treat labour as conferring entitlement at all, or does it quietly assume Watney owns nothing (nobody seems to think NASA owes him the potatoes)?
