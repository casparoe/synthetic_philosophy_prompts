I'm trying to get my head around evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) -- the idea that even if you're causally isolated from distant agents (other Everett branches, far-off regions of a huge universe, etc.), your choices can still correlate with theirs via shared decision procedures, and this can matter for what you should do.

Here's the specific thing I keep getting confused about. In the classic setup, you consider a bunch of agents across the cosmos who are reasoning similarly to you, and you pick between, say, cooperating to benefit them vs. defecting to benefit yourself. The standard worry is: your decision algorithm is presumably the same no matter what, so you can't "choose" to defect without the others also "defecting" in some correlated sense -- the evidential link does the work.

But here's my puzzle, and I'd like you to explain your way through it: suppose you can vary *which population* of similar reasoners you're deciding in coordination with. E.g., when you act, you get evidential influence over everyone whose decision algorithm is sufficiently similar to yours *at the moment of your choice*. But your choice itself partially determines which algorithms count as similar to you (because how you reason about the problem -- what considerations you weigh, what framing you use -- shapes your final algorithm). So there's a bootstrapping issue: you don't just pick an action given a fixed reference class of correlated agents; the reference class is endogenous to the reasoning process that produces the action.

Does this bootstrapping break the standard ECL argument, or just complicate it? In particular:

1. Is there a coherent way to define the correlated population without circularity (like a fixed-point construction where your algorithm and the reference class are mutually determined), or does ECL need the reference class to be fixed exogenously?
2. If we accept some version of this, does it give you a way to "choose" better counterparts -- e.g., reason in a way that makes your algorithm similar to cooperators and dissimilar to defectors? Is that just wishful thinking dressed up formally, or a real consideration?
3. How does this interact with the worry that evidential correlation is screened off once you know your own decision -- like, after you've decided, learning that the distant agents cooperated gives you no extra info, so is there anything left for the "cooperation" to do?

Please explain this at the level of someone who knows Newcomb's problem, EDT vs CDT, and the basic idea of updateless/functional decision theory, but hasn't thought much about the reference-class-of-similar-reasoners angle. Concrete toy examples (even ugly numerical ones) welcome. I mostly want to understand whether the endogeneity problem I'm gesturing at is a genuine hole in ECL or something that dissolves once you're careful.
