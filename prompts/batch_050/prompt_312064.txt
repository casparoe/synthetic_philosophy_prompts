Two research groups in AI alignment have been arguing for years about what kind of agent we should actually be trying to build. The first camp holds that the right target is an approval-directed agent: a system that takes whatever action its overseer would, on reflection, judge best to take in that situation, with the overseer's judgment itself built up through a hierarchy of such evaluations rather than through any explicit specification of outcomes in the world. The second camp insists that approval-direction is a way of dodging the hard problem rather than solving it: what we want is a goal-directed agent that robustly pursues an outcome we have specified, and any agent that merely seeks approval is either secretly pursuing the goal of being approved (with all the standard failure modes of optimizing a proxy) or else is not really an agent at all but a very expensive mirror of its overseer.

Draft the joint statement these two camps would produce after a serious adversarial collaboration. Assume both are technically competent, both accept that current systems fall short of either ideal, and both have agreed to publish the result under both names. The statement should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. Push past the trivial (both obviously agree that misspecified objectives are dangerous). Look for substantive agreements: about what current training methods actually produce, about whether an overseer can be systematically and predictably deceived, about whether an agent's internal search process is legible in principle, and about which historical failure modes (reward hacking, goal misgeneralization) each framework must answer for.

2. A statement of the residual disagreements, each phrased in two versions: one the approval-director would accept as describing her own position, and one the goal-director would accept for his. Where a disagreement turns out, on inspection, to be verbal -- the two sides using "agent," "goal," or "approval" differently -- say so explicitly and explain which usage each side needs for the rest of its view.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to substantially revise its position. For each crux, record what each side predicts. I suspect at least two cruxes and want them addressed: first, whether an approval hierarchy bottoms out in something that is itself goal-directed (if the overseer's reflective judgments are the terminal standard, ask what prevents the whole structure from collapsing into goal-direction toward the overseer's future judgment, and whether either side should welcome or fear that collapse); second, whether there exist tasks where the overseer cannot in principle evaluate individual actions but can evaluate outcomes -- think of a research program whose intermediate steps are only assessable in light of results years later -- and what each framework does with such tasks. Feel free to identify further cruxes I have missed.

4. Where possible, a concrete case, formal result, or experimental observation that both sides agree would bear on a given crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. It is acceptable for both sides to agree that no near-term observation could settle a crux, but only if they can say why, and what kind of thing would.

Conclude with a section each side writes about the other: the strongest consideration the goal-director thinks the approval-director is systematically underrating, and vice versa, in wording the criticized side endorses as fair.
