Write a poem that enacts a spectrum argument against the transitivity of "better than," in the style of Parfit's or Temkin's cases, without ever naming them or using the word "transitivity." The poem should be six to eight stanzas, and it should itself be a spectrum: each stanza differs from the one before it by a single small change -- a word lost, an image dimmed, a year subtracted from a life, a note flattened in a melody -- small enough that no reader comparing only two adjacent stanzas could honestly call the later one clearly worse, yet such that the last stanza is unmistakably worse than the first. The closing stanza or couplet should turn on the question the argument forces: if every step was innocent, where did the ordering break? Let the form carry the philosophy rather than the diction -- the accumulating near-repetition should do what the spectrum does. After the poem, add a short prose note stating how confident you are that each adjacent pair really is evaluatively indistinguishable-or-better, and single out the one step where you are least sure the local innocence holds.
