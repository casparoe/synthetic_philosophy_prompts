I'm presenting a paper at a moral philosophy colloquium defending the claim that there is no significant moral difference between lying and merely misleading: if I get you to believe something false by asserting it outright or by saying something technically true that I know you'll take the wrong way, my culpability is the same, since in both cases I exploit your trust in me to plant a false belief. My argument leans on the idea that what matters morally is what the hearer is induced to believe, not the linguistic mechanism used. The audience will include Kantians who hold that lying is a distinct wrong against rational agency, philosophers of language in the Gricean tradition who will press me on what counts as "saying" versus implicating, and a couple of legal theorists who will point out that perjury and fraud law deliberately treat outright falsehoods and misleading truths differently. Prepare me for the question period: list the objections I'm most likely to get, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase them. For each, give a reply I can deliver in under a minute and flag where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should concede up front rather than fight. And identify the objection I'm hoping nobody asks -- the example or distinction most likely to force me to admit the lying/misleading boundary does real moral work after all.
