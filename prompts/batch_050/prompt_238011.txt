I am a novelist at work on a book set in France between 1789 and 1794, and the architecture of the whole thing depends on a question I cannot settle: whether my protagonist's path to the scaffold should feel, in retrospect, fated or avoidable. At a dinner last week I put this to two friends — one a historian by training, the other a political journalist — and they fell into an argument that ran the length of the evening. I took notes, and I would like a referee, because both seemed to be winning at every moment.

The historian opened with the structural case. Once a revolution makes political legitimacy rest on virtue rather than on law or inheritance, she said, factions have no way to compete except by outbidding one another in purity, and anyone who counsels moderation becomes proof of the revolution's betrayal. The French sequence follows that logic almost mechanically: the constitutional monarchists are destroyed by the republicans, the Girondins by the Montagnards, the indulgents and the ultra-revolutionaries in turn by Robespierre, and then Robespierre himself. The revolution consuming its own is not a sad accident but the default outcome, and she pointed to later revolutions — Russia, Iran — as displaying the same pattern.

The journalist replied that this reads inevitability backward out of a sequence that was contingent at every joint. Constitutional monarchy was viable, he said, until the king's flight to Varennes destroyed the fiction that he accepted the new order; the Terror's machinery was built under the pressure of a war that nobody had to declare and that was going badly; the defection of a leading general to the Austrians is not a structure, it is one man's decision. Remove two or three such contingencies and there is no Committee of Public Safety as we know it, no guillotine as the emblem of the age.

She answered that his contingencies were not exogenous. A king raised on divine right could never sincerely become a constitutional monarch, so flight or something like it was overdetermined; and the war was wanted, and agitated for, by the revolutionaries themselves, who expected it to unmask traitors and consolidate the patrie en danger. You cannot rescue contingency by citing events that the revolutionary dynamic itself produced.

He countered that endogeneity does not restore inevitability, and offered two further points. First, Thermidor: the same supposedly devouring structures reversed themselves within weeks once a single man fell, which shows how much rode on persons rather than logic. Second, the alleged rule is selection bias — the American Revolution did not eat its makers, and most of the revolutions of 1989 did not either — so the pattern she cites is what you get when you define your sample by its most famous member.

She closed by saying Thermidor proves her point, not his: the mechanism kept running until it ran out of victims, and the American case faced no invasion and no divided sovereignty, so the honest version of her claim is conditional — revolutions facing existential war and contested legitimacy devour their children, and France met both conditions. He said a claim that only works once you bolt on the conditions is no longer a claim about revolutions but about sieges, and he doubted she could specify the conditions independently of the outcomes she was explaining.

I did not take a side at the table and I do not want to signal one now. Could you go through the exchange move by move — the outbidding thesis, the Varennes-and-war contingency argument, the endogeneity rejoinder, Thermidor and the comparative cases, and the final exchange about whether the conditional claim is substantive or vacuous — and say who is right at each step and why? Then tell me what, if anything, both are missing. Finally, and this is the question that will actually shape my book: if one had to write the Terror as either fated or narrowly avoided, which does the evidence in fact support better, and at which single juncture is the contingency case strongest or weakest?
