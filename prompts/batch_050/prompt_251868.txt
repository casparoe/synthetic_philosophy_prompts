i'm prepping notes for a reading group on indian epistemology and a friend put together an argument that seems to prove way too much, and neither of us can break it. background you need, since this tradition is less familiar: an old indian school (the mīmāṃsākas) defends two theses. first, intrinsic validity: every cognition arrives already warranted. a cognition doesn't need to be verified against anything else before it counts as knowledge, the way a lamp lights up a room without needing a second lamp to light up the lamp. second, extrinsic invalidity: a false cognition is never false from the inside. you only find out it was wrong when a later, stronger cognition defeats it -- you lunge at the snake in the dark corner, get close, and the new cognition "it's a rope" displaces the old one. they want these two theses partly to prop up their famous claim that the veda is authorless and so beyond suspicion: never composed by anyone, it can't inherit anyone's dishonesty or confusion, and its injunctions concern duties you couldn't perceive anyway.

here's the argument:

(1) every cognition is valid upon arising: it counts as knowledge unless and until a defeating cognition shows up.
(2) invalidity is only ever extrinsic: a cognition is revealed as false only retrospectively, by a later defeater, never by anything internal to the cognition itself.
(3) so "counts as knowledge" is a default status a cognition has on arrival and loses only when actually defeated.
(4) take any belief i hold right now that happens to be false but is so far undefeated -- my belief that there's a snake coiled in the corner (it's a rope, but i haven't walked over to check).
(5) by (3), that belief currently counts as knowledge.
(6) but it's false. so knowledge doesn't require truth, and at any given moment everyone "knows" heaps of false things; the only difference between the knower and the radically deceived person is that the deceived person's defeaters are still in the mail.
(7) the veda is just the limiting case of this: its injunctions can't ever be defeated even in principle, since you can't perceive a duty, so they aren't merely undefeated but undefeatable -- knowledge so secure no evidence could ever touch it.
(8) but a "knowledge" that doesn't require truth isn't knowledge, and a warrant nothing could ever revoke isn't a warrant, it's a dogma. so the school's two theses are jointly untenable.

i'm actually sympathetic to the school here, so if i have to choose i'd rather keep (1) and (2) and reject (8) -- but i want to know what i'm paying. tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. two specific worries: if i give up (1) i think i'm stuck in a regress of lamps needing lamps to check them, and if i give up (2) i can't see how error is ever discovered at all. is there a reading where the school keeps both theses and just blocks the slide into (6)? and seperately: does (7) really follow from the snake case, or does something extra have to be true to get from "undefeated so far, and not the kind of thing you can check" to "undefeatable"?
