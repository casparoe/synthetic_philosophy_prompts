As a retired biochemist recently arrived in philosophy, I am preparing a thirty-minute talk to a mixed audience of philosophers committed to defining death biologically, neurologists who regard current brain-death criteria as clinically indispensable, and transplant ethicists primarily concerned with public trust. My thesis is that brain death and circulatory death are not rival discoveries of a single biological instant: both are practice-authorizing thresholds, constrained by the irreversible loss of an organism’s capacity for integrated functioning but not uniquely determined by it. I illustrate this with a hypothetical technology that could revive isolated organs days after circulatory arrest without restoring consciousness or whole-organism regulation. Prepare me for the question period: identify the six objections most likely to come from this particular audience, phrased as each camp would actually put them and ordered by the damage they could do; for each, say what I should concede, what I should defend, how to reply in about two minutes, and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I should hope nobody asks. Please write in continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
