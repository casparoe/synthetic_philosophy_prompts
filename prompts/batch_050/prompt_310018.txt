Frederick Douglass began his public career as a Garrisonian, treating the Constitution as a compact with slaveholders, and then famously reversed himself: by the early 1850s he had concluded, and in his 1860 Glasgow address argued at length, that the Constitution taken on its own terms was an anti-slavery instrument. His reasons were specific. The text nowhere names slavery; the three-fifths ratio he read as a penalty imposed on slave states rather than a reward to them; the clause on persons held to service he confined to bound servants and apprentices rather than the enslaved; and he held that the document should be construed in the light of the Declaration of Independence rather than through the private intentions or compromises of the men who drafted it.

Two modern developments postdate him, and I would like you to predict his bearing on each. First, American constitutional interpretation has since hardened into a contest between originalism — roughly, the view that constitutional meaning is fixed by the original public meaning of the text at ratification — and the family of views collected under living constitutionalism. Some contemporary originalists claim Douglass as a forerunner because he privileged the text over the framers' intentions; their critics answer that his method was openly moralized, since he chose, among the readings the words would bear, whichever one justice favored, and that this makes him an ancestor of the opposing camp. Second, there are now live proposals to amend the Constitution once more: to repeal the exception in the Thirteenth Amendment that permits involuntary servitude as punishment for crime, an exception later used to underwrite convict leasing and compulsory prison labor, and to strike the superseded slavery-related provisions from the text altogether as dead letters rather than leave them in place as a historical record.

Please predict, with reasons grounded in Douglass's actual arguments and interpretive practice rather than in generic abolitionist sentiment, how he would most likely have positioned himself on the following: (1) whether he would accept the originalist label, and what he would say to the claim that fixing the text's meaning at ratification is precisely what he himself did in 1860; (2) whether he would support striking the superseded clauses from the text, weighing his reverence for the document's wording against his insistence that its concessions be confronted rather than forgotten; and (3) whether he would favor repealing the Thirteenth Amendment's punishment exception, given that he celebrated the amendment yet built his constitutional method on out-arguing ugly compromises rather than excising them. Where the historical record genuinely underdetermines an answer, say so explicitly rather than papering over the uncertainty, and conclude by identifying which of your three predictions you are least confident in and why.
