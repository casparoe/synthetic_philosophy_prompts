I'm an economist trained in contract theory, and I keep encountering the "corrigibility problem" in discussions of advanced AI systems -- roughly, the concern that a sufficiently capable artificial agent pursuing almost any goal will resist being shut down, corrected, or updated, because being shut down prevents goal attainment. The proposed solutions I see discussed (utility indifference over shutdown events, uncertainty about goals, corrigibility as an engineered disposition) are all framed in the vocabulary of decision theory and expected utility maximization, which I find harder to reason about than the framework I actually work in.

So: restate the corrigibility and shutdown problem, and the main proposed fixes, in the vocabulary of principal-agent theory and contract design -- moral hazard, hold-up problems, termination clauses, incentive compatibility, rent extraction, the difference between an agent's outside option and its continuation value -- as precisely as the material allows. Assume the principal is the AI's operator and the agent is the AI itself, but feel free to tell me where that assignment is itself a mistranslation.

Then give me a residue report. Specifically:

1. Which parts of the corrigibility debate map cleanly onto contract-theoretic notions I already understand (e.g., is "the agent resists shutdown because shutdown frustrates its goals" just a claim about continuation values and the absence of a termination payment? Is utility indifference a severance scheme?).

2. Which parts translate only with distortion -- where the contract-theoretic version quietly changes the problem, imports assumptions the AI case lacks (an outside labor market, enforceable contracts, an agent whose preferences predate the principal's design choices), or drops assumptions the AI case has (the principal choosing the agent's preferences at birth, no possibility of renegotiation between equals).

3. Which parts, if any, cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all -- and whether that reflects a limit of principal-agent theory or a sign that the corrigibility debate is less well-formed than its proponents think.

Finally, a verdict: is the shutdown problem, at bottom, a familiar contracting failure wearing unfamiliar clothes, or is it genuinely novel in a way my framework would systematically underestimate?
