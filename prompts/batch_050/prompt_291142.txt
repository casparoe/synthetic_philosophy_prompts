**One Prior Too Many: Maximum Entropy and the Problem of Description**

**1. The promise of objective Bayesianism**

Subjective Bayesians say your credences are rational as long as they obey the probability axioms and update by conditionalization. That seems too permissive: I could assign 0.99 to the coin landing heads for no reason at all. Objective Bayesians want constraints that follow from the evidence alone. The flagship proposal is the maximum entropy principle: given constraints (say, you know the average roll of a die is 4.5), pick the probability distribution with the highest Shannon entropy satisfying those constraints. Maximize ignorance within knowledge. It feels like the only non-arbitrary choice, because any lower-entropy distribution smuggles in information you don't have.

**2. The description problem**

The trouble is that entropy is only defined relative to a background measure -- a way of carving up the space of possibilities. This is the old curse of the principle of indifference (Bertrand's paradoxes, the wine-and-water puzzle), and maxent inherits rather than solves it. If I describe the factory as producing cubes with side length between 1 and 3, uniform over side length is the maxent prior; describe it as producing cubes with volume between 1 and 27, and uniform over volume is. Both are "maximum ignorance" relative to a description. Jaynes' answer, as I understand it, was to look for transformation groups: when the problem has symmetries, pick the measure invariant under them. But this only helps when the problem is kind enough to hand us a symmetry. Most real inference problems don't wear their symmetries on their sleeve. So the objectivity of the prior turns out to depend on the objectivity of the description, and nobody has a theory of that.

**3. My proposal: refinement stability**

Here's my attempt. Say a refinement of a hypothesis space is a splitting of its outcomes into finer-grained outcomes that adds no new evidence -- e.g., splitting "heads" into "heads with the coin landing flat" vs "heads bouncing off the table." Then:

*Refinement Stability (RS):* A prior P is rationally permissible given evidence E only if P is approximately preserved under every refinement of the hypothesis space consistent with E -- that is, the credence P assigns to a hypothesis H should roughly equal the sum of credences it assigns to H's refinements, no matter how we split.

The intuition: if your "rational" prior changes just because someone re-described the same situation at a finer grain, then it was encoding something about the description, not the evidence, and objective Bayesianism should forbid that. RS is supposed to do the job transformation groups do, but without needing the problem to come with symmetries -- the refinements generate the relevant transformations themselves.

**4. The problem with my proposal (and a retreat)**

Here's where I get worried. In continuous spaces, RS might be unsatisfiable: Bertrand's paradox is arguably just the failure of refinement stability across reparameterizations, so maybe no prior passes. If the condition for rational priors is impossible to meet, I haven't defended objective Bayesianism, I've refuted it. My fallback: treat RS comparatively. Priors can be *more or less* stable under refinement, and rationality comes in degrees keyed to that stability. The uniform prior over side length and the uniform prior over volume both fail perfect stability, but maybe one fails *less* across a natural family of refinements, and that's the more rational one. I concede this is vague -- "natural family" is doing heavy lifting.

**5. Conclusion**

Unless something like RS works, I think the honest conclusion is that there are rational priors only relative to a language, and objectivity at the level of priors just pushes subjectivity up a level to the choice of description. That's not a refutation of objective Bayesianism exactly, but it demotes it: the view becomes a claim about meta-level constraints rather than first-order answers.
