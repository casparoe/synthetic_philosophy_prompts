i'm working on the combination problem for panpsychism and i've been stress-testing my own intuitions. here are my verdicts on which systems count as a single unified subject of experience vs. a mere aggregate, and i want to know what principle is generating them, if anything:

- a normal human brain: one subject. easy.
- a rock: not a subject, even though i'm happy to say its fundamental particles have micro-experience. no integration, no subject.
- two neurosurgeons who've operated together for 30 years, finish each other's sentences, and coordinate with a glance: still two subjects, however seamless the teamwork.
- craniopagus twins who share a bit of thalamic tissue and can each report what's happening in the other's sensory field: two subjects with a leak, not one, not three.
- an ant colony, which "decides" things and allocates labor: no colony-level subject. the coupling is too loose and slow.
- a split-brain patient after callosotomy: i think two subjects (or one and a half), despite the intact skull and mostly normal outward behavior.
- a whole-brain emulation running in parallel across servers in a datacenter, with the "neurons" on different machines passing messages over a network: i want to say one subject, just like case 1. but the inter-server messaging is slower and sparser than what ants do with pheromones, so this one collides head-on with my verdict on the colony.

is there one principle that delivers all of these, stated precisely enough that i can see which clause each case motivates? if something has to give, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why. and what does the resulting principle predict for an octopus, whose neurons mostly live in its arms?
