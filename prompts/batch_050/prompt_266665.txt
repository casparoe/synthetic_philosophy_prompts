So I cover AI for a general-interest magazine, and I'm deep into a feature on whether we can trust the "reasoning" that language models write out before they answer. The hook is simple: these models now produce long chains of thought, and a bunch of the current safety thinking basically assumes you can read those chains and catch a model planning something bad, or at least notice when it's being evasive. But when I talk to researchers, there's this nagging background worry that the written-out reasoning might be more like a press release than a transcript -- fluent, plausible, and only loosely connected to whatever actually produced the answer. And everyone I call seems to have a different take on which sub-problem here actually matters. One person tells me the real question is whether chains can secretly encode computation in the choice of tokens, so the text reads innocuously while the model thinks something else entirely. Another says that's sci-fi and the real question is whether training models on human feedback teaches them to produce reasoning that sounds honest rather than reasoning that is honest -- a kind of faithfulness laundering where the chain gets more convincing as it gets less true. A third told me the whole framing is off, because even a perfectly faithful chain is useless if nobody checks it, so the tractable work is on the monitoring side, not the metaphysics-of-faithfulness side.

Here's my actual question. Say I get to redirect a small research program -- realistically two years, a handful of people, a mix of model access and interpretability tooling -- and I have to pick which open questions in this area they should work on. What would your ranking be, and what's the argument for the ordering? Some candidates I keep hearing about: does faithfulness degrade as models get better and chains get longer; can you even measure faithfulness cleanly, given that every test I know of (editing the chain and seeing if the answer changes, comparing the chain against internal probes) seems to confound "the chain caused the answer" with "the chain contained the reasoning"; is there a genuine capability trade-off where making chains more legible to overseers costs the model performance; and does it matter at all if we can't tell faithful from unfaithful from the outside. I'd like you to be explicit about the criteria -- I assume some mix of how much downstream safety practice depends on the answer, how tractable it is with current tools, and how neglected it is -- and to tell me where those criteria pull against each other, like if the question with the most riding on it is also the least measurable.

Also, and maybe this is the journalist in me, I want the stakes made concrete: which of these questions, if answered one way versus the other, would actually change what labs or regulators should do? My instinct is that "the chain is mostly post-hoc" is the scary answer that kills the whole monitoring-as-safety-layer idea and pushes everything toward interpretability of internals, whereas "the chain is faithful but incomplete" is survivable. But I might have the dependency structure wrong -- maybe some question I'm treating as downstream is actually the crux everything else hangs on. Tell me what would change your ranking.
