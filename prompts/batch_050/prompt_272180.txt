One of my grad students brought me an argument on advance directives that she's rather proud of, and I honestly can't tell if its brilliant or broken. It goes: (1) The moral authority to direct someone's medical care rests on the values of the person who has to live with the results at the time. (2) A patient with advanced dementia neither endorses nor even remembers the directive she signed twenty years ago; psychologically speaking, the signer is gone. (3) So enforcing the directive means one person ruling over the life of another — a dead woman's vote counting against a living one's. (4) What the patient now seems to want (her routine, her sweets, not being distressed) is what should actually govern her care. (5) Therefore advance directives have no authority whenever the patient's present wishes diverge from them. Here's my problem: swap "advanced dementia" for "has changed a lot over ten years" or even "is asleep," and the argument seems to run just the same — and then no promise, debt, or marriage vow survives it, since every commitment becomes a past self trying to boss around a present one. My student cheerfully bites that bullet and says so much the worse for promises. I suspect its a reductio instead. Which step do you reject, why does it look so innocent, and whats the cheapest way out? I'd most like to keep (4), if that narrows things down.
