A friend walked me into this argument last week and I still can't find the exit. He's into higher-order theories of consciousness — roughly the idea that a mental state is conscious when the creature has another state representing it, being in some sense aware of the first one. Here's his argument, cleaned up the way he gave it:

(1) A mental state is conscious only if the subject is in some way aware of having it.
(2) Being aware of a state you have means representing it — some thought or perception-like state directed at it.
(3) To represent something as an F, you need the concept F. A creature that has no way of classifying its own states as pains cannot represent itself as being in pain.
(4) So a creature's pain is conscious only if that creature possesses the concept of pain (or something in the neighborhood).
(5) Human infants and most animals don't have the concept of pain — they can't think anything like "I'm in pain" or sort their inner states that way.
(6) So infants and most animals have no conscious pain. There's nothing it's like for them to hurt.
(7) But that's insane — infant pain is like the paradigm case of suffering, the one we'd point to if asked what we're sure matters morally.
(8) So higher-order theories of consciousness are false, since (1) and (2) are just that theory written out.

He thinks this sinks the whole approach in six moves. My problem is that each step looks okay on its own, and I've heard some higher-order people genuinely do bite the bullet on animals, which I refuse to do. So: where does the argument actually break? Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?

Two things I'd like to hang onto if at all possible: something like (1) seems almost definitional to me, and I don't want to rescue animal pain by just flatly denying (5) with no argument. Also I have a vague worry that the fix depends on whether a higher-order state has to *get its target right*, because if the theory allows higher-order representations that misdescribe what they're about, then something sneaky is happening somewhere around (3) — but I can't pin it down.

And one more thing that bugs me: doesn't the same argument prove my headaches only became conscious once I learned the word "headache"? Because that can't be right either, and if the reply that blocks that case also blocks the animal case, I want to see it explicitly.
