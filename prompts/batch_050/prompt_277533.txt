I help run a little reading group on the philosophy of disagreement, and last week a properly heated argument about AI doom got shut down by one member saying: look, two rational people with the same evidence can't agree to disagree, that's literally a theorem, so if we've talked this through and still disagree then one of us is just being irrational. The room went quiet and we moved on to biscuits, which annoyed me, because it felt like the slogan did the work rather than any actual maths.

My problem is I've since heard the same line used for loads of different things: that you should update hard towards anyone clever who disagrees with you, that persistent disagreement proves bad faith somewhere, even once that markets must be efficient because traders can't rationally disagree about prices. These can't all be the same claim. So: can you unpack "rational agents can't agree to disagree" into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, laid out as a menu? I'm thinking things like: readings where both parties must share priors vs ones where they needn't; readings where the disagreement itself has to be common knowledge vs merely known by both; readings about what "rational" is even doing in the statement (Bayesian updating? honest reporting of posteriors? just consistency?); readings that are really about beliefs about beliefs rather than first-order beliefs full stop. For each one, tell me whether it's a triviality, a genuine theorem (and roughly what its assumptions are), a plausible-but-contestable norm, or just false.

And the payoff question: which of these readings, if any, would actually justify the move my reading-group bloke made — i.e. going from "a theorem of this shape exists" to "our continued disagreement shows someone here is irrational" — given that real humans presumably don't share priors, don't have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, and aren't Bayesians? If no reading gets him there, say so plainly rather than hedging.

Two requests on form: keep the whole reply under 800 words, and for each reading on the menu say how confident you are in your verdict (high/medium/low is fine), then finish by singling out the one point in your answer you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the shaky ground is than recieve something that sounds uniformly authoritative.
