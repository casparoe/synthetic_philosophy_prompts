So I'm in a second-year philosophy course on decision theory and we just spent a whole seminar on the St. Petersburg paradox. Standard setup: fair coin, flip until it comes up heads, get paid 2^n dollars where n is the number of flips, expected value is infinite, and yet literally nobody in the room would pay more than about 25 bucks to play. Our lecturer went through the classic responses (Bernoulli's diminishing marginal utility of money, the point that any actual casino has a finite bankroll, etc) and afterwards I got into a long argument about it with my flatmate that we never actually resolved, which is why I'm asking this.

Write me a dialogue between two people who both understand the paradox properly but draw opposite lessons from it. One of them, call her Priya, thinks the paradox is a genuine problem for expected utility theory: she thinks the "infinite price" conclusion should be rejected, and that the lesson is that rational utility must be bounded, or at least that expected value reasoning breaks down for infinite games like this. The other, call him Dan, thinks we should just bite the bullet: in the idealised game the offer really is worth any finite amount, and our refusal to pay is completely explained by boring contingent facts (casinos have finite wealth, extra money matters less the richer you are, nobody could credibly promise the payout anyway), so there's no deep problem for the theory at all.

Some ground rules so it doesn't become two monologues: they have to actually respond to each other's specific points, concede when they've been beaten on some particular claim, and talk like humans rather than textbooks. Please make sure these moves come up somewhere, worked in naturally: Priya should press the point that even if you make utility concave in money, the casino can just offer prizes denominated in utility directly (years of happy life, say), and unless utility is bounded above the infinity comes straight back, so Dan's "marginal utility of money" story doesn't really solve anything; Dan should press back that bounded utility has embarrasing consequences of its own, like an agent near the bound becoming indifferent between a small sure gain and a gamble with astronomically higher stakes, and that our real-world refusals are exactly what the finite-bankroll version of the game predicts, where the fair price works out pretty small. No strawmen, both should be sharp, and neither should be the obvious loser. That said, I really don't want it ending in a wishy-washy "both sides have a point, agree to disagree" — have them push until one of them is genuinely forced to give ground on something central, or failing that, have them converge on one definite, specific claim about what the paradox actually shows, even if they get there from opposite directions. Aim for roughly 600-900 words.
