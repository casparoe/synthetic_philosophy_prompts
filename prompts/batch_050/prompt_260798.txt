Next month I am presenting at a decision theory workshop hosted jointly by a philosophy department and an economics faculty. My thesis is not the familiar one that Allais-style preferences are rationally permissible; it is the stronger claim that the two classic Allais problems — the common consequence effect and the common ratio effect — resist a unified explanation, and that any theory which models risk attitude with a single parameter is bound to misdiagnose at least one of them. I will argue that curvature of the utility function (as orthodox expected utility must insist) cannot capture the certainty effect in the common consequence case, and that a probability-weighting function of the rank-dependent sort, while it can handle common ratio violations, reproduces the common consequence violation only by smuggling in the very discontinuity near certainty it was supposed to explain. My positive proposal is a two-component view: the common consequence violation reflects how agents evaluate outcomes held fixed across the choice, while the common ratio violation reflects genuinely non-linear treatment of probability itself, and a normatively respectable theory needs separate machinery for each.

The audience composition matters. There will be three committed expected utility theorists: two economists of the revealed-preference school who treat independence as a consistency condition on choice rather than a substantive psychological claim, and one philosopher who defends the independence axiom primarily through dynamic consistency and money-pump arguments. There will also be several behavioral economists broadly sympathetic to my anti-orthodoxy but professionally invested in rank-dependent models, and a handful of formal epistemologists who will want to know what my two components do to transitivity and to the connection between preference and credence.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage each could do to the presentation, and phrased the way each faction would phrase it — the revealed-preference economists will not talk like the philosopher, and neither will talk like the rank-dependent theorists whose work I am criticizing. For each objection, give me a reply deliverable in about two minutes, an explicit statement of what I should concede versus hold, and a note on where the reply is weakest. I particularly need to be ready for: the charge that my proposal is a repackaging of regret theory and inherits the separability problems that defeated it; the argument that the two effects can be made to disappear under a finer-grained description of outcomes, so that my thesis rests on an arbitrary choice of how to individuate consequences; and the identifiability worry that no pattern of choice behavior could distinguish my two-component view from a sufficiently flexible one-parameter rival. Finally, tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and how I should handle it if someone does.
