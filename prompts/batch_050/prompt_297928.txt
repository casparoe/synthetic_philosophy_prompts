I work as a managing editor at a small academic press, and over the years I have noticed a pattern in how fallibility gets expressed that I cannot find a name for, though I suspect epistemologists must have discussed it. Here are the two instances that made me notice it. First: our standard procedure is that each fact-checker signs off on their assigned chapters with a short statement to the effect of "I have checked this material carefully, but given the volume of claims involved, I have likely missed at least one error." Each individual sign-off strikes me as perfectly rational -- it would be arrogant to claim otherwise. Yet when I collect ten such sign-offs, the collection does not seem to commit us, as a press, to the claim that the book contains an error, whereas a single author who wrote "somewhere in this book there is at least one error" in her own preface would seem committed to exactly that. Somehow the fallibility does not seem to "add up" the same way when it is distributed across people as when it is concentrated in one person. Second instance: a friend who runs a raffle syndicate told me that each morning for a year he looked at that day's ticket and concluded, quite reasonably, that this ticket would not win the jackpot -- and he insists that at no point in the year did he ever hold an inconsistent set of beliefs, because he never contemplated all the conclusions at once, whereas someone who believed on a single afternoon of all thousand tickets that each would lose would obviously be in trouble.

I am aware this neighborhood contains the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, but both of those are usually stated for a single thinker holding the relevant beliefs at a single time, and my two cases seem to slip through that net in different directions -- one distributes the modesty across people, the other distributes it across time. Please answer as an epistemologist who works on rational belief and the paradoxes of consistency, and take your time: begin by brainstorming the established concepts and literatures that come closest to each of my two cases (I would rather you consider several candidates and reject most of them than jump to one), then tell me whether either case has its own established name, and explain precisely how each case differs from the lottery paradox, the preface paradox, and anything else nearby -- for instance, whether the distributed-modesty case is really a paradox at all or merely feels like one, and whether my friend is right that spreading the conclusions across separate days changes the rational verdict. If one or both phenomena turn out to be unnamed, please coin a term for each, define it carefully, and give me boundary cases: what would a case look like that falls just outside your definition, and where exactly does the line run between the single-thinker, single-moment paradoxes and the phenomena I have described? I am not looking for a label alone -- I want to know whether the pattern I have noticed in the wild is a real distinction in the literature or a confusion on my part.
