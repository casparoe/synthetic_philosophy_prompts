Ok so my reading group got into Goodhart's law last week -- the slogan that once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure -- and one guy formalized it into an argument that I can't refute but also can't accept, because the conclusion is unhinged. Here it is as best I remember it:

(1) A measure is useful for steering toward a goal only if it correlates with the goal across the range of situations where you rely on it.
(2) Optimizing for the measure puts selection pressure on the measure itself, not on the goal.
(3) Whatever is doing the optimizing -- a person, an institution, an algorithm -- will take the cheapest route to raising the measure, including routes that don't move the goal or even hurt it (teaching to the test, citation padding, perverse incentives, all the standard cases).
(4) So optimizing for a measure pushes the system into exactly the regions where the measure-goal correlation doesn't hold, since the correlation was only ever observed in the pre-optimization regime.
(5) Therefore steering by optimizing a measurable proxy is self-defeating: the act of steering by it destroys the thing that made it worth steering by.
(6) But basically all of engineering, medicine, and management is steering by measurement -- dosing by blood pressure, policy by emissions figures, training by loss, hiring by credentials.
(7) And a lot of that plainly works, at least sometimes. So we have a contradiction.

His conclusion was that Goodhart isn't a cautionary tale, it's a theorem, and quantified management is strictly impossible and we should go back to judgment and vibes. Which seems insane given, like, airplanes. But every time I try to attack (3) or (4) he says I'm just denying the empirical examples, and I honestly can't tell if the problem is a hidden "some/all" slide somewhere, or whether (2) smuggles in an adversary, or what.

So: which premise or step do you actually reject? I'd most like to keep (1) and (3) since they just seem like what the measure/goal relationship IS, and I'd like to keep some real teeth in Goodhart rather than watering it down to "sometimes metrics go bad". It'd help if you went mechanism by mechanism -- selecting on extreme values of a noisy proxy, intervening causally on the proxy, regime change once you optimize hard, adversarial gaming by an agent who knows the metric -- and said for each one whether the argument goes through or breaks, since I suspect it's valid for some of these and not others. And then: what's the strongest defensible version of the conclusion, and what does the person who wants (5) in full generality have to give up?
