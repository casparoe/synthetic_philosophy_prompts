So my friends and I rewatched Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind last week and I got into an argument afterwards that I'd like some philosophical backup on. I've been reading around the ethics of memory stuff -- Avishai Margalit on the duty to remember, David Rieff pushing back with a case for forgetting -- and it struck me that the whole movie is basically a thought experiment for that debate, except nobody in the debate talks about it.

Here's the passage I want the reading to turn on. It's from early in Nietzsche's essay "The Use and Abuse of History" (the second of the Untimely Meditations, 1874), where he's arguing that memory and history are only worth anything insofar as they serve life:

"In other words, we need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action."

My question: is Lacuna's procedure exactly the thing Nietzsche is condemning here -- memory treated as an inconvenience you can pay someone to remove so you never have to act on it? Joel's case seems to fit ("a convenient way to avoid" the pain of the breakup, the humiliation, the having-to-decide-what-to-do). But then the procedure itself is where Joel finally *acts* -- the whole middle of the film is him fighting, inside his own head, to keep the very memories he paid to lose. So does the film actually dramatize Nietzsche's point, or does it complicate it? And what about Clementine, who erases first and never seems to look back -- is she the Nietzschean figure of healthy forgetting, or is she just stuck in a loop, given that she ends up drawn to Joel again? And Patrick, who steals Joel's erased memories and uses them to seduce Clementine -- what is *he*, on this reading? He has the memories but none of the life attached to them, which feels like the inverse of Joel's problem.

I'd also like you to deal with the ending, because that's where my argument with my friend started. Joel and Clementine get the tapes, learn they'll probably wreck each other again, and go ahead anyway with just "okay." My friend says this is the film repudiating Lacuna: knowledge of the past freely accepted, memory's burden chosen rather than escaped. I think it's weirder than that -- they don't actually *remember* anything, they're choosing on the basis of evidence about a past that isn't theirs anymore, more like reading someone else's file. Is that remembering in any sense Margalit would recognize, or is it a third thing the remember/forget binary doesn't cover? And is the ending hopeful or horrifying? Nietzsche's line seems to imply it shouldn't matter whether they remember as long as they *act*, but I'm not sure the film agrees.

Oh and don't let it slide that the film literally has Mary quoting Nietzsche on forgetting at the Lacuna office (the "blessed are the forgetful" bit, paraphrasing) right before she finds out what she herself had erased. That seems like the movie winking at this exact essay -- or warning against it.

Give me a reading that takes the passage seriously as an argument, not just a vibe: where does the film vindicate it, where does it push back, and does the movie get Nietzsche right or does it trade on a softer self-help version of him? End by telling me what Eternal Sunshine adds to the remember/forget debate that the philosophers' arguments on their own definately miss.
