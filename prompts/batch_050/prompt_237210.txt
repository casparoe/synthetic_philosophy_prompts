write me a parody FAQ, maybe 700-800 words, for a fictional startup called Certifiq (or make up a better name) whose business is certifying whether a given musical event counts as a genuine performance of a given musical work. like, you hire them to settle disputes: was that recital last night really Beethoven's Appassionata, or just some sounds? the copy should be written in chirpy, reassuring tech-startup voice, but the actual content should be ruthlessly precise about the ontology of musical works, and the humor should come from watching corporate FAQ-speak strain to hold that precision.

some questions the FAQ has to handle, deadpan:

- if the pianist plays one wrong note, is it still a performance of the work? what about two? the FAQ should gesture at some threshold policy without ever quite stating it
- a performance on synthesizer of a piece written for harpsichord: does the service certify the notes, the timbre, or both, and what happens when a customer demands historically-informed certification for a piece whose composer never heard the instrument it was "written for" in its modern form
- is a recording a performance? if i play the recording at a party, have i hosted a performance? the service should have a firm, slightly unhinged position on this
- does the work exist between performances? a customer asks where their symphony is being stored, and the answer should quietly commit to Platonism without ever using the word
- a jazz musician improvises over the chord changes of a standard: new work, token of the old work, or a billing dispute
- someone completes a dead composer's unfinished symphony and wants it certified: who exactly is the customer here

the target of the satire is the whole authenticity industry around classical music -- period-instrument people, purist critics, streaming metadata disputes -- not any actual person, so keep it fully in-universe, no named real philosophers or real musicians in the FAQ itself. an invented fussy customer or two is fine. the irony should stay deadpan the whole way through, no winking, and a reader should be able to tell that whoever wrote it actually understands the type/token distinction and is having fun watching a business model try to monetize it. by the end the reader should have noticed, without being told, that the service has solved no actual problem and invented several new ones.
