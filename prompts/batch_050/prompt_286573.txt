I'm writing a paper for my moral philosophy seminar on the ethics of blame, and I want to work out what I actually think about standing to blame by starting from cases rather than from a theory. Here are my verdicts:

1. My roommate regularly takes my food without asking, and last week she angrily called out another hallmate for taking hers. Her blame feels forfeited.
2. My uncle smoked heavily for thirty years, quit a decade ago, and now sharply criticizes my cousin for smoking. His blame seems fully in order — arguably especially credible.
3. A friend who lied constantly throughout undergrad but has genuinely stopped now condemns a classmate's dishonesty. Fine.
4. The same verdict does not extend to a friend who lies constantly now and condemns that same classmate. Forfeited.
5. A friend who has never had money condemns wealthy acquaintances for not donating. Fine — though I notice I can't rule out that she'd behave the same way in their position, and I'm unsure whether that counterfactual should matter.
6. A compulsive liar condemns a thief. This one splits me: the wrongs differ in kind, yet the blamer's moral condition seems continuous with case 4.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (2)/(3) against (6): if past wrongdoing in the same domain doesn't strip standing once abandoned, why would present wrongdoing in a different domain strip it — and if it doesn't, does anything other than exact-match hypocrisy ever strip it? The case I keep going back and forth on is blame that is never expressed: my roommate privately seething about the hallmate, saying nothing. Is standing a property of the act of blaming, or of the would-be blamer? Please propose a principle (or two serious candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, state precisely which clause each case motivates, say what it implies for unexpressed blame, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it has to give up.
