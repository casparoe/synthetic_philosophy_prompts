I'm working through a philosophy of biology book that leans heavily on the claim that a species is best thought of as an individual -- a particular thing, like a person or a nation, rather than a class of things united by shared traits. On this picture, the tiger species is not a set whose members are tigers; it is one large, spatially scattered object whose parts are tigers, born in a speciation event, persisting through time, and dying in extinction. The author uses the picture to argue that trying to define a species by essential properties is as misguided as trying to define Napoleon by a list of necessary and sufficient attributes, and that talk of extinction should be understood literally as a death. A colleague of mine objects that this is a metaphor dressed up as an ontology: organisms are causally cohesive in ways species plainly are not -- no species metabolizes, no species has a skin -- so the analogy can't carry the conclusions being hung on it.

Can you audit the analogy? Give me the explicit mapping: what plays the role of birth and death, of the organism's cells and organs, of its boundaries, and of its continued identity through change. Then separate the correspondences that hold from the ones that strain. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports features of organisms -- functional integration, sharp physical boundaries, a life cycle with a definite shape -- that lineages lack, and whether any of those imported features are doing the argumentative work rather than mere decoration. Separately, assess whether the two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are priced in: if species turn out not to be literally individuals, does anything follow about whether they can have defining or essential properties, and does extinction-as-death lose its force or merely its literalness? Finally, if the individual analogy fails as an argument (even if it remains useful as a heuristic), what better comparison, if any, captures what it was reaching for -- or is the right lesson that species are sui generis and no familiar comparison will do?
