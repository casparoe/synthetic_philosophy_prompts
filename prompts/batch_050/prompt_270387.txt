So my roommate has this view that calling something morally wrong is basically just voicing disapproval — like booing at something, but in sentence form. Fine, whatever. But then I started catching him saying stuff like: "Suppose cheating on your taxes is wrong — would it still be wrong if literally everyone did it?" And: "If lying is wrong, then it's wrong even when it spares someone's feelings. Lying is wrong. So it's wrong to lie to spare feelings." And once: "Honestly I still cant figure out whether eating meat is wrong."

Notice in none of these is he booing anything. The moral bit just sits there as one piece inside a bigger sentence — in a supposition, in the front half of an if-then, inside an expression of uncertainty — and it seems to mean exactly the same thing in those spots as it does when he says it flat out. And the inference one really bugs me: if those sentences are just elaborate disapproval-noises, then what exactly is the conclusion *following from*? Noises don't have logical consequences.

Then it got weirder, because I noticed the same shape outside ethics. My sister claims "delicious" is just a yum-noise, but she happily reasons: "If the soup is delicious you'll like it; it is delicious; so you'll like it." Same skeleton.
