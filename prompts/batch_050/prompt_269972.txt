A popular science article I read on the philosophy of probability explains the Pasadena paradox like this: 'The St. Petersburg game already embarrassed decision theory by having an infinite expected payoff. In a 2004 paper, Harris Nover and Alan Hájek devised a nastier variant, the Pasadena game, in which a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads and the payout — stated in utility rather than raw dollars — is 2^k if the game ends on an even-numbered flip k, and −2^k if it ends on an odd-numbered one. Here the trouble is not infinity but non-existence: the series for the expected payoff fails to settle on any value at all, since its positive and negative parts each diverge, and by rearranging its terms one can make the apparent sum come out to absolutely any number, positive or negative. Nover and Hájek conclude that the game is, in effect, a counterexample to expected utility theory, and they reinforce the point with dominance arguments that never mention expectations at all. The upshot is that any finite entry fee for the Pasadena game is as rational as any other: with no meaningful long-run average, there is no fact of the matter about what the game is worth, so nothing can rule a price in or out.' The casino-style conclusion in the last sentence feels too strong to me, but I cannot pinpoint where the article overreaches. Go through the passage claim by claim: which parts would a careful decision theorist accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and does the final inference — from an undefined expectation to every price being equally rational — survive once the earlier mistakes are corrected?
