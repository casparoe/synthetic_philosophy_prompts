I teach philosophy of mind and am currently drafting a chapter on dream skepticism, and I keep returning to a tension between the standard empirical practice of contemporary dream research and the positions that dominated the philosophical debate a few decades ago. Consider the established laboratory paradigm for studying lucid dreaming: participants who are skilled at becoming lucid agree, before sleep, that upon recognizing they are dreaming they will execute a prearranged sequence of left-right eye movements, which appear on the polysomnographic record as a distinctive, time-stamped signature. In some versions, participants then perform further tasks during the lucid episode — counting to ten, estimating the passage of a fixed interval, alternating breathing patterns, tracking a moving target with the eyes — and upon awakening they give a narrative report whose ordering, timing, and content is checked against the physiological trace. Researchers routinely conclude, on this basis, that particular experiences with determinate durations occurred at identifiable moments during REM sleep, and some go on to correlate reported dream content with concurrent neuroimaging data.

Here is what puzzles me as a philosopher rather than as a psychologist. The entire inferential edifice treats the waking report as a fallible but genuine retrieval of an experience that unfolded earlier, in more or less real time, during sleep — which is precisely what Malcolm denied in holding that dreams are not experiences had while asleep at all, and precisely what Dennett's cassette-style alternative resists in holding that the dream narrative may be composed wholesale at the moment of waking, with the felt duration and order imposed retrospectively rather than lived through. And yet the only access anyone has to the putative experience remains the report itself, so the eye signals and task timings are, strictly speaking, correlations between physiology and subsequent storytelling. My question: what conception of dream experience does this experimental practice, taken as a whole, actually presuppose? Please reconstruct its implicit theory with some care — that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep, that they have genuine temporal duration roughly matching the experienced duration, that the subject of the lucid episode is continuous with the reporting subject, that voluntary agency and something like veridical time perception are available within the dream — and then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular: could a committed adherent of the cassette theory absorb the signal-timing data without remainder, for instance by treating the lucid episode itself as the real-time experience and only the non-lucid dream as retrospectively composed, and if so, does the paradigm actually adjudicate between Malcolm, Dennett, and common sense, or is it philosophically underdetermined all the way down? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the methodology — what could no longer be inferred from what — if the retrospective-construction picture turned out to be correct.

Please write your answer in American English. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a rigorous reconstruction: state the presupposed view plainly, identify which of its components the data genuinely force and which are comfortable extras, and be honest about where the laboratory practice simply does not bear on the philosophical dispute.
