I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and last week two of my cohort-mates got into an argument about explanatory priority in spacetime physics that I'd like adjudicated. Lena claimed that Noether's first theorem settles the direction of explanation: time-translation invariance explains why energy is conserved, rotational invariance explains angular momentum conservation, and so on. Marcus objected that the theorem is an equivalence, and a biconditional cannot by itself privilege either direction; he added that working physics often runs the inference the other way, from observed conserved quantities (in scattering experiments, selection rules) to the symmetries of the underlying Lagrangian. Lena replied that general relativity settles it her way: in an expanding universe lacking a timelike Killing field, there is no global energy conservation, and the failure of the conservation law tracks the failure of the symmetry exactly — so the symmetry is doing the explanatory work, since removing it removes the conservation. Marcus answered that this cuts against her twice over. First, the local conservation law (the vanishing covariant divergence of the stress-energy tensor) survives even with no Killing symmetries at all, so what really constrains matter is the dynamical equations, not the symmetry; second, gauge symmetries are symmetries par excellence, yet via Noether's first theorem they yield only trivial conserved currents, which shows symmetry is not even sufficient for physically meaningful conservation. Lena's final move was to say that "trivial" gauge currents are precisely what you'd expect if symmetries explain conservation: gauge symmetries aren't physically real symmetries but redescription redundancy, so the theorem's output correctly diagnoses physical reality in each case. Marcus called that special pleading that redefines "real symmetry" as whatever produces a real conservation law, making her thesis circular.

Could you work through the exchange point by point and say who is right about each claim — including whether the Killing-vector argument shows counterfactual dependence, whether the persistence of local conservation undermines it, and whether Lena's final move is circular or a legitimate disambiguation — and then say who is more right overall, and what both are missing? Please also flag clearly which parts of this are settled (the mathematics, the consensus physics) and which are genuinely contested among philosophers of physics, rather than presenting a contested position on explanatory priority as if it were agreed.
