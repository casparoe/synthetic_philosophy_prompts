I am a senior in a comparative politics seminar, and I help my professor maintain a small coding project that classifies political events in established democracies — our current cases are Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States — as democratic backsliding, constitutional hardball, or ordinary policy change. Four of us code independently, roughly thirty events a term, disagreements get adjudicated in a weekly meeting with about ten minutes per event, and the professor has final sign-off. Write a decision procedure the coders can actually follow: a sequence of tests, run in order, each grounded in what the concept of backsliding minimally requires (the erosion of free and fair elections, liberal rights, or horizontal accountability, as opposed to losing a policy fight), including a symmetry test that checks whether a coder would code the same event the same way if the other side had done it, a way of separating changes to the rules of the game from changes in outcomes, and a treatment of intent versus effect and of temporary versus durable changes. The procedure must handle at least these hard cases: enlarging a constitutional court, prosecuting a former head of government, an emergency power that expires on schedule, and a critical broadcaster losing its license. Mark explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated, and give one worked case where the procedure yields a verdict a sympathetic coder will dislike. Continuous prose only — no headings, lists, bold text, or tables, written the way one would write a letter — and in American English.
