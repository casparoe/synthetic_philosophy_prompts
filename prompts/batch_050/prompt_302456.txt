My philosophy reading group does this thing where after we've slogged through a paper we each have to produce a "creative response" for the next session, and it's my turn and I am completely out of ideas. This month we read about the unilateralist's curse -- the problem where each member of a group is individually able to take some action that affects everyone (a researcher deciding whether to publish a risky finding, one colleague "just flagging something" to the dean), and each person acts when their own private judgment says it's worth it. The nasty bit is that as the group gets bigger, everyone becomes more confident that someone better-informed or more responsible will handle it, yet the chance that *someone* acts goes *up*, not down. So a conscientious, well-intentioned group of thirty can be far more dangerous than a careless group of three, and adding oversight can make things worse.

So here's my ask: write me a satirical internal memo, around 600-800 words, from a fictional research outfit called the Halcyon Institute for Consequential Technologies. The memo comes from the newly appointed Director of Responsible Innovation and announces a "revised and strengthened" Responsible Disclosure Policy following what is only ever refered to as the Third Quarter Transparency Event. The voice should be flawless deadpan corporate-speak -- numbered clauses, an FAQ section, sign-off and all -- and the humor should come from the memo embodying the curse's logic without ever diagnosing it. Some beats to work in (feel free to adjust): the old policy's flaw was apparently that too few employees felt personally empowered to escalate concerns, so the new policy grants every staff member unilateral disclosure authority "to any journalist, regulator, or person with a blog"; the oversight committee has been expanded from 12 to 40 members in order to make accidental disclosure less likely, on the grounds that each member can now reasonably assume someone else is checking; and the Whistleblower of the Month award had to be discontinued after it was won by seventeen people in the same week, each citing their colleagues' silence as proof of the urgency.

The jokes should land for someone who knows the actual structure of the problem -- the winner's-curse-flavored bit where the person who acts is by definition the one with the most extreme private estimate, the way individual diffidence aggregates into collective recklessness, the failure of "err on the side of caution" when everyone errs at once -- so that a specialist laughs in recognition rather than winces at a strawman. But it should stay readable for my group members who skimmed the paper, and the Director should never wink at the reader or realize what's going on. If a sentence would work in a real corporate memo, keep it; if it explains the joke, cut it.
