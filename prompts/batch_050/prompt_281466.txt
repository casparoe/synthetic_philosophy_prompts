My roommate has an argument he thinks shows that judging historical figures morally is always a mug's game, and it's been bugging me for weeks because I can't break it. It goes:

(1) You can only be blamed for doing wrong if you could reasonably have been expected to recognize it as wrong.
(2) What a person could reasonably have been expected to recognize is fixed by the moral reasoning available to them in their time and place.
(3) So if the moral reasoning available in someone's time didn't condemn what they did, they're blameless for it.
(4) But for basically every historical wrong you can name -- slaveholding, conquest, burning heretics -- there were respected people at the time offering reasons in its defence, so the available reasoning didn't condemn it.
(5) So nobody in the past is blameworthy for anything we now consider monstrous. Not Jefferson, but also not anyone worse than Jefferson, which is where I get off the bus.
(6) And by the same logic, our descendants in 2200 can't blame us for whatever we're doing that they'll find horrific.

His conclusion: the whole enterprise of judging the dead is confused. Now, I actually want to keep (1), and I'd even like to keep some version of the idea that Jefferson deserves slack that, say, a modern-day trafficker doesn't. But (5) can't be right, and I don't want the view that lets absolutely everyone off. So: which step do I reject? If the argument has a hidden flaw, tell me exactly where the slide happens and why it's so easy to miss. If it's valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what I have to concede to the "man of his time" crowd by giving it up.
