I run the security team at a mid-sized software company. Several times a year we receive an unsolicited message from an outside party of the form: I have found a vulnerability in your systems; pay me a specified sum or I will publish the details, contact your customers, or sell the information. We already run a bug-bounty programme, so paying for vulnerability reports is routine; the hard question is when a demand of this kind is legitimate negotiation and when it is extortion we must refuse and report. Please write a decision procedure my team can apply to each such message, grounded in the philosophy of the paradox of blackmail -- the puzzle of why it can be wrongful to request payment in exchange for not doing something (disclosing true information) that one would be entitled to do for free. The procedure should be a sequence of questions with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it terminates in a clear verdict (pay, negotiate, refuse, refer to legal counsel), and where it must hand over to judgement because the underlying philosophical accounts diverge. It should distinguish at least: whether the information was obtained within or beyond authorised testing; whether the demand is framed as payment for work or as payment for silence; and whether disclosure would serve a genuine public interest independent of the demand. Then apply it to three cases: a researcher who found a serious flaw through ordinary use and demands ten times our published bounty with a 72-hour disclosure deadline; an actor who exfiltrated customer data and demands payment not to release it; and a researcher whose valid report we ignored for a year and who now says they will go to the press unless we pay. State frankly which features of real extortion the procedure cannot capture and where the philosophical theories it draws on would deliver different verdicts. Please answer in British English.
