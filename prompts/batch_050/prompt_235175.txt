I'm a PhD student in political theory (my dissertation is on transitional justice institutions, mostly post-authoritarian ones, but I've gotten pulled into the legal theory side of things). Our reading group is doing a session on the legality debates around the post-WWII tribunals, and a friend lent me a chapter from a general-audience history of the war crimes trials. I'm not going to name the book since I just want a reaction to the argument, but one paragraph in it is doing a lot of work and I want to know whether I should trust it before I lean on it in my own writing.

Here's the paragraph, more or less as printed: "At Nuremberg the Allies essentially wrote new law after the fact and then applied it to the losers. The defendants were convicted of 'crimes against peace' -- waging aggressive war -- even though no treaty had ever made starting a war a crime; the Kellogg-Briand Pact merely renounced war between states and was a dead letter by 1939 anyway, and the label 'crimes against humanity' was coined in London in 1945 to paper over the gap. When the defense raised nullum crimen sine lege, the tribunal answered that the Hague Conventions already made these acts criminal, which was a fig leaf, since those conventions regulated how wars are fought, not whether they may be started. And the whole proceeding was victors' justice in the plainest sense: the Soviet Union, which had itself invaded Poland under its pact with Germany, sat in judgment over Germans for aggression -- proof that the charges were politics dressed up as law."

My instinct is that this is about 80% right and that the author has compressed some genuinely contested moves in the IMT's reasoning into a cleaner story than the record supports -- e.g. I have a vague memory that the tribunal's legality argument actually leaned much harder on Kellogg-Briand than on Hague, and that war crimes proper had a different status than the aggression charge. But I recieved most of this secondhand and can't tell which parts of the paragraph are the load-bearing errors. Could you go through it point by point: what's accurate, what's a defensible but contested simplification, and what a careful defender of the tribunals (or even just a careful legal historian) would say is misstated -- including whether the tribunal's actual response to the retroactivity objection is the one described here, and whether the tu quoque point does the argumentative work the author thinks it does. Then tell me whether the overall "politics dressed up as law" verdict survives once the errors are corrected, and be explicit about the epistemic status of that verdict: what specific finding -- say, about the pre-1945 state of customary law on individual liability, or about how the IMT judges actually reasoned in the judgment -- would change your assessment in either direction, and where in your own analysis you think you're most likely to be wrong.
