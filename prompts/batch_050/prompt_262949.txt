Write me a short story, maybe 1200 words, set in a small fishing port where, after the fleet is two weeks overdue in storm season, someone opens a betting book in the harbor tavern on whether the ships will come back. Everyone in town is sharp, everyone knows everyone else is sharp, and everyone starts from roughly the same read on the weather. The drama I want is the no-trade theorem as social tragedy: every time someone offers a bet, the offer itself is news. Why would Old Maren, who reads the currents better than anyone, offer three-to-one against the fleet unless she knows something? So bets get quoted, revised, and quietly withdrawn, and the book slowly empties out even as rumors pile up. The one person who keeps wanting to trade is a woman who needs money now for her son's medicine whatever the odds are, i.e. she's trading for a reason that has nothing to do with believing she's right, and the town has to work out whether her presence rescues the market or just makes everyone more suspicious of every price they see.

Please actually dramatize the mechanism rather than having characters explain it at each other over pints: offers functioning as information, everyone taking it as given that everyone else is rational, beliefs grinding toward each other until there's nothing left to bet on. No visiting economist character, nobody saying the words "asymetric information" out loud. End it on the question of whether a market that only exists when someone is desperate is a market at all, or something worse wearing a market's clothes.

Then, after the story, add a short note (separate from the fiction, a paragraph or two): where you bent the economics to make the story work, how confident you are that it faithfully captures the no-trade logic — Milgrom–Stokey-style stuff, common priors plus common knowledge of rationality — and the single point in your own story you're least sure survives contact with the actual theorem.
