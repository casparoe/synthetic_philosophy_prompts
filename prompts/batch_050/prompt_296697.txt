A philosophy of science journal has commissioned an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of mathematics who have been reviewing each other's work, none too charitably, for a decade. The first defends the explanatory version of the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument: when mathematics does genuinely explanatory work in our best science — the standard exhibits being the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas and the hexagonal geometry of honeycombs, where the relevant theorem says the hexagonal grid partitions the plane into equal-area cells with minimal total perimeter — we have roughly the same kind of reason to believe in mathematical entities as we have to believe in unobservable physical ones. The second is a nominalist who concedes that mathematics is practically indispensable but denies that this, or the cicada and honeycomb cases, commits us to mathematical objects: on her view the mathematics is a representational and inferential device, and putative mathematical explanations either are not genuinely explanatory or explain without our quantifying over abstracta.

Draft the joint statement their collaboration would produce, structured as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both parties can sign: on the structure of the original indispensability argument and what it presupposed, including confirmational holism and Quine's criterion of ontological commitment; on what the partial success and present limits of nominalist reformulation programs (such as Field's reconstruction of Newtonian gravitational theory) do and do not show; on the distinction between mathematics as representational shorthand and mathematics as a purported explainer; and on what, at minimum, the cicada and honeycomb cases establish. These claims must be neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side.

2. Disagreements. A table in which each row gives: the disputed question in wording both parties accept; each party's position in terms that party would endorse; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and any case, result, or argument both agree would bear on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include, at minimum, rows on whether explanatory indispensability strengthens the argument over indispensability simpliciter or merely restates it; on whether the cicada case is mathematics explaining biology or biology wearing mathematical notation; on whether the nonexistence of a complete nominalization of contemporary physics is evidence about ontology or only about convenience; and on whether the argument proves too much by committing us to the mathematics of idealized theories we take to be strictly false.

3. Verbal residue. Identify any part of the dispute you judge to be terminological — the senses of indispensable and of explanation are likely suspects — and say why, in wording both parties could accept.

4. Defeaters. For each party, state what they would count as a serious argument or result against their own view, and whether there is anything both agree would actually settle the debate rather than merely shift its ground.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work in this area from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested verdict — for instance, on whether the cicada and honeycomb cases are genuine mathematical explanations, or on whether reformulation programs have succeeded or failed — as though it were the consensus. Where the literature has reached no stable verdict, the joint statement should say so and say why.
