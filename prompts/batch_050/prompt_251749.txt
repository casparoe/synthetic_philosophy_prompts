**Question.** Read the following passage from David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section XII ("Of the Academical or Sceptical Philosophy"), Part I:

"It seems also evident, that, when men follow this blind and powerful instinct of nature, they always suppose the very images, presented by the senses, to be the external objects, and never entertain any suspicion, that the one are nothing but representations of the other. This very table, which we see white, and which we feel hard, is believed to exist, independent of our perception, and to be something external to our mind, which perceives it. Our presence bestows not being on it: Our absence does not annihilate it. It preserves its existence uniform and entire, independent of the situation of intelligent beings, who perceive or contemplate it.

But this universal and primary opinion of all men is soon destroyed by the slightest philosophy, which teaches us, that nothing can ever be present to the mind but an image or perception, and that the senses are only the inlets, through which these images are conveyed, without being able to produce any immediate intercourse between the mind and the object."

(a) What view of perception does Hume here attribute to ordinary people, and what does he claim "the slightest philosophy" does to that view? (b) Reconstruct the line of argument Hume is introducing (now standardly called the argument from illusion), and state which theory of perception it is standardly taken to support. (c) Set out how a disjunctivist about perception responds to this argument, and assess whether that response succeeds.

Here is the student's answer, which you are to grade:

**Student's answer.** (a) Hume attributes to ordinary people the view we would now call naïve or direct realism: by a "natural instinct or prepossession", we take the very images presented by the senses to be the external objects themselves, believing of the table that it exists uniform and entire whether or not anyone perceives it. Hume's claim is that this universal and primary opinion is destroyed by "the slightest philosophy", which teaches that nothing is ever present to the mind but an image or perception. Hume thus endorses the representative theory of perception: the senses are merely the inlets through which images are conveyed, and the mind never enjoys immediate intercourse with external objects, which is the correct account of our perceptual situation as Hume sees it.

(b) The argument Hume is introducing is the argument from illusion. Familiar cases — the oar that looks crooked in water, the double images produced by pressing one eye — show that how things appear to us can vary without any corresponding change in the object. But the experience in the illusory case is, from the subject's point of view, just like the experience in the veridical case. So whatever is present to the mind in the illusory case must be the same kind of thing as what is present in the veridical case. Since in the illusory case what is present cannot be the external object (which is unchanged), it follows that even in veridical perception only an image or perception is present to the mind. This is standardly taken to support indirect or representative realism, on which we perceive external objects only by means of an inner item — in modern terminology, a sense-datum.

(c) The disjunctivist (Hinton, and later McDowell) rejects the crucial move from the illusory case to the veridical one. The argument assumes a "highest common factor": a single kind of mental state present both when one sees the table and when one merely hallucinates it. Disjunctivism denies this. A veridical perception is a state in which the object itself is presented to the subject; a hallucination is a state of a fundamentally different kind, which merely seems to its subject to be a perception. Crucially, the disjunctivist holds that a hallucination is not even subjectively indistinguishable from a genuine perception, since such indistinguishability would imply a shared experiential character, which is precisely what the disjunctivist denies. If there is no common factor, the argument from illusion cannot generalise from illusory cases to all experience, and so the argument is simply unsound and naïve realism is vindicated.
