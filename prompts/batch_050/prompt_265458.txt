I'm a PhD student in political philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on Scanlonian contractualism, and I need a test case for a seminar I'll be running on the aggregation problem. Construct a thought experiment in which the individualist restriction — the idea that a principle's justifiability turns on the strongest complaint any single person could make against it, not on the sum of complaints across persons — delivers a verdict that seems clearly wrong, and wrong in a way the standard "save one or save five" examples don't already show. Constraints: the setting should be realistic and institutional rather than a trolley (a triage protocol, a regulatory cost standard, or a benefits-administration rule would all work); the scenario itself should be one paragraph; and the counterintuitive result must fall out of the complaint-based structure of the theory itself, not from an implausibly narrow menu of candidate principles or from agents behaving irrationally. After the scenario: state precisely what verdict contractualism gives and which feature of the framework produces it; then vary the numbers on each side and say whether the view's responses exhibit a discontinuity problem as the group grows; finally, assess whether the usual amendments — tie-breaking among symmetric complaints, or a proportional lottery weighted by claims — actually preserve what the restriction was meant to capture, or whether they quietly reintroduce the aggregation Scanlon wanted to rule out.
