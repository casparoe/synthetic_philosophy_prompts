I'm taking a course on the ethics of machine learning, and we've just covered the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — roughly, that when base rates differ across groups, a risk score cannot simultaneously be calibrated within each group and equalize false positive and false negative rates across groups, except in degenerate cases. I'd like you to analyze the 2016 ProPublica–Northpointe dispute over the COMPAS recidivism score as a case study in these results. ProPublica argued the tool was biased because Black defendants who did not reoffend were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be labeled high risk; Northpointe replied that the scores were equally predictive of recidivism within each racial group at a given score level. Do the following: first, map each side's complaint onto the formal fairness criteria in the impossibility theorems (calibration versus error-rate parity) and say whether either side actually made a mathematical mistake, or whether they were simply invoking incompatible but individually reasonable criteria; second, explain what the theorems do and do not settle here — in particular, whether they show the dispute was doomed from the start given differing base rates, or whether they leave open genuinely normative questions the math is silent about; third, run the analysis in the other direction: does the episode confirm the theorems' practical importance, or does it reveal their limits — for instance, whether treating the risk score itself as the object of fairness assessment already assumes a framing (who counts as the relevant population, whether recidivism is the right target variable, whether fairness attaches to scores or to decisions downstream) that neither the theorems nor the disputants examined? I want a disciplined mapping, not a verdict on who won the public argument.
