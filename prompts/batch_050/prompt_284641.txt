Imagine a mid-sized country with a weird but simple electoral rule: every ballot includes an "against all" box, and if "against all" gets the most votes, the office stays empty for the term and a caretaker commission just administers the existing budget until the next election. This actually happens sometimes — it's a real provision, not a thought experiment — and in the current race polls put "against all" at around 30%, behind a corrupt but basically competent incumbent (38%) and ahead of a demagogue (29%).

Write a dialogue between two neighbors the night before this election. Marta is voting "against all": she thinks a vote is fundamentally an act of expression, that voting for the incumbent would mean endorsing (and becoming a little bit complicit in) his corruption, and that a caretaker year is a price worth paying to tell the political class that both options are unacceptable. Dani is voting for the incumbent: he thinks Marta is treating the ballot box like a diary, that the content of her "message" depends entirely on what millions of strangers do, and that an expression whose foreseeable result is either entrenching nothing or empowering the demagogue is self-indulgence dressed up as principle.

Both of them should be sharp and good-faithed — no strawmen, and neither one is stupid. Make them actually engage with each other's arguments: I want Dani to press Marta on whether her vote even *means* the same thing if "against all" finishes second vs. first, and Marta to press Dani on what incentive any party ever has to nominate someone decent if people like him will always hold their nose and vote for the lesser evil. Somewhere in the middle, have one of them raise the coordination problem (Marta's message only works if enough others send it too, but each individual sender can't control that) and let the other one notice that this cuts both ways. They don't have to reach agreement, but each of them should concede at least one genuine point by the end.
