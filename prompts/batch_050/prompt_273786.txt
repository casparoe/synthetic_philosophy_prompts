Two of my coworkers got into a long argument after lunch about whether a machine's representations can really be *about* anything, and I'd like a neutral verdict.

It started with a thought experiment one of them, Alex, had read about: a vending-machine coin sorter built in the US to accept quarters gets shipped to Panama, where it starts happily accepting Panamanian quarter-balboas, which are physically similar. Alex said this shows the sorter itself has no real aboutness: nothing inside it distinguishes quarters from balboas, so whether it's "correctly sorting balboas" or "malfunctioning by mistaking them for quarters" is settled entirely by what its designers and users intend. Its meaning is borrowed from us. By the same token, he said, the internal states of an AI system only mean what we interpret them to mean — real, non-borrowed intentionality belongs to minds alone.

Priya pushed back using the same example against him. Nothing inside a frog distinguishes flies from small dark moving dots either, she said; what makes the frog's detection state *about flies* is just its history of natural selection, which is as mindless as the coin mechanism. If the sorter's function depends on design history, so does the frog's — so either both have genuinely grounded content or neither does. There's no extra ingredient in biology that turns borrowed aboutness into original aboutness.

Alex replied that there's a crucial asymmetry: the sorter's function traces back to an actual mind that had quarters in mind, while the frog's function is fixed by selection with no mind anywhere in the chain — and that's exactly why the frog counts as original and the machine as derived. A neural network, he added, is on the machine's side of the line: its weights are shaped by loss functions we wrote, so whatever content it has is inherited from us.

Priya's last move was to ask why provenance should matter at all. If a mindless selection process can confer genuine aboutness on a frog, then mindless gradient descent over training data could confer it on a model — the engineers just set up the selection environment, the way nature set up the frog's. Either provenance matters and the frog's content is as derivative as the sorter's, or it doesn't and the model's content can be as original as the frog's. Alex insisted there was still a difference but admitted he couldn't state it crisply, and there it stalled.

Can you adjudicate this point by point? For each of the four moves above, say who has the better of it and why — and if both are missing something, say what. I'm not telling you which coworker I found more convincing; I'd rather you judge the arguments as made.
