In the preface to Part V of Spinoza's Ethics, "Of the Power of the Understanding, or of Human Freedom," he opens with a promise that has long struck me as sitting uneasily with the rest of the book: "At length I pass to the remaining portion of my Ethics, which is concerned with the way leading to freedom. I shall therefore treat therein of the power of the reason, showing how far the reason can control the emotions, and what is the nature of Mental Freedom or Blessedness ; we shall then be able to see, how much more powerful the wise man is than the ignorant." The unease is obvious. In Part I Spinoza argues that nothing in nature is contingent and that everything, without exception, follows from the necessity of the divine nature, and in the appendix to that part he treats the human sense of freedom as ignorance of the causes that determine us. Yet here, in Part V, he speaks of a way leading to freedom, of the reason's power to control and moderate the emotions, and of how much more powerful the wise man is than the ignorant one, as though there were a path some minds travel and others do not, and as though the book itself might help a reader travel it.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on: Spinoza's Part V project is internally coherent with his necessitarianism, meaning that everything he promises in this passage and delivers in the rest of Part V -- a genuine increase in a mind's power of acting, a real transition from bondage toward freedom, and a distinction between the wise and the ignorant that is more than a relabeling of the already-necessitated -- can be affirmed without equivocation by someone who also accepts that every finite mode, including which readers become wise and when, exists and acts exactly as it does by necessity. Note that the question is not whether Spinoza's necessitarianism is true, but whether the Ethics contradicts itself across the distance between Part I and Part V.

My own credence in that proposition is about 0.4; a colleague of mine puts hers at 0.85. Give me your own number, but more importantly, decompose the question and give a rough probability for each of the following sub-questions, with a sentence or two of justification for each: first, whether Spinoza is genuinely committed to strict necessitarianism about every finite mode, or whether his system leaves any real contingency that Part V could exploit; second, whether his technical notion of activity, where a thing is active insofar as it is the adequate cause of what follows from its own nature, admits of real degrees of power in a way that survives the admission that every increase is itself necessitated, or whether "increase in power" collapses into a merely perspectival description of what was fixed all along; third, whether the practical stance of Part V, with its remedies for the emotions and its implicit address to a reader who might take them up, can be understood as itself a necessitated link in the causal order without becoming idle, or whether advice-giving of this sort tacitly presupposes an open future his metaphysics denies; and fourth, whether the promise of blessedness can be cashed out without leaning on the eternity-of-the-mind doctrine of Part V's later propositions, and how much weight that doctrine can bear given how obscure it is. Tell me how the sub-probabilities combine, whether you are treating them as independent or conditional, and what your final credence is. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, what argument or textual evidence would move that term most, and where the disagreement between my 0.4 and my colleague's 0.85 most likely lives.

Please write your answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Work through the sub-questions in order and let the structure come from the prose itself.
