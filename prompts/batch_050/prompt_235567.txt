I'm a doctoral student in applied ethics working on neural data governance, and I want to know whether the following configuration has an established name in the privacy or neuroethics literature, because the standard categories seem to me to miss what is distinctive about it.

The configuration: a person consents to a neural device collecting signals from her brain for one tightly specified purpose, and that purpose is genuinely and honestly served. Years later, the stored signals are re-analyzed — legally, under the terms of the original consent — and the new analysis reveals things about her that nobody, including her and the original collectors, knew or could have known at the time of collection. Two instances I keep returning to: a consumer sleep headband whose archived raw EEG is later mined for early markers of depression, and a motor neuroprosthesis whose calibration data, recorded to decode intended cursor movements, turns out years later to support inferences about the patient's emotional dispositions. In both cases nothing was intercepted, no thought was read at the time, and the original purpose was faithfully pursued. The wrong, if there is one, seems to consist entirely in the retroactive re-interrogation of consensually collected data whose informational content outruns anything the subject understood she was handing over.

This is obviously adjacent to function creep, to the secondary use of data, and to violations of contextual integrity. But each of those is usually stated for data the subject could in principle inspect — I can re-read my old emails and grasp what an insurer would learn from them — whereas neural signals are epistemically opaque to their own source. I cannot, by any amount of reflection, determine what my archived motor-cortex data says about me; I learn it, if at all, only through the analyst. That asymmetry seems to change the structure of the wrong: the subject cannot consent, even in principle, to what is found, because she cannot know in advance what there is to find.

So: does this triad — consensual collection, deferred inference, and constitutive opacity of the data to the data subject — have a name of its own, or is it merely a species of secondary use? If the latter, I want to know what the opacity adds or subtracts, since it is not clear to me that the standard analysis of secondary use even gets a grip when the subject cannot audit her own data. And how does the configuration sit relative to the mental privacy and cognitive liberty framing, which tends to fixate on intrusion at the moment of acquisition rather than on what archived signals might later be made to say? If there is no established term, please propose one, define it precisely, and mark its boundary cases: does it cover re-analysis of old blood samples, of archived browsing histories, of stored fMRI scans volunteered for an unrelated study? I am less interested in the label than in the exact carving — what the phenomenon includes, what it excludes, and where its edges lie.
