I’m a philosophy professor and after seminar last week some grad students and I got stuck on sweatshops and the old mutually beneficial exploitation problem: if a factory job is badly paid and grinding, but still better than scavenging, subsistence farming, or unemployment, then both worker and employer seem to gain, so what exactly is the complaint? To make it sharper I floated a little test I made up: call a deal baseline-OK if it clears the worker’s next-best alternative, complaint-OK if nobody is threatening, deceiving, or exploiting a desperate constraint the powerful party helped create, and residue-OK if the surplus is divided so the vulnerable party is not left merely non-worse-off while still carrying most of the risk, injury, boredom, and exit costs. Please describe the most important implications of distinguishing those three for real sourcing decisions, especially where a brand hides behind three layers of subcontractors and can honestly say it never met the workers.

I’m less interested in definitions from the literature than in what follows morally and politically: does consent under a rotten baseline still cleanse the transaction, can an interaction be Pareto-improving and yet wrong because of who shaped the menu, and does distance through supply chains launder responsibility or just scatter it? Also say what this implies for boycotts, bans, wage floors, and consumers paying more: when would exiting the market make the worst-off worse off in a way that matters, when is complicity still real even if the workers would beg us not to stop buying, and how should we weigh a small certain benefit now against pressure for better institutions later? Assume no fancy empirical citations, just reason from the setup; I want the cleanest version of the arguement, including the strongest objection that the whole framework is paternalistic.
