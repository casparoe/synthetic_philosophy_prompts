I am a retired research chemist who, after forty years in the laboratory, has lately joined a philosophy reading group at my former university, and we have just spent two sessions on the KK principle -- roughly, the thesis that if one knows that p, one knows that one knows that p -- together with the broader question of whether one's own mental states and epistemic standings are "luminous" to one. I came to the topic expecting it to be quickly dispatchable, and instead found it genuinely puzzling, which I suspect says something about how much philosophy I have left to learn. In preparing for our next meeting I have been collecting the standard objections to KK, so that we do not waste time re-deriving them, and I would like to know whether I have missed one.

My list so far contains three items. First, Williamson's margin-for-error argument: knowing requires a safety margin, iterating that requirement across a series of nearly indistinguishable cases (his example concerns knowing that one feels cold) drives one to an absurdity, and so luminosity fails, taking KK with it. Second, the externalist objection: if knowledge can be reliably produced true belief without the believer having any access to the reliability of the process, then one can know that p while being in no position to know that one knows it. Third, the regress worry: if knowing that p requires knowing that one knows that p, and that second-order state is itself a piece of knowledge, then the principle demands an infinite hierarchy of distinct knowings, which no finite mind could contain, and stopping the hierarchy at any particular level looks arbitrary. What I want is a fourth objection that is genuinely different in structure from these -- not a rewording of one of them, not a special case of the margin-for-error reasoning, and not a mere combination of two of them. If the objection you propose turns out, on reflection, to collapse into one of mine, I would rather you say so plainly than dress it up as novel.

I would be grateful if you could structure your reply in the following sections, in this order: "The proposal" (one short paragraph stating the new objection as crisply as possible); "Why it is not already on my list" (one paragraph locating the difference in structure, not just in subject matter); "An honest assessment" (one paragraph on whether the objection is actually any good, including the best reply a defender of KK would give); and "State of the debate" (three or four sentences). In that last section especially, please distinguish carefully between what is broadly settled among philosophers who work on this and what remains genuinely contested. I have noticed that philosophical writing sometimes presents one camp's position as the consensus view, and having spent my career in a field where one is trained to flag one's own uncertainties, I would like you to mark explicitly where the literature is divided rather than papering over it.
