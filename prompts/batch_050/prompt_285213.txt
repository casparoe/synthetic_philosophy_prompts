At a philosophy department happy hour, two of my colleagues -- one works on game theory, the other on the philosophy of action -- got into an argument about program equilibrium that neither would drop, and I'd like a referee.

The setup they agreed on: in the standard one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, defection strictly dominates, so mutual defection is the unique equilibrium. But Tennenholtz-style program equilibrium changes the epistemic situation: each player submits a computer program, and each program is given access to the other's source code before choosing. A program can then be written to cooperate if and only if the opponent's program is (in the relevant sense) like itself, and mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium, since unilateral deviation is detected and punished by the other program's conditional logic.

The game theorist's position: this is a genuine solution to the Prisoner's Dilemma, not a trick. There is no referee, no contract enforcement, no iterated play, no communication channel -- nothing the original dilemma ruled out has been smuggled back in. What the result shows is that correlation between rational agents' choices can be achieved by purely logical means: the two programs' decisions are causally independent but logically entangled, and that is enough. She thinks this vindicates the intuition that rational agents facing symmetric situations should expect their reasoning to be mirrored, and connects to why one-boxing is correct in Newcomb's problem.

The philosopher of action pushed back on four points. First, that the result doesn't solve the dilemma but dissolves it: giving players read access to each other's source code is functionally a commitment device, and the original problem stipulated that binding commitments were impossible; if you relax the stipulation, of course cooperation follows, but you've answered a different question. Second, brittleness: "cooperate iff the other program is syntactically identical to me" breaks under a trivially reformatted copy, and checking semantic equivalence of arbitrary programs is undecidable, so any workable version needs something like proof-based cooperation -- cooperate if you can prove the other program cooperates with you -- which is a far more delicate construction than the popular summary suggests. Third, the folk-theorem problem: he claimed that in the program setting, essentially any individually rational outcome can be sustained as an equilibrium, and a solution concept that permits everything explains nothing. Fourth, relevance: humans cannot read one another's source code, so the result has no bearing on actual human cooperation, and gestures at reputation, predictability, or "reading people" conflate a clean formal result with messy psychological heuristics.

She replied that the commitment-device objection mistakes the mechanism: nothing binds anyone in advance -- the "enforcement" lives entirely in the opponent's conditional strategy, which is exactly what an equilibrium is -- and that the folk-theorem worry is answered by the salience and robustness of the cooperate-with-your-like program as a focal point. On humans, she conceded the literal setup doesn't apply but argued the result still teaches something general: that the dominance argument for defection covertly assumes causal independence of the deliberations, and once you see that logical dependence without causal contact is possible, you should be less confident the assumption holds in cases of genuine similarity or shared deliberative procedures.

They both seemed to be making good points, and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Please adjudicate: for each of his four objections, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails, and why; assess her focal-point reply to the folk-theorem worry and her claim about the dominance argument's covert independence assumption; and identify anything important you think both of them are missing about what program equilibrium does and doesn't show.
