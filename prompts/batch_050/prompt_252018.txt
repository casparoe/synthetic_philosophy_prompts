Two colleagues at my office -- both engineers, neither with any philosophy training -- got into a recurring argument during our lunch sessions, and after three rounds of it I realized I couldn't settle it, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing. The topic: whether a person can meaningfully assign probabilities to their own future choices while still deliberating about those choices. Andre insists yes. His case: people do this constantly and sensibly -- I'll say "I'm 70% likely to take the Berlin job" to my partner while still mulling it over, and that number tracks real evidence (my personality, my track record of choosing stability over novelty, how I've answered similar offers before). He adds that a good forecaster should be able to predict even their own behavior the way they'd predict anyone else's, since the brain is just another physical system, and refusing to quantify that prediction is a kind of modesty theater. Priya says no, or at least that something goes badly wrong when you try. Her case: the moment you assign probability 0.7 to taking the job, you've either already decided (in which case you're not deliberating, you're reporting) or you're still genuinely open (in which case the 0.7 is a fiction you don't actually believe, because the decision is up to you and isn't settled by anything yet). She says deliberation has to treat your own choice as unavailable for prediction from the inside -- she used the phrase "deliberation crowds out prediction," which she thinks she picked up from some philosopher but couldn't place where. Andre pushed back with a sharp counterexample: if he notices his own deliberation pulling strongly toward the job offer, that very pull is evidence about what he'll do, so the deliberation itself generates the prediction rather than excluding it. Priya replied that that's exactly the trap -- using the anticipated outcome of deliberation as evidence for an outcome smuggles the conclusion in, and if you genuinely believed you'd pick Berlin at 90%, asking "should I pick Berlin?" becomes empty theater. Andre's last move: he said her position proves too much, since it would also condemn a manager forecasting her own team's decision, or anyone using their past self as data, which we plainly do rationally all the time. Priya answered that past-self data is fine for other people and fine for your settled dispositions, but not for the token choice currently being made, and Andre called that an unprincipled carve-out.
