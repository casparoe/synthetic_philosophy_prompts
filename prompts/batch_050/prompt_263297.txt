I TA a third-year undergraduate philosophy of science seminar (eighty minutes, about eighteen students, a quarter of them math majors taking it as an elective). Next week I'm teaching mathematical explanation in natural science -- the territory of the cicada prime-period case, hexagonal honeycombs, and the "enhanced" indispensability argument that runs from explanatory indispensability to mathematical realism. Don't give me the seminar content; give me the plan for teaching it.

Specifically, I want: (1) The order in which the pieces must be introduced and the dependency structure -- e.g., why the distinction between mathematics representing a fact and mathematics explaining a fact has to be secure before the cicada case will land, and where the generic Quine-Putnam indispensability argument belongs relative to the "enhanced" version (my instinct is: after, so students can see what work "explanatory" is doing; argue for or against this). (2) The carrying example. I plan to build the session around periodical cicadas, not honeycombs. Explain what the carrying example needs to have and lack, and evaluate my choice -- including the worry that the honeycomb case is the more famous one, and the separate worry that the honeycomb story may be scientifically shakier than its philosophical afterlife suggests. (3) The two objections students reliably raise and where in the sequence to preempt each: first, that the mathematics is just bookkeeping or redescription and the real explanation is evolutionary; second, that saying the prime structure "explains" the life-cycle just means the description is more compact. For each, say what a satisfying reply requires me to have already established. (4) The misconception map beyond those two: what the math majors in particular will get wrong (I suspect they'll treat any derivation as an explanation) and what the pure philosophy students will get wrong (I suspect they'll think the debate is just about whether numbers exist, full stop). (5) A check for understanding I can run in the last ten minutes -- ideally one where students have to produce, on their own, a case where mathematics is indispensable for describing a phenomenon but explanatorily idle, and say what distinguishes it from the cicada case. (6) What I should openly declare out of scope at this level -- I'm assuming confirmational holism and nominalist reformulation programs are on that list -- and the one-sentence justification I'd give students for excluding each.
