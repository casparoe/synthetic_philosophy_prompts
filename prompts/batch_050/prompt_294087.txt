I am an undergraduate taking an upper-level course on democratic theory and institutional design. For my final seminar presentation, I will defend an original proposal that combines sortition with term limits rather than treating them as separate reforms. Please act as an expert in democratic theory and comparative institutional design, as well as a skeptical seminar moderator. Think carefully, brainstorm objections silently before organizing the final answer, and focus on objections this particular audience is likely to raise rather than producing a generic list of pros and cons.

My proposal is to replace a legislature’s elected upper chamber with a Civic Chamber of 150 randomly selected adults, stratified by region, age, sex, and education. Members would serve one nonrenewable two-year term, with one-third rotating every eight months. They would receive the national median wage, legal and administrative support, childcare, accessibility accommodations, and protection from employment discrimination. The chamber could amend or delay ordinary legislation once, but an elected lower chamber could ultimately override it. It would have stronger authority over electoral administration, legislative procedure, conflicts of interest, and anti-corruption rules. Former Civic Chamber members would be barred from elected office for eight years, while elected lower-chamber legislators would be limited to three consecutive terms.

My central claim is that legislative rotation should target political careers, not merely individual incumbents. Ordinary term limits attack incumbency but often leave intact the career path connecting officeholders, party officials, lobbyists, consultants, and permanent staff. My combination of lottery selection, staggered service, and an eight-year political cooling-off period is supposed to disrupt that path without creating a wholly inexperienced chamber or replacing electoral accountability altogether.

The audience will contain four identifiable kinds of questioners: a democratic theorist who cares about authorization, accountability, and the difference between representing someone and merely resembling them; an empirically oriented graduate student who will ask about selection effects, competence, capture by staff, and whether citizens would regard the chamber as legitimate; a constitutional-law student concerned with veto points, separation of powers, and the chamber’s asymmetric authority over electoral rules; and a student political organizer who will worry that random selection depoliticizes organized participation and that term limits weaken groups without the resources to replace experienced representatives.

Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me. Give me approximately ten objections in the order they are most likely to arise, or explain why severity rather than chronology is the better ordering. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it. For every objection, provide:

1. the strongest version, not a strawman;
2. what I should concede immediately;
3. what I should continue to defend;
4. a concise reply I could deliver in under two minutes;
5. where that reply remains vulnerable;
6. any distinction I should introduce to prevent the discussion from becoming confused.

Also identify the hidden premise in my argument most likely to be challenged, the objection I should most hope nobody asks, and the one concession I can offer that would make the proposal more defensible without abandoning its core. Distinguish objections to sortition from objections to term limits and objections specifically caused by combining them. Do not provide citations or a reading list; base the preparation on conceptual analysis and widely familiar institutional concerns.
