I got into a running argument with a friend who does ML theory, and I want ammunition -- or, honestly, to find out if I'm wrong. My position, cobbled together from reading around online: Occam's razor isn't just a pragmatic heuristic or an aesthetic preference, it's actually justified, and Solomonoff induction is the justification. Roughly my story: any hypothesis about the world can be treated as a program, shorter programs get exponentially more prior weight (2^-K), so simpler theories are literally more probable in the one correct prior, and the convergence results show that predicting with the universal prior does nearly as well as predicting with the true distribution itself, so long as the truth is computable. So when someone asks "why prefer the simpler theory?", the answer is "theorems", not "taste". My friend thinks this is wildly overrated and falls apart the moment you poke it, but when I ask her where exactly it breaks, she just gestures at "the machine problem" and changes the subject.

So: what would count against my position? I want a map of its exposure, not a laundry list of the standard textbook caveats, and I want it sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Some specific pressure points I'm unsure about -- reframe them if I've got them wrong: (1) The choice of reference machine. Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to an additive constant, and that constant can be big relative to any finite amount of data. Does that just make the razor approximate, or does it gut the objectivity claim entirely? If I pick a machine on which some crank hypothesis happens to have a short encoding, is the "justification" only as good as my choice of machine -- and is there any non-question-begging way to say one machine is the right one? (2) Uncomputability. Nobody can actually run Solomonoff, and the computable approximations people really use (MDL-style methods, compression-as-intelligence stuff, Levin-search-flavored ideas) don't obviously inherit the theorem's guarantees. Is my position about anything anyone could ever do, or only about an oracle? (3) What the convergence theorem actually proves. Doesn't a Bayesian with a crazy but "open-minded" prior also converge eventually? If so, is 2^-K only special with respect to speed -- and can you get from "converges fastest in a certain sense" to "the truth probably looks like this" without just re-stating Hume's problem in program-size terms? (4) The assumption that the true environment is computable at all. How much weight is that carrying, and does the position have anything to say if it's false? For each of these, say whether a sophisticated defender -- someone who's actually worked through the proofs, not a forum reader like me -- would concede it as a real cost or dismiss it as a misunderstanding, and what the strongest surviving version of my position would be. And if there's some result I should already know about that cuts against me (say, about how the prior behaves badly on particular sequence classes), don't spare me.
