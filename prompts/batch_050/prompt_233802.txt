I chair the appeals panel for a consortium of university art galleries that share an insurance scheme, and I need to draft our decision memo on a denied claim. The facts: a member gallery shipped a large blown-glass installation to a partner museum. The policy covers accidental damage in transit but excludes loss caused by the "inherent fragility of the insured object". The conservators built a custom crate for it -- rigid shell, suspension mounts, and an active damping system whose sensors detect shocks and fire counterweights to cancel the vibration before it reaches the glass. On the motorway the van was rear ended hard enough to crush part of the crate and overload the dampers; the piece shattered.

The insurer refused to pay. Their argument is simple: the object was glass, glass is fragile, the loss was caused by its fragility, exclusion bites. The gallery's reply, in effect, is that the fragility never got a chance to do anything: so packed, the piece would have shrugged off any ordinary knock, and what destroyed it was a collision violent enough to wreck the crate itself -- a covered accident, not an inherent quality of the glass. The insurer counters that on the gallery's logic the exclusion could never apply to anything that was packed at all, which makes it a dead letter.

Draft the memo: recommend upholding the denial or directing payment, with reasons the losing side could actually respond to. Things I'd like the memo to get right: the difference between a protective measure that leaves the object as it is while blocking the effect (packaging) and one that arguably changes what the object would do at the very moment of stress (the active dampers) -- does that difference matter here, and if so where does the damping system fall?; the insurer's strongest point, taken seriously rather than waved away; and some usable guidance for future claims, since "everything breaks if you hit it hard enough" and "it broke, so it was fragile" both seem like terrible rules. Aim for something the panel could actually vote on. British English please.
