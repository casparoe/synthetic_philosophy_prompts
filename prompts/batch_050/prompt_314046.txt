I help run a small reading group on questions adjacent to effective altruism, and for two years we have been circling a single dispute without any movement: whether habitat destruction — deforestation, the draining of wetlands, the paving of grassland — is, on balance, good or bad for wild animals themselves, considered purely as a matter of their own welfare. Nobody in the group doubts that wild animals suffer enormously, and everyone accepts roughly the same natural history: most wild animals are r-strategists producing vast numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young of starvation, predation, disease, or exposure, and habitat destruction works chiefly by reducing the number of such lives that get lived at all. One side argues from these shared premises that less habitat means fewer such lives and therefore less suffering in total, so that destruction, however counterintuitively, amounts to a welfare improvement. The other side finds that conclusion monstrous: it insists that the absence of suffering is not the whole of welfare, and points out that the same logic would seem to favour sterilising entire ecosystems, draining the oceans, or paving the Serengeti. Both camps can now recite the other's arguments by heart, and neither has budged an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about ecology at all. I suspect it is population ethics wearing a disguise: the habitat-destruction-is-good side seems to be tacitly reasoning as total utilitarians with a critical level near zero, so that lives barely worth living — or even slightly worse than that — still count in favour of creating them, while the opposing side is tacitly person-affecting or negative-leaning. On that hypothesis, the identical fight would reappear, unchanged, if the subject were insect farming or fish stocking rather than forests, which suggests the ecology is decorative. But I might be wrong. Perhaps there is a genuine empirical disagreement buried in the debate about whether the typical wild life is net-positive or net-negative, which better field science on stress, pain, and mortality could in principle settle; or perhaps the real fault line is a conceptual disagreement about what welfare even consists in for creatures with no projects, no plans, and no preferences beyond the moment.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Taking what each side actually says — not a caricature of either — tell me what kind of impasse this is: a hidden factual question masquerading as a moral one, a bedrock disagreement in axiology that argument cannot reach, two different questions being answered under a single heading, or something else again. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a research programme, a formal result in population ethics, or an honest argument that the impasse is permanent because it reduces to starting intuitions neither side can give reasons for. I would rather hear 'this one cannot be resolved, and here is precisely why' than a forced synthesis.
