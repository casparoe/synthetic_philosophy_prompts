I run a monthly adult-education evening at my synagogue, aimed at congregants who are intellectually curious but have no formal background in medieval philosophy. Next session I want to teach Maimonides' account of the animal-sacrifice commandments in Part III of the Guide for the Perplexed -- specifically the famous strategy of the chapter where he argues that God did not demand sacrifice because it is intrinsically fitting worship, but permitted and channelled it as a concession to habits the Israelites had absorbed from the idolatrous cultures around them, since abruptly abolishing the only form of worship they understood would have been intolerable to them, and instead redirected those habits toward the true God. Plan the session for me; don't just explain the idea to me. Roughly ninety minutes, and I can assume the group has read nothing in advance.

What I want is the explanatory architecture. First, the order in which the components have to be introduced and why -- what must be in place before the very question "why would God command something He doesn't want?" can be asked without sounding either blasphemous or trivial, and where in the sequence the general principle (that divine law works with human nature as it finds it, changing people gradually rather than demanding the psychologically impossible) must be stated so that the sacrifice discussion lands as an application of it rather than as a suspicious ad hoc rescue. Second, the one concrete example that should carry the whole evening. I don't want the worn-out "training wheels" line; give me a modern analogy with real structure to it (harm-reduction medicine, staged habit change, or something better), specify which features of it map onto Maimonides' argument and where the mapping breaks, since the group will push on it. Third, the misconception map. I anticipate at least these, but tell me if I'm missing the important ones: that Maimonides is a modern liberal debunking ritual as such (he isn't; he keeps the commandments' authority fully intact); that he thinks sacrifice was a mistake or a fraud; that his "pedagogy" is just the modern idea of progress read backward into the twelfth century; and the objection that elsewhere in the Guide he gives detailed rational reasons for the particulars of the sacrificial laws, which seems to sit awkwardly with the claim that the whole institution was a concession. For each misconception, tell me at what point in the planned sequence I should pre-empt it and how, rather than correcting it after it has hardened.

Close with two things: a single question I can put to the group in the last ten minutes whose answers would show whether they have actually grasped the structure of the argument -- that a law can be binding, divinely commanded, and historically conditional all at once -- rather than merely absorbed a slogan; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level (I'm guessing the esotericism debates and the scholarship on Maimonides' sources, but tell me if I'm wrong), with a one-sentence honest placeholder I can say aloud when those topics threaten to swallow the evening.
