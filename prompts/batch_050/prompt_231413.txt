I've been in a reading group on epistemology for about a year now and we've spent the last three months on the contextualism / pragmatic encroachment fight, and honestly it feels completely frozen. Everyone at the table knows the bank cases and the airport cases and the high/low stakes pairs backwards. The contextualists say "knows" shifts its standards with the context of the ascriber, the interest-relative invariantists say knowledge itself depends on the subject's practical situation, and the purist invariantists say both groups are just mistaking when it's appropriate to *say* someone knows for when they actually know. What gets me is that all three camps seem to have an internally tidy story about the exact same intuitions, and the arguments just bounce off each other. Someone presents a case, and each side redescribes it in their own vocabulary, and then we're done for the week.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about knowledge at all anymore but a disagreement about what job a theory of knowledge is supposed to do -- like, the contextualists are doing linguistics (how do we actually talk), the pragmatic encroachers are doing something more like decision theory or ethics of belief (what can you properly rely on), and the purists are defending a kind of theoretical hygiene where nothing practical gets into epistemology. If that's right, the debate looks stalled because the sides keep scoring points on different scoreboards. But I'm not sure. Maybe there IS a genuine shared question -- something like "does whether p is epistemically good enough for you depend on what rides on p" -- and the disagreement about it is substantive, just hard, and one side is simply wrong. Or maybe it all turns on a hidden empirical question about how ordinary speakers actually use "knows" that philosophers have been arguing about from the armchair when they could in principle just go test it, and the whole thing could be settled by x-phi people with surveys. Or maybe the intuitions themselves are unstable and each side is cherry-picking which case to treat as datum and which to explain away.

So what I'm asking for is a diagnosis, not a verdict about who's right. Use what each side actually argues (not caricatures -- e.g. the purist's warranted-assertability maneuver, the encroacher's argument from reasoning and action, the contextualist's linguistic evidence like third-person denials), and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, empirical-in-disguise, different-questions-under-one-heading, or bedrock methodological intuitions neither side can argue the other out of. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress -- a distinction nobody's drawn, an experiment, a formal result, reframing the question -- or argue that we should expect no progress and say why. Please say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the specific claim in your answer you're least sure about.
