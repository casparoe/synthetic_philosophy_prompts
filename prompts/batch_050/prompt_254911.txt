Here's some background: I'm a retired chemist (thermodynamics, mostly) who got bored about two years into retirement and started reading moral philosophy. I've been chewing on Scanlon's contractualism -- the idea that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles nobody could reasonably reject -- and specifically the fight over whether it can aggregate. The usual story is that Scanlon says we have to compare individual complaints one at a time, so if I can save one person's life or save five strangers from some lesser harm, I can't just add up the five smaller burdens; each person's complaint stands alone. Critics say this gives absurd results when the numbers get big enough, defenders say aggregating already smuggles in the utilitarianism contractualism was supposed to avoid.

Here's my half-formed thought, and tell me if it's worth anything. Coming from measurement theory, it strikes me that the whole debate assumes complaints come with enough structure to be compared at all. If complaint-strength is only ordinal (this burden is worse than that one, full stop) then aggregation can't even get started, there's nothing to sum. But -- and this is the bit I can't get out of my head -- pairwise comparison is itself a kind of aggregation across people, and pairwise majority rules are famous for producing Condorcet-style cycles. So maybe the "no aggregation" position is less stable than it looks: either you smuggle in cardinal information about burdens (and then some limited aggregation might be principled rather than a betrayal) or you stick to pure pairwise ordinal comparisons and risk intransitive verdicts about what we ought to do.
