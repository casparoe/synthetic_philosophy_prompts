So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a profile of one of those startups that claims it will eventually do "destructive uploading" -- scan your brain down to the synapse, simulate it, compost the original. The founder keeps citing Parfit at me like that's a settled matter, and over drinks last week a philosopher I know walked me through an argument that she said shows the founder is right, and I wrote it down on a napkin because the conclusion can't possibly be right but I've been poking at it for days and cant find the loose thread. Here it is, reconstructed as best I can:

(1) In the teletransporter case, the person who steps out on Mars has all my memories, character, intentions, plans -- full psychological continuity (Parfit's Relation R) with the person who stepped in.
(2) In ordinary boring survival, all that connects me-now to me-in-thirty-years is that same Relation R, just carried by a brain instead of a radio signal. There is no extra "further fact" of identity in the everyday case that the Mars case lacks. So ordinary survival just is R continuing, and R is what matters.
(3) R doesn't require uniqueness. If two copies get made, each is equally R-related to me, and it would be arbitrary to say the relation "counts" when there's one copy but somehow stops counting when there's two.
(4) If R is what matters and R doesn't require uniqueness, then whether the continuant is this particular body can't itself matter. What matters is just that someone fully R-related to me goes on existing somewhere.
(5) So there's no difference that can matter to me between (a) this body waking up tomorrow and (b) this body being painlessly vaporized tonight while a perfect psychological continuant wakes up in its place.
(6) So a murderer who flips the switch that instantly reconstructs his victim -- same memories, same personality, victim's family never finds out -- does the victim no prudential harm whatsoever. At worst he does something like property damage to the rest of us.
(7) (6) is insane. Something in the chain is broken.

Here's my constraint, and it's the whole reason this matters for the article: I want to keep (1) and (2), because I want the piece to be able to say the uploading founder isn't obviously deluded -- I don't want to win the argument by insisting there's some magic further fact that uploads destroy, because honestly that move smells like vitalism to me. And obviously I want to keep (7). So I need to know which of (3), (4), (5) is the cheapest one to kill, or whether the rot is actually back in (2) and I have to swallow it. I've heard there are moves in the neighborhood -- the Lewis thing where the two copies are somehow both me because persons are worms with shared stages, something about "what matters" being ambiguous between what I have reason to care about and what is impersonally good, something about fission showing R was never the right relation to begin with -- but I don't know what each of them costs, and specifically which ones let me say uploading is survival AND murder-then-reconstruction is a harm, which is the combo I need. Diagnose the argument, tell me exactly where it goes wrong or which premiss I should pay to reject, and be honest with me if the combo I want isn't actually available.
