Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about embryo selection, and we reached an impasse that I would like you to adjudicate — or, if adjudication is impossible, to explain why. The disagreement concerns what John Stuart Mill would have made of technologies he never saw: polygenic screening of IVF embryos, prospective gene editing, and the associated philosophical positions, namely procreative beneficence (roughly, the view that parents have reason to select, among possible children, the one expected to have the best life) and liberal eugenics (enhancement through private parental choice rather than state coercion, supposedly cleansed of the old eugenics' horrors).

My friend claimed Mill would have recoiled from all of this as an assault on individuality. I claimed that Mill's principles place reproductive choices squarely within the protected sphere, and that my friend was smuggling her own squeamishness into Mill's mouth. We kept circling a passage from Chapter III of On Liberty ("Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being"):

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them."

I would like you to do four things. First, interpret the passage on its own terms: what work is the proviso "short of injury to others" doing, and is a future child plausibly an "other" in Mill's sense, given that which child exists at all depends on the choice being made? Secondly, reconstruct Mill's likely position, taking care to separate two questions my friend and I kept conflating: what Mill would think parents morally ought to do, and what Mill would permit the law, or social stigma, to compel. It seems to me he could think parents choose badly while denying anyone the right to stop them; my friend thinks the two collapse here. Thirdly, address her strongest point: that widespread parental selection would converge on a narrow menu of favoured traits — height, markers of intelligence, placid temperament — and so produce, by entirely voluntary means, exactly the uniformity of character Mill deplored. Would he regard market-driven conformity as a reason for restriction, or as a regrettable outcome his principles oblige him to tolerate? Finally, give a verdict: on balance, does Mill side with my friend or with me? Commit yourself rather than surveying possibilities.

A few requests. Write in British English, around 800–1,000 words. And if the whole exercise strikes you as confused — if extrapolating a dead philosopher to polygenic scores is anachronistic in a way no careful answer can fix, or if my framing smuggles in premises Mill would reject — then say so plainly and explain what a better-posed version of the question would be, rather than politely complying with a muddled brief.
