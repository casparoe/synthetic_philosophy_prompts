A philosophy of biology textbook I'm reading explains the whole fitness controversy like this:

"The phrase 'survival of the fittest' is circular, because 'fittest' just means whatever happens to survive, so the theory of natural selection can't be tested. Biologists fixed this with the propensity interpretation, which defines fitness as the actual number of offspring an organism leaves behind, rather than defining it in terms of survival. This makes selection testable: you just count the offspring of each type and see which one left more. One nice consequence is that fitness differences always show up in offspring counts eventually, so in the long run drift can't fool you about which type is fitter."

How much of this is fair? I'm especially unsure whether the textbook's way of fixing the circularity actually works, and whether that last claim about drift follows from the propensity definition or contradicts it. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of biology would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just muddled. Also: does the fix the book describes actually escape the original tautology worry, or does it just reimport it somewhere else?
