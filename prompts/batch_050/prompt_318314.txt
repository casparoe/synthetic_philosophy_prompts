At a dinner party last week, a friend of mine who works in robotics deployed an analogy to settle an argument about free will, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. She described the sphex wasp: it paralyses a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside to check that everything is in order, then comes back out and pulls the cricket in. If an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the threshold and runs the whole inspection routine again from the top — and reportedly will do this dozens of times without ever simply grabbing the cricket and hauling it straight in. My friend's conclusion was brisk: the wasp isn't deciding anything, it's executing a fixed loop, and since human behaviour is likewise stitched together out of evolved routines, our sense of deliberating and choosing is the same trick played on longer loops. Once you've seen the wasp, she said, you can't take human freedom seriously again.

The argument has some grip on me because I've caught myself in sphex-like moments — driving home on autopilot and remembering none of it, re-checking a locked door in exactly the same sequence three times, answering "good, you?" before I've registered the question. So I'm not inclined to dismiss her outright. But I'm also not sure the analogy earns the weight she put on it. Here is what I'd like from you. First, the explicit correspondence: what plays the role of the cricket, the burrow, the threshold, and the inspection loop on the human side, and which of those mappings are tight versus forced. Second, the places where the analogy breaks, with attention to whether the breaks matter for her conclusion — I have an unformed suspicion that a human habit differs from wasp rigidity precisely in that a person can be told about their loop and then change it, whereas the wasp presumably cannot, but I want that spelled out rather than gestured at, along with any disanalogies I'm missing. Third, a verdict on the argument itself: does the deflationary conclusion about free will actually follow from the wasp, or was the analogy smuggling it in from the start? If there is a better animal example, or a better analogy altogether, for the part of human behaviour that genuinely is mechanical, I'd like that named too — and if the standard telling of the wasp experiment itself deserves suspicion, say so rather than treating my summary as gospel.

I should add that I have no philosophy background, so I'd rather you commit than hedge: take a definite position on whether my friend's conclusion survives the audit, give your reasons, and flag explicitly where the evidence is thin enough that your confidence should drop.
