I cover AI for a magazine, and I keep tripping over the word "hallucination" that everyone in the industry uses for when a chatbot confidently makes stuff up. Look at how the practice actually works: labs publish "hallucination rates" scored as simple accuracy percentages, they fine-tune models to produce fewer false statements, and every product ships with a disclaimer like "may produce inaccurate information." The whole setup treats model outputs as claims that pass or fail a truth check.

But I've been re-reading Harry Frankfurt's On Bullshit, and there's a moment where he says: "It is a further question whether there are any features essential to humbug or to lying that are not dependent upon the intentions and beliefs of the person responsible for the humbug or the lie." That seems like a problem. Frankfurt's whole distinction between lying, bullshit, and honest error runs through the speaker's state of mind, and it's at best unclear whether a language model has the relevant states of mind at all.

So here's my question: what theory of assertion does the industry's way of measuring and policing "hallucination" actually presuppose? Does grading outputs purely on truth value quietly assume the model is something like a Frankfurt-style bullshitter, something like a liar, or something his framework just doesn't cover? Reconstruct the implicit view, tell me whether it's coherent given the passage, and say what the practice would have to look like if we took seriously that these systems don't have intentions or beliefs in the relevant sense — would "hallucination" still be the right concept, or would the labs have to do something different entirley?
