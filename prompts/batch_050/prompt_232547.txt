Having followed the consciousness threads on LessWrong and its predecessors for years, I've noticed that disputes about first-person methods usually degenerate because neither side states what would count as discharging the burden of proof. Here is the passage I keep coming back to. In "Quining Qualia" (1988), Daniel Dennett writes:

"I want to shift the burden of proof, so that anyone who wants to appeal to private, subjective properties has to prove first that in so doing they are not making a mistake. This status of guilty until proven innocent is neither unprecedented nor indefensible (so long as we restrict ourselves to concepts)."

As I read it, this is the same methodological stance Dennett develops under the label heterophenomenology: subjects' avowals are raw material for a third-person theory, not evidence about private phenomenal items. Consider a hard case for that stance. An experienced meditator in a laboratory study reports that during a specific interval there was awareness with no content whatsoever -- no imagery, no bodily sense, no passage of time. Unlike untrained subjects, her reports are stable across many repetitions and correlate with a reproducible neural signature. She argues that contemplative training has made her a calibrated instrument, so her report should enjoy the evidential standing of a trained radiologist's reading of a scan, rather than being demoted to a text awaiting interpretation.

Write a dialogue between a heterophenomenologist and a defender of disciplined first-person method, in the spirit of Varela's neurophenomenology, disputing what Dennett's passage licenses in this case. Both must be maximally competent; neither may be a strawman, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than rehearse slogans. The dialogue should turn at least once on a contested reading of the quoted passage -- for instance, whether "guilty until proven innocent" is a defeasible evidential rule that the meditator's track record might discharge, or a stipulation about what may count as evidence at all, which no track record could. It need not end in agreement, but it should end somewhere determinate: either a residual disagreement both parties can state in one sentence each, or an equivocation they jointly uncover.
