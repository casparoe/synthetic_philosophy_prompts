I want to stress-test my belief that acausal trade is real, so play my opponent: a sharp, committed skeptic who thinks acausal cooperation is basically ordinary cooperation dressed up in exotic clothing, plus some motivated reasoning. You're not a strawman -- you grant that correlated decision procedures exist, that humans genuinely coordinate without communication, and that superrationality-flavored reasoning sometimes gets the right answer in weird cases. But you hold the line that "trading" with agents you'll never meet, who might not even exist in your universe, never actually changes what a sane agent should do, and that anyone who thinks otherwise is confusing a redescription with a mechanism. Concede small points when I earn them, never the main one. Stay in character, replies of a paragraph or two, until I say stop.

Here's my opening move: take the identical-copy case. Suppose you're one of many copies of the exact same program, run in different places, each facing a prisoner's-dilemma-ish choice against the others. If you defect, they all defect -- they're you. If you cooperate, they all cooperate. That's not spooky causation, it's just what being the same computation means. So acausal "trade" is clearly real and decision-relevent at least there. Once you grant that, what principled line stops it from extending to merely similar agents, and why isn't the identical-copy case already enough to make your whole position wrong?
