I run a small interview series for my university's philosophy society, and our next guest is an epistemologist who defends a "blindspot" resolution of the surprise exam paradox. Roughly, her view is that the teacher's announcement -- there will be an exam on one day next week, but you won't know on the evening before which day it is -- is not contradictory, self-refuting, or false. Instead, she says it is a proposition that may well be true but that the students are structurally unable to know at the relevant time, because their knowing it would require retaining knowledge across nights in a way the backward-induction reasoning itself undermines. The paradox dissolves, on her account, once we stop assuming that whatever is true can in principle come to be known by the parties involved.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions, arranged in the order I should ask them, that push past a restatement of the view. A few pressure points I want covered, in whatever order you think flows best: why the blindspot diagnosis is preferable to the simpler claim that the announcement is self-undermining and hence can't be known; what the view says about the one-day case, where the teacher announces there will be a surprise exam tomorrow and the backward induction never gets started; whether the resolution forces her to deny that knowledge is automatically retained from one evening to the next, and if so, whether that's an independent commitment or just a restatement of the conclusion; and whether her account still works when the teacher strengthens the announcement by adding that she knows the students know she is scrupulously truthful. For each question, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left flat-footed when she responds.

For the closer, the grad students in the society devised a variant I want to put to her: the teacher announces that the exam day will be chosen by lottery from among the five days, that the students will be told the exam is happening only when the proctor walks in, and that the teacher will bet a large sum, at even odds, that no student will be able to correctly announce the exam day on the morning of. The students all decline to bet, the exam happens Wednesday, and the teacher collects. I'd like the final question to ask whether the students' collective refusal to bet shows they lacked knowledge or merely lacked confidence -- that is, whether the blindspot view can distinguish the two -- with a follow-up pressing her on what observation, if any, would count against her diagnosis rather than being absorbed by it.
