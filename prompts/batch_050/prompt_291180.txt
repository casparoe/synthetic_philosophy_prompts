I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a long feature on thought experiments — Galileo dropping balls of different weights, Einstein chasing a beam of light, Schrödinger's cat, Maxwell's demon, and so on. Here's the puzzle I want the piece to confront. Philosophers of science have argued for decades about how thought experiments can generate knowledge: no new empirical data comes in, yet somehow we seem to learn something about the physical world. The main positions are well established — that thought experiments are really just arguments with the logical work done by the premises, that they're a priori windows into the structure of nature, that they work by tapping the same tacit physical intuitions that guide ordinary practical reasoning — and as far as I can tell, none of these camps has conceded anything to the others in thirty years. Everyone knows the same canonical cases and the same objections.

My working hypothesis is that the debate isn't really about thought experiments at all. It's a proxy war about the legitimacy of armchair methods in science generally, and it stays stuck because the two sides apply different standards for what would count as explaining how a method works. But I could be wrong: maybe there's a genuine hidden disagreement — about what intuitions are, or about whether the famous cases actually produced knowledge in the first place — doing all the work.

Take a definite stand. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive-but-intractable, or two camps answering different questions under one label — and commit to a view of why it has stalled, with reasons drawn from how the positions are actually argued. Then commit to a view of what would count as progress: a specific distinction, a case study, a result from cognitive science, or an argument that nothing will resolve it and that the field should stop trying. I'd rather be convinced by a committed position I end up disagreeing with than given a neutral survey of the terrain I already know.
