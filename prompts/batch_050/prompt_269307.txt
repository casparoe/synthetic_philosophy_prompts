ok so background first: I'm a PhD student in political theory (dissertation on whether liberal neutrality presupposes value comparability, long story) and I've spent two years circling the hard choices literature, mostly Chang-style stuff about parity. Here's roughly where I've landed and I want to know if the package hangs together before I build a chapter on it:

1. Hard choices are real. Some options -- career as a musician vs. career as a lawyer, staying in your city vs. moving for a partner -- are neither better, worse, nor equal, but on a par. Parity is a fourth, sui generis comparative relation, not ignorance about which is better and not just rough equality.

2. Parity is robust to small sweetenings. If the musician's life and the lawyer's life are on a par, then the lawyer's life plus a slightly nicer office is still on a par with the musician's life. Otherwise parity is knife-edge thin and collapses the moment anything changes, so it does no explanatory work.

3. I'm a voluntarist about what to do in hard cases: when reasons run out, you can throw your agency behind one option -- commit to the musician's life -- and thereby make it the right one for you. That's what self-authorship is, and it's a feature not a bug that you could have done the same for law.

4. Regret after a hard choice you opted into is never fitting. If the options were on a par and nothing went wrong, there's nothing for regret to be about (grief over the road not taken is fine, that's different).

5. I'm against epistemicism about comparability: it's not that one option is secretly better and we just can't tell. Parity is out there in the values themselves, determinate -- for any two options there is a fact about which comparative relation holds between them.

Here's what I want: act as a value theorist who is sympathetic to the general program but ruthless about internal consistency.
