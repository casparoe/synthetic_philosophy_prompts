My roomate from college is getting married next month and I'm giving the toast. She's a metaethicist, a committed expressivist, and her dissertation was on the Frege-Geach problem — roughly, if saying "lying is wrong" is really just expressing disapproval of lying, then what are you doing when you say "if lying is wrong, then lying to spare someone's feelings is wrong"? You aren't disapproving of anything in the antecedent. She's marrying a moral realist who thinks value facts are as solid as physical facts, and half the guests will be their department colleagues who catch every move, while the other half are aunts and cousins who just want something warm. Write me a five-minute toast that lands for both halves. The part I actually care about: take the Frege-Geach problem seriously instead of name-dropping it, so every normative claim the toast needs — that these vows matter, that these two are good for each other — gets phrased in a way my roomate could sincerely say herself given what she thinks moral talk is doing, while the groom still hears it as straight talk about what's true. It occured to me the vows themselves might be a way in, since "I do" was never a description of anything, but use that or don't. Continuous prose only, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — I'm reading this out loud, not presenting it.
