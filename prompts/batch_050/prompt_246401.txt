I serve as the volunteer secretary of a neighborhood association, and part of my job is running our decision meetings. We are about to vote on what to do with a vacant plot the association has acquired, and there are three serious proposals on the table: turning it into a community orchard, building a small playground, or paving it for extra parking. Last year, when we voted on a different matter by simple show of hands for one favorite, I watched several members openly announce they would vote for their second choice because their true favorite "had no chance," and the winner was something most people privately ranked last. This year I introduced a ranked ballot where we eliminate the least popular option and reassign its votes, and I was proud of it until a member cheerfully explained to me at the pub that she had ranked her true second choice last, because doing so knocked it out early and cleared the way for her favorite. She had voted against her own honest preferences, and it worked, and I could not tell her she had broken any rule.

What troubles me is that I had assumed the problem with our old system was just a flaw in that particular method, and that with enough cleverness someone could design a procedure where no one ever gains by misrepresenting what they actually want. I have spent two weekends sketching alternatives on paper -- points systems, pairwise comparisons, elimination orders -- and every single one I invent seems to have some scenario I can construct where a member who knows how others will vote can profit by lying on their ballot. I even considered extreme fixes: one idea was to have each member vote for their least favorite rather than their favorite, another was to let the chair simply decide (which at least makes ballots irrelevant, but is obviously unacceptable for other reasons). I am starting to suspect the difficulty is not in my ingenuity but in the problem itself, though I cannot tell whether that is a deep truth or just a failure of imagination on my part.

So my practical question is this: should I keep searching for a method where honesty is always the best strategy, or is that a fool's errand, in which case the real question becomes how to choose among methods that are all vulnerable to manipulation? If the latter, what should I be comparing -- how often manipulation is possible, how much coordination it requires, how bad the outcome is when it succeeds? I would also welcome hearing whether the answer changes depending on the kind of choice being made, since I have a vague sense that voting on, say, the date of a meeting feels different from voting among three genuinely unlike proposals, but I cannot articulate why. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would in a letter -- no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
