I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a recurring part of my beat is conflict reporting: campus disputes, online pile-ons, defamation suits, workplace grievances, and fights over the boundaries of academic fields. Sociologists describe a rough historical sequence of moral cultures. In honor cultures, reputation is a scarce good that must be defended personally, and an unanswered insult is a real loss. In dignity cultures, worth is presumed inherent, insults are to be shrugged off or handled privately, and serious disputes are routed to third parties such as courts or HR departments. In what some sociologists call victimhood cultures, people appeal to third parties precisely on the basis of their victim status, and public demonstrations of sensitivity to slight and oppression carry moral weight rather than shame.

I want you to turn this framework into a working classification procedure I can actually run: an ordered set of tests, or a flowchart in words, that takes the raw material of my reporting (what the parties say, what they demand, whom they appeal to, what they treat as unforgivable) and outputs a defensible classification of a conflict as honor-, dignity-, or victimhood-structured, including mixed cases, transitional cases, and cases where a culture's repertoire is being used strategically rather than sincerely.

Constraints on the deliverable. I write roughly two conflict stories a month; I can dedicate at most one hour of analysis per story beyond ordinary reporting; my editor and a lawyer sign off on characterizations, so every classification must come with the specific evidence that supports it. The procedure must handle at least these hard cases: participants using dignity-culture vocabulary (procedures, rights, due process) strategically inside what is really an honor conflict; institutions that are simultaneously the arena of the dispute and the third party being appealed to; conflicts that cross subcultures, so the same act reads as an insult in one milieu and as a microaggression in another; and bad-faith performances of victim status. For each step, state explicitly whether it is mechanical or a judgment call, and specify what evidence would falsify a tentative classification rather than merely complicate it.

Apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases of your construction, chosen to exercise different branches. One of the three must be a case where the procedure yields a classification I will find uncomfortable in print -- for instance, classifying the conduct of a sympathetic complainant, whom readers will instinctively side with, as operating on honor logic.

Close with a section on what the procedure cannot do. In particular: does any defensible move run from this descriptive classification to the conclusion that one of the three cultures is morally superior, or does such a conclusion always require additional normative premises that the sociology cannot supply? If the latter, list what those premises would have to look like, so that I can recognize when a source is quietly smuggling a moral verdict out of a descriptive taxonomy -- and so that I never do it myself.
