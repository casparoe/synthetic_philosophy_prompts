I’ve been volunteering with a zoo conservation breeding program and got suprised by how the staff talk about fitness. The pairing software sometimes recommends animals that are expected to have fewer surviving offspring in the next year, because they are genetically underrepresented in the captive population, and the managers still describe those pairings as maximizing the fitness of the population. In one case a recommended pair produced no cubs at all because of a random infection, but the annual report still classified the pairing as a high-fitness choice. Meanwhile a closely related pair that happened to produce six healthy cubs was treated as a kind of fitness loss for the population, because it made future inbreeding worse. Nobody there thinks they are contradicting themselves.

What conception of fitness does this practice presuppose? Does it have to treat fitness as a propensity, like an expected number of descendants under certain environmental and genetic conditions, rather than as actual reproductive output? And does the propensity have to attach to a lineage, genotype, or breeding policy over several generations rather than to an individual animal in a single season? I’m especially curious whether this lets the program answer the old tautology objection to evolutionary fitness: if fittest just means whatever ends up leaving the most descendants, then claims about fitness look empty, but if the probabilities are estimated from pedigree, survival data, and models of inbreeding depression, maybe the practice is committed to fitness being predictive and explanatory rather than merely retrospective. Reconstruct the implicit theory, say what the practice strictly presupposes versus what is just a convenient modeling choice, and tell me what would have to change if fitness meant only actual descendants produced.
