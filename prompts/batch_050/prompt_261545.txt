Act as a philosopher of mathematics who takes Benacerraf's dilemma seriously but isn't doctrinaire about how to solve it. Think carefully about this one — I'd suggest brainstorming the full space of demands people put on a philosophy of math first, and only then writing up the polished list.

Background: I keep seeing the standard philosophies of mathematics (platonism, Field-style fictionalism, structuralism, good old if-thenism/deductivism) get knocked down by opponents wielding Benacerraf at them, and I want the spec before the scorecard. As I understand the dilemma: an adequate account of math should give mathematical statements the same kind of truth conditions as ordinary statements (which pushes toward treating numerals as referring to objects), but it should also make mathematical knowledge explicable for embodied, causally embedded knowers like us (which pushes away from causally inert abstracta) — and satisfying one horn seems to wreck the other.

So: write the desiderata that any adequate philosophy of mathematics must meet, in light of the dilemma. Beyond the two horns themselves, I want you to consider at least the following candidate requirements and argue whether each one genuinely earns its place on the list:

- the theory must make sense of episodes where mathematicians apparently gained real knowledge from theories later found inconsistent or unrigorous — naive set theory, the early infinitesimal calculus — rather than retroactively declaring that it was never knowledge at all
- it must handle unsurveyable proofs (computer-checked ones, say) where no single human grasps the whole argument
- it must explain why results transfer across foundations — why we say it's *the same* theorem whether proved in ZFC or in a category-theoretic setup
- it must handle mixed and applied statements (like claims about how many moons Jupiter has) within one semmantics rather than a patchwork
- it must be at least roughly compatible with how children and ordinary non-mathematicians actually acquire arithmetical competence

For each requirement on your final list: give the case or consideration that motivates it, say whether it's genuinely binding or just a prejudice of one camp, and mark it negotiable or non-negotiable. Then the two things I care most about: which single desideratum does the most work in excluding contenders, and whether any pair of desiderata on the list is jointly unsatisfiable — if the spec itself forces a trade-off, that is the result I most want to see. Only after all that, briefly score the big four against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring.
