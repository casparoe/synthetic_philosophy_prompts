I teach philosophy of biology, and in preparing a seminar on the species problem I have been reconsidering the well-known thesis, associated with Michael Ghiselin and David Hull, that species are individuals rather than natural kinds. The analogy runs roughly as follows: a particular horse is not a member of its species in the way a sample of gold is an instance of the kind gold; rather, the horse is a part of the species, as a cell is a part of a body. The species, like the organism, is a historically bounded, spatiotemporally restricted entity: it comes into existence (speciation as a kind of birth), persists through change, and goes out of existence (extinction as death). Defenders put the analogy to real argumentative work. Because an individual organism can change nearly all of its intrinsic properties over its lifetime without ceasing to be itself, the analogy is taken to show that species need no shared intrinsic essence either; and because there are no laws of nature about Socrates, only about the kinds he falls under, it is taken to show that there can be no laws about species, so that the search for an essentialist species definition was misconceived from the start.

Please audit this analogy. First, set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of parts, birth, death, persistence through change, and so forth (a table of correspondences is acceptable). Second, identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the argumentative uses just described, as opposed to those that merely record that species and organisms are different sorts of thing: organisms exhibit functional integration and tight causal cohesion among their parts; organismal fission does not normally force the conclusion that the parent ceased to exist, whereas on some views an ancestral species does cease to exist when it splits; an organism's parts typically cannot outlive it, while the fate of a species' populations is messier. Third, say which features of organismhood the analogy quietly imports into the species case — I suspect strong cohesion and sharp boundaries — and ask whether the anti-essentialist and no-laws conclusions genuinely require full individuality, or only the weaker claim that species are historical entities held together by descent rather than by shared intrinsic properties. Finally, give your verdict on how far the analogy holds and what, if anything, it entitles its defenders to conclude. State also what would change your mind: which disanalogy, if it turned out to cut deeper than you judge, or which feature of species, if it resembled organismhood more closely than you allow, would shift your verdict. No more than 900 words, in British English, with your verdict stated plainly rather than hedged into invisibility.
