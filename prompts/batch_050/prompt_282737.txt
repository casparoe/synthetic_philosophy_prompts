I host a monthly book club for a bunch of friends who mostly aren't academics (two software engineers, a nurse, a retired schoolteacher, one linguistics grad student who keeps us honest). We're working through Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, and I drew the short straw for the opening session, where I have to get across the book's central thesis: that analogy-making isn't one mental tool among many but the core engine of cognition, and that categorization and analogy are actually the same process operating at different scales. We get about 40 minutes before people start bringing up their own anecdotes and the wine wins.

Don't explain the thesis to me -- I've read the chapter twice. Instead, plan how I should teach it. What's the right order of ideas, given that most of the room will arrive thinking categorization is the boring, well-behaved process (match things against a definition) and analogy is the occasional poetic flourish you use in speeches? I suspect I need to make everyday categorization feel strange and effortful BEFORE claiming it's analogical, but tell me if you think the order should go the other way. Suggest one carrying example to build the whole session around -- I'm tempted by the ordinary "put your cup on the table" scenario and the endless borderline cases it hides, but argue for an alternative if you think there's a better one (and say what the example needs to have and conspicuously lack). Also map the misconceptions I should expect: that analogy means those old SAT-style proportional puzzles, that analogies are deliberate and slow while categories are automatic, that treating thought as analogy-making makes thinking sound sloppy and unreliable, and that this is all just a claim about language rather than perception and action. For each one, say where in the sequence I should head it off and with what move. Then give me one closing question or mini-exercise that would show me the idea actually landed, plus one thing from the book I should openly refuse to get into with this crowd (I'm guessing the Einstein material).

Keep the whole plan under 800 words, and structure it as the order of the session, not as an essay about the book.
