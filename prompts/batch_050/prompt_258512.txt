So I'm on the mod team of a mid-sized online community, and for the last few months we've been rewriting our rules, mostly around the concept of civility. The old rule just said be civil and it was a disaster -- everyone agreed civility was good, nobody agreed what it was, enforcement was pure vibes. So we eventually stopped asking what civility really means and started asking what we want the concept to do for us (predictable enforcement, not punishing blunt wording when the content is fine, etc.) and built a definition around that.

Then someone on the team found the actual philosophy literature on this move -- Carnap's explication, Haslanger's ameliorative analysis, the whole conceptual engineering thing -- and also the pushback: Strawson's old complaint that if you replace a concept with a more useful one you've just changed the subject, plus the more recent worry that if the two sides end up using different concepts they're not even disagreeing anymore, just talking past each other. And now our internal debate has completely stalled. Half the team thinks we fixed the concept. The other half thinks we started talking about something else entirely and the original question about civility is still sitting there unanswered.

What I want is a diagnosis of the philosophical version of this fight, because I'm pretty sure ours is just a small copy of it. Is the disagreement between conceptual engineers and their critics actually substantive, or is it a verbal dispute about what analysing a concept is even supposed to deliver? More specifically: is the changing-the-subject objection resting on a factual claim (that engineered concepts really do fail to address the original questions people had), or on a normative claim about what inquiry is for, or on a picture of concepts as fixed targets that the engineers just reject outright? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Is there any real test for whether an engineered concept preserves enough of the old one to count as an answer rather than a topic switch -- continuity of inferential role, of paradigm cases, of the practical problems it was meant to solve -- or is that question itself ill-posed? And be honest about whether the did-we-change-the-subject worry is just overrated in cases like ours where the goal was practical from day one, because I suspect it might be but I can't tell if that's motivated reasoning.
