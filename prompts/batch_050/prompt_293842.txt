I'm on the governance committee of a community land trust in a mid-sized city -- about 380 member-households, mostly people who bought their units through the trust at below-market prices. We have to run a binding membership vote this fall on whether to sell the ground floor of our main building to a private medical clinic. The sale would fund repairs the whole membership needs, but it would also roughly triple car traffic on the block, probably push two small storefront tenants out when their leases renew, and, honestly, reduce the amount of affordable space the trust can offer in future, which matters because we have a waitlist of around 600 households, some waiting seven-plus years.

Here's the fight we're having in committee, and it's turned out to be way more philosophical than I expected when I volunteered. Who gets to vote in the referendum? Current members say it's obviously us -- we're the ones on the hook financially, it's our asset, our bylaws say one member one vote. But the storefront tenants and the other renters on the block will live with the traffic and the rent pressure and get no say at all; the waitlist households have their future stake in the trust diminished and no say either; and a couple of committee members keep arguing that the people the trust was *founded to serve* -- low-income households who aren't members yet and mostly never will be -- are exactly the ones a decision like this should answer to. When someone on the committee proposed letting affected non-members vote, the treasurer shot back that there's no principled place to stop: why not the whole neighborhood, the whole city, everyone whose housing market we're technically a part of? I actually couldn't give a great answer to that, and I've been chewing on it for weeks.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before our September meeting. It should recommend a specific electorate for this referendum -- who votes, who doesn't, and how we'd actually verify eligibility -- plus whatever process accommodations (a consultative hearing, a weighted advisory ballot, a supermajority threshold, whatever you think) make sense for those excluded. I want the memo to pick a principle for drawing the line and show its work, because "everyone affected gets a say" and "only members get a say" both sound fine in the abstract and both seem to go crazy at the edges. Give the strongest version of the treasurer's reductio before answering it, and be honest about whether your recommended line is principled or just a defensible place to stop. One to two pages, something the committee can actually vote to adopt.
