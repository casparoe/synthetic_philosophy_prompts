Last week two friends of mine -- one a third-year medical student, the other a philosophy graduate student -- got into an argument after a news segment about organ transplantation, and it went well past where I could follow. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

It started with Mina defending brain death as genuinely death: death, she said, is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, and since the brain is the integrator, total brain failure is death even if the heart still beats on a ventilator. Daniel replied that this fails empirically: ventilated brain-dead bodies maintain temperature and fluid balance, heal wounds, mount immune responses, and in rare documented cases gestate pregnancies for weeks -- that is a living organism by any biological standard. What brain death actually and irreversibly ends, he said, is any possibility of consciousness, and we should be honest that the criterion protects the interests that matter morally rather than pretending the organism has died.

Mina countered that if consciousness is the real criterion, then patients in a persistent vegetative state and anencephalic infants would also count as dead, and treating them as corpses is monstrous -- so the brain-death criterion at least draws a defensible biological line that avoids that result. Daniel said that was an appeal to consequences, not a criterion, and then raised controlled donation after circulatory death: patients are declared dead a few minutes after circulation stops, yet in some protocols the heart is later perfused and restarted inside a different body. If circulation can be restarted, he argued, the donor was never irreversibly dead in the first place. Mina answered that "irreversible" here means the circulation will not be restored, not that it could not be; spontaneous resumption after the observation period essentially never happens. Daniel's final move: then whether the patient counts as dead depends on what the surgical team intends to do next -- the same physiology is death if no one intervenes and not death if a surgeon is standing by -- and a fact like death shouldn't be a decision.

Please go through the exchange step by step: for each move, say whether it's sound, confused, or partly right, and what the strongest version of each side's position is. In doing so, distinguish carefully between what is well established (the relevant physiology, what current legal criteria actually say) and what remains genuinely contested among clinicians, bioethicists, and philosophers (whole-brain versus consciousness-based accounts, the permanence-versus-irreversibility reading) -- don't present either side's contested commitments as settled consensus. Finally, say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
