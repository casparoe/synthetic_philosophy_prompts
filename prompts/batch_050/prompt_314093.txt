I'm in a second-year course on early modern philosophy and we've just spent two weeks on Leibniz's theodicy, and now my prof has assigned Candide as a "supplementary text" with an essay to go with it. The obvious reading is that Voltaire is dunking on Leibniz: Pangloss keeps insisting everything happens for the best in the best of all possible worlds while he gets hanged, whipped, and half the cast drowns in the Lisbon earthquake, so clearly optimism is absurd. But our course drilled into us that Leibniz's actual claim is subtler than Pangloss's slogan — God doesn't create a perfect world, he creates the best *possible* one, where goods can be incompossible and permitting some evil is unavoidable if you want certain goods at all. So my essay question is basically: does Voltaire actually refute Leibniz, or does he only skewer a pop version of him?

Here's what I want you to do. Read Candide through Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds argument, taking both sides seriously. On the one side: where does the satire genuinely bite against the real doctrine and not just the caricature? (I'm thinking of the Lisbon earthquake episode especially — Leibniz would presumably say the earthquake's badness is bound up with goods that couldn't exist without the physical laws that also produce earthquakes, and Voltaire seems to just... not engage with that kind of move at all.) On the other side: the El Dorado episode fascinates me — Candide finds a world that is basically Pangloss's dream, peaceful and rich and without suffering, and he *leaves voluntarily* because it's boring and he wants Cunégonde. Doesn't that scene weirdly vindicate something Leibnizian, i.e., that a world with less evil isn't automatically a world with more good, and that what we actually value involves risk, striving, loss? And the ending, where everyone is miserable and the answer is to cultivate your garden — is that a counter-theodicy, a retreat into quietism, or actually a conclusion Leibniz could live with?

Push the reading as far as it honestly goes, and say where Candide resists being read as philosophy at all — like, can a satire with characters as thin as Pangloss even count as an argument against a metaphysical position, or is it doing something else (rhetorical, emotional, political) that argument can't answer? I want a verdict, not both-sides mush: does Voltaire beat Leibniz, miss him, or accidentally end up agreeing with him more than he intended? Defend one and justify it against the text. No need for citations or secondary literature, just the reading itself.
