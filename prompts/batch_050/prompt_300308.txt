So I've been thinking about the cogito lately and I've basically talked myself into the Lichtenberg reading: when Descartes says "I think therefore I am", the most he's actually entitled to is that a thought is happening right now, in this very token of thinking. Not that *I* am doing it, not that there's a persisting subject behind the thought, not even that there's a thinker distinct from the thought itself. The "I" in "I think" is doing grammatical work, not ontological work -- language forces a subject-slot on us and Descartes filled it in with a substance without noticing the move. And once you deflate it like that, the cogito becomes pretty much useless as a foundation for anything: you can't build a self, a soul, or a theory of mind on the bare datum that thinking is occuring.

Here's my question: what would count *against* this deflationary reading? And I want this done honestly, not as a list of generic pro-Descartes talking points. Specifically:

1. Which arguments for a subject over and above the thought actually survive contact with the deflationary reading, and which ones just smuggle the "I" back in through the same grammatical trick?
2. What cases or phenomena would the deflationary view struggle to even *describe*? (I'm thinking about things like: whose thought is it when a thought occurs, can there be ownerless thoughts at all, what distinguishes my episode of thinking from yours if not a subject?)
3. If Kant-style arguments work -- that the mere possibility of attaching "I think" to representations requires some unity that isn't itself just another representation -- does that defeat the deflationary reading, or just force it to shrink its claim?
4. Where does the deflationary reading itself become unfalsifiable or merely verbal? Like, is there a real disagreement here between "there is a subject" and "there is only thinking", or are both sides saying the same thing in different vocabularies?

Rank the defeaters from the ones that would actually sink the view to the ones that are just awkward for it. And be upfront about your own verdict: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and which single argument or consideration, if it turned out to hold, would flip you to the other side.
