I have no academic background in biology or philosophy, but I recently heard DNA described as a code, and two friends of mine came to opposite conclusions about whether that description is literal.

Amara argues that DNA is literally a code. A DNA sequence is built from a small alphabet; consecutive triplets are systematically associated with particular amino acids; and changing a triplet can predictably change the resulting protein. To her, this has the essential features of a code: discrete symbols, combinatorial rules, and a mapping from one representation to another. She also says that the association between triplets and amino acids is not simply the result of each triplet physically attracting its amino acid. Cellular machinery mediates the association. If the relevant transfer molecules were altered so that a triplet now led to a different amino acid, the same DNA sequence would be “read” differently. She thinks this is analogous to changing the key to a cipher: the symbols remain, but the decoding convention changes. Since codes need decoders anyway, the dependence on cellular machinery does not bother her.

Jonah answers that this proves too much. A thermostat reliably maps temperature onto furnace activity, a lock maps a key’s shape onto mechanical movement, and a mold maps its contours onto the shape of a cast object, but none of these mappings alone makes the first thing a coded representation of the second. In his view, a genuine code requires convention or meaning: something must stand for something else, and it must be possible to distinguish a correct decoding from a misreading. DNA, he says, participates directly in chemical processes. Calling those processes “reading,” “translating,” or “interpreting” is useful but still metaphorical.

Amara replies that correctness really does apply. If the cellular machinery incorporates an amino acid contrary to the established triplet association, that is a translation error, not merely a different chemical event. She also distinguishes that kind of error from a harmful mutation: the machinery may translate a mutated sequence perfectly while producing a protein that harms the organism. According to her, chemistry alone cannot explain that distinction, since chemically unusual events are not automatically errors. She thinks natural selection can supply the needed standard without requiring a conscious code-maker.

Jonah responds that “correct” there means only that a process conforms to a historically successful biological pattern. A river that changes course is not making an error, and an enzyme that behaves differently is not misreading anything unless we already describe its function using human purposes. The word “error” therefore borrows the appearance of meaning rather than showing that the DNA sequence itself has meaning.

Amara then offers another case. Suppose someone deliberately designs a DNA sequence so that the initial letters of the resulting amino acids spell a message. Jonah says this only shows that humans can use DNA as a medium for a code, just as they can arrange stones to spell a sentence; it does not show that ordinary genes are messages. Amara answers that ordinary genes differ only in who or what supplied the mapping. If selection can make a structure have the function of indicating or directing something, then an intentional designer is unnecessary.

Finally, Jonah argues that a DNA sequence by itself does not determine what a cell makes: the cellular environment affects whether and how it is transcribed, processed, and translated. Amara agrees but says that no code operates without a larger system. Jonah says that if the code is only the sequence together with the whole decoding system, then calling the DNA itself the code is misleading.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Who is right about the altered-decoding thought experiment, the appeal to translation errors, the distinction between a harmful mutation and a decoding mistake, and the deliberately message-bearing DNA sequence? Explain whether the words “code,” “information,” and “instruction” must stand or fall together here. Then give an overall verdict: is DNA literally a code, only metaphorically one, or is the dispute caused by an ambiguity that neither friend adequately states? If your answer depends on a definition, provide the definition and apply it rather than merely saying that both sides are right in different senses.
