Write a dialogue between two philosophers who've both spent a sabbatical embedded in an AI interpretability lab, and who now disagree about what their time there showed. One is an eliminative materialist about beliefs and desires: she thinks folk psychology's posits are like 'phlogiston' or 'the humors', and that the lab work confirms it — because when you actually crack open a capable language model, you don't find discrete, sentence-like stored beliefs that cause behavior the way folk psychology says beliefs do. You find smeared-out, superposed features that do different jobs in different contexts, and "the model believes X" is at best a sloppy compression. Her friend is a realist about propositional attitudes who draws the opposite lesson from the same experience: probing and causal-intervention techniques let you localize and manipulate something that behaves remarkably like a belief — you can find where a false "belief" lives, flip it, and watch the downstream behavior change coherently — and he thinks this shows that belief-talk was always tracking real, causally efficacious structure, just not where introspection said it was.

Both of them should be genuinely competent: the eliminativist has to deal seriously with the intervention results (you really can patch a representation and reliably change what the model outputs about, say, the capital of France), and the realist has to deal seriously with the messiness (the "same" feature does different things in different prompts, localization is fuzzy, and the probes partly create what they find). Neither should be a strawman and neither should win cleanly. They should actually respond to each other's arguments — when one makes a point about causal intervention, the other should grapple with it, not pivot to a rehearsed speech.

Somewhere in the middle, have them hit the crux: is the disagreement about whether models "really have beliefs" an empirical disagreement that interpretability will eventually settle, or a disagreement about what beliefs were supposed to be in the first place — and does it matter for that question whether the thing under discussion is a neural net or a human brain? Also have at least one of them notice, in the flow of argument, the awkward point that the eliminativist is describing her own view using belief-talk ("I think the lab work shows...") and deal with that honestly rather than as a gotcha that ends the debate.

Keep it to maybe 1500-2000 words, naturalistic dialogue, no lecture-format paragraphs where each person just gives their canned position.
