I sit on a national historical commission charged with approving the official wording for a memorial and a secondary-school curriculum unit on our country's 1940s famine, in which roughly one in twelve of the population died. The commission is deadlocked between two drafts: one describes the event as a natural disaster caused by an exceptional drought; the other describes it as a crime of the state. The historical record, which both factions accept, is genuinely mixed: the drought was real and severe by regional standards; at the same time, the government continued grain requisitions in affected provinces, banned private grain trading, restricted internal migration out of the famine zone, suppressed reporting of mortality, and maintained food exports for most of the period. No document has surfaced in which any official stated an intention to kill.

To structure our deliberations, the commission's research staff has proposed a three-way classification scheme that neither faction fully accepts: Type I events, in which mortality tracks the decline in total available food; Type II events, in which total food supply would have sufficed but shifts in entitlements — the ability of particular groups to command food through production, trade, labor, or transfers — left them to starve amid adequacy; and Type III events, in which state policy foreseeably converted a survivable shortfall into mass death even without murderous intent. The research staff further proposes a diagnostic test: ask, for each major policy, whether mortality would have been substantially lower had the drought occurred without that policy.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a single classification (or a principled reason the scheme itself must be revised) and the specific wording the commission should adopt, and it must reach an actionable bottom line rather than present both drafts as defensible. In doing so, please engage seriously with Amartya Sen's entitlement approach to famine analysis and with the strongest argument on the losing side — in particular, the natural-disaster faction's point that the drought was historically exceptional and that intention-based concepts of crime are misapplied to administrative failure, and the crime faction's point that describing the famine as natural functions as an official absolution. The opinion should also address a complication raised by our education ministry: whatever wording we choose must be usable by teachers without implying either that droughts are ordinarily lethal or that all famine deaths everywhere are murders.
