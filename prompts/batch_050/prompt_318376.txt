So my brother-in-law signed up with a cryonics company a few months ago, and it's been the source of endless argument at family dinners. For anyone unfamiliar: the deal is that once you're declared legally dead, the company rushes in, cools your body down, replaces your blood with antifreeze-type chemicals, and stores you in liquid nitrogen at some facility in Arizona or Michigan, on the bet that future medicine can repair whatever killed you plus the freezing damage and wake you up. Here's the thing that keeps bugging me. The whole arrangement is legally structured around death -- you have to be pronounced dead before they can touch you, your life insurance pays out, your will executes -- but the entire point of the enterprise is that you are NOT dead in the sense that matters, just "paused." The same person is dead enough to probate but alive enough to be worth saving. That seems like it has to smuggle in some particular theory of what death and personal identity even are, and I'm not sure the legal system and the cryonics people are operating with the same one.

So my question: what philosophical commitments does the practice of cryonics presuppose? I don't just mean optimism about future technology -- I mean the identity stuff. It seems to presuppose that what makes you you is something like the information encoded in your brain's structure, such that if that's preserved and later read off, the person who wakes up is you and not just a very convincing copy. But does it strictly presuppose that, or does it just sit comfortably with it? Consider two revival scenarios the cryonics people seem fine with: in one, nanomachines repair your actual frozen brain cell by cell; in the other, your brain is scanned and the resulting pattern is run as a simulation or installed in a freshly grown body, and the original is destroyed. Are those the same outcome on the view cryonics presupposes? If the practice presupposes a patternist view of identity, then a simulated revival still counts as the customer -- but then it's hard to see why preserving this particular lump of frozen tissue matters at all, since a scan made before death would do just as well, and nobody sells that. If instead it presupposes something about the continuous survival of the biological brain, then the scanning scenario is a copy problem and the cryonics folks should be much more nervous about it than they seem to be. There's also a gradual-replacement wrinkle: suppose revival involves repairing neurons one at a time over months, swapping damaged ones for synthetic equivalents until the brain is mostly synthetic. At what point, if any, does the patient stop being the customer? Does the practice have to take a stand on that, or can it shrug?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person (and of death) that cryonics actually runs on, tell me whether the legal-death-for-the-papers / not-really-dead-for-the-patient split is coherent or a fudge, and say what would have to change about the practice if the presupposed view turned out to be false -- e.g., if identity requires continuous biological life, is a cryonics contract just an extremely expensive way of commissioning a doppelgänger who inherits none of your obligations? And flag where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, where several rival views would all license it and we shouldn't pretend it settles them. Please answer in American English.
