So last night I had this hours-long argument with my roommate that's still bugging me. It started from Escher's Drawing Hands, which he'd just seen a print of, and turned into a fight about whether any system can actually have a level that stands outside it and isn't tangled up in the loop. His example was constitutions and supreme courts: the court gets its authority from the constitution, but the court is also what decides what the constitution means, so which level is really "above" which? His position was that every supposedly inviolate level is fake -- the court is just as tangled as the hands, and the idea of a level that constrains the system without being constrained by it is a comforting illusion. My position was that the tangle is real in edge cases but that in practice some levels function as inviolate, and you can't even describe how systems hold together at all without the concept.

We went in circles for ages. Every time I gave an example of a level that holds -- the rules of chess vs. playing chess, an operating system's kernel vs. the programs running on it, whatever -- he said that's just because nothing has pushed hard enough yet, and every time he gave an example of a level collapsing (a constitutional crisis, a chess player knocking over the board) I said that proves the level was doing real work before it collapsed.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Are we disagreeing about a fact of the matter -- whether genuinely untangled levels exist somewhere -- or are we just using "inviolate" at different strengths, where he means "absolutely metaphysically untouchable" and I mean "stable enough that the system can be described in terms of it"? Or is one of us answering a question about how systems are and the other a question about how we have to talk about them? Give me a diagnosis based on the kinds of moves we each kept making, and tell me what would actually settle it -- a better example, a sharper distinction, a formal result about self-reference -- or whether it's the kind of argument where neither of us can win because we were never asking the same question. And be honest about which side you think the diagnosis ends up favoring, if either.
