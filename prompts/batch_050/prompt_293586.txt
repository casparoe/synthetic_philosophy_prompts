The question: "Describe the color phi phenomenon. Explain the two hypotheses Dennett calls the 'Orwellian' and 'Stalinesque' accounts of what happens in the subject's experience, making clear what gets revised and when on each account. Then explain why Dennett thinks no experiment could settle which hypothesis is correct, and what conclusion he draws from this for theories of consciousness."

The student's answer: "The color phi phenomenon, first studied by Dennett, works like this: a red dot is flashed briefly on a screen, then a green dot is flashed a short distance away. Subjects report seeing a single dot that moves from the first position to the second and changes color from red to green partway along the path. The puzzle is that the color change appears to happen mid-path, before the second dot has even been flashed, so the experience seems to contain information that wasn't available yet. Dennett offers two hypotheses. On the Orwellian account, the brain fabricates a smooth motion-and-color-change experience before the subject becomes conscious of it, so what reaches consciousness was never actually experienced any other way. On the Stalinesque account, the subject genuinely experiences the red dot sitting still and only later does memory get rewritten, so that by the time the subject reports the experience they sincerely misremember having seen continuous motion and a mid-path color switch. Dennett argues that the two hypotheses are empirically distinguishable in principle -- for instance by measuring the timing of neural events -- but that current technology is too coarse to do it, so for now we can't tell which is right. His conclusion is that consciousness researchers should keep looking for better experimental methods to resolve the question."
