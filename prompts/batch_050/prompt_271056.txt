I'm putting together a syllabus for an undergraduate seminar on rational choice and political philosophy, and I want to build one session around Anthony Burgess's A Clockwork Orange (students may know either the novel or Kubrick's film, and I don't mind which they draw on). The session's theme is Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment: the idea, familiar from the Odyssey onward, that a person or a polity can rationally bind its future self against temptations it expects to face later -- Ulysses ordering his crew to keep him lashed to the mast, voters locking fiscal rules into a constitution, a recovering alcoholic pouring the whisky down the sink.

Here's the puzzle I want the session to turn on. A Ulysses contract is supposed to be self-binding: the earlier, clear-sighted self constrains the later, compromised self, and the legitimacy of the constraint is meant to come from that identity between binder and bound. But the Ludovico technique in A Clockwork Orange looks structurally similar while being nothing of the sort: the state binds Alex against his future violent impulses, and the chaplain's objection that Alex can no longer choose to be good is usually read as the book's moral center. So I'd like a careful reading of the work through the precommitment lens: is the Ludovico technique a perversion of a Ulysses contract, or is it a Ulysses contract in which the state has simply taken over the role of the sober earlier self? And does the book's critique depend on Alex never having consented, or would it survive even if he had volunteered -- as one might argue an addict who checks himself into a locked clinic has done?

Then push the reading in the other direction, toward constitutionalism: when a founding generation entrenches a constitution, an independent central bank, or supermajoritarian amendment rules, later citizens are bound by decisions they never made -- so is constitutional precommitment more like Ulysses at the mast or more like Ludovico's technicians? I'd like the essay to end by assessing what the novel adds to this debate that the philosophical literature on precommitment tends to miss, and to be honest about where Burgess's treatment (for instance, the novel's final chapter, which Kubrick's film omits) resists the simple anti-precommitment reading. Around 1,200 words, written as the seminar's framing essay.
