You are grading a short-essay response on a philosophy of physics exam. The exam question was:

"In the late 1940s Kurt Gödel constructed solutions to Einstein's field equations describing rotating universes, and drew from them a striking conclusion about the nature of time. Explain Gödel's argument (or arguments) for the conclusion that time, in the sense of an objective lapse or passage, is not a feature of reality. In doing so, address: (a) the role of the relativity of simultaneity in special relativity; (b) what the Gödel solutions are and which of their features matter for the argument; (c) why Gödel's conclusion is not simply the empirical claim that our universe contains closed timelike curves; and (d) one serious objection to the argument and how Gödel or a defender might respond."

Here is the student's answer:

Gödel's conclusion that the lapse of time is merely ideal rests on a single argument with two parts. First, from special relativity: because simultaneity is relative to an observer's state of motion, different observers will disagree about which events have already happened and which are still future, so there can be no observer-independent division of the universe into past, present, and future, and hence no objective "becoming." Gödel takes this to show that the passage of time is subjective, since what counts as "now" is settled only relative to an arbitrarily chosen reference frame.

Second, in 1949 Gödel discovered solutions to Einstein's field equations for a rotating, expanding universe filled with dust. In these solutions, spacetime contains closed timelike curves: a sufficiently fast spaceship could travel along a worldline that returns to the very event from which it departed. Since our universe is governed by general relativity, and these solutions satisfy its equations, Gödel concluded that time travel into the past is physically possible in our universe. But if an event in one's past can also lie in one's future, then the temporal ordering of events is not well-defined globally, and so the lapse of time cannot be real.

Gödel further argued that the possibility of such loops does not generate genuine paradoxes like the grandfather paradox, because consistency constraints built into the laws of physics ensure that a time traveler cannot actually change anything. This, he held, removes the main objection to taking closed timelike curves seriously.

The standard objection is that Gödel's solution describes a merely possible universe and says nothing about ours. Gödel's reply, as the student understands it, is that if time lapses objectively it must do so in every possible universe governed by general relativity, so the existence of one counterexample shows the lapse of time is not a necessary feature of physical reality and hence is ideal rather than real.
