I'm putting together a handout for a mixed philosophy-and-CS discussion group on griefbots, and I want a decision-tree classifier that sorts ethical positions by what they actually commit someone to, not by whether the person finds the technology moving or creepy.

Use this setup: a company offers to build an interactive conversational simulation of a dead person from their messages, emails, posts, recordings, and photos. Unlike a static memorial page, the bot can produce new claims, give advice, remember later conversations, and adapt to the bereaved user. Assume there is no serious possibility that the bot is conscious or literally identical to the deceased unless a position explicitly treats some weaker kind of continuation as morally important.

Please make a decision tree with roughly 12–16 branch questions and 8–10 leaves. Build it from the logical space first: figure out which distinctions genuinely change the ethics, then locate positions in that space. Each branch should be a concrete yes/no or three-way question that a thoughtful person could answer without already knowing the name of any view. Don't use vague nodes like Is it respectful? or Does the benefit outweigh the harm? Say what the relevant respect or harm consists in.

The tree should at least consider:

- whether the dead person gave prior consent, and whether consent to being remembered also covers being made to say new things
- whether private material from living correspondents can be used without their consent
- whether the user always understands that they are interacting with a simulation
- whether the bot is merely archival or can develop opinions and personality traits the source person never had
- whether the survivor's psychological benefit or risk of dependency should carry decisive weight
- whether children, people in acute grief, and mentally vulnerable users require different rules
- whether relatives or executors may override the dead person's likely wishes
- whether commercial incentives change the ethics independently of consent and welfare
- whether editing the bot to make it kinder, less political, or more approving of the survivor is different from editing an ordinary biography
- whether deleting or shutting down the bot can itself wrong the user, the deceased, or the digital persona

The leaves should include the major families of view, such as categorical anti-simulation, prior-consent permissionism, survivor-welfare consequentialism, bounded memorialism, relationship-continuation, some form of digital-personhood or partial-continuation, fiduciary stewardship of the dead person's legacy, and relational-data or third-party-rights views. If one of these is not really a standard named position, give it a neutral coined name and label it as coined rather than pretending there is settled terminology.

For every leaf, include:

1. the name of the position,
2. its organizing principle in one sentence,
3. its most awkward implication,
4. what it says about a hospital, therapist, or funeral provider offering griefbots as part of bereavement support.

After the tree, run these calibration cases through it and say which leaf or leaves each reaches: a widow creates a bot from private chats although her husband only consented to preserving an archive; a deceased man explicitly authorized a bot but not later updates based on his daughter's preferences; a bot is trained solely on public posts; a bot trained on private conversations reveals embarrassing facts about a still-living freind; a clearly labeled bot gradually becomes a teenager's main source of emotional support; a company has the bot mention how much it misses the user shortly before asking her to renew; a bereaved partner edits the simulation so it approves of his new relationship; and a care team deletes the bot because the user appears stuck in mourning.

Finish by listing any coherent combinations of answers your tree leaves unoccupied, saying whether they seem incoherent, morally unattractive, or just underexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that the diagnostic questions cannot reliably seperate. No citations or reading list — this is for people thinking through the cases from scratch.
