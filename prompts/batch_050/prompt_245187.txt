I direct a small philanthropic research program that funds early-career work in philosophy and political theory, and our next three-year funding line is on the epistemology of tradition: the rationality of deferring to inherited institutions and practices, and the conditions under which reform is justified. I am putting together the call for proposals and need to decide which open questions the program should actually prioritize.

The framing I want the agenda to engage with comes from a passage near the start of Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), before the Terror, where Burke insists that he wishes France to be animated by a spirit of rational liberty and that the French are bound to "provide a permanent body in which that spirit may reside, and an effectual organ by which it may act." What interests me is that this cuts against the way Chesterton's-fence-style reasoning is usually deployed. The standard picture treats conservatism as a doctrine about removal: do not tear down the fence until you understand why it was built. But Burke's sentence is about construction. On his view, the French problem was not too many inherited fences but the absence of an institution that could house a value everyone claimed to want. So the research area, properly understood, seems to have two halves that are rarely connected: when may an existing practice be reformed or discarded, and how can new institutions ever be responsibly built if individual reason is as thin as the conservative says it is — since on that premise we cannot reason our way to good institutions from scratch, yet they demonstrably do get built.

With that as background: which open questions in this area should the program prioritize, and in what order? Give me roughly five questions, ranked, and defend the ranking rather than just listing. For each question, say which other questions in the area depend on its answer, how tractable it is for philosophers and political theorists rather than for historians or social scientists, and how neglected it is relative to the attention the fence-as-removal framing currently gets. I would also like your judgment on at least the following candidate issues, whether or not they make your final list: when the inference from a practice's long survival to its serving some function is warranted, given that practices can persist through drift, coercion, or mere vested interest; how to identify the boundaries of the "fence" in the first place, since reformers and defenders routinely describe the same institution at different levels of abstraction; whether there is a principled standard of understanding that licenses removal, short of complete knowledge of a practice's origins; how deference to tradition should change when the environment has changed faster than the tradition could have adapted; and what to say when a tradition's survival was purchased partly at the expense of people it excluded, which is the standing abolitionist and feminist objection to the whole framework.

Two further constraints on the answer. First, flag any question that gets a great deal of discussion but that you think would change little downstream if settled — I would rather fund questions whose answers move other questions than ones that are merely contested. Second, tell me what would change your ranking: if a particular question would rise or fall depending on some empirical or conceptual development, name the development. The deliverable is a defended funding agenda, not a survey of the debate and not a reading list; assume I can find the literature myself once I know which questions deserve the money.
