Question: State Goodman's new riddle of induction. How is it meant to differ from Hume's problem of induction? Then set out one attempted solution to the riddle and say whether it succeeds.

Student's answer: Goodman defines a predicate "grue": an object is grue iff it is examined before some future time t (say, 1 January 2100) and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald examined so far is green, but it follows that every emerald examined so far is also grue, so the evidence that confirms "all emeralds are green" confirms "all emeralds are grue" to exactly the same degree. Yet the two hypotheses make opposite predictions about emeralds first examined after t. So the problem is: given that the evidence fits both, what licenses projecting "green" rather than "grue"? This differs from Hume's problem, which asks whether inductive inference can be justified at all. Goodman takes that question to be basically dissolved -- induction is justified by its conformity with our accepted inferential practice, in the same way deduction is -- and his riddle instead asks which regularities our evidence actually supports, i.e. which predicates are projectible. As for a solution: the natural one is that "grue" is not a purely qualitative predicate, because its definition essentially mentions a particular time, and only purely qualitative predicates are projectible. This solves the riddle, since it gives a principled line between "green" and "grue" without any appeal to the evidence. Goodman's own answer, entrenchment, points in the same direction: "green" has a much longer track record of being successfully projected in past inferences, and that past success is evidence that green-inductions are reliable, which is why we project it rather than "grue". So between the qualitative-predicate criterion and entrenchment, the riddle is more or less resolved.
