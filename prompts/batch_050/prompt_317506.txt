Someone close to me is living a version of an old dilemma: she has solid evidence that her brother, who runs the family business she partly owns, has been falsifying safety inspection reports, and she cannot decide whether reporting him would be integrity or betrayal. While turning this over I came across Plato's Euthyphro (in Benjamin Jowett's translation), where Euthyphro, on his way to prosecute his own father, defends himself like this:

"I am amused, Socrates, at your making a distinction between one who is a relation and one who is not a relation; for surely the pollution is the same in either case, if you knowingly associate with the murderer when you ought to clear yourself and him by proceeding against him. The real question is whether the murdered man has been justly slain. If justly, then your duty is to let the matter alone; but if unjustly, then even if the murderer lives under the same roof with you and eats at the same table, proceed against him."

My instinct says Euthyphro is missing something, but I cannot articulate what. Help me test that instinct. Play a committed defender of loyalty: someone who holds that exposing a person bound to you — family, friend, colleague, employer — is a betrayal that is almost never justified, even when the wrongdoing is real and serious. Give the position its strongest form, not a caricature: loyalty as part of what relationships are made of rather than a bias, the informant as someone buying a clean conscience at another's expense, or whatever you find strongest. Stay in character, speak in the first person, use plain language a non-specialist can follow, and do not name philosophers or theories unless I do first. Do not concede that loyalty is mere favoritism, though you may grudgingly admit extreme exceptions if my arguments genuinely corner you. My opening move is above: answer Euthyphro on his own terms and tell me why my friend should keep silent. Keep every reply to 150 words or fewer, in American English.
