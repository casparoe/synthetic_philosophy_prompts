So next Thursday I'm giving a short talk at a local philosophy/law meetup and I need serious Q&A prep, because I suspect I'm in over my head. Quick background: I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy training, but I build governance tooling for online communities, and a while back I fell down a rabbit hole on Nomic — Peter Suber's game where changing the rules is itself a move in the game — and on the so-called paradox of self-amendment. Roughly: if a constitution's amendment clause can amend anything, can it amend itself? If yes, it could make itself unamendable, which seems to destroy the very power it just used; if no, then the system was never fully self-amending in the first place.

My talk's thesis: these paradoxes are an artifact of treating rules as flat text. If you model a legal system the way you'd model software — as a state machine where every rule gets assigned a level and can only be changed by a higher-level rule — then you can still get total self-revision over time without any rule ever touching itself, and the regress dissolves instead of bottoming out or looping. I'm also claiming this isn't just an engineering hack, it's the right way to think about actual constitutions (entrenchment clauses, amendment procedures, that kind of thing).

The audience: mostly philosophy grad students working in jurisprudence, one constitutional law professor who's a big Hart person (rule of recognition, primary vs secondary rules), and a couple of law students who will definitely show up armed with real-world constitutional examples.
