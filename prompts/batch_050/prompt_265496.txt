just got out of a two hour argument with my roommate about Grice's account of speaker meaning and i think he walked me into a corner but i cannot find the bad step. his argument:

(1) On Grice's picture, I mean that p by an utterance only if my audience comes to believe p at least partly *because* they recognize that I intend them to.
(2) When I say something in plain English to another English speaker — "there's beer in the fridge," whatever — they come to believe it because they understand English. Recognizing my intention isn't doing any work; they'd get the same belief from the words alone, intentions or no.
(3) So when I speak a shared language literally, I don't actually mean anything by my words, in Grice's sense of "mean."
(4) That's absurd, ordinary literal talk is the paradigm case of meaning something.
(5) He says the only fix is to weaken (1): intention-recognition doesn't have to *produce* the belief, it just has to be present, or be a route the belief could have taken.
(6) But that re-admits exactly the cases Grice built the account to exclude — me quietly leaving someone's handkerchief at a crime scene hoping the detective draws the conclusion, or Herod presenting Salome with John the Baptist's head. In those the intention is present and recognizable too, but intuitively nothing is *meant*.
(7) So Grice's account either says I never mean anything when I speak English, or says Herod means that John is dead by showing his head. Both are wrong.

I really want to keep (1), it feels like the whole insight of the account, and obviously I want to deny (3). Which step actually goes wrong, and why did it look fine? Also, if the answer is to lean on some fancier condition (the reflexive/overt intention stuff, Strawson's version, whatever), tell me what that condition has to be such that it blocks the handkerchief case *without* collapsing back into (7). And is the real problem in my roommate's argument about the actual cause of the belief, or about something else entirely?
