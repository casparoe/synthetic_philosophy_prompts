I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed matter, mostly low-temperature work — and I have lately joined a reading group at my parish that is making its slow way through Augustine's Confessions. We are now in Book XI, and while everyone else wanted to linger on the famous passage about not knowing what time is until one is asked, I found myself stuck much earlier, at the very opening of the book, where Augustine addresses God with a question I cannot stop turning over: "O Lord, since eternity is Yours, are You ignorant of the things which I say unto You? Or see Thou at the time that which comes to pass in time?" (Confessions, Book XI, chapter 1; I am reading the old Pusey translation.) What strikes me is that Augustine poses this as a genuine dilemma and then spends the rest of the book refusing both horns: God does not wait for events to happen, does not watch them at the time they occur, and was not doing anything before creation, because there was no time in which He could have done anything. I want to understand whether, and how far, this position can be restated in the vocabulary I actually think in.

So here is my request. Translate Augustine's account of time and eternity, as developed across Book XI, into the vocabulary of modern physics — the four-dimensional spacetime manifold, worldlines, proper time versus coordinate time, the relativity of simultaneity, and the thermodynamic arrow of increasing entropy — as faithfully as you can. In my old seminar-room idiom, Augustine's God sounds remarkably like someone reading off the entire spacetime diagram tenselessly: no privileged "now," no succession on God's side, all times equally present to Him rather like all events laid out on the manifold. And his dismissal of the question what God did before creation sounds like the move one makes when one says that "before the Big Bang" is malformed because time itself begins with the metric. Please tell me whether these correspondences are real or superficial. Does Augustine's eternity map onto the tenseless, "block" reading of relativity, or is that a seductive false friend? Where he writes, a little later in the same opening chapters, "At Your nod the moments flee by," and confesses that "the drops of time are dear to me" — is he saying anything a spacetime diagram could register?

Most important to me is what I would call the residue report: which of Augustine's claims translate cleanly into the physics vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in it at all. I have in mind particularly his eventual suggestion that time is a kind of stretching of the mind, and his division of the present into the present of past things, of present things, and of future things — memory, attention, expectation. Nothing in my training gives these a physical counterpart; entropy gives me an arrow, but not an experiencer stationed at a moving present. So please end by adjudicating: is the untranslatable residue a defect of the physical vocabulary — evidence that physics genuinely leaves something out — or is it a sign that Augustine, in those stretches, was doing something other than describing the world a physicist could in principle measure? I do not want the translation rigged in either direction; I want to know what is left on the table, and why.
