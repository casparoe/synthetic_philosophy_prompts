I'm a philosophy professor. I teach a grad seminar on moral responsibility, and next semester I want to run a session connecting that literature to the historiography of the First World War -- specifically the Fischer controversy and the broader question of what it means to say Germany (or Austria-Hungary, or Russia, or whoever) was *responsible* for the war. The problem is I know the responsibility lit cold and the historiography only at the level of a well-read amateur, so I want the translation done in the direction I'm fluent in.

So here's the task. Take the Fischer debate -- Fischer's claim that Imperial Germany deliberately exploited the July crisis to launch a war of expansion, the counterattacks from Ritter and others that this conflated war aims developed *during* the war with prewar intentions, the later softening into claims about shared responsibility or structural drift (the sleepwalkers picture, if you know it) -- and restate the whole thing in the vocabulary of moral responsibility attribution. I mean stuff like: the distinction between intending an outcome, forseeing it, and merely being negligent with respect to it; strict liability versus fault-based responsibility; the control condition and moral luck; excuses and justifications; complicity and joint action; responsibility for outcomes versus responsibility for conduct.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which moves in the historians' debate translate cleanly (my guess: the Ritter objection is basically just the point that you can't infer intention ex post from war aims documents, but tell me if I'm wrong), which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in the responsibility literature's vocabulary. For that last category, say whether the residue is (a) something the moral philosophy framework genuinely can't express and that's a real limitation -- e.g., historians seem to fight about things like counterfactual necessity ('would war have come anyway without Bethmann Hollweg's blank cheque?') which don't map neatly onto any responsibility concept I know -- or (b) a sign that the historians were sometimes arguing past each other in ways the philosophy vocabulary would have cleaned up.

Also flag the reverse direction: is there anything in the responsibility literature (collective agents, corporate blame, distributing responsibility across a decision-making chain) that the historians *should* have been using but mostly weren't? I'm suspicious that 'blaming a nation' is doing weird work in this whole debate -- a state isn't obviously the kind of thing that can have mens rea -- so say something about that too.

Don't just give me a summary of the historiography; I can read that myself. The deliverable is the translation plus the residue report, with a verdict on which side the residue indicts.
