I'm enrolled in a political theory seminar, and a long-running dispute with a classmate about the wisdom of crowds has pushed me to take stock of my own intuitions about when a group's verdict deserves more credence than any individual's. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, stated as plainly as I can manage, followed by what I want done with them.

Case one: a hundred radiologists each read the same scan independently, and each is right about tumors of this kind roughly sixty percent of the time; eighty of them judge the scan malignant. I am strongly inclined to trust the majority — indeed, I'd trust it over any single member, including the most experienced. Case two: same hundred radiologists, same majority, but I learn that they all trained at the same institution and all viewed the scan only after a senior colleague presented it as probably malignant. My confidence now collapses, even though no individual has become less skilled. Case three: a thousand voters, each only slightly better than a coin flip at judging which of two economic policies will reduce unemployment, make up their minds independently; I think their majority verdict deserves real deference — more than any single voter's — though markedly less than the radiologists' majority. Case four: pit a single economist with a seventy-percent track record against that same thousand barely-competent voters; I go back and forth, but on reflection I side with the crowd, provided they genuinely formed their own views. Case five: instead of a yes-or-no question, a hundred fairgoers independently estimate the weight of a prize hog; several are badly off in one direction or another, yet I feel that the average of their guesses will be excellent, even though I would not say of most of them that they are more likely right than wrong — so the standard I applied in case three seems not to be what is doing the work here. Case six: a jury of twelve, each moderately competent, deliberates for three days and reaches unanimity; I find the unanimous verdict after deliberation more impressive than the same twelve voting privately by ballot, even though in case two I said that influence among group members is precisely what destroys my confidence.

Is there a single principle — presumably something about the competence of individual judges and the independence of their judgments — that captures all six verdicts, or must one of them go? If there is, please state it precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause; tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed (say, a panel on which the most competent members vote last, after hearing the others); and be candid about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, rather than complicating the principle to save them all. Cases two and six are the pair I suspect cannot both survive, and I would rather be told which to drop than have the tension papered over.
