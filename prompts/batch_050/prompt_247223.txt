I spent my career in experimental physics, mostly fluid turbulence, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with the diligence of a convert. I have lately become stuck on an argument of my own construction. It seems to show, from premises I would have considered truisms of my own discipline, that determinism is not a scientific thesis at all. I find the conclusion repugnant, but I cannot locate the fault, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A claim about the physical world is scientifically meaningful only if some conceivable observation could count against it; a claim compatible with every possible observation is metaphysics, not physics.

(2) Determinism, as applied to a physical system, is the claim that the state of the system at one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes its state at every later time.

(3) The only way to test such a claim is to ascertain the system's state as accurately as one can, apply the laws, and compare the predicted later states against observation.

(4) Every measurement of a continuous quantity is of finite precision; there is always some nonzero uncertainty in the ascertained initial state.

(5) In systems exhibiting sensitive dependence on initial conditions, small uncertainties grow roughly exponentially in time, with a rate set by the system's dynamics, so that for any fixed measurement precision there is a finite time horizon beyond which the predicted trajectory diverges from the true one no matter how the measurement is refined.

(6) Therefore, beyond that horizon, the failure of a prediction is consistent both with a deterministic system whose initial state we know only imperfectly and with a genuinely indeterministic system; no observation can discriminate between the two hypotheses.

(7) Sensitive dependence is not an exotic exception but is ubiquitous wherever many bodies interact nonlinearly, so the argument extends from particular laboratory systems to the universe at large.

(8) Therefore determinism is untestable in principle and hence, by (1), not a scientific claim; Laplace's intelligence belongs to the same category as the angels on the head of a pin.

The conclusion cannot be right -- or rather, I badly want it not to be right, since it would render a great deal of what I took myself to be doing for forty years an exercise in theology. I am most reluctant to give up (4) and (5), which are less premises than theorems, and I am most willing to entertain doubts about (1), which I took on faith from a philosopher of the last century, and about the step from (6) to (7). But I do not want my preferences to do the work; I want to know which step actually fails, or, if the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to surrender and what exactly one forfeits by surrendering it. In particular, I would like to know whether the defect lies in a conflation I suspect but cannot articulate -- between what a system does and what an observer can foresee -- or whether (1) is simply a stronger demand than science in fact meets, in which case I would like to know what weaker demand would still exclude the angels without excluding determinism.
