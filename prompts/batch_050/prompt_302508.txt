I want to know whether the following pattern has an established name, and under what literature, if any, it is studied. I will describe it through three instances I have observed at close hand, because the details seem to carry the phenomenon.

First, a hiring committee at my university. The most senior member speaks first and says, with visible hesitation, that she leans toward one candidate. Three of the remaining five members then express agreement. In the corridor afterward, two of those three admitted to me that they had privately ranked that candidate third, and that they had agreed because they assumed the senior member had read the file more carefully than they had. She had not. Her hesitation was genuine uncertainty, not tact.

Second, an investment committee at a friend's firm. A partner opens by saying a deal looks promising. The junior analysts, each of whom had independently noticed the same weak revenue figures in the materials, stay silent, each reasoning that the partner has presumably seen projections they have not. The deal proceeds. The projections did not exist.

Third, a hospital ward round. The consultant offers a tentative diagnosis. Two residents, who had each formed a different view from the same chart, say nothing, each supposing that the consultant noticed something at the bedside that they missed.

What strikes me is the shared structure: (i) an early speaker offers a view held with low confidence; (ii) later participants hold contrary views of their own but set them aside on the assumption that the early speaker is better informed; (iii) the assumption is false — the early speaker had no extra information, sometimes less; (iv) the group thereby produces a decision that, in a relevant sense, no member privately endorses; and (v) — this is the part I most want preserved — no one is pretending in order to be liked or to avoid conflict. The later speakers are not conforming; they are deferring. Each would sincerely insist that he acted reasonably, and I am not sure he is wrong.

I am aware of several nearby concepts, and a bare name will not satisfy me; I want the carving. This is not what is usually called groupthink, as I understand it: there is no drive for cohesion, no loyalty, no suppression of dissent — dissent never surfaces because each dissenter independently concludes that he is probably mistaken. It is not the Abilene paradox, since no one is acting against his judgment to please the others or to spare feelings; each person genuinely discounts his own view. Nor is it the familiar phenomenon of people professing beliefs they privately reject to protect their standing — here the stated position is adopted in good faith, on epistemic grounds. And it plainly differs from cases where deference is simply correct, because the senior person really does know more.

So my questions: Is there an established term, and a research tradition, for exactly this mechanism — sequential deliberation in which participants rationally downgrade their own information in favor of information they merely infer earlier actors to possess, so that the group's choice tracks no one's private judgment? Under what conditions is the inference that generates it individually rational, and what marks the point at which individually rational deference aggregates into collective error? How is it distinguished, at the level of mechanism rather than outcome, from each neighbor I listed and from well-founded deference to genuine expertise? What are its boundary cases — does the phenomenon still obtain when the first speaker really was better informed, or when the later speakers' private beliefs genuinely change rather than being merely overridden? If no established term covers exactly this configuration, say so explicitly, and propose one with a definition tight enough to exclude its neighbors.
