So I just spent like three hours arguing with a friend and I need a referee. The topic: wether it makes sense for a country to apologize for historical wrongs (slavery, colonialism, treatment of indigenous peoples, that kind of thing). My friend's whole case rests on one analogy: a state apology is just a personal apology scaled up. If you wrong someone, you acknowledge what you did, you say sorry, you commit to not doing it again, maybe you make it up to them -- and a country should do exactly the same things for exactly the same reasons. I pushed back: a state isn't a person, there's nobody there to feel remorse, the people doing the apologizing aren't the ones who did the wrong, and the people being apologized to mostly aren't the ones it was done to. She said none of that matters.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the mapping explicitly -- what in a personal apology corresponds to what in a state apology (the wrongdoer, the remorse, the addressee, the promise not to repeat, the making amends, the relationship the apology is supposed to repair). Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion she wants, namely that states owe apologies in the same way people do. Some candidate disanalogies I want a verdict on:

- A state apology is issued by people who didn't do the wrong, addressed to people who often didn't directly suffer it. In personal life, an apology from the wrongdoer's grandson to the victim's grandson would be strange -- is the state version strange in the same way, or does the state's continuity do the work a person's memory does?
- A state can't feel anything. So is the remorse component faked by definition? And if an apology without felt remorse still counts, what exactly did the remorse add in the personal case -- and can a state get that thing by other means?
- When a person says "I won't do it again," the guarantee is character; when a state says it, the guarantee is laws and institutions. Is that a worse version of the promise or a better one?
- In personal life an apology can close the matter. Can a state apology close anything, and should it? What if the addressees reject it -- is an apology that isn't accepted still an apology?

Also: does the analogy smuggle anything in? For instance, in personal life an apology often substitutes for further action -- once I've apologized sincerely, pressing for more starts to look vindictive. Does that feature transfer to the state case, and is it a bug or a feature there? I'm suspicious that governments lean on exactly this: perform the apology, treat the books as balanced.

If the analogy can't carry her conclusion, what's a better one for what a state is actually doing when it apologizes -- a legal settlement, a funeral eulogy, a corporation recalling a defective product? -- and does the better analogy make state apologies look more or less worth having than the personal-apology framing does?

Don't just tell me all analogies break down somewhere, I know that. Tell me which specific break kills my friend's argument if any does, and what's left standing after.
