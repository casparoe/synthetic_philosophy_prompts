Last weekend I had an argument with an old friend that ran past two in the morning, and I still haven't managed to let it go. I'd like help thinking through whether my objection to his position is actually sound, because he is a thoughtful person and I couldn't articulate it clearly in the moment.

We were discussing politics, and I admitted to feeling demoralized about various recent developments I take to be serious regressions. My friend told me I was missing the big picture. His view, roughly: history has a discernible direction. Across centuries, human societies have abolished slavery, extended suffrage, reduced everyday violence, and progressively widened the circle of those whose interests count. He insists these are not disconnected accidents but surface signs of a deep underlying tendency, driven by the accumulation of knowledge and the logic of cooperation, so that anyone resisting the tendency will eventually lose, whatever short-term victories they rack up. On his view, the setbacks that demoralize me are eddies in a river that flows one way, and my side of the argument will win in the end whether or not people like me do much of anything.

I pushed back with counterexamples — societies that collapsed into barbarism after long periods of civilization, moral gains reversed for generations at a time. He conceded every case and replied that each was a temporary deviation which the long run corrected. When I asked what could possibly count as evidence against his thesis, he said I needed to take the long view and stop thinking in decades.

Two things bother me, and I want them examined carefully rather than just agreed with. First, if every apparent refutation gets reclassified as a deviation awaiting correction, I can't see what observation would ever disconfirm his theory — and I have a nagging sense that a claim compatible with everything predicts nothing, even though the historical record he's pointing to is genuinely impressive. Second, there's the practical consequence he drew: that our side needn't fight very hard, since the outcome is guaranteed. It seems to me that a prediction whose truth depends on people working to bring it about can't also license their complacency — but he thinks this is just a failure of nerve on my part.

Is there a principled problem with inferring what the future must hold from alleged large-scale patterns in the past? And even granting that his centuries-long trend is real, does the fact that a trend has held so far license treating it the way we treat a law of nature — as something that will continue no matter what anyone does? Or am I wrong, and is my friend simply seeing clearly what I'm too impatient to see?
