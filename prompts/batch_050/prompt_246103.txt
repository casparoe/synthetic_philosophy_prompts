I'm writing a piece for the magazine on whether delusions are actually beliefs, and I keep trusting my gut on cases and getting an incoherent picture. So: the Capgras patient who insists his wife has been swapped for an impostor -- feels like a genuine belief, he acts on it, he argues for it. The Cotard patient who says she's dead -- also a belief, even if a bizarre one. The anosognosia patient who flat-out denies her left arm is paralyzed while clearly never even trying to use it -- feels like not a belief, more like a refusal. The Korsakoff's patient who confabulates a whole story about meeting his brother this morning and drops it completely ten minutes later -- not a belief either, though he'd swear it is. Erotomania, the person convinced some celebrity is secretly in love with them and reads proof into everything -- belief, definitely, it's stable and guides their whole life. But then the anosognosia case bugs me, because she'll also give you confident confabulatory reasons ("I just used it to turn the page"), so by the Korsakoff verdict shouldn't that be a belief too? And the Capgras guy doesn't act on it the way you'd expect -- he doesn't call the police or look for his real wife. Is there a principled account that gets all these verdicts, or close to it, and does whatever principle you land on vindicate or sink the two-factor idea that a delusion is the best available belief-explanation of a weird experience? Tell me which of my gut calls has to go if the principle's going to stay simple.
