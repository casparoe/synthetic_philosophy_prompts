Longtime LW/ACX reader here (like, since the Sequences were still being posted), and I've been stuck on this one for years: the argument between EA-ish people and the "systemic change" critics. You know the shape of it. The critics say EA's obsession with measurable, counterfactual, RCT-able interventions systematically screens out the most important stuff — political change, cultural shifts, regulatory reform, things where you can't estimate impact in DALYs. The EA side says "tractability" isn't a bias, it's just taking seriously that most interventions do nothing, and the systemic-change crowd has no way to tell their good ideas from their bad ones. Both sides have been making basically these moves since at least the mid-2010s and I have never once seen anyone change their mind.

My question isn't who's right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Some hypotheses I've considered: (a) it's a straightforward empirical dispute about how often systemic interventions succeed vs. direct ones, and both sides are just pointing at different historical case studies; (b) it's an epistemic disagreement about what counts as evidence — one side treats "no rigorous evaluation exists" as evidence of nothing, the other treats it as a red flag; (c) it's really about time horizons and discount rates wearing a costume; (d) it's bedrock clashing intuitions about how social change happens that neither side can argue for from shared premises; (e) they're answering different questions (what should I fund vs. what should the world prioritize) under one heading.

Give me an actual diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what the two sides standardly say — not a summary of the debate, I've read it a hundred times. And then: given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like? A distinction nobody's made yet? A particular case study both sides would have to agree on? Or is this one of those disagreements where nothing could resolve it and the honest answer is to split the question?
