I run a free monthly "big questions" evening group at my local community center -- mostly retirees, a few nurses, a couple of tradespeople, nobody with any philosophy training, including me. About 14 people usually show up, and we get the room for 75 minutes. Lately I've been chewing on a question and I want to build a session around it, but I need help designing the actual evening. Here's where my own thinking has gotten to.

When does "yes" actually count? I keep noticing three different ways a yes can go rotten, and I've started calling them my three failure modes. First, the yes can be built on a lie -- but I've realized this splits into two very different cases: being lied to about *what the thing is* (the surgeon operates on the wrong knee) versus being lied to about *some fact surrounding it* (a contractor lies about being licensed, but does the exact job agreed). Intuitively the first destroys consent and the second maybe just grounds a lawsuit, and I can't articulate why. Second, the yes can be squeezed out of you -- but a threat ("sign or you're fired") feels different from an offer you can't afford to refuse ("sell me your kidney for $50,000" said to someone about to lose their house), and I'm not sure whether the second one even counts as coercion or is something else entirely. Third, the person saying yes can be in no shape to mean it -- drunk, concussed, manic, fourteen years old -- and here the wrinkle that bugs me is *fluctuating* competence: someone with early dementia who's lucid at 10am and lost by 4pm. When did their yes expire, and who gets to say?

So: design me a runnable 75-minute activity for this group. Act as a professional philosopher who also has real experience facilitating workshops for general audiences, and think carefully about what will actually land with people who have never read a word of Kant or Mill. Before settling on a design, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming two or three genuinely different formats (a staged role-play, a game with hidden information, a sequence of forced votes, whatever you think of), weigh their strengths and failure risks, and then pick one and commit to it.

Requirements for the final design:

- The three failure modes must show up in what participants *do and experience*, not in anything I announce. People should get lied to, squeezed, or be asked to rely on someone whose judgment is visibly impaired -- in a safe, low-stakes, clearly playful frame -- and feel the wrongness themselves before anyone names it.
- The activity must force the group to confront the two hard wrinkles I mentioned: the difference between lies about the act and lies about surrounding facts, and the problem of competence that comes and goes. Build these in deliberately, and tell me how.
- Use only materials I can get at a dollar store or print at home. No money stakes; points, tokens, or chocolate are fine.
- Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, any scripts or card texts I'd need, and rules simple enough to explain in under five minutes.
- Include a debrief plan: the questions I'd ask to draw out what happened, and how to map specific moments of the evening onto the three failure modes without lecturing.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (people treating the deception as real betrayal, one dominant voice hijacking the debrief, whatever you foresee) and how to handle each.
- Be honest about which feature of the consent problem the activity *cannot* reproduce in a room full of volunteers who know it's a game, and what I'll have to supply in words instead.
