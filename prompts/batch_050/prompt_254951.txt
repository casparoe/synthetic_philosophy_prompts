I have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past couple of years I have been turning over the difference between lying and merely misleading people, and I have written down where I have landed. Before I build anything further on these commitments, I would like to know whether they actually hold together.

Here is what I currently believe:

(a) There is a real moral difference between lying and misleading. Lying — asserting something you believe to be false — is seriously wrong in a way that uttering a literal truth chosen to create a false impression is not. Misleading someone can be shabby, but it is a lesser wrong, if it is a wrong at all.

(b) The reason lying is wrong is that it hijacks another person's beliefs: it deprives them of the accurate picture they need to make their own decisions, and so treats their judgment as something to be manipulated rather than respected.

(c) Accordingly, a job candidate who says "I spent last year in consulting," knowing full well the interviewer will picture years of professional work when the reality was a two-week unpaid arrangement, has done something mildly blameworthy — but nothing close to as bad as stating an outright falsehood on the same point.

(d) Lying can nonetheless be justified in extremis: if a violent man knocks on my door demanding to know where my friend is hiding, I may tell him a flat falsehood without doing anything wrong.

(e) Deception by omission can also be genuinely blameworthy: someone who sells a car saying only "it runs great," while staying silent about the transmission having been replaced twice, has deceived the buyer and deserves real criticism for it, even though the sentence spoken was true.

My question is which of these pull against each other and how badly. I already suspect (d) sits awkwardly with (b), and perhaps (e) with (a), but I am more interested in problems I have not seen. Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: first the genuine tensions among (a) through (e), ordered from most to least severe, each with what would have to give; then the pairings that only appear to conflict and why the conflict dissolves; and finally the single least costly revision to the set as a whole.
