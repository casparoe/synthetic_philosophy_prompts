Suppose a small philosophy department is founding a working group on higher-order evidence: two faculty, three graduate students, four years of funded meetings and dissertation supervision. They must decide which open problems in this literature to organize the group around, and they cannot cover everything. Advise them. The candidates I have in mind include, but are not limited to: whether rationality ever requires level-splitting — holding a belief about the world while believing that this very belief is irrational; the status of epistemic akrasia, and whether the apparent irrationality of it collapses into a puzzle about the normativity of consistency more generally; how to characterize evidence of one's own unreliability (memory failures, biases, disagreement) so that it is neither ignored nor allowed to swamp first-order reasoning; whether disagreement among peers deserves special treatment or is just one species of higher-order evidence; and whether standard accounts of higher-order defeat are self-undermining in domains where we have strong evidence of our own systematic error.

Rank these (or better candidates I have omitted) by priority for this particular group, and defend the ranking rather than merely asserting it. In particular: say which questions are upstream of which, so that settling one does substantial work on others; which are tractable with purely philosophical tools within four years and which would stall because they depend on empirical psychology the group lacks; which are most neglected relative to how much downstream work implicitly assumes an answer; and which you would deprioritize as overrated — for instance, because the question's urgency rests on a conflation, or because any plausible answer leaves practical reasoning untouched. If you think the whole framing of some candidate is defective, say so and rank it low on those grounds rather than pretending to settle it. Finally, indicate what would change your ranking: what kinds of arguments or results in adjacent areas (formal epistemology, ethics of belief, social epistemology) over the next few years would move a question up or down the list. The deliverable I want is a defended agenda with explicit criteria, not a survey of positions.
