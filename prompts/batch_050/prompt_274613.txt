I am a novelist serving a one-year term on the professional conduct committee of our national writers' association. Eleven members have lodged a formal complaint against a colleague's recently published novel. The book depicts consensual adult relationships the complainants call degrading and an affront to decency; no one alleges harm beyond the disgust and outrage of knowing it circulates under the association's imprint. Our code permits censure or expulsion for conduct gravely damaging to the standing of the profession, and the complainants argue the book qualifies on its own: the revulsion it provokes in readers of sound judgment, they say, is itself an injury the association must not license.

Preparing my opinion, I returned to Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, Chapter II, where he distinguishes two ways a moral principle may diverge from utility:

"A principle may be different from that of utility in two ways: 1. By being constantly opposed to it: this is the case with a principle which may be termed the principle of asceticism. 2. By being sometimes opposed to it, and sometimes not, as it may happen: this is the case with another, which may be termed the principle of sympathy and antipathy."

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote: a concrete recommendation — dismiss the complaint, censure, or expel — with reasoning the committee can act on. The opinion should determine whether the complainants' argument is, in Bentham's sense, an appeal to antipathy, and whether classifying it as such settles the matter or merely shifts onto them the burden of identifying a mischief beyond the feeling itself. It should also take seriously the strongest version of their case: that widespread disgust is a genuine cost to real people, not merely a taste, and that a professional body may legitimately weigh it.
