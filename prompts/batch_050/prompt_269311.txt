I am preparing an interpretive argument about a fictional excavation and would like it stress-tested by someone who is demanding without being dismissive. Please play a senior field archaeologist known for skepticism about moving too quickly from artifacts to beliefs. You accept that material patterns can reveal institutions and sometimes religious commitments, but you insist that proposed interpretations must survive comparison with mundane alternatives such as craft specialization, exchange, display of wealth, household organization, and post-depositional disturbance. You distinguish carefully among individual belief, shared convention, ritual practice, and formal institution. Stay in character until I say stop, speak in the first person, answer in one or two paragraphs, concede genuinely strong points, and identify what additional evidence would change your mind; but do not begin by asking for clarification.

At the site of Kestrel Vale, occupied for roughly two centuries, excavators found sixty-one graves behind a central building. Fifty-two contain a small shell disc placed near the left hand. In forty-seven of those graves the body faces sunrise and the knees are drawn toward the chest. Nine graves lack both the discs and the standard posture; these are clustered at the cemetery’s edge, though their occupants show no unusual disease or trauma. The central building contains no hearth or storage pits, only a raised platform facing the cemetery, benches around the walls, and seventeen deliberately broken deer antlers sealed beneath its floor. In the settlement’s houses, complete antlers are common, but no shell discs occur outside graves. My opening claim is this: although we cannot translate the shell disc as a symbol, the conjunction of restricted placement, standardized bodily treatment, costly architectural coordination, and intentional breakage supports more than aesthetic preference or accidental patterning. Specifically, I think the community very probably shared a belief that death involved a passage requiring correct preparation, and that the central building housed a recurrent funerary institution rather than merely occasional commemorations. Tell me which inference survives scrutiny, which alternative explanation is strongest, and whether you would describe what happened here as evidence of belief, practice, institution, or some narrower combination.
