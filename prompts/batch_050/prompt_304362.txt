"Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason refers to a conflict between two self-evident axioms of practical rationality that he arrives at near the end of The Methods of Ethics. On the one hand there is rational egoism, the principle that each person ought to aim at their own greatest good or happiness. On the other there is rational benevolence, the principle that one ought to aim at the good of all, taking one's own good to be no more important from the point of view of the universe than anyone else's. This second principle is the rational foundation of utilitarianism as Sidgwick understands it. Sidgwick holds that both principles, when clearly reflected on, present themselves as self-evident, and he denies that either can be derived from the other or from anything more basic. The dualism is the problem that they can conflict in particular cases, where pursuing one's own greatest happiness requires acting against the general happiness, so practical reason seems to issue contradictory verdicts. Sidgwick thinks this cannot be dissolved by empirical arguments, such as the claim that doing one's duty always turns out in the long run to be in one's own interest; he examines these coincidence arguments and finds them overstated, since there are cases (for instance where virtue demands sacrificing one's life or prospects with no one the wiser) where duty and self-interest genuinely come apart. He also thinks the conflict cannot be resolved within common-sense morality, since common-sense morality is precisely the egoistic side of the dualism. The only complete solution Sidgwick offers is theological: if God exists and apportions happiness to virtue in an afterlife, duty and interest coincide, and Sidgwick accordingly concludes that theistic belief resolves the contradiction and restores the unity of practical reason. Short of that, the dualism leaves the rationality of ethics fundamentally compromised."
