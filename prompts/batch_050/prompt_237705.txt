Beginning in the 1980s, the United States government commissioned linguists, semioticians, anthropologists, architects, and artists to solve an unusual design problem. The Waste Isolation Pilot Plant in New Mexico was being built to store plutonium-contaminated waste that remains dangerous far longer than recorded human history, and regulators required the site to be marked so that people ten thousand years from now would understand it as hazardous and stay away. The design panels proceeded on the explicit assumption that English, the United States, and every existing institution would likely be gone long before the waste became safe. Their proposals fell into two broad families. The first encoded the warning in dead matter: vast fields of stone spikes, menacing earthworks, and jagged black slabs — deliberately hostile architecture meant to make someone who shares no language with us feel, bodily, that the place was made to repel. The second encoded the warning in living transmission: messages in several languages accompanied by instructions to retranslate them as languages changed, and, most radically, a proposal for an atomic priesthood, a self-perpetuating order that would preserve the warning as ritual knowledge handed down through generations.

Samuel Scheffler has argued that we depend more than we notice on the assumption that humanity will continue after our own deaths — what he calls the afterlife conjecture — and that much of what we value would lose its point if we knew no one would outlive us. What, then, does this marking program presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory: about why far-future strangers are owed costly effort that no reciprocity could motivate, and about what must survive — words, bodily feelings, an unbroken chain of people — for anything we make now to mean anything then. Consider whether the program is internally consistent: its brief presupposes both that humanity will still exist in ten thousand years, since otherwise the markers have no point, and that everything specifically ours will not. Say which commitments the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits well with — would a bare duty not to poison strangers license the entire project, leaving Scheffler's conjecture irrelevant, or does the program's deeper ambition quietly depend on something like it? Assess, too, whether the two families of proposals presuppose compatible theories of how meaning survives, or whether building both hedges between rivals. Finally, say what would have to change — in the designs, or in whether we fund them at all — if we replaced the presupposed view with the conviction that human extinction is imminent and unpreventable.
