Write a dialogue between two old college friends who both donate seriously but have drifted apart on how to do it. One, Priya, has gone all-in on earning to give: she took a job at a proprietary trading firm she finds morally pretty neutral-to-slightly-dodgy, donates about 40% of her salary to GiveWell-style charities, and thinks her marginal dollar counterfactually saves more lives than any other use of her time. The other, Marcus, has become convinced of the institutional critique of effective altruism: he thinks global poverty is mainly produced by things like trade rules, tax havens, and debt structures, and that Priya's approach at best treats symptoms while leaving the machine running, and at worst launders the harms of the very institutions causing the problem.

Give their conversation a concrete occasion: Marcus is about to accept a job as a policy researcher at a mid-size NGO that lobbies on international tax law, and Priya has just told him she thinks he'd do more good quitting that and becoming an accountant. They start arguing over whose career choice actually does more good in expectation, and the argument should pull them into the real meat of the disagreement: replaceability and counterfactual impact (would someone worse take Marcus's NGO job if he declined? would someone slightly-less-ruthless take Priya's trading seat?), whether measurable short-term outcomes bias EA toward what's countable, and whether institutional change can ever be expected-value-compared against bednets at all.

Both of them should be sharp and should actually answer each other's points rather than doing dueling monologues. Don't let either one win cleanly -- end with them having genuinely narrowed their disagreement to one crux they can state precisely, and maybe noticing one thing they still share. Around 600-900 words of dialogue, natural speech, no narration beyond the occasional minimal stage direction.
