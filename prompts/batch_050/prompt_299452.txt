So I just had this long argument with a friend of mine about AI safety and I can't tell who actually won. The topic was whether you could sidestep the whole "AI with goals goes wrong" problem by training AI to want human approval instead of giving it a goal in the world. My friend had read a blog post about this idea — I think it's called approval-directed agents — and she summarised it for me like this, and I want to know how much of her summary is actually fair:

"The proposal is that instead of specifying some goal like make money or cure cancer, you train the AI so that the only thing it optimizes is approval from a human overseer. Every action it takes is one a human has rated highly, and approval is literally all it ever maximizes. That means it has no reason to seek power, deceive anyone, or resist being shut down, because none of those things are what it's optimizing — its instrumental reasoning points at approval rather than at the world. The author's argument is that this is roughly how well-socialized people already work: most of us do things because we'd be approved of for doing them, not because we've calculated long-run consequences, so this kind of agent would be cautious and correctable instead of scheming. And since the whole setup is just training the AI to do what a human would approve of, it's really a fancier version of imitation learning and inherits whatever safety imitation learning has. The author presents this as basically dissolving the alignment problem for goal-directed systems, which is the source of his optimism."

I pushed back on her during the argument because it seemed way too clean, but I couldn't put my finger on exactly where it goes wrong. In particular she kept insisting that an approval-directed agent *couldn't* want to manipulate the human overseeing it, since manipulation isn't approval, and that felt off to me but I lost that exchange.

Can you go through her summary and tell me which parts are an accurate description of the idea, which parts overstate or conflate things, and which parts a defender of the proposal would object to? And once the summary is corrected, does her conclusion — that alignment worries just don't apply to systems built this way — still follow, or does it rest on the parts that are wrong?
