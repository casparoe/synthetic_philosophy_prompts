I run a small reading group that has spent the last year on popular books about societal collapse — Jared Diamond's Collapse, Joseph Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies, Eric Cline's 1177 B.C., Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo's The Statues That Walked, and the essay collection Questioning Collapse edited by Patricia McAnany and Norman Yoffee, which argues against Diamond that most allegedly collapsed societies merely transformed. None of us has any formal training in archaeology or history, and our meetings keep stalling because we talk past each other: one person says the Maya "collapsed," another says there are millions of Maya alive today, and it turns out they agree on every fact and disagree only about what the word means.

I'd like you to build us a decision tree we can use as a handout, titled something like "So you think that society collapsed — what do you actually believe?" The tree should take a reader from the starting commitment "something dramatic and bad happened to this society" to a named position, with roughly seven to nine branch nodes and five to six leaves. At minimum the leaves should include: Tainter-style diminishing returns on complexity; Diamond-style ecological overshoot plus bad choices; Cline-style systems collapse of an interconnected network; the resilience/transformation view associated with McAnany and Yoffee; and at least one more leaf your own analysis of the debate suggests (for instance, collapse as a label imposed by later observers, or collapse as real demographic catastrophe). If a leaf on my list turns out to be unreachable or incoherent, say so and explain why rather than forcing it in.

Three requirements matter most to us:

First, build the tree from the logical space, not from the reading list. Start by identifying the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart — what exactly ends when a society "collapses" (population, state apparatus, cultural tradition, level of complexity), what the mechanism is, and whether collapse is a tragedy, a rational adaptation, or a story we tell afterward — and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Note any unoccupied combination of answers and diagnose it briefly: is that view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

Second, every branch node must be a question a reader can answer from the evidence without knowing which author it leads to, and at least four nodes should hinge on a concrete case: Easter Island (deforestation with introduced rats versus a population crash that arguably begins only with European contact and slaving), the Classic Maya (southern lowland cities abandoned in the ninth century while northern cities thrived and Maya communities persist), and the Late Bronze Age (Mycenaean palaces destroyed and Linear B lost while ordinary habitation continued, and Egypt surviving where Ugarit and the Hittites did not).

Third, flag explicitly any pair of positions your tree cannot cleanly separate, and tell us what further question — factual or conceptual — would be needed to split them.

The goal is to sort people by what they conclude from the evidence, never by asking "which author do you find most persuasive?"
