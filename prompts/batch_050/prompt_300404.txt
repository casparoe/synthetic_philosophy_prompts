I'm a PhD student in philosophy (formal epistemology side of things, dissertation on rational update rules) and this term I'm running one 75-minute session of an advanced undergrad / early grad seminar on probability and rational belief, about 14 students, mixed philosophy and CS backgrounds, and most of them already know basic conditionalization and have at least heard of Jeffrey conditioning. The session is on the Judy Benjamin problem and I really don't want to just do a worksheet -- last time I ran a problem-set session half of them mechanically computed things and nobody was bothered by anything. In case it's not at your fingertips: Judy is airdropped into a square region split into four equal quadrants, Blue-North, Blue-South, Red-North, Red-South, prior of 1/4 on each. HQ radios her exactly one thing: if you're in Red territory, the probability that you're in the northern half is 3/4. Question: what should her credence in being in Blue territory be now? The stubborn intuition is 1/2, since the message says literally nothing about Blue. But if you update by minimizing relative entropy from the uniform prior subject to the new constraint, you get Blue at about 0.53, other reasonable-seeming rules give other answers, and the whole embarassing point (this is van Fraassen's case) is that our favorite update rules disagree here and it's genuinely hard to say which answer is right, or even what "learning a conditional" amounts to.

What I want: an in-class activity or game that makes the students feel this tug-of-war -- the 1/2 intuition versus the machinery's refusal to respect it -- before I ever write "Judy Benjamin" on the board. It has to be runnable with cheap materials (envelopes, cards, tokens, dice are fine), stakes that don't involve real money, and the problem has to emerge from what they do rather than from me telling them: they should make actual choices or public commitments under the rules of the game, then discover that they and their classmates have committed to incompatible answers, and only then see the formal statement. Roles are fine (someone can play HQ sending the message), structured deliberation is fine, but it can't be a lecture with extra steps. In the debrief I want to tie specific moments of the game to the three live positions: the 1/2 intuition and its defenders, the ~0.53 answer from relative-entropy minimization, and the meta-lesson many people draw that the choice of update rule is underdetermined here.
