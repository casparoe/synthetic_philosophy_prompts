Next month I give a department colloquium defending the etiological (selected-effects) account of biological function: roughly, that a trait's function is the effect for which its ancestors were selected, so that function attributions are essentially historical. The audience is a philosophy department with two philosophers of biology who favor causal-role accounts, one philosopher of medicine who cares about how the theory classifies dysfunction, and several generalists; I've also invited a couple of physiologists from the life sciences faculty, who I suspect think the history of a trait is irrelevant to what it does. Prepare me for the Q&A: the objections I'm actually likely to get from this specific mix, ordered by how much damage they would do to the talk, each phrased the way that audience member would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. In particular, make sure to cover the objection that the account cannot distinguish a trait's function from an accidental effect of its ancestors, the objection that malfunction is impossible to ground historically (an organ that never does what it was selected for seems to have a function it fails to perform, not no function at all), the Swampman-style worry about a molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no evolutionary history, and the physiologists' complaint that working biology ascribes functions without consulting phylogeny. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
