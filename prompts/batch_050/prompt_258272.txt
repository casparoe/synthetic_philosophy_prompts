I'm taking a philosophy of language seminar this term, and we just spent two weeks on Quine's radical translation thought experiment — the field linguist, the native informant, the rabbit scurrying past, and the fateful utterance of "gavagai." What struck me, in discussion with a few classmates afterward, is how much quiet philosophical machinery the linguist has to assume before he can even begin: that assent can be recognized independently of translation, that pointing picks out enduring objects rather than temporal stages, undetached parts, or recurrent manifestations, and that our own apparatus of identity, individuation, and counting can simply be projected onto the alien language. Our professor kept emphasizing that the thought experiment is meant to humble the linguist, but in the standard retellings the linguist always sounds so briskly confident. I'd like to see that confidence itself made into the joke.

Please write a parody in the form of excerpts from an old-fashioned field linguist's expedition journal — the sort of cheerfully self-assured monograph a Victorian or early-twentieth-century researcher might publish upon returning from the field — in which the narrator works through the gavagai scenario and triumphantly "solves" the translation problem roughly every other entry. About 600 words, deadpan throughout, dated entries. The comedy should come entirely from the linguist's blindness: each entry should show him eliminating a worry by means of an assumption he never notices making, so that a reader who knows the material watches him smuggle in the entire ontology (objects rather than rabbit-stages, whole animals rather than undetached rabbit-parts, assent identified before anything has been translated, "analytical hypotheses" dressed up as discoveries) while congratulating himself on his rigor. The final entry should declare the translation complete and the matter settled, in a tone of total satisfaction, at precisely the moment the reader can see that nothing has been settled at all.

The irony must stay legible without winking: no narrator asides, no lampshading, just the linguist's own complacent prose doing the work. A reader unfamiliar with Quine should still find it funny as a portrait of scholarly overconfidence, but someone fresh from the seminar room should recognize that every joke lands on a real feature of the indeterminacy argument rather than a caricature of it. The target is the fantasy that ostension and patient observation could ever, by themselves, pin down reference — not Quine, and not field linguistics as a discipline.
