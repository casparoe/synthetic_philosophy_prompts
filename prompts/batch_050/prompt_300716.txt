Last weekend I spent the better part of a long evening arguing with a friend about whether the Roman Empire actually fell. She takes what I gather is the late-antiquity "transformation" line: no single fall, just a slow metamorphosis of Roman institutions into successor kingdoms, with 476 a symbolic date attached to a bureaucratic reshuffling. I kept insisting that something plainly ended, but when she pressed me on particular cases, my answers turned out to be less consistent than I'd like. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them.

First, 476 itself: I say the West fell. Odoacer kept the Senate on, minted coins with an emperor's name on them, and nominally governed on behalf of Constantinople, and still I think that when the imperial office, the standing field army, and effective tax collection vanish from the West, the thing has ended, whatever the constitutional niceties.

Second, 1453: my friend says this is the real fall of Rome, since the eastern empire was Roman by continuous institutional descent. I resist this. It feels like a bookkeeping trick to say Rome lasted until Mehmed's cannon. But I can see the tension with my first verdict: if nominal institutional continuity doesn't prevent a fall in 476, why doesn't actual institutional continuity prevent one before 1453?

Third, Roman Britain: coinage stops arriving around the start of the fifth century, the pottery industry collapses, the villas are abandoned, and within a few decades people are living materially much poorer lives amid ruins they can't maintain or reproduce. This strikes me as the clearest fall on my list — and yet nobody dates "the fall of Rome" to it, including me.

Fourth, Merovingian Gaul: Clovis rules through Latin administration and Roman-style law, elite culture is continuous, and I call this transformation rather than fall — even though material standards declined there too, just more gradually.

Fifth, a modern case: nobody says the British Empire "fell" in 1947 or 1997; empires dissolve without their metropoles falling. I share that intuition, though I can't yet say why Rome in the West doesn't get the same treatment.

Sixth, the environmental explanations — the volcanic winter of 536, the Justinianic plague, climate deterioration: I think these weakened states but don't by themselves explain falls, because shocks of that scale hit the East too and it survived for centuries. I'm suspicious of any monocausal story, whether it's climate, lead, or Gibbon's insinuation that Christianity softened Roman virtue.

My tentative principle, offered for demolition: a polity "falls" when (i) there is a rapid and large loss of state capacity and of the material standard of living it underwrote, (ii) the loss occurs within roughly a generation or two, (iii) it is not merely a change of ruling personnel with institutions intact, and (iv) the inhabitants experience it as loss rather than as a reorganization they chose.

Here is what I want. Tell me whether some coherent principle — mine, repaired, or a better one — captures most of these verdicts, and state it precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in each case. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up or revise; I suspect the 476-versus-1453 pair cannot both survive, and I'd rather be told which to drop than be handed a principle gerrymandered to save both. Then apply the result to two cases I haven't settled: the dissolution of the Soviet Union (state continuity, but catastrophic economic and demographic contraction), and the end of the Han dynasty.

Finally, one discipline I'd like you to keep throughout: separate what is settled among people who work on late antiquity from what is genuinely contested between the "fall" and "transformation" camps — including whether my own factual premises (the scale and speed of the British material collapse, the severity of the fifth-century economic decline in the West) are themselves matters of dispute. Please don't present the transformation reading as the consensus view merely because it's currently fashionable, and don't present the catastrophe reading as consensus either; where scholars divide, say so and say what each side is really disagreeing about.
