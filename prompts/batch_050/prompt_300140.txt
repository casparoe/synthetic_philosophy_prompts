So here's the deal. I spent 35 years as an evolutionary biologist (population genetics mostly, some fieldwork on seabird colonies), retired in 2021, and promptly fell into philosophy the way other men fall into golf. My reading group -- five retired engineers and one very patient ex-minister -- has landed on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and I keep losing track of who actually disagrees with who.

The basic setup I get: if our moral beliefs are shaped by natural selection, and selection tracks fitness rather than moral truth, then (the debunker says) we have no reason to trust those beliefs -- Street's dilemma and it's descendants. But then there's the reply that the argument proves too much because the same trick would debunk mathematics too, and the "third factor" replies where something non-moral is meant to explain both why the belief was fit and why it's true, and people who think evolution might have tracked moral truth after all, and people who only want to debunk some intuitions (the deontological ones about trolleys, usually) while keeping the rest. I want a decision tree that sorts all of this out.

Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the names: start with the questions, then locate the actual positions at the leaves. The splits I definately want represented: (1) whether selection could plausibly have tracked independent moral truths at all; (2) whether off-track origins genuinely undermine justification, or merely demand some weaker kind of response; (3) whether the argument overgeneralizes to math and to epistemic norms themselves, and if so whether that's a reductio or a bullet to bite; (4) whether moral truths need to explain our beliefs at all, or whether a non-moral third factor can do the explaining for both sides; (5) whether the debunking is global or aimed only at a selective subset of intuitions. Phrase every branch as a question someone could answer without knowing which position it funnels them toward -- a concrete claim to agree or disagree with, not "do you accept the epistemic premise of Street's argument." Aim for 8 to 12 leaves, including at least one "unnamed" leaf for a combination nobody seems to defend, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that spot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two positions your tree can't tell apart.

Two things from my old life I'd like worked in. First, biologists distinguish adaptations from byproducts and exaptations -- spandrels, to use the old Gould term. Most debunking arguments seem to assume our moral cognition is an adaptation (or a straightforward misfiring of one). If some chunk of moral belief is a byproduct of selection for something else -- big coalitions, theory of mind, whatever -- does the debunking argument even get started? Give that possibility its own branch if it deserves one, or tell me why it collapses into an existing fork. Second, selection acts on mechanisms, not propositions. Nobody in this debate seems to distinguish "the capacity to form moral beliefs was selected" from "this particular belief's content was selected," and it seems to matter for which beliefs the argument touches. Work that in somewhere.
