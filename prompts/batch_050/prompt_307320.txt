I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a profile of an epistemologist known for her work on testimony. I have her verdicts on a series of cases, and before the piece goes to print I want to predict how she would rule on a new one, so that I don't misstate her position. Her verdicts so far: a visitor to an unfamiliar city asks a stranger for directions and believes what he is told -- knowledge. A six-year-old believes her parents' explanation of thunderstorms without checking anything -- knowledge. A man believes a colleague's office gossip despite knowing the colleague has lied about such matters before -- no knowledge. A reader believes a magazine claim only after independently confirming the author's track record -- knowledge. Someone believes a passerby's confident answer to "what time is it?" -- knowledge. A hearer believes testimony that flatly contradicts what she saw with her own eyes -- no knowledge. The new case: a researcher overhears, through a wall, an unidentified voice stating that a particular bridge is closed; she has no information about the speaker, no way to check their reliability, and no particular reason to doubt them; she believes it, and it is true. Predict her verdict and say which rule she appears to be running -- a reductionist requirement that hearers possess positive reasons to trust a speaker, an anti-reductionist default entitlement absent defeaters, or some third principle, such as one keyed to whether testimony is addressed to the hearer rather than merely overheard. Identify which of her earlier verdicts genuinely constrain the prediction and which are equally compatible with the rival rules, and name the further case that would most cleanly settle the matter. Finally, state how confident you are in the prediction overall, and single out the specific point in your reasoning about which you are least sure.
