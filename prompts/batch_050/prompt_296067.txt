ok so I've been TA-ing a formal epistemology seminar and I've basically become the Jeffrey conditionalization person, and I want to check whether the package I've been defending actually hangs together or whether I've been helping myself to inconsistent things. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Uncertain evidence is everywhere — the jellybean glimpse in bad lighting, the announcement I half-heard, the taste I'm not quite sure is almond — and Jeffrey's rule is the uniquely rational response: reprice the partition of propositions the experience directly bears on, while holding my conditional credences given each cell of that partition fixed (the rigidity bit is what makes it *my* evidence doing the work rather than free-floating reshuffling).

2. Ordinary strict conditionalization is just the degenerate case where a cell goes to 1, so there's one unified Bayesian story here, no separate update rule for certainties.

3. Ideal rationality is order-insensitive: if me and my doppelgänger recieve the same total evidence in different orders, we should converge on the same final credences. Any theory that lets my endpoint depend on the sequence in which I happened to open my mail is pathologial.

4. Path-independence more generally: if I Jeffrey-shift on {E, ¬E} tonight and then tomorrow I get conclusive, take-it-to-the-bank proof of E, I should end up exactly where I'd have ended if I'd just learned E outright tonight. The detour through uncertainty shouldn't leave any residue once certainty arrives.

5. Costless evidence is always welcome: before making a decision it can never be rational to refuse free, decision-relevant information. I'm a Good's-theorem-style person on this — more info, weakly better off, end of story.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I'd like an audit, not a survey of the literature: the tensions ranked by severity, and for each one what specifically has to give and what it costs me to drop it. If one of my commitments is doing hidden work in an argument for another, flag that too.

Lead with the verdict in your first sentence — which commitments can't survive together — then give the audit. No preamble, and don't restate my list back at me.
