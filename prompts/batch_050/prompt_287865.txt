I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am trying to build a reusable pre-publication check into my own workflow for stories with an ethical dimension: pieces about researchers accused of misconduct, reporting on contested findings whose coverage could affect public behavior, profiles where a subject has disclosed something damaging in confidence, and the like. I make such decisions perhaps twice a month, usually within a day or two, sometimes within hours, and I answer to an editor but the first call is mine.

I have been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I would like his device of the impartial spectator turned into a procedure I can actually run. Smith's idea, as I understand it, is not simply to ask how others would judge me, since actual spectators are partial, ill-informed, or swayed by fashion, but to imagine how a well-informed and disinterested onlooker, with no stake in the outcome and full knowledge of my situation and motives, would view my conduct; and he insists this internal judge is needed precisely because our self-love distorts what we can see when our own interests are engaged.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what a passing answer looks like at each step, where the procedure terminates in a clear do-not-publish or do-not-name, and where it must hand the decision to an editor or colleague because the remaining question is one of values rather than of judgment. I want the translation to be honest about what it preserves and what it flattens. In particular, tell me how the procedure should handle two features of Smith's account that seem to resist operationalization: his claim that the imagined spectator is itself shaped by the norms of one's own society, which threatens to make the test a rubber stamp for whatever my profession currently tolerates, and his distinction between what is actually praised and what is genuinely praiseworthy, since my newsroom rewards speed and traffic rather than fairness.

Then run the procedure on two cases: a profile subject who told me, on the record but in an unguarded moment, about a past episode of research sloppiness, where naming it would make the piece; and a story about a small study with alarming findings that I could publish tonight, ahead of competitors, before independent replication exists. I expect at least one of these to produce an answer I will dislike. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where Smith's framework itself, rather than my application of it, runs out.
