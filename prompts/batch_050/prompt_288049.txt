I am to give the keynote at a dinner marking the retirement of my father, a machinist of thirty-eight years, and the occasion is an awkward one. His plant is converting to largely automated production; roughly half the guests are his fellow tradespeople, several of whom expect their own positions to be eliminated within a few years, and the other half are the engineers who designed and installed the new systems, some of whom my father genuinely likes and mentored. My father is also a careful, longtime reader of Marx, and the view he has argued for as long as I can remember is not the cartoon version: he holds that Marx's actual position is that the machinery itself is not the workers' enemy, that machine-breaking mistakes the instrument for the arrangement, and that the very same automation which is dissolving his trade could, under different relations of ownership and different decisions about what the saved labor-time is for, have shortened his working life instead of ending his craft. Write a ten-minute speech that honors him by getting this distinction right -- between machinery as such and the particular social use to which it is put, and between labor-saving in principle and the deskilling and pace-setting he in fact experienced -- without lapsing into either of the two failures the room is primed for: anti-technology resentment that would insult the engineers and, worse, misrepresent his thinking, or a cheerful ode to progress that would tell his angry friends their anger is a misunderstanding. The speech should build on one concrete image from the shop floor (you may invent a plausible one), should contain no jargon that the engineers' non-technical spouses couldn't follow, and should end in a register that lets both halves of the room raise a glass to him without either half feeling the toast was addressed to the other.
