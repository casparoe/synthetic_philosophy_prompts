got into an argument at a dinner party last week where someone wheeled out the lottery analogy against Hume on miracles: if your friend tells you she won the lottery, that's testimony for something astronomically improbable, and yet you believe her on her word alone, so Hume's idea that the improbability of the event has to be outweighed by the unlikelihood of the testimony being false can't be the whole story. the conclusion it was made to carry was that testimony for a miracle could in principle be rationally believed, even for us moderns.

audit the analogy properly. give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the prior probability of the specific event, the base rate of that *kind* of event happening at all, the reliability of the testifier, and the number of alternative would-be testifiers who didn't get a ticket. then the breaking points. the one i noticed at the time is that we know for a fact there are lottery winners every single week, whereas whether the relevant kind of event ever happens is exactly what's in dispute with miracles -- but i suspect there are other disanalogies that matter more for the anti-humean conclusion, like whether the pool of possible witnesses scales with actual winners or with all ticket-holders, and whether the improbability of a miracle is the same *type* of improbability as a lottery win at all.

then the verdict: does the anti-humean conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? does the answer change depending on whether you read Hume's argument as basically Bayesian or as something else? and if neither the lottery analogy nor Hume's own appeal to uniform experience quite fits the structure of the problem, propose the analogy you'd use instead and say what it would license that theirs can't.
