A recent episode of a philosophy podcast summarized Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness (written in the 1740s, published only after her death) as follows: "Du Châtelet's recipe for happiness has five ingredients: freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, lively tastes and passions, and -- her most original condition -- susceptibility to illusions. On her view, seeing things exactly as they are is the enemy of happiness: since almost all our pleasures are born of illusion, the wise person deliberately cultivates comforting self-deceptions and keeps expectations modest. Among the available pleasures she ranks the love of study first, because it is the one good that depends entirely on ourselves, and she writes ruefully of her own passions for gambling and love as cases where desire mastered her. The upshot is a proto-Stoic doctrine: happiness is wholly a matter of managing one's own mind, so fortune is irrelevant to it." How fair is this summary? Which parts would a careful reader of the Discourse accept, and which subtly distort her position? I am particularly suspicious of the move from "susceptible to illusions" to "deliberately cultivates self-deceptions," and of the Stoic gloss, but I can't quite articulate what is wrong with them. Does she really think clear-sightedness is incompatible with happiness, and is the tension the summary papers over -- between prizing liberation from prejudice and recommending illusion -- resolvable within her view? Finally, does the concluding claim that fortune is irrelevant follow from anything she actually argues?
