The debate about whether you can assign probabilities to your own future choices while you're still deliberating has been going for decades and seems completely stuck. On one side, people (following Levi, Spohn, that crowd) say deliberation requires treating your own acts as *not yet* subject to prediction -- that if I genuinely saw my choice as having a probability, I'd be spectating on myself rather than choosing, and deliberation would collapse into just waiting to see what I do. On the other side, plenty of decision theorists and pretty much every forecaster, bookie, and calibration nerd treats self-prediction as obviously fine and often useful -- I can know I have about a 30% chance of caving and ordering dessert and still deliberate about whether to. Nobody seems to move anyone.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions -- one about what the *act of deliberating* presupposes from the inside, the other about what credences a rational agent can stably hold -- and the whole thing looks stalled because 'assigning a probability to your own choice' means something different in the two frames. But maybe it's more substantive than that: maybe there's a real question about whether deliberation-credible-while-predicted is psychologically or formally coherent, and one side is just wrong. Diagnose it using what each side actually argues, and tell me what would count as progress -- a formal result (does the impossibility stuff around self-prediction and reflective stability settle anything?), a distinction that dissolves it, or an argument that the impasse is here to stay. Please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's still contested, and don't present a contested take as the consensus view.
