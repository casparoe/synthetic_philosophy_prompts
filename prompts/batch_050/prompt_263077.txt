**How Deep Are a Chatbot's Beliefs?**

Forum fights about whether language models "really believe" anything follow a script by now. One side says: the system predicts tokens, there is no fact of the matter about what it believes, attributing beliefs to it is anthropomorphic projection. The other side says: ask it where the Eiffel Tower is, it tells you, end of story. I want to suggest both sides share a mistake: they treat belief as a binary property a system either has or lacks, and then fight over which side of the line LLMs fall on.

Start with Dennett's intentional stance, which I take to be the natural home turf for this community. On that view, to attribute beliefs to a system is to adopt a predictive strategy: treat it as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, and predict its behavior accordingly. The attribution is legitimate when the strategy works — when there is a real, compressible pattern in the system's behavior that the belief-talk tracks. A belief's reality is the reality of a pattern; the pattern need not be explicitly written into the mechanism anywhere, any more than a center of gravity is.

So far, so familiar. But LLMs pose a problem Dennett's usual examples do not. A thermostat, a chess computer, and a human all exhibit behavior that is relatively stable across how you frame your interactions with them. An LLM's outputs, by contrast, are exquisitely framing-sensitive. The same model will assert a proposition in one conversational register and deny it in another; it will defend consequentialism if you address it as a philosopher and mock consequentialism if you address it as a critic. What the behavioral pattern actually supports, then, is not "the model believes p" but something indexed and local: "the model, addressed in register r, behaves as one who believes p." This is still a real pattern — the stance is permissive — but it is a shallow one. The binary debate dissolves, and what replaces it is a question of degree: how deep are these attributions?

I propose we make depth a graded, measurable notion along three axes. An attribution is deeper to the extent that (i) it survives perturbations of surface framing — rephrasing, persona shifts, changes of language; (ii) it is load-bearing in predicting behavior in domains far removed from the evidence that motivated the attribution; and (iii) it integrates coherently with the other attributions one must make to predict the system, forming a web rather than a pile of disconnected local dispositions.

Here is a first pass at operationalizing this, which I'll call a belief audit. Elicit a commitment from the model in context A — say, that some medical intervention is risky. Then, in separate sessions, construct contexts B, C, D that are downstream of that commitment: a triage scenario, an insurance-pricing scenario, a scenario where a fictional character holds the view — each designed so that the model cannot answer by echoing A. The depth of the original attribution is, roughly, how far it travels.

The obvious objection: the audit doesn't measure belief, it measures role-play fidelity — the model's skill at inferring what behavior the conversational frame calls for. My reply is that this objection proves too much and then exactly the right amount. For stance purposes, if role-play fidelity transfers robustly across arbitrary contexts, the distinction from belief has stopped doing predictive work and may be no distinction at all. But if it fails to transfer — and my strong suspicion is that for current models it fails quickly and characteristically — then the audit tells us precisely what the alignment-relevant property is and how much of it there is.

This brings me to why I think the depth vocabulary matters. Alignment discussions in our corner of the internet oscillate between "it's a mere autocomplete" and "the model wants X and will scheme to get it," often within the same comment thread, without flagging that these are claims at different levels of description that require different evidence. Recast in terms of depth, the disagreement becomes empirical rather than metaphysical: are there attributions — about goals, about self-models, about the training process itself — that are deep in my three-axis sense? My tentative claim is that current chat-facing models support mostly shallow attributions, and that the serious alignment concern is not about present chat behavior but about whether training processes can produce depth as a side effect of producing capability — a claim about the future trajectory of audits, not about essences.
