I spent my career in experimental physics, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy of mathematics and probability, so please bear with me if my terminology is imprecise. Here is the puzzle that brought me to you. Some years ago our lab used a Geiger-counter-based device to generate strings of bits for calibration purposes. Out of curiosity, a colleague once checked whether a particular 2,000-bit string we had recorded could be compressed, and found that it compressed down to about 1,400 bits -- not because the device was faulty, as far as we could tell, but simply by chance. My physicist's instinct is that the string is random because it was *produced* by a random process, full stop; but my recent reading suggests that the algorithmic tradition (Kolmogorov, Martin-Löf, and so on) wants randomness to be a property of the *string itself*, roughly: a string is random if no substantially shorter description of it exists. By that standard our string seems to fail, and moreover I gather the standard theorems about incompressibility are rigorously proven only for infinite sequences, with finite strings left in an awkward position because any complexity measure depends on the choice of universal machine, up to an additive constant.

Could you explain, at an intuitive but not superficial level, why the algorithmic tradition deliberately relocates randomness from the process to the product, and what the strongest reasons are for thinking a finite sequence nonetheless cannot be *absolutely* random -- only random relative to a machine, a threshold, or some stipulated background? I am particularly interested in whether the "up to an additive constant" problem is merely a nuisance that washes out for long strings or whether it points to something conceptually deep, and in how defenders of finite-string randomness try to draw a non-arbitrary line between, say, a string that compresses by one bit and one that compresses by half. Please avoid pointing me to the literature; I would like the conceptual landscape itself laid out, including where the view strains.

Finally, I would be grateful if you would address a tension I cannot resolve on my own: if no finite string can be definitely classified as random, then it seems my colleague and I could never be *entitled* to reject the hypothesis that a suspicious-looking output came from a fair random process, no matter how compressible it was -- which would make statistical testing of physical random number generators philosophically groundless. But surely something licenses our everyday and scientific practice of declaring a generator biased. Is the resolution that randomness-of-finite-things is really always shorthand for a claim about the generating process, or can the algorithmic view recover a legitimate sense in which a finite output itself counts as random or non-random?
