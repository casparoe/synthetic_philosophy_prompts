I'm a retired biochemist who spent forty years on protein folding and only started reading philosophy properly last year, and I keep coming back to A. J. Ayer. Most people know him as the logical positivist of Language, Truth and Logic who spent his career saying talk of the afterlife wasnt even false, just meaningless. But late in life, in 1988, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, his heart stopped for several minutes, and he was technically dead. He wrote about it in an essay called "What I Saw When I Was Dead" in the National Review, and then, after some of his humanist friends got nervous, he wrote a follow-up piece in The Spectator.

The part that interests me as a scientist is what he did and didnt concede. In his account of the experience he saw a strange red light he took to be governing the universe, and some figures he understood as the ministers in charge of space and time, quarrelling on the bank of a river he kept trying and failing to cross. But in the follow-up he was very careful: the episode had not shaken his conviction that his real death, which he knew was coming soon, would be the end of him -- he even said he hoped it would be, since he had no taste for an afterlife. What it had weakened, he said, was not the belief itself but his rigid, inflexible way of holding it. He'd gone from being certain to being almost certain, and he insisted those were different things.

I'd like a sonnet (Petrarchan or Shakespearean, your choice, but say which and why) about this. Here's the constraint: the volta should land exactly on that distinction -- belief unchanged, attitude toward the belief softened. The octave should give us the dying man and the red light and the quarrelling ministers of space and time, and the turn should be the moment of waking and taking stock, where the poem has to dramatize how someone can keep the same view and yet no longer hold it in the same way. Don't let the poem turn him into a deathbed convert, because he explicitly wasnt one, but dont let it pretend nothing happened either. If the closing couplet or final tercet can capture why "almost certain" is a genuinely different intellectual position than "certain" -- not a weaker version of the same thing -- that would be ideal.

Aim for accuracy enough that someone who knows the episode would nod along. Please write in American English.
