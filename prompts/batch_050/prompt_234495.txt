So here's the situation. I spent fourty years as an industrial chemist (process safety, mostly -- I'm the guy who used to sign off on whether a plant could vent trace amounts of something into a neighborhood where nobody signed anything consenting to it). Since retiring I've been reading moral philosophy, and I've gotten stuck on the ethics of imposing risks on people without their consent.

Here's my half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. Everyone talks about consent as the thing that legitimizes risk imposition, but it seems to me real life runs on a different mechanism that nobody has properly theorized: reciprocity. I impose a small risk on you by driving to the store; you impose the same risk on me driving to work. Neither of us consented, exactly, but the symmetry seems to be doing moral work -- we'd both think it very different if one person got to drive and everyone else had to walk. My suspicion is that "symmetric risk imposition" functions as a kind of consent-substitute in practice, and I want to know whether that can be defended philosophically or whether it collapses under scrutiny (e.g., what happens when the risks are symmetric in probability but asymmetric in who bears the actual harm, or when some people can't participate in the risk-imposing activity at all -- kids can't drive).

I'm pitching a small independent-research grant (think 4-5 months, part-time). Write me the proposal, maybe a page and a half. It needs: the question stated precisely enough that a referee could tell whether I'd answered it; why the existing positions on risk imposition and consent don't settle the reciprocity question; what I'd actually do (this is armchair work -- what does armchair work concretely consist of, month by month); what a positive and a negative result would each look like; and what would count as the project failing. Skip citations, I'll dig those up myself.

Then at the end, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "research_question" (one sentence), "core_hypothesis", "main_objection" (the strongest reason the hypothesis might fail), "method" (list of strings, one per project phase), "deliverable", "failure_condition" (how I'd know the project didn't work), and "why_fundable" (one sentence a program officer could repeat). Valid JSON please, no trailing commas.
