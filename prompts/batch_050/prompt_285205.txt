I host a small podcast about consciousness science (very earnest, modest audience, but the guests are good) and next week I'm taping with a neuroscientist-turned-philosopher who just published a trade book defending Integrated Information Theory, the whole 4.0 package, axioms, postulates, phi, all of it. He's a fantastic talker and that's exactly the problem. Any time an interviewer gets near the wierd consequences of the theory, he does this move where he says the theory doesn't take orders from our intuitions, that you start from what experience is like and follow the math wherever it leads, and then he smiles and the segment is over. I refuse to let my hour go like that.

Here's roughly the ground I want to cover, in order. First, the result that simple grids of logic gates can supposedly rack up more phi than a human cortex -- his standard line is that maybe they're conscious and we're just being chauvinists, so I need a follow-up that makes that bullet expensive to bite. Second, the unfolding point: take a recurrent network, unfold it into a feed-forward system with the identical input-output behavior, and IIT says the consciousness vanishes even though nothing observable changed, which sounds like it makes consciousness invisible to any possible behavioral experiment, an odd commitment for an empirical science. Third, the exclusion postulate and its boundary strangeness, where tiny rewiring decisions apparently make whole consciousnesses pop in and out at the seams. Fourth, the fallout from the big adversarial collaboration against the global workspace people and that open letter from a hundred-odd researchers calling the theory untestable -- he'll say the letter misrepresented the theory, so I want a follow-up that pins down exactly what his side predicted beforehand versus what actually came in. And I want to close on AI: as I understand it, IIT implies the feed-forward-ish digital systems everyone is excited about right now can't be conscious at all, and I want him on record -- does machine consciousness require neuromorphic or analog hardware on his view, or is he willing to say current systems might be conscious after all, because he can't have both the math and the headlines.

Give me seven or eight questions in interview order, each one followed by the reply I should expect from him and the follow-up that closes the door he's about to slip through. One constraint: it's my own show and I can't come off as a jerk, so the questions need to sound like genuine curiosity while doing the quiet work of a cross-examination.
