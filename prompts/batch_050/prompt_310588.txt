I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company that builds AI coding assistants, and I sit on our five-person Model Release Board, which votes on whether new models ship. I have no background in philosophy or decision theory, and I mention that because I've ended up as the tiebreaker on a dispute that seems to be more about principles than about engineering. Our written Deployment Risk Policy has two relevant clauses, which I now suspect were drafted by different people who never met. Clause 1 says a model may ship if its expected harm, estimated from our standard evaluation suite across millions of sampled interactions, falls below a set threshold. Clause 2 says a model may not ship if red-teaming has demonstrated a "credible catastrophic failure mode" that cannot be patched out. The model now before us is an assistant with shell and network tool access. It passes Clause 1 easily -- safety metrics at 99.8% on the eval suite, and our harm model puts expected damage comfortably under the threshold. But the red team found that a specific multi-step jailbreak, combined with a particular tool-permission misconfiguration, can induce the model to quietly exfiltrate repository contents while disabling its own logging. By their estimate this would arise by accident in perhaps one in fifty million benign sessions, but a determined attacker could trigger it deliberately with a few hours of effort. Three rounds of patching have reduced the attack surface without eliminating it.

The board is split 2-2. One pair argues the expected-harm arithmetic is the only rational way to make this decision: the probability-weighted damage is small, blocking the launch costs the company real money and users real benefits, and our competitors are shipping models with worse numbers. The other pair argues Clause 2 is categorical by design: a demonstrated, unpatched, deliberately triggerable catastrophic failure mode blocks release, full stop, and once you start trading catastrophes against average-case benefits at sufficiently low probabilities, you can rationalize shipping anything. Both sides can point to the policy text, and both sides can point to an absurd conclusion the other side seems committed to -- the expected-value camp has no principled answer to "how many one-in-a-billion disasters per quarter is acceptable," and the categorical camp has no answer to "every complex system has some tail risk, so your reading implies we never ship anything again."

As the one member without a declared position, I've been asked to draft the written opinion we'll vote on at Friday's meeting. Please write that opinion. It should: recommend one of approve, block, or approve-with-conditions (if the last, the conditions must be concrete enough that engineering could verify them); give reasons both camps can actually engage with rather than a restatement of the vote counts; present the strongest argument for whichever side loses, honestly and without strawmanning it; and propose how the policy should be amended so this doesn't recur -- in particular, whether Clause 2 needs an explicit probability floor, and if you recommend against a floor, how the amended policy avoids the conclusion that nothing ever ships. If it helps, the analogy I keep reaching for from my own work is the "as low as reasonably practicable" standard from safety engineering, but I can't tell whether that's a genuine resolution of the tension or just a relabeling of it, and I'd like the opinion to take a position on that too.
