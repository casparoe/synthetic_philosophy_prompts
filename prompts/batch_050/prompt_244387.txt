I've been teaching an undergraduate seminar on liberty for years, and every time I reach the unit on positive, negative, and republican freedom I find that my own considered verdicts on cases resist the tidy three-way taxonomy I put on the syllabus. I'd like help figuring out what principle, if any, actually underlies my judgments. You should approach this as a political philosopher with deep expertise in theories of liberty: think carefully, and before settling on an answer, privately sketch a few candidate principles and test each against the full set of cases rather than latching onto the first one that fits a majority. Here are my verdicts. A prisoner locked in a cell is unfree to leave, obviously. A traveler handed a credible threat by a highwayman is unfree to keep his money, even though he technically hands it over himself. A severe addict is unfree with respect to the drug, even though no one and nothing external stands in her way. Odysseus lashed to the mast at his own prior request is, I want to say, free in the relevant sense, despite straining against the ropes. A servant whose master is kind and never interferes, but who retains the unchecked power to interfere at whim, is unfree even on days when the master is on holiday. Yet a citizen living under an archaic statute that criminalizes her conduct and is never, ever enforced strikes me as free, and I confess I cannot fully articulate why this differs from the servant's case. A person too poor to afford the flight out of a disaster zone is, I judge, unable to leave but not thereby unfree. And finally, a person raised from birth by an authoritarian order so that she only ever desires what the order permits, and gets all of it, seems to me unfree despite satisfying every desire she has. I notice tensions here: my addict verdict and my poverty verdict pull apart on whether constraint must be external, and my servant verdict and my dormant-statute verdict pull apart on whether unexercised power dominates. What I'd like from you is the most precise principle, or small family of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated with enough care that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case; an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one or two you would counsel me to abandon; and the resulting view's verdict on two cases I have not listed: a person whose choices are quietly and reliably steered by a recommendation algorithm she trusts, and a dissident whom the state could easily silence but has, so far, chosen not to touch. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
