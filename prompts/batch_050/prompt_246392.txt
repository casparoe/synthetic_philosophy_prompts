I am a novelist, and my trade depends on sentences of the form "she decided to leave, and so she left" — sentences in which a state of mind makes something happen. Write me a poem about the causal exclusion argument: the worry that if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, then the decision is either identical to some neural event or causally idle, and the further worry that calling the act "overdetermined," like a death by two firing squads, does not actually help. Give the poem a form that enacts the problem: exactly five stanzas, each built from two competing voices — one speaking in the vocabulary of reasons (wanting, believing, meaning to), the other in the vocabulary of neurons, muscles, and physics — with the rule that in each stanza only one voice may close, so the reader can feel completeness doing its excluding work. In the fifth stanza let the two voices attempt the final line together, and let the poem itself judge whether that is a solution or a refusal. No prose commentary before or after — just the poem. A philosopher of mind should be able to recognize closure, exclusion, and the overdetermination reply at work in the structure rather than merely named in the lines; a novelist should be able to feel what is at stake for the character.
