I am designing the problem set for a graduate seminar on essentialism about persons, and before I assign any readings I want the seminar's shared standard on the table. The topic is the necessity of origins: the claim, associated with broadly Kripkean views, that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones she in fact came from, and the disputes over how far such a claim extends.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the essentiality (or contingency) of a person's origins must satisfy. By a theory here I mean any account that purports to say which alternative origins for a given person are genuinely possible, whether framed in terms of transworld identity across possible worlds, counterpart relations, haecceities, or something else.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum, your list should address the following, but add any requirements you think I have missed:

1. The theory must deliver a verdict on the canonical case: whether you could have come from a numerically different sperm-egg pair. It must also say whether the verdict is a deliverance of intuition, a theorem from more basic principles, or a stipulation, and the list should require the theory to be honest about which.
2. It must handle the symmetric "reverse" case: whether the very same sperm and egg, united at a different time or under different conditions, could have produced a numerically different person. A theory that answers only the first question is incomplete.
3. It must respect the difference between the necessity of an origin and the sufficiency of an origin, and not let an argument for one masquerade as an argument for the other.
4. It must give a principled treatment of gradual-replacement cases: a sequence of possible worlds in which the originating gametes are altered one molecule at a time, with adjacent worlds differing by an amount too small to change anyone's identity. Either the theory blocks the Sorites somewhere defensible or it accepts the slippery conclusion and owns the cost.
5. It must say something non-embarrassing about monozygotic twins, where one fertilized egg yields two people.
6. It must be consistent with, or explicitly revise, the deliverances of reproductive biology it implicitly relies on — for instance, that "the same sperm" across possible worlds is itself a notion that may need the very identity principles the theory is supposed to supply, so that the account risks circularity.
7. It must not smuggle in the conclusion by choosing a modal framework in which the rival view cannot even be stated (for instance, a counterpart-theoretic framework should be able to represent the necessity-of-origins claim as a substantive thesis about the counterpart relation, not as an artifact of the semantics).

For each desideratum, state the motivation, and mark it as non-negotiable for any adequate theory or as a commitment of a particular camp. Then identify any subset of the desiderata that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, whether the Sorites-blocking requirement conflicts with the requirement that identity facts not turn on arbitrarily small physical differences — since an inconsistent specification is itself a substantive result about the debate. Conclude with a brief scorecard: how the straightforward necessity-of-origins thesis, a sufficiency-only view, a haecceitist view on which origins are irrelevant, and a counterpart-theoretic treatment each fare against the list, and which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidates. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scoring.
