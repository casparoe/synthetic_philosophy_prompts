I spend too much time reading AI labs' safety frameworks and alot of alignment forum posts, and there's a move that shows up everywhere: the plan for staying safe once we have very capable AI systems is basically to have a very capable AI on our side. It monitors the other AIs for scheming, hardens our infrastructure, red-teams our deployments, maybe does alignment research better than humans can. Good AI as the answer to bad AI.

Buried under this, I think, is an assumption I've seen called the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, that for any strategy a misaligned AI could use to pursue its goals, an aligned AI of similar capability could run the same strategy (or an analogue of it) to advance human values just as well — so being on humanity's side doesn't put a system at a systematic strategic disadvantage.

What I want to know is what this whole good-AI-stops-bad-AI approach philosophically presupposes. Some things I suspect are in there but can't pin down:

- Something about the relation between values and strategies — that strategies are basically value-neutral tools, and any goal can be slotted into whatever works? That having good ends doesn't constrain your means in any way that actually costs you?
- Something about what human values even are — does the plan only make sense if values are a kind of objective function you can throw compute at, rather than things that can only be advanced through particular kinds of action or relationship (trust you can't fake, legitimacy, care, solidarity)?
- Something about offense and defense being at least roughly symmetric?

And here's what really bugs me: the same community that leans on good-AI-defense also produces arguments that misaligned AI would have big structural advantages — deception being easier than lie-detection, first strikes, treacherous turns, the attacker only needing one gap while the defender has to cover everything. So does the practice presuppose things that contradict the same people's own risk arguments, or can both be true at once? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with — maybe you could justify AI-based defense on much weaker assumptions, or maybe it works for some tasks (monitoring, verification) and not others (actually producing a future worth wanting). And if strategy-stealing turns out to be false — either because value is fragile and arbitrary goals are just easier to pursue, or because offense dominates at high capability levels — what would a safety approach have to look like instead? Don't just say "international coordination" and call it a day.
