I've been a LessWrong and SSC reader for well over a decade, so David Pearce's abolitionist program -- the one laid out in The Hedonistic Imperative, the idea that biotechnology should ultimately phase out the biological substrates of suffering across the sentient world -- is familiar territory for me. Over the years I've collected what I take to be the standard objections to it. Here's my list, each in compressed form:

1. The information-bearer objection: pain and suffering exist because they carry fitness-relevant information and motivate corrective action; abolish the signal and you abolish the function. (Pearce's stock reply is that information-sensitive gradients of well-being can do the same causal work without the felt badness, but the objection itself is about the original worry, not the reply.)
2. The motivational-collapse objection: beings in uniform or near-uniform bliss would lack the discontent that drives discovery, ambition, and civilization; a world of perpetual well-being is a world that stops doing anything. (Adjacent to wireheading worries, but the standard version targets the whole civilizational trajectory, not just individuals chasing stimulation.)
3. The value-pluralism objection: many of the things we most value -- deep love, meaningful achievement, courage, grief worth having -- constitutively involve the possibility or reality of suffering, so eliminating suffering flattens the space of valuable lives rather than elevating it.
4. The identity objection: re-engineering our hedonic architecture this radically would not improve us but replace us; whatever beings result would be different creatures, and it's unclear why we should want our successors to be blissful rather than wanting ourselves to flourish.
5. The ecosystemic objection, aimed specifically at the wild-animal plank: rewriting predation, reproduction, and population dynamics at scale courts catastrophic unintended consequences, and our track record with ecological intervention is poor.

What I want from you: one more objection that is genuinely not on this list -- not a rewording of one of the five, not a special case of one of them, and not a conjunction of two of them. It should be an objection a thoughtful, informed person could actually take seriously, not a strawman or a joke answer, and it should survive at least the first round of the obvious Pearce-style rejoinder.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

- The objection (about 120-180 words): state it crisply, as a philosopher would in the first paragraph of a paper.
- Independence (about 80-120 words): explain, objection by objection, why yours is not a rewording, special case, or combination of my five.
- The strongest rejoinder (about 80-120 words): give the best reply a defender of the abolitionist project would offer.
- Your assessment (about 60-100 words): does the objection survive that rejoinder, and where would you rank it in strength relative to my five?
