I work as a database engineer. My native vocabulary is relational schemas, SQL, view definitions, self-joins, recursive queries, and the practical discipline of what a given query language permits you to express. I have been reading about Russell's paradox and the collapse of naive set theory, and I want the entire episode restated in that vocabulary, followed by an accounting of what the restatement loses.

Here is what I am asking for, in three parts.

First, the setup. Restate naive comprehension -- the principle that every well-formed condition determines a set -- as a claim about what a sufficiently liberal query language would let you define. Then restate Russell's construction, the set of all sets that are not members of themselves, as a definition within that language. Be careful about what self-membership corresponds to: is it a view defined in terms of itself, a catalog table that lists all tables, including itself? I want the mapping to be exact enough that the contradiction emerges from the database analogue for the same reason it emerges in set theory, not by stipulation.

Second, the repairs. Take the major historical responses one at a time -- Russell's type theory, Zermelo's separation axiom (you may only carve subcollections out of collections already given), the limitation-of-size idea associated with von Neumann-Bernays-Godel, and Quine's New Foundations with its stratification requirement -- and for each one identify the closest analogue in actual database and programming practice: layered schemas, the requirement that queries range over previously declared tables, type disciplines that prohibit mixing levels of relations, linters that reject unstratified definitions. Where the analogue is close, say so; where you had to stretch it, mark the stretch explicitly rather than papering over it.

Third, and this is the part I care most about: report what did not survive. Which elements of the paradox and its solutions have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Candidates I have in mind: the question of which collections exist, as opposed to which are definable; the cumulative hierarchy understood as a picture of a mind-independent mathematical universe rather than a design convention; the widespread sense among set theorists that the paradox was a discovery about reality rather than a bug in a formalism. For each piece of residue, give me your judgment on whether it shows that my working vocabulary lacks something the set theorists genuinely need, or whether it shows that part of the philosophical debate runs on notions that evaporate once you ask what work they do.

Underlying all this is a suspicion I want tested, not confirmed: that every set-theoretic repair, like every restriction in a database system, succeeds by making the dangerous construction inexpressible rather than by showing it to be harmless -- and that if this is right, the distinction between a solution to the paradox and an engineering workaround is thinner than the philosophical literature admits. If that suspicion conflates inexpressibility with contradiction, or confuses what a language forbids with what the world contains, say exactly where the conflation occurs.

One constraint on method: keep the translation honest. Do not smuggle set-theoretic assumptions into the database framing and present them as translations, and do not let the database framing quietly decide the philosophical questions by making them unstatable. Where the target vocabulary simply cannot say something, the right move is to flag the silence, not to fill it.
