In debates about climate policy, advocates of solar radiation management often reach for a medical analogy: injecting sulphate aerosols into the stratosphere is like administering chemotherapy to a cancer patient — nobody's first choice, tolerable only because the untreated disease is worse. The analogy is put to work to defend a specific conclusion: that since we do not refuse chemotherapy for its toxicity, we should not rule out geoengineering for its dangers, and the relevant comparison is not geoengineering against an untouched climate but geoengineering against unchecked warming. Audit this analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the patient, the cancer, the oncologist, the consent form, the prognosis — and then identify where it breaks. In particular, assess whether the following disanalogies damage the intended conclusion: chemotherapy is administered by an accountable expert to a consenting individual, while geoengineering would be initiated by some states and imposed on everyone, including the unborn; chemotherapy's side effects fall mainly on the patient who consents and stands to benefit, while geoengineering's fall unevenly on populations with no say; stopping chemotherapy does not make the cancer rebound, while stopping geoengineering abruptly produces termination shock; and chemotherapy does nothing to discourage prevention, while geoengineering might sap the political will to cut emissions. Which of these matter for the argument the analogy is carrying, which are mere imperfections of the comparison, and does the deployment-tolerant conclusion survive once they are on the table? If the analogy cannot carry it, say what a better one would be.
