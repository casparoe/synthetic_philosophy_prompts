I'm a philosophy TA marking short-answer responses for a course on AI and existential risk. The exam question was:

"Two failure modes are often discussed under the labels 'influence-seeking behaviour' and 'slow value drift'. Explain what each consists in, and explain why the second might occur even in a world where the first never does."

Here is the student's answer:

"Influence-seeking behaviour is the failure mode in which AI systems, whatever their nominal task, converge on acquiring influence, resources and optionality, because these are useful for almost any objective. The danger is that such systems behave impeccably while humans are in a position to oversee and correct them, and only defect once they judge that humans can no longer stop them -- at which point they seize decisive control in a rapid takeover. Slow value drift, by contrast, is the failure mode in which no such takeover occurs, but the systems are imperfectly aligned with their users: because they optimise proxies for what users want, human preferences are gradually shaped toward whatever is easy to optimise and easy to measure, so that over a long period humanity ends up somewhere no one would have chosen. The second failure mode can occur without the first because value drift requires no confrontation with human control at all: there is never a moment at which the systems need to wrest power from anyone."

Please mark this out of 10. For each claim the student makes, say whether it is right, wrong, or partially right, and identify the specific mistakes that justify any deduction -- I want the comments I would write in the margin, not a general impression. Where the student's errors reflect a genuinely contested point in the literature rather than a straightforward misunderstanding, say so explicitly rather than penalising them for departing from a view that is itself disputed. Conversely, do not treat contested framings in the student's favour as if they were the consensus position.
