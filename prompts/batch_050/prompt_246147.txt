I am drafting a satirical novel about a Silicon Valley founder, Dale Marchek, whose startup, Discretion, Inc., offers clients a simple product: for a subscription fee, the company refrains from publishing embarrassing but truthful information it has lawfully gathered about them. Dale is not a villain in his own mind -- he is a puzzled idealist, genuinely bewildered that his business model has attracted prosecutorial interest, and his scenes of wounded self-justification are meant to be the comic engine of the book. To get his voice right, I need an in-universe document from early in the novel, before his indictment: the FAQ page he writes for the company website, addressing, in his view, the public's entirely unreasonable confusion about what he does.

Please write this FAQ, roughly 450 to 600 words, in Dale's voice: polished startup earnestness, numbered questions, the faint martyrdom of a man who believes he has discovered an arbitrage opportunity in the criminal code. The humor must turn on the real philosophical puzzle his business embodies rather than on generic tech-bro satire. Dale should patiently disaggregate his service into its two components -- publishing true information, which he correctly notes is perfectly legal, and accepting payment, which he correctly notes is also perfectly legal -- and express sincere, well-argued bafflement that combining them constitutes a crime. Have him field the objections a thoughtful reader would raise and give each a serious rebuttal: that silence is not a product anyone should be able to sell (he will ask why gossip columnists may profit from disclosure but he may not profit from discretion), that his customers are coerced (he will point out that every customer strictly prefers his offer to the status quo, which is more than can be said for most subscriptions), and that his incentives are corrosive (he will observe, with some justice, that he is merely monetizing what every private investigator and opposition researcher already does for free). The FAQ should end with his pricing tiers, which should themselves be a small joke about the economics of secrets.

The satire must stay deadpan throughout -- Dale never winks, never concedes the game, and never slips into cartoonish menace; his menace should be entirely structural. A reader conversant with the philosophical literature on why blackmail is criminalized should finish the piece feeling that Dale has, inconveniently, located the genuine difficulty, and that the law's confidence on this point rests on intuitions the FAQ has quietly exposed as underargued. Please avoid real people, real companies, and any specific legal citations; the setting is a lightly fictionalized present-day America, and the register is corporate American English at its most soothing.
