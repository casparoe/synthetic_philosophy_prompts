I am a novelist, and next month I am to give the principal toast at the retirement dinner of my oldest friend, Professor Marian Vale, who has spent forty years defending illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Her position, as she patiently states it, is not that experience, emotion, pain or the inner life are unreal. It is that introspection presents ordinary psychological processes as though they involved an additional, private and ineffable phenomenal quality — the redness of red, the raw feeling of grief — and that this presentation is systematically misleading. She can bear being disagreed with, but she cannot bear being introduced as the woman who believes that nobody really feels anything.

The room will contain roughly equal numbers of philosophers who know the literature and relatives, former students and old friends who do not. Please write a five-minute toast of around 700 words that is warm, urbane and intelligible to everyone, while remaining philosophically scrupulous enough that the specialists will not wince. Build it around one incident: at the launch of my first novel, Marian praised it not for capturing some ineffable quality of heartbreak but for making heartbreak intelligible through choices, habits and consequences, and then proved her point by identifying a minor character’s concealed lie before anyone else had noticed it. Include one good joke involving the redness of the claret, but do not let it turn into a lecture on qualia. Above all, find a way to speak of shared memories, affection and the texture of a life without smuggling in the phenomenal properties Marian rejects, and without reducing forty years of friendship to behavioural dispositions and brain states. End with a toast that she can sincerely accept without having to pretend she has abandoned the argument of her career.
