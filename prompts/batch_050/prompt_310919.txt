So I'm in this reading group on Fear and Trembling and last week we got into a pretty heated argument that nobody could settle, so I figured I'd ask here. Basically the question was: what would Kierkegaard have thought about the Danish resistance hiding Jewish families from the Gestapo in 1943? One person in the group argued that since Kierkegaard praises Abraham for being willing to suspend the universal ethical for the sake of an absolute relation to God, the fisherman who lied to the authorities and ferried people across to Sweden is basically in Abraham's position -- he's violating the ethical (lying, breaking the law, betraying his country's occupied government) in obedience to something higher, and he can't justify himself to anyone in universal terms. Someone else pushed back hard: Abraham's sacrifice has no humanly intelligible purpose at all, that's the whole point of the absurd, whereas the rescuer's action is obviously comprehensible and even admirable to everyone afterwards, so it's really just the ethical winning out over positive law, which is a much more boring story that Hegel could already tell. There's no suspension of anything.

So here's what I'd like: reconstruct what Kierkegaard himself (or Johannes de Silentio, if you want to be careful about the pseudonym thing) would most plausibly say about the Danish rescue, given the framework of Fear and Trembling. Would he count the rescuers as knights of faith, or does the very fact that their deed is publicly vindicated afterwards disqualify them? And does the answer change if you consider a rescuer who never told anyone, was suspected by his neighbors of collaborating, and died with his reputation ruined -- does that secrecy and isolation move him closer to Abraham? Please don't just give a generic "ethics vs religion" answer, I want the argument run through the actual machinery of the book -- the universal, the absolute, the knight of faith vs the tragic hero, the inability to make oneself understood. Bonus if you can say where you think Kierkegaard would have to bite a bullet that modern readers find uncomfortable.
