I've been reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the adjacent blogs for the better part of two decades, and I keep watching people on both sides of my feed cite the same episode as decisive proof of opposite conclusions about how we should think about machine minds. The episode: in June 2022, Blake Lemoine, an engineer in Google's Responsible AI organization whose job involved probing the LaMDA language model for bias, concluded after many hours of open-ended conversation that the system was sentient, released an edited transcript of an interview he and a colleague had conducted with it, was placed on administrative leave for violating confidentiality, and was later dismissed. Google said his claims were unfounded; the overwhelming majority of AI researchers and philosophers of mind who weighed in agreed with Google. I want a disciplined case analysis of this episode through the lens of the Turing test and, more broadly, the family of behavioral criteria for mind: the view, descending from Turing's 1950 proposal of the imitation game, that outward performance in open-ended conversation is the only intersubjectively available evidence of mentality we have or could have.

Do the mapping carefully. First, what evidence did Lemoine actually possess, and was it the right kind by the criterion's own lights? Turing imagined a controlled interrogation; Lemoine had free-ranging conversations in which he asked many of the leading questions, set the framing, and later edited what he published — say whether those features merely weaken the evidence or change its kind. Second, LaMDA was trained explicitly on human dialogue to produce humanlike text: was Turing's proposal tacitly premised on the machine not having been optimized to pass it, and if so, does that optimization Goodhart the behavioral signal out of evidential force, or is that objection just moving the goalposts in a way a consistent behaviorist must refuse? Third, the scope mismatch: Turing deliberately asked whether machines can think, while Lemoine claimed sentience — can any behavioral criterion speak to phenomenal consciousness at all, or was Lemoine demanding an answer from a framework that was built to decline the question? Then run the analysis in the other direction: is the episode a vindication of behavioral criteria, since Google's rebuttal and most expert commentary leaned on architecture and training process rather than behavior, which is arguably abandoning behavioralism exactly when it got uncomfortable? Or is it the reductio — the moment behavior became cheap to fake, and thus the moment behavioral criteria died? And say what the criterion, on your reading, gets right about why the transcript was so persuasive to Lemoine and many readers, and where it is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak.

I don't want a balanced survey of positions I already know; I want a verdict. Commit to one of the available diagnoses — that Lemoine had the right kind of evidence but corrupted the sample, that he had the wrong kind of evidence entirely, that behavioral criteria survive but only under conditions he never came close to meeting, or some fourth option you can state crisply — and defend it, including what accepting it costs you elsewhere (for instance, what it commits you to about the minds of animals, or of future systems that pass cleanly). If you think the episode shows behavioral criteria were always confused, say which alternative criterion you would actually apply and show it doing work on this case. Please answer in American English.
