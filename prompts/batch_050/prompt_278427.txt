So I read Lewis's *Convention* a while back and I keep tripping over a real-life pattern that doesn't seem to fit his definition, and I want to know if there's a name for it. Here's the pattern, in two flavors.

First: at my office everyone stays until like 7pm even when there's nothing to do. I've talked to enough people privately to be fairly sure almost nobody wants this. But nobody leaves early, because everyone assumes everyone else would judge them for it. So each person's belief about what the others want is wrong, and the wrongness is what keeps the thing alive.

Second: my extended family does this gift exchange every christmas that everyone complains about in smaller groups, but when someone floated dropping it a couple years ago it somehow died in five minutes because everyone assumed the aunts would be devastated. I happen to know the aunts hate it too.

Now, as I understand Lewis, a convention needs people to prefer conforming *given that the others conform*, and there's stuff about everyone knowing that everyone knows etc. These cases seem close but off: the behavior is self-perpetuating, it has that arbitrary "we could all do otherwise" flavor, but it only survives because of a systematically false picture of what everyone else prefers. If everyone actually learned the truth at once, it would collapse overnight, which a proper Lewis convention wouldn't (if we all found out the others *like* driving on the right, nothing changes). So is a stable-on-false-beliefs norm a convention by his lights, or something else? Is there an established term for this exact configuration -- stable pattern, near-universal private dislike, maintained by wrong second-order beliefs -- as opposed to a genuine convention where people actually do want the conformity? And if there are several nearby concepts (I've heard vague mention of norms nobody believes in, of people hiding their real preferences), tell me which one fits best and where my description fails to fit each. If nothing fits exactly, coin something and give me boundary cases: what happens when only *most* people misread the room, or when the false belief is about enforcement rather than preference?

Also: please be upfront about how confident you are in each part of the answer, and single out the specific claim you're least sure about rather than hedging everything equally.
