I am a novelist preparing a chapter in which two characters argue late into the night about whether philosophical skepticism can ever be answered on its own terms. One character, a confident autodidact, relies on an unnamed study guide’s account of G. E. Moore’s Proof of an External World. I want the guide to be broadly competent rather than foolish, but I also want the other character—who has some philosophical training—to notice that something in its presentation is slightly off. The guide says: Moore begins from premises he claims to know immediately: that this is one hand and this is another. Since hands are objects existing independently of any mind, the existence of two hands entails that an external world exists. The reasoning is deductively valid, and Moore regards his ordinary premises as more certain than any philosopher’s grounds for doubting them. His principal target is the dream hypothesis, and his answer is that no sane person can sincerely suspend belief in the existence of their own hands. The proof therefore refutes both skepticism and idealism outright rather than merely giving us a practical reason to ignore them; anyone who remains unconvinced must be demanding a kind of absolute certainty that ordinary knowledge never requires.

Please audit this summary sentence by sentence. Which parts would a careful philosopher accept as accurate, which are defensible introductory simplifications, and which are misleading or wrong? In particular, distinguish Moore’s claim to know the premises from the mere psychological inability to doubt them; consider whether the argument’s validity settles its epistemic force; and assess whether it is fair to say that Moore refutes skepticism and idealism outright. I would also like the strongest objection that could still be raised after the summary has been corrected—not an easy dismissal, but the sort of objection that would make the argument feel philosophically alive rather than settled.

Finally, advise me how a knowledgeable character should describe the dispute in one concise speech: neither portraying Moore as naïvely asserting that he has hands because he can see them, nor portraying the skeptic as someone who simply refuses to accept any proof. No bibliography or citations are needed; I am interested in the conceptual distinctions and in making the dialogue accurate.
