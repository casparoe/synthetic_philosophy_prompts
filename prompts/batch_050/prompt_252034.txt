I'm designing an exercise for my graduate seminar in the philosophy of criminal law on how a theorist's case-level intuitions can underdetermine the principle they are actually running. For several years I have been informally recording a colleague's verdicts on strict criminal liability — that is, liability where the prosecution need not prove any culpable mental state as to some element of the offense. Her record so far: a shopkeeper who sold contaminated food despite maintaining a reasonable, well-documented inspection regime, convicted under a strict-liability statute and fined — she finds this justified; a driver convicted for a brake-light failure she could not reasonably have discovered, punished with a small fine — justified; a bartender who served a minor presenting a convincing forged ID, fined and briefly suspended — justified; a defendant who reasonably believed his partner was of legal age (she had presented falsified documents) convicted of a strict-liability sexual offense and imprisoned — unjustified; and a hunter who failed to check what lay beyond his target and wounded a hiker, convicted on a negligence standard — justified, though she insists this last is no exception to any fault requirement, since negligence is itself a genuine form of fault.

The case I want to put to her is a proposed statute under which a second strict-liability food-safety violation within five years — say, selling goods past their labeled date — is punishable by up to six months in jail, applied to an operator whose hygiene and record-keeping were exemplary and whose mistake was entirely reasonable. Predict her verdict on this case, and do so by setting out the candidate principles that each fit her entire existing record: I can see at least two (one keyed to the severity and mode of the penalty, one keyed to the regulatory versus morally stigmatic character of the offense, and perhaps a hybrid), and the new case seems to me deliberately situated between her earlier verdicts — sharing its penalty with one precedent and its subject matter with another. Identify which precedent is the closest analog and why, say which principle you think best explains her record overall and what tips the balance, and name the further case — the one verdict I have not yet collected — that would most efficiently discriminate among the candidate principles. Finally, since this is a prediction about a person's dispositions rather than a theorem, tell me where your inference is most likely to fail: what fact about her, or what argument I might put to her, would most plausibly reverse your predicted verdict?
