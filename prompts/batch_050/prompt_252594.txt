I am a novelist revising a courtroom chapter and have consulted two philosopher friends, Elena and Tomas, about the moral logic of the central case. In the chapter, Ingrid deliberately transfers a charity’s reserves to an extremist political organisation. Unknown to her, a neural governor had previously been implanted in her brain. Its first safeguard would detect and erase any intention not to make the transfer. If that safeguard failed, a second would commandeer her motor system and make her body execute the transfer anyway. On the occasion in question, Ingrid calmly considers withholding the money, rejects that course out of greed, and transfers it through her own unimpaired deliberation. Neither safeguard activates. She understands what she is doing and is neither coerced nor deluded. She could have formed an intention not to transfer the money, but she could not thereby have prevented the transfer.

Elena and Tomas read the chapter and disagreed as follows.

Elena began by arguing that this is a successful Frankfurt-style case. Ingrid has no complete alternative course of conduct open to her, since every possible refusal would be overridden, yet her actual behaviour issues from her own assessment of reasons. She is therefore responsible even though she could not have done otherwise, which refutes the principle that moral responsibility requires alternative possibilities.

Tomas replied that the governor merely fixes the financial outcome. It does not prevent Ingrid from choosing, intending, or attempting to refuse. If moral assessment principally concerns the agent’s choice, then an alternative remains at precisely the point where responsibility is grounded. If it concerns the unavoidable movement of the money, the case proves little, because responsibility for an inevitable outcome is questionable from the outset.

Elena answered that the charge in the chapter is transferring charitable funds, not merely entertaining a wrongful intention. Ingrid’s intention and bodily behaviour together constitute the transfer. A momentary contrary intention that could never become a course of conduct is only a flicker of freedom, not a morally robust alternative.

Tomas objected that this relies on an undefended distinction between real alternatives and flickers. If the only alternatives that count are those capable of affecting the outcome, Elena has simply defined alternative possibilities in whatever way allows the governor to eliminate them. Moreover, a thwarted attempt is still something an agent can do; it should not disappear from the analysis merely because it cannot succeed.

Elena then pointed out that the second safeguard avoids the standard objection that Frankfurt cases must predict the agent from some deterministic prior sign. Her governor need not foresee Ingrid’s decision. Even in an indeterministic world, it can wait until a contrary intention or attempt emerges and then override it. Hence the familiar dilemma — either determinism has been presupposed or an alternative remains — does not arise in its usual form.

Tomas accepted that the backstop avoids reliance on a deterministic prediction, but denied that this settles the matter. The device removes alternative outcomes only by allowing alternative mental episodes and preventing them from becoming effective. It therefore separates responsibility for choosing from responsibility for producing an outcome. Ingrid may be blameworthy for willingly endorsing the transfer without being responsible for the transfer considered as an event that would have occurred regardless.

Elena concluded that Tomas is multiplying principles unnecessarily. At least the version of the principle of alternative possibilities concerned with courses of action is false: Ingrid acts voluntarily, for her own reasons, and with full understanding, although no alternative course of action is available. Her responsibility is grounded in that actual sequence, not in unused possibilities.

Tomas concluded that Elena has at most shown that one crude formulation of the principle needs refinement. The chapter does not refute every version of it. The better principle may be disjunctive: responsibility requires either a robust alternative or authorship through an appropriately reasons-responsive process. Ingrid satisfies the second condition for her intention, but it remains unclear whether she is responsible for the inevitable financial result rather than merely for her willing participation in it.

Please adjudicate the disagreement as it was actually made, rather than simply presenting both positions as equally plausible. In particular, determine whether the motor backstop really avoids the deterministic-sign objection; whether an unrealisable contrary intention or thwarted attempt counts as an alternative; whether the governor fixes only the outcome or eliminates alternative action; whether Ingrid is responsible for her intention, her conduct, and the resulting transfer respectively; and what survives of the principle of alternative possibilities.
