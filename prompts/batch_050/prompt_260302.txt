I am preparing a session of our graduate reading group on free will and moral responsibility, and I have been trying to steelman Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the regress argument intended to show that true moral responsibility is impossible. My worry is that my reconstruction, whose premises each strike me as hard to deny, appears to prove far more than Strawson needs and more than I can bring myself to believe. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. Please answer as a philosopher who specialises in free will and moral responsibility, write in British English, and think through where the dialectical pressure actually sits before committing yourself.

Here is my version of the argument:

(1) Whenever you act, what you do flows from the way you are at the time — your character, motives, values and habits of mind.
(2) So you are morally responsible for an action only if the action is genuinely attributable to you: only if the self it flows from is a self for which you are responsible.
(3) A self is one for which you are responsible only if you are its author — only if you deliberately chose, and thereby brought about, the character from which you now act.
(4) Any such choice would itself have flowed from the way you already were, so by (1)–(3) it is attributable to you only if you were responsible for that earlier self as well.
(5) Hence responsibility for any action requires either an infinite regress of prior acts of self-authorship or a first act of self-creation from nothing.
(6) Neither is possible for finite beings.
(7) Therefore no one is ever morally responsible for anything.

The conclusion I cannot accept is not quite (7) itself but what the reasoning seems to drag along with it. Consider Miriam, who has spent twenty years deliberately reshaping her character — therapy, habit-training, sustained reflection, the whole project of making herself patient where she was once cruel — and compare her with someone who has never once reflected on himself, and with someone acting under post-hypnotic suggestion. On my argument all three stand exactly alike: each traces back to an unchosen origin, so none is responsible for anything, and Miriam's decades of self-authorship earn her precisely nothing. That flattens every distinction on which our practices of praise, blame and apology are built. Worse still, the schema nowhere mentions morality: swap in justification for responsibility and cognitive dispositions for character, and the same steps appear to show that no belief is ever justified — including my belief in premises (1) to (6). An argument schema that has global scepticism as a special case is, I take it, proving too much.

So: which step should I reject, and why did that step look fine in the first place? If possible I would like to keep the impossibility of self-creation in (5)–(6) and the datum that the hypnotised subject really is different from Miriam, but I want to know what each alternative costs. If you reject (2) or (3), tell me what I must then say about manipulation cases, and about whether responsibility is a historical property at all. If you think the argument is sound but my two extensions fail, identify the feature of the moral case that breaks the parallels rather than merely asserting that they break. And if my reconstruction departs from Strawson's own version at the crucial point, say where, and whether the departure is what generates the trouble.
