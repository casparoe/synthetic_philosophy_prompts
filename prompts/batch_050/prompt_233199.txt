I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I run a philosophy-of-computation reading group at my company (we've been meeting for a year, working through logic, computability, and some philosophy of mathematics). We've decided to close the year with a take-home written exam, and I've volunteered to write the question on the Church-Turing thesis. The group has read the standard material, so everyone can already recite that the thesis identifies the effectively calculable functions with the Turing-computable (or recursive, or lambda-definable) functions. I want a question they can't answer by reciting that.

Draft one essay question, answerable in about two hours of writing, that forces candidates to take a position on the *epistemic status* of the thesis rather than its content. The crux I want the question to press on is something that has been bothering me as an engineer: the thesis can't be an ordinary mathematical theorem, because one side of the identification (effective calculability) is an informal, pre-mathematical notion that no proof can get a grip on; but it also doesn't seem to be a mere stipulation or definition, because it seems like it could turn out to be *wrong* in a way definitions can't -- and yet it's not clear what kind of evidence could show it to be wrong, since any candidate "effective method" that outstripped Turing machines would have to be assessable as genuinely effective, which seems to presuppose exactly the notion under dispute. I'd like the question to make candidates grapple with at least the following tensions: (a) the convergence of many independently motivated formalisms (Turing machines, general recursive functions, lambda calculus, and others) on exactly the same class of functions -- is that convergence evidence for the *truth* of the thesis, or only for the mathematical naturalness of that class? (b) the difference between the thesis as a claim about what a human computist following an algorithm can in principle calculate, and the stronger claim (often confused with it) about what physical machines or physical processes in general can compute -- does the epistemic status differ between the two, and could the second be refuted by physics in a way the first could not? (c) what, if anything, would count as *settling* the thesis -- could it be proven, refuted, or only ever made more or less plausible, and what does the answer to that reveal about what kind of claim it is (theorem, empirical hypothesis, conceptual analysis, definition, or something else)?

Along with the question itself, please provide:

1. A model answer of roughly 700-900 words, written at the level of a strong non-specialist who has done the reading, showing what a top answer looks like. It should take a definite position on what kind of claim the thesis is while acknowledging the strongest considerations on the other sides.

2. A marking scheme out of 30 marks, built around five or six must-hit points with the marks each is worth. For each point, include a one-or-two-line note on the specific way weaker candidates typically get it wrong -- for instance, treating the convergence of formalisms as if it were a proof; assuming a hypothetical physical "hypercomputer" would straightforwardly refute the original thesis rather than at most a physical strengthening of it; or asserting that the thesis is "just a definition" without explaining why it appears to be vulnerable to counterexample in a way definitions are not.

3. A short paragraph explaining what separates an answer in the top band (say, 26-30) from an answer in the next band down (21-25) -- the specific intellectual moves the former must contain that the latter typically lacks, such as distinguishing the claim about idealized human computation from the claim about physical processes, or articulating precisely why the informality of "effective method" blocks an ordinary proof without making the thesis immune to rational assessment.
