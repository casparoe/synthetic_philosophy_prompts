For an epistemology reading group I prepared a one-paragraph handout summarizing G. E. Moore's 1939 lecture Proof of an External World, drawing on a few secondary accounts rather than the original. A friend who has read Moore more carefully than I have glanced at it, told me it misrepresents Moore in at least one important place, and refused to say where, on the grounds that finding the error myself would do me good. I have failed to find it. Here is the handout:

Moore's proof is among the simplest arguments in philosophy. Addressing his audience, he raised each of his hands in turn and gestured toward them, concluded that at least two things exist outside his mind, and inferred that an external world exists. Moore presented this performance as a conclusive refutation of philosophical skepticism about the external world: once the audience has seen his hands, no one can consistently deny that external things exist, and the skeptic is thereby silenced. He took the same performance to establish that he knew he was awake and not dreaming, since the hands were plainly there to be seen. Critics object that the argument is circular: Moore simply helps himself to the reliability of his senses, which is precisely what the skeptic calls into question, and a circular argument cannot establish its conclusion. For this reason the proof is generally regarded as a curiosity — memorable for its boldness, but a failure as an argument, and now useful chiefly as a textbook illustration of begging the question.

I would like an accuracy audit before I circulate the handout. Specifically:

1. Go through the summary claim by claim and classify each as accurate, a defensible simplification, or a misrepresentation. For anything in the last two categories, state what Moore's actual position or the standard scholarship supports instead.

2. In particular, examine what the handout has Moore claiming about dreaming and about refuting or silencing the skeptic. What did Moore himself explicitly concede he could not prove, and does that concession undermine the proof as he conceived it, or does it concern a question he treated as separate?

3. Evaluate the circularity objection as stated. Is the argument formally circular — does its conclusion appear among its premises? If not, identify the more careful version of the objection that the handout's version gestures at, and say whether that stronger objection licenses the verdict that the proof is simply a failure.

4. The final sentence draws a general conclusion about the proof's philosophical standing. Does that conclusion follow from whatever in the paragraph survives correction, or does it depend on the errors?
