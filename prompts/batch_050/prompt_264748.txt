So I've been in this running argument with a friend from my old study group about whether calling DNA a "code" is just a handy metaphor or literally true. She says it's literal, I'm skeptical, and last night I tried to steelman her side into a crisp argument. The problem is I think I've overshot and built something that proves way too much, but I can't find the joint where it bends. Here's what I have:

(1) A code is just a systematic, arbitrary mapping between elements of one medium and elements of another -- "arbitrary" meaning nothing about the physical nature of the elements forces the mapping, so the very same medium could have implemented a different mapping, like how Morse could have assigned dot-dot-dash to Q instead of Z and nothing about dots and dashes would have objected.

(2) The genetic code fits this exactly. Triplets of bases map systematically onto amino acids, and the mapping isn't chemically forced -- the machinery of translation (tRNAs, aminoacyl synthetases) is what pins cytosine-cytosine-guanine to proline, and life clearly could have evolved a different assignment table, since in fact a few organisms and mitochondria actually use slightly different ones.

(3) So DNA is literally a code, not a metaphor.

(4) But now notice that tons of things fit the same definition. The grooves on a vinyl record map systematically onto sound waves and nothing in the physics of vinyl forces that assignment -- the same groove shapes could have meant different notes under a different playback standard. A thermostat dial's angle codes for temperature. The sediment layers in a cliff face code for which eras were wet and which were dry, and geologists "read" them off. Even a puddle's shape "codes for" the exact configuration of rain that made it, since a different downpour would have left a different puddle, and nothing about water forces one mapping rather than another.

(5) So either literally every stable causal mapping in the universe is a code -- which makes "DNA is a code" completely trivial and boring -- or (1) is wrong and needs some extra ingredient. But every extra ingredient I try to add (the mapping being *used* by something, the mapping being *exploitable* by an interpreter) seems to just push the problem around, because geologists exploit sediment layers and crows exploit the correlation between certain sounds and food, and I still don't want to say the cliff face is written in a language.

My friend of course wants to keep (3), and honestly I'd also like to keep (1) since it captures what's special about Morse and ASCII better than anything else I've come up with. Which step should I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If the answer is something like "codes need an interpreter for whom the elements stand for things," I'd especially like to know whether that lands me in trouble given that nobody intends anything in a cell -- the ribosome isn't reading anything, it just does what it does. Or is that exactly where I'm confused?
