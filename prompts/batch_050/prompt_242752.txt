For my department's annual research statement, doctoral candidates have been asked to set out their working commitments in metaphysics plainly enough that the faculty reading them can identify where the positions strain. Below is my list, verbatim. What I want from you is an audit, not a tutorial: I am not asking whether each thesis is individually defensible, but whether the set hangs together. Identify which pairs or triples of commitments are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is; for each, specify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision costs elsewhere in the system. If some of these look like they conflict but the conflict dissolves on closer inspection, say so explicitly and explain why. Close by telling me which single revision would resolve the most problems with the least collateral damage to the rest. I have not indicated which commitments I hold dearest, and I'd rather you didn't assume.

My commitments:

1. Grounding is a single, unified relation — the relation in virtue of which some facts obtain because others do — and it is a strict partial order: irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive. Nothing grounds itself, grounding never runs in circles, and chains of grounds transmit.

2. Necessitarianism about grounding: if some facts Γ fully ground a fact p, then necessarily, whenever all of Γ obtain, p obtains. Necessitation is not sufficient for grounding — not every necessitated fact is grounded by whatever necessitates it — but it is necessary.

3. Well-foundedness: every grounded fact's grounding chains terminate in facts that are themselves ungrounded, and those ungrounded facts are exactly the fundamental ones. There are no infinitely descending chains of grounds.

4. Physicalism about the base: the fundamental facts are all and only the fundamental physical facts, and those facts are contingent — the actual microphysical distribution and the actual laws could have been otherwise.

5. Consciousness: phenomenal facts are fully grounded in physical and functional facts about brains. At the same time, a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of a human being that entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness is genuinely metaphysically possible, and conceivability is here a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility.

6. A principle of sufficient reason: every contingent fact has an explanation in terms of something distinct from itself. Nothing contingent is simply brute.

7. Grounding facts are no exception to the general economy: facts of the form "Γ grounds p" are never part of the fundamental base but are themselves grounded in the non-metaphysical facts they concern. Otherwise the fundamental level would be cluttered with infinitely many pieces of metaphysical machinery riding on top of the physical facts.

One clarification so you don't chase a non-issue: I know that thesis 2 alone makes necessitation merely necessary and not sufficient for grounding, so don't flag cases of necessitation-without-grounding as objections to me. I'm interested in tensions internal to the list — places where accepting some of these theses commits me to denying others — ordered by severity, each with what would have to give.
