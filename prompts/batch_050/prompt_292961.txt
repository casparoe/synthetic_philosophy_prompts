Everyone came away with the standard picture: the self-sampling assumption is the immodest one, since it treats your existence as guaranteed under every theory under consideration and so lets a slim prior against a many-observer cosmos do all the work, whereas the self-indication assumption is the generous one that happily boosts theories containing more observers. Then someone claimed you can run the whole thing backwards — that there's a setup where SIA is the one that saddles you with the embarrassing conclusion and SSA is the one that talks you out of it. Nobody could construct it on the the spot, so I'm asking you.

Build me that thought experiment. Specs: finitely many observers, with the numbers stated explicitly and kept small enough that I can put the arithmetic on a single slide; no quantum branching and no infinite multiverses; the observers should be ordinary humans in an ordinary epistemic position (a survey, an experiment, a census — not Boltzmann brains or simulated minds); and the reversal has to fall out of how each assumption processes the numbers, not from a bare stipulation about which theory is true. In the usual presumptuous philosopher SSA looks presumptuous and SIA looks sensible; in your case it must go the other way round — SIA commits you to the theory under which your own evidence is wildly atypical, while SSA recommends the theory under which your evidence is exactly what you'd expect.

After the scenario: state the verdict each assumption delivers, showing the actual calculation for each; name the structural feature of the case that produces the reversal; say what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature (does the case collapse back into the standard presumptuous-philosopher shape, or into something else?); and sketch the best line of attack available to someone who wants to defend SIA across the board rather than bite the bullet here.
