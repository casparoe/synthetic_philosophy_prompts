I host a small philosophy reading group that meets at a pub, and last month someone brought up the doomsday argument -- the Carter-Leslie thing where your position as a relatively early human supposedly shifts the odds toward extinction coming sooner rather than later. It split the group completely, and not along the lines I expected: two people both said "the math is fine but the conclusion is wrong" and then gave totally different reasons, while one guy rejected the whole setup as meaningless and another said he's basically convinced. I want to turn this mess into a decision tree handout for next session, so people can trace out which response to the argument they actually hold by answering questions, rather than just picking whichever label sounds smart.

Here's what I need. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names: start with the questions on which the responses genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions in that space. The leaves have to include at least these: full acceptance (the argument works and doom soon really is much more likely than we'd have thought), the move that denies you count as a random sample from all humans (so your birth rank isn't usable evidence), the self-indication response (the assumption that you'd be more likely to exist in a world with more observers cancels the shift toward doom), the reference-class move (the argument only works relative to a chosen reference class and the choice is underdetermined or gerrymandered), and the prior-probability response (even granting the update, a sufficiently low independent estimate of extinction risk swamps it). Each branch question should be phrased as something a smart non-philosopher can answer without knowing which position it funnels them toward -- so not "do you accept the self-sampling assumption?" but something like a concrete case about drawing balls from urns or waking up in an experiment. Seven or eight branch questions max, and where two leaves can't actually be separated by any question I'd want to ask, say so rather than papering over it. Also note any combination of answers that's unoccupied, and give a one-line diagnosis of why -- is it incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored territory?

One more thing: I'd rather you flag the soft spots yourself than have my group find them for me. So end with a short note on where this taxonomy is most likely to be wrong or to mislead -- which distinction you leaned on hardest, which leaf you squeezed in despite it maybe not being a stable position -- and what argument or consideration, if someone raised it at the table, should make me tear up the tree and restructure it rather than just patch one branch.
