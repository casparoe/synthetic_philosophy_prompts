A colleague and I have been preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and we keep circling back to a question we disagree about. It is often said, somewhat loosely, that the solution concept of Nash equilibrium is justified by the assumption that players are rational and that this rationality is common knowledge. My colleague defends this claim; I am skeptical. I would like you to argue for my side: that common knowledge of rationality, on its own, does not and cannot explain or justify Nash equilibrium play, and that the natural epistemic output of common knowledge of rationality is instead the weaker notion of rationalizability.

Please develop the argument carefully rather than merely asserting the conclusion. In particular, I would like you to make precise what is missing: Nash equilibrium requires not only that each player best-responds to her beliefs about others, but that these beliefs be correct — and explain why correctness of beliefs is not something that rationality, even iterated to arbitrary depth, can manufacture. It would strengthen the argument to construct or describe a simple game (two players suffice) in which every player is rational, rationality is common knowledge, and yet the resulting play fails to constitute a Nash equilibrium. Please also engage with the strongest rejoinder my colleague offers: that once one adds suitable assumptions about players' conjectures — say, that conjectures are mutually known, or that beliefs derive from a common prior — equilibrium play can be recovered. Explain whether this rescues the original claim or concedes the point, given that these extra assumptions are doing the real work and are not themselves consequences of rationality.

I would prefer the argument to stay at the level of conceptual analysis and simple examples rather than heavy formalism, but please be exact about the epistemic operators involved (belief, knowledge, common knowledge) and about which directions of the implication hold and which fail.
