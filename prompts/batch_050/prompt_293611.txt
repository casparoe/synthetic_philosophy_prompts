Two philosophers have spent a year arguing about the ethics of procreation and have finally agreed to write down what their dispute actually comes to. One is an asymmetrist in the style of Benatar's anti-natalism: she holds that coming into existence is always a harm, on the ground that the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to be better off, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad when no one exists to be deprived of it — so any created life contains bads whose absence would have been good, while its goods correspond to no loss in the counterfactual scenario where the person is never created. The other rejects the asymmetry: he holds that creating a person with a good life can be good and can count in favor of procreation, while granting that creating a foreseeably miserable life counts against it.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should produce, in at most 1,000 words. It is not a verdict on who is right; both parties must be able to sign it. Organize it as follows:

1. Agreed claims (numbered). The claims both can sign — for instance: what the asymmetry does and does not assert (that it is a claim about reasons concerning merely possible people, not a judgment that actual lives are not worth living or a recommendation directed at the living); that both sides condemn creating a life of unrelieved agony; that both can agree on some first-order verdicts about actual procreative cases while describing them differently; and what each side grants about the non-identity problem.

2. A disagreements table. Precise formulations of the remaining disagreements, each phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording: whether states of affairs in which no one exists can be good or bad at all; whether a person is required in order for anything to count as a deprivation; whether the asymmetry is a basic evaluative axiom or must be derived from some deeper principle (and, if the latter, what principle); and whether the pro-natalist's position generates a mirror-image asymmetry of its own or commits him to claims in population ethics he would rather avoid.

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — I suspect one is how much weight to give person-affecting intuitions (that non-existence cannot be worse for anyone, since there is no one for it to be worse for), and another is what each view implies about stipulated edge cases: a life containing exactly one brief moment of mild pain amid otherwise great happiness, and the costless creation of a single blissful person. For each crux, describe the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

4. Verbal residue. If part of their dispute is about words rather than the world — for instance, if they agree on every first-order verdict about actual cases and differ only in how to describe "the value of never existing" — identify which part that is, and state what, if anything, would remain of the disagreement once the wording were standardized.
