I want to defend a position and I want to know what it would take to dislodge me from it. The position has two parts, one decision-theoretic and one political. First: there is a real distinction between risk and ignorance — between facing states of the world with unknown probabilities and facing states to which no probability distribution is warranted at all — and in genuine cases of the second kind it can be rational to choose by maximin, securing the best available worst-case outcome, rather than by any rule that manufactures probabilities out of thin air. Second: Rawls's appeal to maximin reasoning in the original position is not refuted by the Harsanyi-style objection that rational parties, knowing only that they could turn out to be anyone, must assign equal probabilities and maximise expected utility. My defence of the second part leans on Rawls's own three conditions: the parties have no basis for probabilities, they care little for gains above a guaranteed minimum, and the worst outcomes available under rival principles are intolerable. I accept that maximin is only licensed when all three hold.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want a map of its exposure, organised roughly as follows. One: the formal front. There are axiomatisations purporting to characterise each criterion — expected utility, maximin, minimax regret, and optimism–pessimism hybrids — by the axioms they satisfy and violate. If an axiom I would independently accept turns out to exclude maximin (as, for instance, certain independence or column-duplication axioms are said to exclude regret-based rules), how decisive should that be? Which axioms should I actually be moved by, and which are question-begging in this context? Two: the principle of indifference. My whole case rests on denying that ignorance licenses a uniform distribution. Take the strongest available version of that principle, not the strawman, and tell me whether it survives; if it does, my view collapses into expected utility maximisation. Three: the empirical front. Behaviour in Ellsberg-type situations suggests that real agents facing unquantifiable uncertainty follow something like an optimism-weighted rule rather than either maximin or expected utility. Does descriptive evidence of that kind count against a normative view at all, and if so how much? Four: the internal front. Identify defeaters specific to the Rawlsian corollary — for example, if it turned out that maximin in the original position does not in fact select the difference principle, or that the strains-of-commitment argument underwrites the guarantee condition independently of any decision rule, does the political claim fall while the decision-theoretic one stands? Five: treat minimax regret as a rival rather than an ally. It shares my premise about ignorance but diverges in its recommendations, sometimes sharply. If neither criterion is clearly more rational than the other under ignorance, does that refute my view or merely pluralise it?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender — a Rawlsian, or a decision theorist who takes Knightian uncertainty seriously — would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as resting on a contested premise. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: the three conditions are strict enough that one might suspect maximin has been insulated by being restricted to cases that never occur, and if so, say whether that is a fatal defect for a view of this kind or simply the price of a limited claim honestly stated. Please write your answer in British English.
