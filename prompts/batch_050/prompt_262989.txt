Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through Adam Smith's account of sympathy in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and take both sides of the encounter seriously. Smith's mechanism is specific: sympathy is not feeling what another feels but imagining oneself into their situation; a spectator can only go along with a passion pitched at a level he can share, so harmony requires a double adjustment, the sufferer moderating his grief and the spectator stretching his imagination; and our judgments of propriety are indexed to the agent's circumstances rather than to outcomes. The novel reads almost like a series of experiments on this machinery. Victor at the moment of animation responds to the creature's appearance rather than his situation, a failure of imaginative projection in Smith's exact sense. The creature's plan at the De Lacey cottage — to reveal himself first to the blind father, counting on sympathy unmediated by sight — is as close as fiction gets to a controlled test of whether Smithian sympathy survives contact with the visible, and it fails the moment the sighted family returns. And the nested narrative structure, Walton hearing Victor hearing the creature, forces the reader to sympathize with a sympathizer sympathizing, with each frame granting the inner voice more eloquence and therefore more of the moderation Smith says suffering must purchase. I'd like a reading that works through these scenes in Smith's terms: where the novel confirms the theory (does the creature's eloquence vindicate Smith's claim that the sufferer must lower his passion to win company?), where it complicates it (can a reader go along with the creature's rage after William's death, and what does Smith's account of the limits of resentment say about where exactly our sympathy revokes?), and where it exposes something Smith cannot accommodate — for instance, that the impartial spectator may not be impartial toward the deformed, or that the creature, having no community and assembling his moral standards from three books, is a being whose internal spectator was built without the social feedback Smith assumes. I'm writing an essay pairing the two texts and want to know whether Frankenstein is best read as an illustration of Smith, a critique of him, or something stranger: a demonstration that sympathy as Smith describes it is real, powerful, and still insufficient. End by saying what, if anything, the novel contributes to the theory of moral sentiments that philosophical argument alone does not.
