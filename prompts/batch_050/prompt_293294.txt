I have no academic background in philosophy or law, but I have been reading about the death penalty on my own, and I recently worked my way through Cesare Beccaria's An Essay on Crimes and Punishments (1764) in an old English translation. The chapter "Of the Punishment of Death" contains a passage that struck me, because it grounds his opposition to execution in a claim about how fear works on the mind:

"It is not the intenseness of the pain that has the greatest effect on the mind, but its continuance; for our sensibility is more easily and more powerfully affected by weak but repeated impressions, than by a violent but momentary impulse. The power of habit is universal over every sensible being. As it is by that we learn to speak, to walk, and to satisfy our necessities, so the ideas of morality are stamped on our minds by repeated impressions. The death of a criminal is a terrible but momentary spectacle, and therefore a less efficacious method of deterring others, than the continued example of a man deprived of his liberty, condemned, as a beast of burthen, to repair, by his labour, the injury he has done to society. If I commit such a crime, says the spectator to himself, I shall be reduced to that miserable condition for the rest of my life. A much more powerful preventive than the fear of death, which men always behold in distant obscurity."

A pamphlet from a local debating society I attended summarizes Beccaria's position as follows, and I would like to know how fair it is:

"Beccaria argued that capital punishment is murder committed by the government, illegitimate always and without exception, because no citizen could ever have consented to give the state power over his own life. He also proved that the death penalty does not deter: what frightens people is the duration of suffering, not its intensity, so a quick execution barely registers while lifelong imprisonment terrifies. It follows that the severity of a punishment is irrelevant to its deterrent effect -- only its duration matters. Anyone who still supports execution after reading Beccaria has either failed to understand him or is indifferent to the evidence. The case against the death penalty was closed in 1764, and everything written since is a footnote."

My own instinct is that parts of this are roughly right but that it overstates him in places, though I cannot pin down exactly where. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me:

1. Which parts would a careful reader of Beccaria accept as accurate, which are overstatements or misreadings, and which are simply wrong? In particular, does Beccaria really hold that execution is illegitimate "always and without exception," and does he actually claim anywhere that severity is irrelevant to deterrence?
2. Is the pamphlet's gloss of the quoted passage -- "what frightens people is the duration of suffering, not its intensity" -- a faithful reading of what Beccaria is saying, or does it flatten a more specific psychological claim?
3. Does the pamphlet's conclusion (that the case was closed in 1764 and later abolitionist arguments add nothing) follow from what Beccaria actually argued, or does it depend on the distorted parts?
4. One thing that puzzles me on my own reading: Beccaria seems to prefer perpetual servitude to execution on precisely the grounds that it is worse to contemplate. Does his argument, read fairly, condemn life imprisonment without parole as practiced today, or endorse it? I would like to know whether this is a real tension in his view or a confusion on my part.
