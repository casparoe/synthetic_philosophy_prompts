I have just come from a long argument with a close friend about Keynes and the good life. Next week I am giving the main address at my economics department’s senior dinner. The audience will be graduating majors, their families, and several Keynes scholars. My friend reads Keynes’s speculation about economic necessity receding as an anti-ambition argument: once society has enough, careerism and competitive accumulation become collective bad habits. I think the harder point is that abundance removes the excuse for confusing means with ends before it supplies shared ends, leaving desires formed under scarcity to govern lives they no longer fit.

Act as a professional philosopher of economics and an experienced ceremonial speechwriter. Think carefully and brainstorm silently before drafting. Write a six-minute address built around an original distinction between emergency ambition—activity valuable because it relieves necessity—and cultivated ambition—activity valuable because of what it allows people and communities to become. Give graduates a definite standard for choosing work under relative abundance: neither maximize income indefinitely nor simply downshift because scarcity is over. The interpretation must structure the speech rather than decorate it, and the address must remain engaging to nonspecialists without becoming simplistic. Include one concrete career example and avoid quotations, citations, and reading lists.

Take a committed side in my dispute with my friend rather than presenting both readings as equally plausible. After the address, add a brief note explaining the main interpretive choice and where it depends on a contested reading of Keynes.
