I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all (did a CS conversion masters), and I need help refereeing an argument that blew up at lunch last week.

Some context. I fell down a Wikipedia rabbit hole about the axiom of choice — basically the principle that given any collection of non-empty sets, you can form a new set by picking one element out of each, even when there's no rule for picking. That led me to the 1904 paper where it first got stated, Ernst Zermelo's "Proof that Every Set Can Be Well-Ordered" (I read it in English translation). The bit that got me is this passage, where he's setting up his proof:

"Imagine that with every subset M′ there is associated an arbitrary element m₁′, that occurs in M′ itself; let m₁′ be called the "distinguished" element of M′. This yields a "covering" g of the set M by certain elements of the set M. The number of these coverings is equal to the product [of the cardinalities of all the subsets M′] and is certainly different from 0."

That last sentence is doing something weird — he just asserts the coverings exist, with a "certainly". I showed it to two coworkers and they immediatly started arguing.

Dan's opening move: the passage gives the game away. Zermelo doesn't argue that these coverings exist, he just says the number of them is "certainly different from 0", which means the axiom of choice is an assumption, full stop — a rule of the game you opt into, like picking a linter config. And the consequences show it's not a fact about anything real: Banach–Tarski, non-measurable sets, a "well-ordering of the real numbers" that provably nobody can write down or describe.

Yuki pushed back: if it's just a convention, it's strange that mathematicians had been relying on the principle for decades without noticing — stuff like "pick a representative from each equivalence class" was standard practice before anyone flagged it. Conventions that keep turning up unforced, in independent work by people who weren't trying to use them, don't look like conventions. They look like something being tracked.

Dan: long tacit use proves nothing. People tacitly assumed Euclidean geometry was built into physical space. People happily reasoned as if any collection of things forms a set until Russell's paradox. A widespread intuition is fully compatible with being wrong — and anyway we now know from Gödel and Cohen that the axiom is independent of the other standard axioms, so it's genuinely optional. Like a compile flag: some builds want it on, some off, and there's no fact of the matter beyond that.

Yuki: independence doesn't show there's no fact of the matter, it shows the other axioms are too weak to decide it. If a spec underdetermines some behaviour of a system, nobody concludes the system's actual behaviour is "conventional" — the spec is just silent. And rejecting choice isn't free: you lose that every vector space has a basis, that products of compact spaces are compact, that every field has an algebraic closure. If it's "just a convention", it's one you can't drop without breaking half the build.

Dan's last word: those are theorems *given* the axiom, fine, but "you'd lose theorems" is a practical point, not a truth-apt one — and Yuki still hasn't touched the actual passage. Zermelo writes down the *number* of these coverings as if the existence of vastly many choice functions could just be read off the concept of a set. Stating the thing you're supposed to be justifying and slapping "certainly" on it is the question, not the answer.
