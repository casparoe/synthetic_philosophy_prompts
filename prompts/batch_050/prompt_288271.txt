Two members of our hospital ethics comittee have been arguing for two years and the rest of us are tired of it. One defends a fairly strong version of the therapy/enhancement distinction: medicine's job is to restore or preserve species-typical normal functioning, and interventions that push people beyond that are a different kind of thing — maybe permissible, maybe not, but not medicine, and with no claim on healthcare priority. The other is a welfarist who thinks the distinction is at best a rough statistical heuristic and morally inert: what matters is expected benefit, risk, and fairness, and you can't read any of that off whether something gets labeled therapy. I've voluntold them for an adversarial collaboration and now they have to produce a joint statement. Draft it for them.

What I want: a numbered list of claims both can actually sign (I suspect they agree on more than they admit — e.g., that some interventions would be wrong for reasons having nothing to do with the therapy/enhancement line, and that "normal functioning" needs a reference class that isn't just smuggled-in normativity); then a disagreements table where each position is stated in wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where an answer would genuinely move at least one of them.

Please use these cases to pin everything down, since they're the ones the committee keeps circling: (1) the same growth hormone given to a kid with a diagnosed deficiency vs. a healthy kid in the 10th percentile; (2) a gene edit that confers malaria resistance in a healthy traveler — prevention or enhancement?; (3) a safe drug that cuts your sleep need from 8 hours to 5; (4) a cognitive intervention with an identical risk profile that raises a kid's IQ from 70 to 100 vs. from 100 to 130; (5) treating age-related muscle loss, which only recently became a billable disease, vs. extending healthy lifespan past anything humans have ever had.

For each crux, name which of these cases (or what evidence about them) both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts the case shows. A few cruxes I suspect are in there: whether there's a non-arbitrary account of normal function that survives case (5) without collapsing into "whatever's currently typical for us"; whether therapy has distributive priority even when an enhancement buys more welfare per dollar (imagine the committee's actual budget choice between a rare-disease treatment and a cheap safe boost for everyone); and whether positional goods — where the value of my enhancement depends on you not having it — break the welfarist framing or merely complicate it. Finally, if part of their fight is really verbal (I suspect the bit about what counts as "medicine" is), the statement itself should say which part and why.
