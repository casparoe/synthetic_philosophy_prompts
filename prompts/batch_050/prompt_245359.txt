In my political theory reading group we've been stuck for months on a fight that started with Han Feizi and has since metastasized into everything. One side argues that well-designed institutions make character irrelevant: get the laws, incentives, and role definitions right (fa and shu) and even mediocre, self-interested people will govern fine. The other side says no incentive system survives contact with actual officials unless the people running it have some virtue, and that designing for the worst case produces exactly the cynical, rule-gaming machinery that made the Qin infamous. The wierd part is both sides cite the same evidence -- the Qin collapse, principal-agent problems in economics, modern corporate compliance scandals -- and read it in opposite directions. My working hypothesis is that this is two disagreements wearing one hat: an empirical one about how much of official behavior incentives can actually control, and a conceptual one about whether "the institution works" means producing good outcomes or being robust to bad people. Am I right, or is there a deeper bedrock dispute underneath -- about human nature, say -- that neither side can argue its way out of? I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.
