I am trying to adjudicate among rival accounts of hinge commitments — the commitments that, following the remarks collected in Wittgenstein's On Certainty, stand fast for us and thereby make doubt and justification possible — and I keep finding that proponents argue past one another because no one first states what such an account is supposed to accomplish. Before I read any further, I want the specification.

Write the list of desiderata that any adequate theory of hinge commitments must meet, where adequacy is assessed relative to two fixed purposes: (i) underwriting a response to radical scepticism, and (ii) explaining what is going on when two parties disagree at the level of hinges rather than at the level of ordinary claims — religious converts, holders of opposed political worldviews, conspiracy theorists. If you think either purpose should be dropped or narrowed, say so at the outset and adjust the list accordingly.

For each desideratum: state the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is a genuine constraint on any theory in the area or instead a prejudice of one particular camp. The list must address at least the following:

1. Classification. Which cases the theory must get right: Moore-style certainties about one's hands and name, empirical propositions that come to function as hinges, and the boundary between hinge commitments and ordinary beliefs. What would count as getting the boundary wrong?
2. Distinctions. Which distinctions the theory cannot collapse: certainty versus knowledge, psychological indubitability versus methodological unrevisability, hinge commitments versus mere presuppositions or tacit assumptions. For each, a case in which collapsing it produces a false verdict.
3. The revision problem. On most accounts hinges are neither known nor justifiably believed, yet people plainly do change them — through conversion, therapy, coming to trust a community's testimony. The theory must explain how such change is possible without retroactively convicting the person of irrationality. Say why this is harder than it looks.
4. Hinge migration. Propositions appear to move in and out of hinge status over a life or a culture: a cosmological claim once beyond question becomes an empirical hypothesis. Must the theory treat this as a change in the proposition's epistemic role, or in its content, and what follows from each option?
5. The citation of hinges. People offer their hinges in justification of further claims all the time. Must an adequate account explain how this practice can be legitimate if hinges are not themselves epistemically assessable, or may it simply diagnose the practice as confused?

Then, three further tasks. First, flag any subset of your desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension — this is the part I care most about, since an unsatisfiable conjunction would itself be a result. Second, identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most existing positions, and say which ones it eliminates. Third, score the main families of accounts briefly against your list: non-propositional or anti-doxastic readings on which hinges are ways of acting rather than beliefs; propositional readings on which they form a sui generis kind of commitment; entitlement or rational-trust accounts; and naturalistic readings on which hinges are a kind of animal certainty. Keep the scoring short — half a page at most. The criteria, and the case behind each, are the deliverable.
