Take NASA's decision to launch the space shuttle Challenger in January 1986 and analyze it as a case of sphexishness — the pattern, named after the digger wasp, in which seemingly intelligent behavior turns out to be a rigid loop that repeats even when the world stops cooperating: the wasp deposits its paralyzed cricket at the burrow's entrance, goes inside to inspect, and if the cricket is moved a few inches while it is below, it drags the cricket back and inspects the burrow all over again, indefinitely. Map the case carefully: which parts of the decision process — the flight-readiness reviews, the late-night teleconference, the handling of the O-ring erosion data from earlier flights, the gradual acceptance of anomalies as normal — look genuinely sphexish, and which look like something else, such as deliberate trade-offs, motivated reasoning, or institutional pressure? Then press on the diagnosis: the engineers who argued against launching in the cold were clearly not sphexish, so if the disaster exhibits sphexishness, the rigidity must live in the organization rather than in its members — what would it mean for a procedure or institution to be mechanically rigid while every person inside it remains flexible and reflective? Finally, run the analysis backward: does the Challenger case show that sphexishness is a real and useful category for explaining failures of judgment, or does it instead suggest that calling a decision process sphexish is just a label we paste on deliberations whose outcomes we dislike?
