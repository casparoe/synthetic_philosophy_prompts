I help run the ethics review panel for a mid-sized defense contractor (don't ask), and we've got a new system to rule on next month. I want to predict how my fellow panelists will vote based on how we've ruled before. Our track record so far:

- We approved a ship-based point-defense system that detects and fires on incoming anti-ship missiles completely autonomously, on the grounds that no human could react in the miliseconds available. Human crew can only switch the mode on or off.
- We approved an autonomous system that jams and kinetically destroys enemy drones mid-flight with no human confirmation per engagement, again mostly on reaction-time grounds.
- We rejected a loitering munition that could identify and strike enemy radar vehicles on its own, even though the manufacturer offered a version where a human watches the feed and can veto. The panel said the veto would be rubber-stamping in practice.
- We rejected an upgrade that would let an existing human-supervised system select which of several tracked enemy soldiers to target, with the human merely confirming. The panel's writeup said the human was "in the loop the way a chaperone is in a room."
- We approved with conditions an AI targeting-assist that ranks potential targets (vehicles and artillery, not people) and a human pulls the trigger on each one.

The new system: a defensive weapon for protecting bases against swarming fast attack boats -- small boats with human crews that approach too fast and in too great a number for a human operator to engage each one individually. It autonomously selects and fires on the boats, killing their crews. There's an on/off switch and a human supervisor watching, but per-engagement decisions are machine-speed.

Predict how the panel votes and which prior ruling is doing the most work in your prediction. The tricky part, as I see it: one plausible reading of our record is "autonomy is fine for time-critical defense against objects, never against people," another is "time-critical defense gets a pass regardless of target," and a third is "the problem is always fake human oversight, and this one has it too." Say which rule you think actually fits our verdicts best, and if more than one fits, tell me what earlier case we'd have to relitigate -- or what hypothetical case we'd have to answer -- to find out which rule we were really following. Also flag where you think the panel is just being inconsistent, if anywhere.
