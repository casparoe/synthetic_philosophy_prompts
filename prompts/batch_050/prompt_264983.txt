I retired two years ago after four decades in entomology, and in retirement I have been working my way through the philosophy of action and free will. I keep returning to an animal I once studied in the field: the digger wasp, whose female paralyses her prey, drags it to the threshold of her burrow, sets it down, descends to inspect the chamber, and then emerges to haul the prey inside. If an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she is below ground, she retrieves it, sets it at the threshold again, and repeats the entire inspection from the beginning -- and will do so dozens of times, never varying the sequence. Behavior that looked like foresight turns out, on a single nudge, to be a loop. Philosophers have borrowed 'sphexish' for this kind of mechanical rigidity masquerading as intelligent flexibility, and its opposite for the revisable, responsive control we like to think distinguishes us.

I would like you to write me a satirical piece in the form of the employee handbook of a mid-sized corporation that has adopted 'antisphexishness' as an official core value. Roughly 600-750 words, formatted as numbered sections with the usual deadpan bureaucratic headings. It should cover, among other things: mandatory 'flexibility audits' in which managers covertly rearrange an employee's environment -- moving the stapler, reordering the agenda, shifting the cricket, so to speak -- to catch anyone executing a subroutine; disciplinary procedures for staff found to be 'operating in loop mode'; and an appeals process for contesting a finding of sphexishness.

The humor must come from real features of the underlying idea, not invented ones. Three I particularly want pressed: first, that the corporation's test for mechanical rigidity is itself a fixed, mechanical procedure, and the handbook should follow it with total earnestness, never noticing; second, that a worker who calmly adapts to the rearranged environment has arguably just learned a more sophisticated subroutine, so that the audit can only ever push the loop one level up; third, that an institution demanding spontaneous, unrehearsed flexibility from its employees is demanding it on schedule, in writing, with forms. The target is both the corporate co-option of genuine philosophical ideas and the deeper worry the wasp raises -- that the test which exposes the wasp might, applied at the right level, expose us. Keep the irony deadpan throughout: no winking, no narrator stepping in to explain the joke, and the final section should leave a careful reader genuinely unsure whether the handbook's authors or its employees come off worse.
