I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. Recently I fell down a rabbit hole about the Gettier problem: the idea that "justified true belief" doesn't quite add up to knowledge, because you can have a belief that is justified, and true, but true only by luck. The classic cases are things like glancing at a stopped clock that happens to show the right time, or inferring something true from a premise that's false. What really got my attention is what came after: apparently epistemologists spent decades proposing a fourth condition to patch the definition — no false lemmas, no undefeated defeaters, the right kind of causal connection — and each patch promptly generated new counterexamples. As someone who has lived through exactly this development cycle, I find the whole saga painfully recognizable.

Please write an incident postmortem, around 650 words, in the house style of a tech company's engineering blog: blameless, deadpan, with sections like Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis, Lessons Learned, and Action Items. The postmortem treats "justified true belief" as a legacy definition-of-knowledge service that has suffered repeated production incidents since a competitor (a junior analyst with a three-page writeup) shipped the first known exploits. The stopped clock and the false-premise cases should appear as incident tickets, and at least one later incident should involve fake barns in a county that functions as a contaminated staging environment — importantly, one where the "no false lemmas" hotfix offers no protection, since nothing false was ever assumed.

The satire should land the real structure of the debate rather than just mocking philosophers for being impractical.
