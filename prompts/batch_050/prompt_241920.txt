Last night I had a three-hour argument with a close friend that ended with us agreeing to disagree, and I'd like help understanding why we couldn't converge -- because I suspect our stalemate is a miniature of a stalemate that has existed in print for about a century and a half.

The setup: my friend is about to move across the country for a job with a startup whose prospects are genuinely uncertain, and she told me she has decided to simply believe the company will succeed, because acting as if it will fail would make her half-hearted and the half-heartedness would help cause the failure. I said she was doing exactly what W.K. Clifford condemned in his essay on the ethics of belief -- the shipowner who lets an unseaworthy vessel sail by stifling his doubts -- and that believing on insufficient evidence isn't just risky, it's a dereliction of a duty we owe to everyone who depends on our beliefs being well-formed. She countered with what I take to be William James's position: that some questions are forced, momentous, and live, that on such questions refusing to believe is itself a choice with consequences, and that certain truths (about trust, ventures, relationships) only become accessible if you commit before the evidence is in. I said James licenses wishful thinking; she said Clifford's standard is one nobody actually lives by and that pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty.

Neither of us moved. What struck me afterward is that the published debate seems equally frozen -- evidentialists keep restating the duty, Jamesians keep restating the exceptions, and the exchange has a scripted quality.

So here is what I'd like, and please answer in American English: a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the Clifford-James dispute (a) substantive, (b) at bottom verbal -- say, a disagreement over what counts as 'sufficient' evidence or over whether withholding belief is really a neutral act, (c) a case of two sides answering different questions under one heading -- one about the ethics of inquiry in general, the other about a special class of self-fulfilling or action-enabling beliefs, or (d) resting on a hidden empirical question, for instance about what habits of credence-formation actually produce true beliefs and good outcomes over a lifetime? My own working hypothesis is (c) plus a bit of (b): I think my friend and I never disagreed about the shipowner, and never would, and that Clifford and James might not have either -- that James carved out a class Clifford never meant to cover and then everyone argued past each other anyway. Test that hypothesis against what the two positions actually say rather than just accepting it.

Then, given your diagnosis: what would count as progress? Is there a distinction that, once drawn, dissolves most of the debate? A class of cases both sides must treat identically on pain of inconsistency? Or is this one of those disagreements -- like my friendship, I now fear -- where each side is protecting something real (intellectual integrity on one side, the possibility of a committed life on the other) and no resolution should be expected, only a principled division of territory? If the latter, tell me where the border should run.
