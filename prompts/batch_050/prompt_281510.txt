I'm head of partnerships at a mid-sized drone company, and a few times a year we hit decisions about whether to make some hard-to-reverse move early in order to lock in favorable terms: signing exclusivity with a supplier before our main rival does, announcing a public deadline for a deal, visibly cutting off talks with alternative vendors so our counterparty knows we have no fallback. Being first to commit feels like it wins. But a professor of mine used to talk about "commitment races" -- situations where both sides *can* commit, both know the first credible commitment tends to grab most of the surplus, and so both sprint to commit, and somehow everyone ends up worse off than if nobody could commit at all (incompatible commitments, value burned to make threats credible, deals that get worse as each side pre-emptively hardens its position). I want you to turn whatever is actually true about this into a decision procedure my team can run.

Before writing the procedure, spend some time brainstorming what the relevant distinctions even are -- things like: is a commitment actually irreversible or does it "leak" (i.e., can be renegotiated later when both sides have an incentive to), can the other side even verify we've committed, what happens when both parties commit to incompatible demands, and when the supposed race is illusory because there's no real first-mover advantage. Then give me the procedure itself: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on, the points where the procedure just stops and says "don't commit," and the points where it's honest that this is a judgment call that no checklist can settle. It should be runnable in an hour-long meeting by smart non-theorists. Board sign-off is required for anything exclusivity-related, so factor in that slow legal/board review means our "fast" commitments aren't always that fast.

Then run the procedure on two live cases of mine: (a) our battery supplier hints a rival is circling them too, and offers us a three-year exclusivity deal if we sign within six weeks, at terms slightly worse than we were hoping for; (b) whether to publicly announce we'll leave an industry standards consortium unless our communications protocol is adopted, which would be genuinely costly for us to walk back.
