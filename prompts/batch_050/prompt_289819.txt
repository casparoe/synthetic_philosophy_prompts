Read Kurt Vonnegut's Harrison Bergeron through the "equality of what?" debate. The Handicapper General enforces equality by imposing weights, masks, and distracting earpieces, dragging everyone down to the level of the least advantaged. Which metric of equality, if any, is the story actually targeting: welfare, resources, capabilities, or something cruder like equality of outcomes? A capability theorist, for instance, would presumably say that stripping Harrison of his strength and intelligence reduces his functionings without making anyone else capable of more, so the regime equalizes nothing anyone should care about; but does the story have a more sophisticated target in mind, such as the claim that any single metric, enforced without remainder, becomes tyrannical? I also want to know where the story resists the philosophical reading: Vonnegut wrote it as satire of a particular political moment, and the ballerina's death and Harrison's brief self-coronation may dramatize something about excellence, envy, or televised spectacle that the egalitarianism debate doesn't capture. Say whether the story is a fair criticism of any egalitarian view philosophers actually hold, or only of a caricature — and if the latter, whether that matters for its force as a thought experiment. Finally, flag which parts of the "equality of what?" debate are genuinely settled (e.g., that leveling down is widely rejected) and which remain live disputes among egalitarians, so the reading doesn't present a contested position as consensus.
