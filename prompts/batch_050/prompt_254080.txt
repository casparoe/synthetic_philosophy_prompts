The classic options are superiority (Hobbes-ish: laughter as sudden glory over someone), incongruity (expectation violated in some way), relief (Freud/Spencer: nervous energy discharged), and benign violation (something's a violation but comes off as okay). My own half-baked candidate is what I'm calling idle-machinery theory: humour happens when your interpretive machinery keeps churning after the meaning's already been settled, and the laugh is basically the mind noticing it's running on empty — so a pun is funny because your parser built two readings when one would've done, and anti-jokes work because the resolution arrives so early that the search process has nothing left to chew on.

Here's what I want. Take these five theories and score each one out of ten for how well it explains each of these cases:

1. The groan-at-a-pun response (people laughing and being annoyed at the same time)
2. Laughter at funerals or in tense meetings, with no joke involved
3. A joke that stops being funny the moment it's explained to you
4. Tickle-laughter, which doesn't seem to involve getting anything
5. Laughing at yourself slipping on ice before you check if anyone saw

Then give me an overall ranking of the five from best to worst general theory, based on the scores but weighted however you reckon is fair. Be honest about how confident you are in the ranking, and flag the single judgement you're least sure about — I suspect it'll be the tickling one, but prove me wrong. Feel free to be brutal about idle-machinery theory, I'm not precious about it.
