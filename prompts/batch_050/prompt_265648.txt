Last weekend a few of us were arguing over dinner about the utility monster — you know, Nozick's creature that derives vastly more pleasure from any given resource than the rest of us, so that maximising total happiness means handing absolutely everything over to it. Everyone round the table agreed the monster outcome was monstrous, but nobody could say *why*, because we kept finding cases where the same-looking reasoning seemed perfectly fine. So I'd like help sorting out what I actually think. Act as a moral philosopher who's especially good at reflective equilibrium — the business of going back and forth between gut verdicts on cases and general principles until they fit. Think carefully, and maybe sketch two or three candidate principles to yourself before settling on one.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. Splitting a birthday cake so the child who adores cake gets a bigger slice than the others: completely fine. A society diverting all of its surplus resources to one person who converts them into happiness far more efficiently than anyone else: not fine, and still not fine even if the total really is maximised. A hospital giving a scarce drug to the patient who stands to gain the most life-years from it: fine. Our friend group pooling money to buy concert tickets for whichever one of us would be most thrilled: fine. Harvesting one healthy person's organs to save five others: not fine. Giving the entire office pay rise to the one colleague who'd be ecstatic about it rather than splitting it evenly: feels wrong, though I genuinely can't tell whether that's the cake case writ large or the monster case writ small. And the case I keep going back and forth on: prospective parents who could genetically tweak their child so she experiences about ten times as much pleasure from everything — is that creating a little monster, or just giving her a better life?

My tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: maximising aggregate welfare is the right rule for distributing a fixed windfall among people who are already roughly equal, but the wrong rule once it means someone ends up with nothing. Test that against my cases, repair it or replace it. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each no more than two or three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your best principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases myself; (2) which of my cases motivates each clause of it; (3) its verdict on the pay rise; (4) its verdict on the engineered child; (5) the one verdict of mine your principle can't save — or, if it saves them all, the price it pays in complexity that should make me suspicious of it.
