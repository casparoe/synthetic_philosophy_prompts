I serve on the grants committee of a privately endowed astronomical institute whose remaining endowment can fund exactly one more major project. Two rival cosmological models are on the table, both consistent with every observation to date: the Populous model, on which civilizations like ours arise on roughly one planet per galaxy, and the Solitary model, on which the emergence of intelligence is so improbable that our own existence was a near-miracle. A proposed next-generation deep survey would settle the question within a decade but would consume the entire endowment; the only alternative is a diversified portfolio of smaller studies in unrelated fields. Several senior members of our scientific advisory board argue we should decline the survey on purely armchair grounds: given that we exist, they reason, and given that our existence is vastly more probable under the Populous model, that model is already sufficiently confirmed, and spending the endowment to check it would be waste. Their opponents reply that this style of reasoning would license dismissing empirical questions without ever collecting data — that a theory ought not to be accepted merely because it makes our existence unsurprising. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend either funding the survey or redirecting the endowment; state whether, as a matter of institute policy, anthropic evidence of this kind should weigh in future funding decisions; and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you reject, including whether the inference from our existence to the Populous model is legitimate reasoning or an abuse of it.
