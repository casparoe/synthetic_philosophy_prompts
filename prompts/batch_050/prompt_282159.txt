Spent like three hours last night arguing with my friend Marcus about Pascal's mugging and I walked away feeling like he actually won, which is irritating because his conclusion is clearly nuts. Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it, cleaned up a bit:

(1) Rational choice is maximizing expected utility: for each option, multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability, sum it up, pick the biggest total.
(2) For any finite amount of value you care about, and any probability bigger than zero, there's always some larger amount of value such that probability-times-that beats your original number.
(3) So however tiny the probability you assign to some wild claim, there is always a version of the claim big enough that acting on it comes out ahead in expectation.
(4) The mugger says give me your wallet or I'll use my powers to torture a trillion trillion conscious beings forever. Whatever probability you assign to that, there's some number of tortured beings large enough to make handing over the wallet the expected-utility winner.
(5) So expected utility maximization tells you to hand over the wallet.
(6) But that's obviously wrong -- a decision rule that can be yanked around by any stranger willing to say a sufficiently big number is useless, you'd be mugged constantly, and anyone who actually lived by it would be a walking exploit.
(7) Therefore expected utility maximization is just broken as a theory of rational choice, and we should go find something else.

I have this strong feeling something slipped between (3) and (4) or (4) and (5) but I couldn't pin it down in the moment and it's driving me crazy. Which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to sacrifice something I'd rather keep (1) -- it seems like the one piece of decision theory I really don't want to give up -- but tell me honestly what that commits me to.

Also, Marcus had a fallback I didn't know what to do with: he said if I refuse the wallet-handover, I must be committed to the view that some outcomes are so improbable they should just be ignored entirely, and then he asked where the cutoff is and what happens for probabilities just above it. Is he right that refusing the mugger forces that on me?
