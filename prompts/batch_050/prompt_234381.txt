I am designing a problem set for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time, and I need a thought experiment constructed to order. Here is the passage it must engage, from Augustine's Confessions, Book XI, chapter 1, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation (1886):

"O Lord, since eternity is Yours, are You ignorant of the things which I say unto You? Or see Thou at the time that which comes to pass in time?"

Augustine's question assumes an apparent dilemma: either a knower is ignorant of temporal happenings, or she knows them at a time -- that is, from within the temporal order, her epistemic state changing as events unfold. Your task is to build a single thought experiment that stress-tests this dilemma, and then to demonstrate that the case works. The scenario itself should be secular; the analysis must answer back to Augustine.

Specifications:

1. One scenario, one paragraph (the quoted passage does not count toward length, and your full reply should run no more than about 800 words). Ordinary life only: a newsroom, a control room, an archive, a household -- no exotic physics, no time travel, no perfect predictors. Any knower in the story may be no more cognitively capable than a competent, attentive human being, and may not rely on foreknowledge, precognition, or stipulation that she simply "knows what will happen."

2. The case must be engineered so that two positions deliver different verdicts about the protagonist's epistemic state: (a) the view that knowledge of temporal events is essentially tensed -- one can know an event only as present, as past-via-memory, or as future-via-anticipation, so that knowing everything about a process across time requires one's own knowledge to change over time; and (b) the view that a knower can stand in a knowledge relation to a whole ordered sequence of events without her temporal position fixing or altering what she knows. Both verdicts must fall out of the positions themselves, not from deception, instrument failure, forgetfulness, or anyone being misinformed.

3. After the scenario: state the verdict each position gives and why; identify the single structural feature of the case that makes (b) peel off from (a); and state what happens to the case if that feature is removed -- specifically, whether it collapses into a trivial instance of ordinary memory or prediction.

4. Give the strongest counterattack available to the defender of (a): the move by which she would argue that your protagonist's knowledge is still tensed after all, and why that move should or should not succeed.

5. Finally, commit. Take a definite position -- not a menu of options -- on whether Augustine's question in the quoted passage rests on a false dilemma, or whether your case, despite appearances, leaves the dilemma intact. Give your reasons, and say which part of Augustine's underlying concern your verdict leaves standing even if you side against the dilemma.
