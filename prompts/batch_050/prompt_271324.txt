I am a novelist, and I keep colliding with a philosophical dispute I cannot get to the bottom of, so I would like a diagnosis rather than another survey of it.

When I write that a character dreamed she was back in her childhood kitchen, I take myself to be describing an episode of consciousness that unfolded during the night: something it was like for her, with a duration, occurring more or less when her body lay asleep. Reading around, I have discovered that this naive picture — the received view, as it is called — has been attacked from two directions for decades. Norman Malcolm, in his book Dreaming, argued that the very idea of a dream as an experience had while asleep is confused: the sole criterion for having dreamed is the waking report, a sleeping person is in no position to judge anything, and so a dream cannot be located in the night the way a pain can. Daniel Dennett, in his essay asking whether dreams are experiences at all, proposed instead his cassette theory: the dream narrative might be composed wholesale at the moment of waking, or pre-recorded and merely played into memory, so that even the apparent temporal spread of a dream is an artifact of recollection — which would explain the familiar dreams that seem uncannily tailored to the noise that wakes us.

What puzzles me is that the dispute did not die when sleep science arrived. The discovery of REM sleep and its correlation with dream reports, and later the lucid-dreaming experiments in which subjects signaled with pre-agreed eye movements from inside a dream, seemed to give the naive picture hard evidence: dream content appears to unfold in real time during sleep. And yet the philosophical argument about whether dreams are experiences persists, as if the laboratory results were beside the point.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that the two sides mean different things by experience: one side takes it to be whatever a sincere report is a report of, the other takes it to be a stream of consciousness with a life independent of any later telling, and the empirical findings do not touch the disagreement because each side reads them through its own definition.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, empirical but obscured, or conceptual in a way no polysomnograph could settle? Please ground the diagnosis in what Malcolm and Dennett actually argue, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, an agreed theory of what makes a waking report authoritative about a past night — and say whether anything should make me write my characters' dreams differently. At the end, state how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the specific point in it you are least sure of.
