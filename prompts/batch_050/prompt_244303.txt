A colleague in the physics department has asked me to read a draft chapter of a popular-audience book he is writing on Gödel's theorems and the human mind. I teach philosophy of mind, so he wants my opinion before it goes to press, and I would like a careful second assessment before I respond to him. Here is the core passage of his chapter, which I am reproducing in my own words rather than quoting, since he has asked that the draft not be circulated:

Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that any formal system rich enough to do arithmetic contains true statements that the system itself cannot prove. But a human mathematician, presented with such a system, can always step outside it and see that the unprovable statement is in fact true -- this is exactly what we do when we grasp the construction. Since a computer is nothing but a formal system implemented in hardware, it follows that there will always be mathematical truths the machine cannot establish but which the human mind can know. J. R. Lucas drew precisely this conclusion in 1961, and Roger Penrose later strengthened the argument, using it to show that artificial intelligence is impossible and that human consciousness must depend on non-computable physics, specifically quantum gravitational effects in the brain's microtubules. In short, Gödel proved mathematically that the mind is not a machine.

My instinct is that the first step of the argument is roughly right, that something goes wrong in the move from what the mathematician can do with respect to a given system to what any machine could do in general, and that the final sentence overshoots badly. But I cannot currently say with precision where the defensible claims end and the overreach begins, and I am not confident I could reconstruct the strongest version of the Lucas--Penrose line before criticizing his rendition of it.

Here is what I would like. First, an accuracy audit of the passage: which claims would a careful statement of the incompleteness theorems support, which subtly conflate distinct claims (I suspect at least one equivocation between what is true of a particular specified machine and what is true of machines in general, and possibly a slide between consistency and truth), and which attribute to Gödel, Lucas, or Penrose conclusions stronger than any of them actually established? Second, a statement of the strongest available version of the anti-mechanist argument -- I believe it turns on whether the machine in question is known to be consistent, and I recall that Gödel himself drew only a disjunctive conclusion, though I may be misremembering -- together with the standard objections and whether they are decisive. Third, an assessment of whether my colleague's final sentence follows from the accurate parts of his passage, and if not, what the strongest conclusion is that a Gödelian argument of this kind could support. Please do not recommend further reading; I want the analysis itself.
