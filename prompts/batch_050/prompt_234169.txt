I’m inclined to defend a particular reading of Death in Damascus: once Death has made her appointment and the servant’s temperament is stable enough, neither staying nor fleeing is irrational given the evidence available to him; the story shows that a blameless choice can still make disaster certain, rather than proving that rationality defeats itself. What would count against this? Separate objections that would merely complicate the view from ones that would sink it: cases where the servant could acquire genuinely decision-relevant information, accounts on which Death’s prediction depends counterfactually on his deliberation rather than on a fixed trait, and arguments that rationality should assess the whole deliberation-prediction loop rather than just the final choice. Also say which concessions a defender should make, whether the view collapses into fatalism, and which objection you’d find most decisive. British English please.
