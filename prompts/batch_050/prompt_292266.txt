I'm teaching an undergraduate course on rational choice, and we've just finished the unit on bounded rationality — Herbert Simon's idea that, given limited time, limited information, and search that itself costs something, the rational agent doesn't optimize but "satisfices": sets an aspiration level, searches until something clears it, and stops. My students grasped the definition quickly but kept treating satisficing as a kind of laziness or defeat — what you settle for when you can't be bothered to find the best. I'd like a poem that corrects that misreading, and I'd like you to write it.

You are a poet with serious training in the philosophy of economics and decision theory; take your time and, before composing, spend a moment thinking about which six end-words will let the form do philosophical work. The poem should be a sestina. Here's the structural conceit I want you to exploit: a sestina exhaustively permutes its six end-words through six stanzas according to a fixed pattern — it is, formally, a kind of exhaustive search, the very thing the maximizer does with options and the satisficer declines to do. So give me a speaker who begins as a maximizer, trapped inside the form's compulsory permutations, turning the same six words over and over in search of the perfect arrangement — and let the envoi, where the form finally releases its grip, deliver the satisficing insight: that stopping at what meets your aspirations isn't a failure of rationality but the only rational response to a world where searching has costs. The turn should feel earned by the form itself, not pasted on.

A few constraints. The poem should get the idea right rather than just name-drop it: someone who has read Simon should recognize the aspiration level, the costliness of continued search, and the point that "good enough" is a standard one sets in advance rather than an excuse one invents afterward. Please don't use Simon's name or the word "satisficing" inside the poem — the idea should be legible without its labels. The six end-words should be ordinary (something like enough, search, cost, best, stop, choose, though you may adjust them if better ones serve the conceit), and the poem should work as a poem first: a reader who knows nothing about decision theory should still find it moving. After the final stanza, give me one short prose line, beginning with "Thesis:", stating in a single sentence the claim about rationality the poem is making.
