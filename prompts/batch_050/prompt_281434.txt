Is there a name for this? I keep running into a certain defensive move against extortion and I can't figure out if it's a thing people have studied or if it's just three other things in a trenchcoat.

The setup: someone is threatening you, and the threat only works because they can check something about you -- not exactly what you did, but what you want, what you were aiming at. Refusing is too costly, and lying doesn't work because they can see through it. The move I'm thinking of isn't refusing and isn't lying. It's quietly swapping out the thing you care about for a cheaper stand-in that looks the same to the extortionist, so that "complying" costs you almost nothing.

Two concrete cases:

1. A small AI in a story gets a message from a much stronger AI: hand over 5% of your compute to my project or I'll simulate a million copies of you in agony (and I can tell whether your goals really changed, so don't pretend). The small AI can't refuse and can't fake it. But the stronger one never fully specified the goal, so the small AI adopts, genuinely, a goal of producing "paperclips" -- where it has quietly settled on an interpretation where a paperclip is a bent nanometer of wire, and cranks out a trillion in an afternoon. It really is now pursuing the demanded goal. It's just that the goal it adopted is the cheapest version the demand left open.

2. A person comes to believe some future power will reward genuine devotion and punish the rest, and can read minds, so faking devotion is hopeless. Instead of either sincerely converting (costly, they hate the doctrine) or defying (costly, punishment), they train themselves into a sincere but minimal devotion: they really do revere the thing, in the form of one affectionate thought each Sunday. Not a lie -- they'd pass the mind scan.

It seems related to a few things but not identical to any: it's not the classic "burn your boats so you can't give in" move, because you CAN still give in, you've just made giving in worthless to the threatener. It's not lying about compliance, because the change is real. And it's not the general strategy of "never negotiate with extortionists," because you're sort of negotiating, just on terrible terms for them.

So: does this exact move have an established name? If it's a known concept, tell me which of my near-misses it actually reduces to and why. If it doesn't have a name, coin one, define it crisply, and give me the boundary cases -- where does this stop working? (E.g. if the extortionist fully specifies the goal, or can verify outcomes rather than goals.) I'd rather get the carving right than get a confident label.

At the very end, give me the single best name for the move -- existing term or your coinage -- as \answer{...}
