I have a standing disagreement with a colleague about how much the silence of the sky actually tells us. She treats the Fermi observation as strong evidence that technological intelligence is exceedingly rare; I suspect the observation has little bite, because of what I call the quiet-corridor hypothesis: a civilization's detectable era of isotropic, high-power, spectrally inefficient broadcasts lasts only a century or two before its communication becomes directed, compressed, and low-power, at which point it is practically indistinguishable from noise at interstellar distances, so even a crowded galaxy would look empty to searches like ours. Give me your credence that this detectability-window class of explanation, rather than the rarity of life or of intelligence, accounts for the great silence, and derive the number rather than asserting it. Write the response in American English as exactly six numbered items, each no more than a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1 through 4 should each state one sub-question your credence depends on (for example, the probability that simple life is common, the probability that technological intelligence arises given life, the probability that detectable eras are short given technological intelligence, and the probability that a short window would leave the sky looking as quiet as we find it), assigning a rough probability and one line of justification to each; item 5 should show how the terms combine into your final credence; and item 6 should name the term your answer is most sensitive to and the observation or argument that would move it the most.
