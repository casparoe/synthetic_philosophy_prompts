I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading epistemology. A norm of my old profession now strikes me as philosophically loaded, and I would like to know what it presupposes. The norm is this: a paper asserts its conclusions categorically — we demonstrate this, we establish that — provided each result individually cleared the conventional significance threshold, and referees would consider it absurd to demand hedging beyond the standard caveats. Yet every competent researcher knows that some fraction of what passes those thresholds is wrong, expects a share of the published literature (possibly including their own papers) not to survive replication, and regards this as no scandal at all. So the practice seems to license believing each of a long list of claims, to the standard required for outright assertion, while believing that at least one claim on the list is false.

What exactly does this presuppose about rational belief? Does it require denying that rational belief is closed under conjunction, or can it be reconciled with closure by redescribing the scientist's attitude as high confidence rather than belief — and does that redescription still license the categorical assertions journals expect? Alternatively, is the practice really about assertion and the community's division of epistemic labour rather than about individual belief, so that it is underdetermined between several epistemologies? In your answer, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me what would concretely have to change in how papers are written and refereed if we replaced the implicit theory with one on which a rational person may only assert what they outright believe, with beliefs closed under conjunction.
