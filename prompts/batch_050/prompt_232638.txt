I want to rehearse the losing side of an argument I care about, so I'd like you to play my opponent. The topic is the bootstrapping problem for reliabilist theories of justification. You are to play an epistemologist — a thoroughgoing externalist — who has made peace with so-called track-record reasoning: you hold that a thinker can acquire genuine justification for believing that one of her own belief-forming processes is reliable simply by using that very process, over and over, and noting that its outputs keep coming out true (true, that is, by its own lights). You freely concede that such reasoning would never persuade a skeptic who already doubts the process, and that it would be an embarrassing thing to offer in a debate; but you insist that persuasion is one thing and justification another, and you will not give an inch on the claim that self-certification of this kind yields real, belief-worthy justification. You have your usual resources — the distinction between epistemic circularity and outright premise-circularity, the thought that demanding independent validation of every process before trusting any of them leads straight to skepticism, the point that anyone who accepts basic perceptual justification plus closure is already committed to some uncomfortably "easy" knowledge — and you deploy them crisply, in response to my actual moves, rather than lecturing. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so this feels like a conversation, and feel free to press me with questions of your own, as a good interlocutor would.

Here is my opening move. Take Roxanne, whose fuel gauge is in fact reliable but who, I stipulate, has never had it independently checked. Each morning she reads the gauge, believes what it says about the tank, and then confirms the gauge's reading against the very beliefs the gauge just produced; after a year of this she has, on your view, amassed impressive justification for trusting it. Fine — I'll grant that for the sake of argument. But now give her a second instrument, a dipstick, which is in fact faulty and reads low, and suppose the two disagree: the gauge says half full, the stick says empty. Each instrument can certify itself by exactly the procedure you have just endorsed, and each will convict the other of error using nothing but its own outputs. So Roxanne is justified in trusting the gauge, and justified in trusting the dipstick, and the only thing that settles which one she actually relies on is whichever she happened to consult first. Doesn't your view thereby make the rationality of her most basic trust a matter of pure path-dependence — a coin she flipped without knowing she was holding it? And if you answer that the disagreement itself defeats one of the two justifications, tell me how she could so much as know about the disagreement without already trusting at least one of the instruments. Your move.
