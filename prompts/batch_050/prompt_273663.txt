So I work on a risk-scoring tool at my job (nothing criminal-justice-y, more like flagging transactions for review) and I keep going in circles on fairness. Every time I convince myself we should enforce one notion, someone points out a case where it looks terrible. Equal false positive rates across groups? Sure, until base rates differ. Calibrated scores? Sure, until the error rates diverge. Treat similar people similarly? Sure, until you ask what "similar" means when the features encode group membership three layers deep.

Before I pick anything to actually implement, I want the spec first: what would a theory of fairness for a deployed scoring system have to get right to count as adequate? Write the desiderata list. For each requirement, give me the case or scenario that motivates it (concrete, like an actual scoring situation, not a thought experiment about trolleys), and say whether it's a genuine constraint or just something one camp insists on. I especially want:

- which distinctions the theory can't collapse (e.g., between fair scores and fair decisions downstream of scores, or between fairness at the group level and the individual level)
- which statistical facts it has to respect (base rates, imperfect labels, feedback loops from acting on the score)
- which pairs of desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable given known impossibility results, and roughly why -- this is the part I care most about, since if my list is inconsistent I need to know before I build to it
- which single requirement rules out the most of the criteria people actually propose in practice

Also flag any desideratum that sounds obviously right but turns out to be a matter of taste rather than something a theory could be forced to satisfy.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. If you want to briefly note how the usual suspects (calibration, equalized error rates, demographic parity, individual fairness) fare against the list at the end, fine, but don't turn it into a scorecard -- I can do that part myself.

End with a one-sentence summary as the last line, starting with "Summary:" -- something like the single most important thing I'd get wrong if I just picked a criterion without doing this exercise.
