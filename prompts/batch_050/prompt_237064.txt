I'm a retired biochemist (enzyme kinetics, mostly) who has gotten way too deep into philosophy since hanging up the pipettes, and I've ended up in a reading group at the local library. Last week two of the other regulars -- a retired GP and a guy who used to write software -- got into a real back-and-forth about Libet's experiments, and I've been turning it over since. Their dispute went roughly like this. Round one: the GP said the experiments basically kill off free will in the everyday sense, because the readiness potential in the motor cortex ramps up well before the person reports first feeling the urge to move, so the conscious decision is just the brain announcing to itself something it already decided unconsciously. The software guy replied that this is a wild overreach, because the task is about flicking your wrist at an arbitrary, meaningless moment when nothing whatsoever hangs on the outcome -- of course a decision with zero stakes and no reasons attached gets delegated to some background neural machinery, but that tells you nothing about deliberated choices like whether to accept a job offer, where reasons are actually weighed. Round two: the GP answered that if unconscious brain activity settles even one class of decisions before consciousness gets involved, the burden shifts to anyone claiming consciousness does causal work elsewhere, and besides there are later experiments (he mentioned the Soon study where they predicted which button someone would press from brain scans several seconds in advance, albeit not perfectly) suggesting the effect isn't confined to wrist flicks. The software guy's counter was twofold: first, the prediction accuracy in those studies is only modestly above chance, which is consistent with the early signal just being a slight bias or a buildup of neural noise that hasn't committed to anything yet -- he waved his hands about how the readiness potential might just reflect random fluctuations crossing a threshold rather than a fait accompli. Second, he pointed out that even Libet himself found that subjects could veto the movement after becoming conscious of the urge, so if anything the experiments discovered a conscious brake, not the absence of conscious control. Round three, where it got heated: the GP said the veto is no help because the veto presumably also has unconscious precursors, it's just turtles all the way down, and the noise-accumulation story concedes the whole game anyway -- if your "decisions" are noise crossing a threshold, that's not exactly the rational free agency people think they have. The software guy said that's moving the goalposts: the original claim was that experiments prove the brain decides first and consciousness is a spectator, and now he's defending something much vaguer, that determinism-ish processes underlie decision-making, which nobody at the table was disputing.

I genuinely can't decide who got the better of it, partly because my instincts as an experimentalist keep butting in (the GP seems to me too confident about what these paradigms measure, but the software guy's "that only applies to trivial choices" move feels a bit too convenient). Can you go through the exchange point by point and say who's right about what -- e.g. whether the veto argument actually rescues anything, whether the noise/threshold reinterpretation concedes too much, and whether "it's only arbitrary motor decisions" is a fair limitation or a dodge? Also: is there something both of them are missing, some assumption they're sharing that maybe shouldn't be shared? And if you had to call it, who's more right overall?
