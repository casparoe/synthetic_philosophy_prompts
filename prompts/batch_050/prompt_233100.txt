Every fall I teach a grad seminar on contractualism, and I keep a running handout of objections to Scanlon's view in What We Owe to Each Other. The students recieve the same six every year and honestly I'm getting bored of them. The handout currently has:

1. The numbers problem: since wrongness is fixed by whether any single individual could reasonably reject a principle, the sheer number of people affected can't directly count, so the theory struggles to say why we should save the many rather than the one when the individual claims are comparable. The patches (tie-breaking, letting the size of one person's loss stand in for numbers, complaint-weighting) all look ad hoc.

2. The redundancy objection: once you've laid out the personal reasons individuals have, the extra layer of "could not reasonably reject" does no real work. Justifiability-to-each is a wheel that turns nothing.

3. The direction-of-explanation objection: what makes an act wrong is what it does to the victim, not anything about hypothetical agreement. Contractualism treats rejectability as the disease when it's really just the symptom -- the features that ground the rejection are doing the actual moral explaining.

4. The exclusion problem: nonhuman animals, the severely cognitively impaired, maybe future persons, can't be owed justification or couldn't hold anyone to a principle, so the theory either leaves them morally invisible or imports a trustee fudge that isn't really contractualist anymore.

5. The non-identity problem: person-affecting justifiability can't condemn choices that predictably harm people who wouldn't have existed under the alternative (the risky policy, the depleted world), because no actual individual has a complaint.

6. The indeterminacy objection: "reasonably rejectable" is too vague to settle hard cases, so the theory is a schema without verdicts.

Here's the assignment I want to set for the last week of the seminar, and I want to road-test it on you first: come up with a seventh objection that is genuinely not on this list. Ground rules. It can't be a special case, rewording, or combination of 1-6 (so no "contractualism can't handle climate change" -- that's 4 and 5 wearing a hat). And here's the kicker: the objection has to survive every concession Scanlon could plausibly make on those six fronts. So suppose he fully cracks aggregation, generously extends justification to animals and future people, and tightens up the rejectability standard until it's as precise as you like. What, if anything, is still wrong with the core claim that an act is wrong iff it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject?

Give me the one objection, stated as sharply as you can manage, and say which of the six it comes closest to so I can check it's not a disguised variant. Then play defense: how would Scanlon's best reader respond, and does the response actually work? I'd rather have one objection developed properly than a laundry list of five.
