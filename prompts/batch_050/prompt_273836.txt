I run a final-year undergraduate seminar in political philosophy, and next term I have a 75-minute session on the evolution of cooperation. There will be about 24 students, and by that point they will already have met the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and an informal statement of the folk theorem, so they all "know" that repetition sustains cooperation. What I want them to feel, and what none of the standard treatments conveys, is how fragile the famous reciprocal strategies become once moves can be misimplemented or misperceived: the way two players who both intend to cooperate can slide into a vendetta because one tremble gets echoed back and forth, and the way a capacity for forgiveness or a win-stay-lose-shift-style rule suddenly matters in a way it never does in the noiseless case. Most textbook accounts, in my experience, present Tit-for-Tat as the simple winner of the early computer tournaments and stop there, so my students come away thinking the problem of cooperation is basically solved. I want to un-solve it for them.

Please design an in-class game that stages exactly this. It should have at least two phases so the contrast does the teaching: a baseline phase of finitely iterated play in pairs with clean moves, and then a phase in which each player's intended move has a small, known probability of being flipped (or misread by their partner) before it counts, using a mechanism that is transparent and quick, such as dice or marked cards. Give me concrete rules, a payoff scheme with actual numbers that works without real money (points, and what if anything they convert into), a procedure for pairing and re-pairing that avoids both permanent pairs and total anonymity, instructions for what players may and may not say to one another between rounds, and a scoresheet layout I can photocopy. Crucially, I do not want to announce the noise in advance of the second phase; I want the students to discover that something is going wrong and to work out for themselves why their partner "betrayed" them, so please think through how to run the noise mechanism without revealing it and how to handle the moment when someone accuses the apparatus of cheating.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps what actually happened in the room onto the theory: which moments correspond to the echo or feud problem for unforgiving reciprocal strategies, which correspond to the advantages of generosity and contrition, and how the shadow of the future and the endgame effect showed up (or failed to) in finite play. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I am thinking of things like students coordinating on always-defect out of boredom, the noise mechanism slowing the game to a crawl, or someone gaming the points scheme — and how to pre-empt or recover from each. Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce: human participants with reputations, chat, and a finite clock are not an evolutionary replicator dynamic, so tell me which features of the noise-and-forgiveness literature I will have to supply by hand in discussion rather than letting the game generate them. Please write your answer in British English.
