I've settled on a view about contested monuments: a public statue is best understood as ongoing speech by the community that maintains it. Each year the monument is left standing, the community says the thing again, so the ethics of keeping it is the ethics of saying it now, not the ethics of judging the dead. On this view, removal debates should be settled by what the monument expresses to the people who currently live under it, and by whether that message can be changed short of removal (relocation, added context, counter-monuments). What would count against this position? I'm not interested in the standard "who decides?" objection; I want the deeper defeaters: cases where a monument's meaning seems not to be up to the present community at all; commitments the view carries about other inherited public speech (street names, anthems, ceremonial language) that might prove unacceptable; the objection that it makes every monument hostage to present majorities and thereby destroys commemoration's point of speaking across generations; and any fact about how meaning actually works, in semantics or in social practice, that would break the analogy between statues and utterances. Which of these would a defender of the view concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the position has been qualified into something that risks nothing, and if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind.
