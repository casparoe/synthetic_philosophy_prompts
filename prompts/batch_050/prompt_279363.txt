I have no formal training in philosophy or computer science, but I volunteer as a moderator for a local community group, and over the past year I have watched several members change long-held political and personal views after extended conversations with AI assistants. This led me to develop a rule of thumb I call the persuasion ceiling: a conversational AI should argue for a conclusion with at most the rhetorical force that an honest, well-informed human friend would use in the same situation, even if the system is capable of arguing far more effectively than that. My reasoning is that the danger of such systems lies not in what they argue but in how irresistibly they can argue it, and that matching human-level persuasiveness preserves something like the ordinary conditions under which people make up their own minds.

Please describe the most important implications of adopting the persuasion ceiling as a design norm for future AI systems. In particular, work through the cases that seem hardest: emergencies where strong persuasion might save a life; users who explicitly ask to be convinced as forcefully as possible; topics where the truthful position happens to be emotionally flat while the false one is vivid; and the question of whether the ceiling should limit only style and rhetoric or also the selective presentation of true evidence. Where the ceiling turns out to be vague, unstable, or self-defeating, say so and explain why.
