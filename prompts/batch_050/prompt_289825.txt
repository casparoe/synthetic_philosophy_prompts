For the better part of a decade, the discussion of whose values an aligned AI system should serve has consolidated into three positions that appear to talk past one another. The preference-aggregation camp holds that a system should learn and combine the stated or revealed preferences of the people affected by it, treating the problem as one of extrapolation and social choice. The moral-deference camp holds that alignment means conforming to whatever is actually morally correct, with human input functioning as fallible evidence about that rather than as constitutive of the target. The legitimacy camp, drawing on the liberal-neutrality tradition, holds that no specification of correct outputs can settle the matter at all: what is needed is fair procedure, constitutional constraint, and recourse, on the model of legitimate political authority rather than of a well-chosen utility function. Each side's arguments are well rehearsed, the literature largely recycles them, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed camps in years.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse persists because the three camps are answering different questions under a single heading: the aggregationists are asking a question about fidelity to principals, the deference camp a question about moral correctness, and the legitimacy camp a question about authority, with the word alignment quietly doing duty for all three. Assess this. Is the disagreement genuinely first-order — about what values are or what morality requires — or is it a hidden factual disagreement (for instance, about whether human preferences are coherent and stable enough to aggregate), a disagreement about what counts as evidence in ethics, a disagreement about which cases are data, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what each position actually commits to, not with summaries of it. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that forces the camps onto common ground, an empirical finding about preference structure, a formal result in the spirit of social-choice impossibility theorems, or an abandonment of the shared question — or argue instead that no progress should be expected, and explain why the impasse is a feature of the subject matter rather than a failure of the participants. I am not asking which camp is right; I am asking what kind of disagreement this is.
