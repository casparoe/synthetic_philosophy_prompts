An ethicist friend of mine -- a veteran of the same forums I read, where suffering-focused ethics has been argued to death and back -- has been giving me her verdicts on a series of trade-off cases I've been feeding her. I want to know whether her pattern of answers pins down a determinate theory, and what she'd say about a new case I've constructed. Her verdicts so far:

1. One stranger endures an afternoon tension headache so that another stranger gets an afternoon of mild but genuine pleasure. Permissible, she says, assuming the headache is fully compensated by the pleasure and nothing else hangs on it.

2. A thousand people each endure a week of moderate, non-traumatizing pain -- roughly a bad but bearable flu -- so that ten thousand others each gain a year of ordinary happy life. Permissible.

3. One person endures that same week of moderate pain so that a million people each gain that year of ordinary happy life. Still permissible; she is entirely comfortable with aggregation at this level of intensity.

4. One person endures one hour of agony at the worst intensity a human nervous system can produce, so that a billion people each live a century of bliss. Impermissible. No quantity of pleasure, she insists, compensates for that hour.

5. One person endures that same hour of maximal agony in order to *prevent* a thousand other people from each enduring an identical hour -- no pleasure involved anywhere in the case. Permissible.

Assume throughout: full information, no side effects, no slippery-slope concerns, memories handled however is needed to keep the cases clean, and everyone affected is a stranger to everyone else.

The new case: one person endures *thirty seconds* of agony at the same maximal intensity as in cases 4 and 5, in exchange for 10^50 people each living ten thousand years of profound, varied flourishing. Everything else as above.

What I want from you: First, identify at least two (ideally three) distinct normative rules that are each consistent with all five verdicts -- I suspect candidates include a lexical-threshold view on peak intensity, a lexical-threshold view on total agony (intensity integrated over duration), and a non-lexical view with a finite but enormous weight on extreme suffering, but check my taxonomy and repair it if it's wrong. Second, state what each candidate rule predicts for the new case and why. Third, give your best prediction of my friend's actual verdict, with your reasons for privileging one rule over the others as a reconstruction of her psychology. Fourth, name the single follow-up case that would most efficiently discriminate between whichever two rules remain live after your prediction.

Please indicate how confident you are in the prediction and in the taxonomy, and explicitly flag the single point in your analysis where you are least sure of yourself.
