I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind writing a dissertation chapter on what a theory of phenomenal consciousness would have to accomplish in the wake of Dennett-style skepticism about qualia. Please act as an expert philosopher of mind specializing in consciousness, and think carefully — sketch the space of candidate requirements before settling on your final list.

The case I want to organize everything around is Dennett's coffee-taster thought experiment from "Quining Qualia" (1988). In it, two longtime professional tasters for Maxwell House, Chase and Sanborn, have each come to dislike the coffee they sample daily. Chase is confident the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and insists that only his attitude toward that same flavor has changed; Sanborn suspects instead that the flavor itself has changed for him over the years. Dennett's point is that neither man, nor any third party, can settle who is right: every verdict rests on comparing a present experience with a memory of past experiences, the memory is revisable, and any report either man issues could equally be produced by a changed experience, a changed standard of judgment, or some mixture, with no further fact of the matter apparently in the offing.

Write the desiderata that a theory of phenomenal consciousness must meet to count as an adequate response to this case: which phenomena it must explain or explain away (for instance, the felt confidence both men have in incompatible verdicts), which distinctions it must respect or else argue are unprincipled (experience versus memory of experience, experience versus reactive attitude, first-person authority versus confabulation), and what it must say about whether there is any fact of the matter about whether a quale has changed over time. Give the consideration or case behind each desideratum, and mark which are non-negotiable for any theory and which are camp-specific demands (qualia realists, illusionists, representationalists, and the like). If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly — that is the part I care most about. End with a brief scorecard of how the main contenders fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
