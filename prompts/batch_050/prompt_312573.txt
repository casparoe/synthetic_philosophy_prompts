A rationality reading group I attend asks each member to circulate a short credo on precommitment for the others to attack, and I'd like an audit of mine before I send it out. Here is where I've landed:

1. Ulysses acted rationally in having himself bound, and remaining bound past the sirens was the rational continuation of that plan, not merely a physical constraint his later self happened to suffer.
2. Whether an action is rational for an agent is entirely a matter of that agent's beliefs and preferences at the moment of acting; no past self has authority over the present one merely in virtue of having come first.
3. Entrenched constitutional provisions — clauses future majorities can amend only with great difficulty or not at all — are legitimate exercises of a people's self-government across time, not domination of the living by the dead.
4. Psychiatric advance directives (Ulysses contracts) should be honored even when the patient, mid-episode, seems competent and demands their revocation, because the lucid self is the proper seat of the patient's autonomy.
5. I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and more generally I think rational agents can rationally hold to a plan even when, at the moment of execution, defecting looks better by their current lights.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and how hard? For each real tension, say which commitment is cheapest to revise and what that revision costs downstream. For any pair that only looks tense, explain why the appearance dissolves. Please rank by severity rather than treating every friction as equal. I have no particular attachment to any one of these surviving intact, so don't soften the verdict.
