Write an Onion-style news article — headline plus roughly 500 words — reporting that a local philosophy lecturer has, after years of teaching P. F. Strawson's work on freedom and resentment, resolved to adopt what he calls the objective attitude toward everyone in his life, full-time and without exception. The piece should report, deadpan, on the consequences: he no longer feels resentment when his brother borrows money and never repays it, only a cool interest in the brother as a phenomenon to be managed; he responds to his wife's thoughtful anniversary gift not with gratitude but with a note that her behaviour is a welcome datum for the household's affective climate; he describes his toddler's tantrum and his colleague's cutting remark in faculty meeting in exactly the same clinical register, since in his view there is no longer a principled difference between them.

The humour must come from getting the philosophy right, not from making the man a generic robot. A reader who knows the Strawsonian picture should recognise each joke as exaggerating something actually in the view: the distinction between participant reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, hurt feelings) and the objective stance we take toward small children, the very ill, and objects of social policy; Strawson's own concession that we can adopt the objective attitude temporarily, for relief or as a resource; his claim that a thoroughgoing objective stance is not something we could sustain given our commitment to ordinary interpersonal relationships; and his insistence that the truth of determinism would not rationally require us to abandon the reactive attitudes anyway. Include at least two quotations: one from the lecturer, who should speak in perfectly respectable seminar-English and thereby condemn himself, and one from his increasingly alarmed wife, who should accidentally raise a serious philosophical worry (for instance, that his newfound even-handedness treats her as a case to be handled rather than a person whose expectations of him are the relationship). End with a closing line in which the lecturer, asked whether he is happy, gives an answer that is chilling precisely because it is internally consistent. Do not break character, do not editorialize, and do not mention Strawson or his paper by name anywhere in the article — the comedy should be legible to a specialist from the content alone, and to a general reader simply as a story about a man who has read one book too many.
