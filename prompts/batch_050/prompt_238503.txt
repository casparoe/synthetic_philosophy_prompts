I am a novelist, and I have recently published a book narrated by a woman blind from birth who works as a sculptor and then, in her fifties, has her sight restored by surgery. The novel is built around what I now know is called Molyneux's problem: whether someone who has known a sphere only by touch would recognise one on first seeing it, before correlating the two senses. I made my narrator recognise objects at once, because the scene demanded it, and a philosopher reviewer took me apart for it — gently, but correctly, since studies of patients given sight in adulthood suggest that immediate transfer from touch to vision largely fails, with cross-modal recognition arriving only after days of learning, and with the interpretation of even that much disputed.

I have been invited to give the closing after-dinner address at a two-day symposium that pairs philosophers of perception with fiction writers. Half the room will know the Molyneux literature intimately; the other half are novelists who have never heard of Locke's correspondent but who have all written characters whose experience they cannot share. Please write the speech, of about eight to ten minutes.

The idea must do real structural work, not decorate the occasion. I want the speech to use Molyneux's question to press on something my half of the room usually gets away with: the craft claim that fiction lets one mind inhabit another. If a newly sighted person cannot carry her tactile knowledge of shape across into vision, what exactly am I doing when I render a blind sculptor's world in prose written by, and largely for, the sighted? Is the novelist's act of imagining across that gap a benign version of the very transfer the newly sighted patient fails at — and if so, does that make the craft humble, or fraudulent, or something third? The speech should include my public concession about the recognition scene and what I would now write instead, and it should land, at the close, on something both halves of the room can take away about what fiction can and cannot honestly claim to do.

Because a speech has no verdict to give, I would instead like a brief afterword, of no more than 150 words, flagging where the speech leans on a contested reading of the empirical and philosophical literature — distinguishing what is genuinely settled (that the question admits of empirical test; that immediate cross-modal matching appears to fail in the cases studied) from what remains in dispute (how those results should be interpreted, and whether the original question has a single answer at all). The speech itself should not present any contested resolution as the received view.

Please write in British English throughout.
