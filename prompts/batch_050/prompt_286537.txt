Last night I had a long and surprisingly heated argument with a friend about Maxwell's demon, and it ended without either of us conceding anything, which is why I'm coming to you. My friend's settled position is that the demon is defeated at the point of measurement: acquiring the information about which side of the partition the molecule is on, he insists, is an irreducibly dissipative act that costs at least kT ln 2 per bit, so the second law is saved before any question of memory or erasure even arises. I tried to argue the more modern line I half-remember from reading about this years ago — that measurement can in principle be done reversibly, and that the unavoidable cost only shows up later, when the demon's finite memory has to be erased to reset the cycle — but my friend waved this off as sleight of hand, and I couldn't reconstruct the argument carefully enough on the spot to force him to engage with it properly.

Here is my opening move, which I'd like your first reply to engage with directly. Suppose the demon begins with its memory register in a known standard state. Correlating that register with the molecule's position — writing a 0 if the molecule is on the left, a 1 if it's on the right — is logically reversible: it maps a known state onto a known state, one-to-one, and could in principle be undone without trace. Reversible computation is not obviously impossible; there are thought experiments in which computation proceeds with arbitrarily little dissipation per logical step as long as nothing is ever erased. So if measurement itself were necessarily dissipative, as you claim, reversible measurement would be a contradiction in terms — yet here it seems to be sitting in front of us.
