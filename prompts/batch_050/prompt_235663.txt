My company just shipped an AI phone agent for customer support (it books appointments, answers questions, the usual) and ever since the launch half the office has been arguing about AI stuff at lunch. Last week two coworkers, Jess and Arun, got into it over Daniel Dennett's essay "The Problem With Counterfeit People" in The Atlantic, which Jess had just read. I'm stuck in the middle and genuinely can't tell who won, so I want a referee.

Quick background: Jess read out this passage from the essay as her exhibit A:

"Democracy depends on the informed (not misinformed) consent of the governed. By allowing the most economically and politically powerful people, corporations, and governments to control our attention, these systems will control us. Counterfeit people, by distracting and confusing us and by exploiting our most irresistible fears and anxieties, will lead us into temptation and, from there, into acquiescing to our own subjugation."

(1) Jess's opening move: the passage proves Dennett's whole case rests on attention and manipulation, not on deception. Counterfeit money is bad because it's fake; counterfeit people are bad because they steer you. So the fix isn't outlawing fake people per se, it's regulating manipulation, and her conclusion was that our phone agent is basically fine as long as it's not manipulative, whether or not callers can tell it's AI.

(2) Arun said she's misreading it. The reason counterfeit people can control our attention and steer us at all is that we can't tell they're counterfeit -- the steering power comes from the fakery. Informed consent requires knowing what you're dealing with, so deception is upstream of manipulation in Dennett's argument, and Jess has the dependency backwards. He said that's why Dennett pushes watermarking and disclosure, not manipulation law.

(3) Jess replied that even granting the dependency claim, the passage overreaches: democracies have always had mass manipulation by real humans (tabloids, lobbyists, demagogues) and we never called consent void over it. If "misinformed" consent doesn't count, then basically no election ever produced valid consent, which is absurd. So the parenthetical is doing all the work and it collapses either into triviality or into denying that any actual democracy is legitimate.

(4) Arun said that's too clever by half. The difference is scale and asymmetry: human demagogues are scarce, expensive, and can be identified and shamed, while counterfeit people are near-free, endless, and there's no one to hold accountable on the speaker's side. "Informed" never meant perfectly informed, it meant informed enough given the environment, and the passage is about the environment degrading past the point where ordinary citizens can keep up. He said Jess was attacking a literal reading nobody holds.

(5) Jess's last move: fine, but then Dennett's own remedy (disclosure, watermarking, liability) doesn't touch the attention problem at all -- a disclosed, watermarked bot can still be engineered to keep you hooked and steer you. So either deception is the real issue (and disclosure solves it) or manipulation is (and disclosure doesn't). Dennett can't have both, and Arun can't either.

Then we all had to go back to work. Can you go through moves 1-5 and tell me who's right at each step, with reasons? Also: is Jess right at the end that Dennett faces that fork, or is there a version of the view where disclosure and anti-manipulation are parts of one coherent position? And separately -- since I'm the one who has to actually ship this thing -- does the winner of the argument change anything about what we should do with our phone agent beyond the disclosure we already have in the greeting? Keep it focused on the arguments as they were actually made, not a general essay about AI risk.
