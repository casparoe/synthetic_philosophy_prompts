I am putting together a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics and want the evaluation criteria fixed before we argue about theories. Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophy of mathematics must satisfy: what it must explain about the truth-conditions of mathematical statements, how it must square those truth-conditions with a credible account of how we come to know them, what it must say about the applicability of mathematics in the empirical sciences, about the apparent necessity and objectivity of mathematical truth, and about the fact that the same structure can be instantiated by many different systems of objects. For each desideratum, give the consideration that motivates it. In particular, treat Benacerraf's dilemma explicitly: explain precisely which pair of desiderata — one semantic, one epistemological — the dilemma presents as jointly unsatisfiable, and take a definite position on whether the tension is genuine and which horn a good theory should accept. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable for any theory and which are commitments of particular camps (platonist, nominalist, structuralist, and so on), and identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most existing positions. End with a brief, committed verdict on which horn of the dilemma you would take and why, rather than leaving the choice open.
