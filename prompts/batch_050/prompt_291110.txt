A popular philosophy-of-science book I've been reading glosses Hume on causation like this: "Hume's argument is that causation just is constant conjunction. When we call one event the cause of another, all we can mean is that events of the first kind are regularly followed by events of the second. Hume proved that there is no necessary connection in nature -- no power or force in objects binding cause to effect -- and that our conviction to the contrary is a habit of mind we project onto the world. Some commentators have tried to soften this by pointing to counterfactual readings of Hume, but since Hume states plainly that causation is nothing but regular succession, the so-called 'realist' interpretations can be set aside." The book is by a reputable philosopher, so I'm hesitant to push back, but the word "proved" bothers me, and I'd heard the counterfactual material is in Hume's own text rather than a later softening. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents Hume -- and tell me whether the book's dismissal of realist readings survives the correction.
