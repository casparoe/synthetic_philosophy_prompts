I teach an intro Buddhism-and-film seminar, and my students always want to read Groundhog Day as a straightforward reincarnation allegory: Phil dies to himself again and again until he becomes compassionate. That feels a bit too neat. Read the film instead through Dharmakīrti’s epistemology and doctrine of momentariness. By momentariness I mean the idea that what is causally real is a momentary particular, while apparently enduring objects and people are constructed continua; perception gives us direct causal contact with particulars, while inference works from signs and causal relations. Phil never actually perceives February 2 as an enduring thing, much less perceives the loop itself. He infers recurrence from remembered breakfasts, conversations, accidents, radio announcements, and bodily skills that survive when nearly everything else resets.

Use that framework to analyze the waking scenes, the increasingly accurate predictions, the piano and ice-sculpture episodes, and his repeated attempts to save the old homeless man. Does Phil’s memory make him an enduring subject smuggled into a momentary world, or can the film’s continuity be redescribed as a causal series without a permanent self? Also consider the ethical arc: is his improvement better understood as the accumulation of dispositions across moments than as the discovery of his true self? Where does the film illuminate Dharmakīrti’s distinction between direct experience and conceptual construction, and where does it resist the Buddhist reading entirely? Please don’t reduce the movie to a sermon on impermanence—I want a reading that notices both the genuine fit and the places where Hollywood metaphysics quietly cheats.
