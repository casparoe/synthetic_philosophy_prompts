I'm in a reading group on philosophy of biology and last week a friend dropped an argument on us that's been bugging me ever since, because it seems to prove way too much but nobody at the table could pin down where. Context: we'd been talking about whether honeybee colonies are superorganisms, whether lichens count as organisms, whether the human-plus-microbiome "holobiont" is one individual or an ecosystem wearing a trenchcoat. His argument goes like this:

(1) A thing is an organism only if it's a genuine unit of selection, meaning its parts contribute to the fitness of the whole instead of pursuing their own reproductive agendas.
(2) This is exactly the criterion everyone uses to sort the cases: lichens and most holobionts fail it because the partners reproduce separately, each keeps its own interests, and there's no shared germline or single-cell bottleneck; honeybee colonies pass it because workers forgo reproduction and the colony propagates through the queen.
(3) Now apply the criterion consistently. The cells in your body pursue their own reproductive agendas all the time: cancers replicate at the host's expense, transmissible cancers have effectively become parasites, selfish genetic elements like segregation distorters and B chromosomes bias their own transmission, and mitochondria get inherited through one parent only precisely because otherwise they'd compete with each other inside the zygote.
(4) So the parts of a human pursue their own reproductive agendas, in exactly the sense that the algal cells in a lichen do.
(5) Therefore by (1), humans aren't organisms either.
(6) And if humans don't qualify, nothing does, short of genetically uniform clones -- and even those mutate, so probably nothing at all qualifies.
(7) But obviously some things are organisms. So the standard criterion must be wrong, there is no principled line between organisms and mere associations, and "organism" is purely a matter of degree: honeybee hives, lichens, humans, and maybe whole ecosystems are all just organisms to varying extents.

He takes this to show that all the organism-vs-superorganism-vs-holobiont debate is just people drawing arbitrary lines on a continuum. That conclusion feels nuts to me -- I feel like something slips between (3) and (4), but when I tried to say that cancers get *policed* and lichen partners don't, he said policing is just part of the agenda too. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) than accept that nothing is an organism. Which step do you actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost -- e.g., if you deny that cancer and segregation distorters count as parts pursuing agendas, what principled story are you committing to, and does that story still let ant colonies in while keeping lichens out?
