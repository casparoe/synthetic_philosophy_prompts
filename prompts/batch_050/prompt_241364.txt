Help me settle a hallway fight that's been going on all semester. A colleague of mine in the department has an argument he thinks kills backward time travel for good, and I've started to suspect it kills a lot more than that. I'm numbering the steps so we can argue precisely instead of gesturing at each other over coffee.

(1) Suppose backward time travel is possible. Then eventually some traveler — call him Tim — goes back to 1920, locates his young grandfather, and takes a shot at him. Tim has a loaded rifle, decent aim, zero scruples, and a firm intention to fire.

(2) Either the shot kills grandfather or it doesn't.

(3) If it does, then grandfather never fathers Tim's father, so Tim never exists, so no shot is ever fired. Contradiction.

(4) If it doesn't, then something always intervenes: the rifle jams, Tim slips on a banana peel, his nerve fails at the last second — and this happens every single time, for every traveler who ever attempts it, no matter how well prepared they are.

(5) A pattern of last-second rescues that reliable is either an absurd run of coincidences or a law of nature forbidding the act. Coincidences that perfect don't happen. And a law like that just is the impossibility of time travel.

(6) So backward time travel is impossible.

Fine so far, maybe. But then he kept going:

(7) Notice that nothing in (3)-(5) depended on the direction of time. Run the same reasoning forward: whatever happens tomorrow either happens or it doesn't. If it happens, then your "choosing otherwise" generates the same contradiction, and if it doesn't, something will always intervene on you too. So the future is exactly as fixed as the past, deliberation is theater, and fatalism wins.

(8) Fatalism is false.

(9) So the argument proves too much — except he can't locate the step to cut, and now neither can I.

Which step goes? Full disclosure on my preferences: I'm attached to (1)-(4) and would happily pay with (5) or (7) if forced, but I want the bill itemized — what exactly does rejecting it cost, and why did it look so harmless going in? One more thing: if the answer is that (7) is where it breaks, explain why it breaks there and not already at (5), because to my eye those two steps are the same move, and if they're not, the asymmetry between them is doing all the work and I want it made explicit. And if the real culprit is hiding earlier — if something in how (3) or (4) is worded already rigs the game — say that instead, but then explain why it fooled two people who do this for a living.
