I help run a small politics and philosophy reading group, and at our last session a member circulated a short essay he had written as background for our discussion of free speech. The essay leans heavily on Popper, and its central passage summarises the paradox of tolerance as follows: 'In The Open Society and Its Enemies, Popper demonstrated that tolerance is logically self-defeating. A society that tolerates everything will, as a matter of logic, eventually be destroyed by the intolerant, just as a liar who always lies contradicts himself. Popper therefore concluded that a tolerant society not only may but must suppress intolerant opinions whenever they appear, since tolerating intolerance is a contradiction in terms. It follows that refusing a platform to speakers whose views are judged intolerant is not censorship at all but simple consistency, and that those who object on free-speech grounds have simply failed to grasp the paradox.' Several of us came away feeling that the essay's conclusion was doing more work than the argument could bear, but none of us had the book to hand, and our own memories of the relevant passage — which I believe is actually a fairly short remark, a note rather than a developed chapter — were hazy.

Could you audit this summary sentence by sentence? I'd like to know which claims a careful reader of Popper would accept as faithful to what he wrote, which are common inflations or distortions that circulate in popular retellings, and which are simply wrong. In particular: does Popper present tolerance's self-defeat as a matter of logic, or as something else? Under what conditions, on his actual view, does suppression become legitimate, and does he say anything about cases where suppression would be permissible but unwise? And does the essay's final move — that deplatforming is thereby vindicated as 'not censorship' and that free-speech objections rest on a misunderstanding — follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it smuggle in additional premises the essay never states?

Please write your answer in British English. Where there is a settled answer about what Popper's text says, say so plainly; but where competent interpreters genuinely disagree — for instance about the intended scope of the remark, about whether tolerance is best understood as a contract or as a first-order value, or about how the paradox should be applied to modern speech controversies — please flag that the matter is contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
