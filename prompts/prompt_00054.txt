I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about updateless decision theory (UDT) after a friend sent me a LessWrong-adjacent blog post comparing it to causal and evidential decision theory. What's grabbing me isn't the toy problems themselves (counterfactual mugging, Parfit's hitchhiker, the transparent Newcomb variant) but the fact that UDT seems to require an agent to commit, before observing anything, to a global policy mapping all possible observations to actions — and to stick with that policy even when, after updating on new information, a "locally rational" agent would clearly deviate. That feels like it's doing something philosophically strange to the concept of rationality itself, since it's explicitly rejecting the idea that you should always act on your current, updated beliefs.

I'm trying to get past the "it wins more money in these specific thought experiments" framing and understand the deeper implications. So: what are the most important implications of adopting an updateless decision theory — for how we think about personal identity over time, the status of Bayesian updating as a rationality norm, commitment and precommitment devices, the coherence of counterfactual reasoning about situations you know can't obtain, and maybe what it would even mean for an embedded, resource-limited agent (like a human, or an AI) to actually implement something like UDT rather than just admire it from a distance? I'm less interested in a rehash of the standard motivating examples and more in what's actually at stake if you take this framework seriously as a normative theory of rational choice.
