I've been sitting with Bernard Williams' and Thomas Nagel's writings on moral luck for a seminar paper, and the case that keeps nagging at me is the two drunk drivers: one gets home safely, the other swerves and kills a child, even though their underlying choices and mental states were identical. My professor keeps pushing us past the "isn't this unfair" reaction and wants us to trace out what accepting the reality of moral luck (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) should actually do to our broader practices of blame, punishment, and self-assessment. I find this genuinely disorienting because so much of how I evaluate myself and others - guilt over a snapped comment that happened to wound someone deeply, pride in a decision that happened to pan out - seems to quietly lean on outcomes we didn't control.

Could you lay out the most important implications of taking moral luck seriously, especially for: (1) the legal system's habit of grading punishment by outcome (attempted vs. completed crimes, manslaughter vs. reckless endangerment) even when culpable mental states are identical; (2) everyday interpersonal blame and praise, like the drunk driver case or a surgeon whose identical technique succeeds or fails due to factors outside their control; and (3) whether moral luck should push us toward revising our concept of moral responsibility itself (e.g., toward more Kantian "good will" purism) or instead toward accepting that our ordinary practices are shot through with a kind of principled inconsistency we just have to live with. I'm less interested in a rehearsal of the standard positions and more in what actually follows if we bite the bullet on any of them.
