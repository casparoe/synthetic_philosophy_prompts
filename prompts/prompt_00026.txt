I've been rereading Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations for a seminar, and my study group got into a long argument about how he would have reacted to the "rule-following considerations" being retooled as an objection to computationalism about mind—specifically Kripke's famous "quus" reconstruction, and its later descendants in debates about whether an AI system that manipulates symbols can be said to be "following" a rule (like addition) at all, as opposed to merely behaving in accordance with it so far. Wittgenstein died decades before anyone was seriously debating whether machine learning systems could be said to grasp meanings or merely mimic them, but he obviously had very developed, idiosyncratic views about meaning, understanding, and what facts (if any) constitute someone's "grasping" a rule, grounded largely in examples about human practice, training, and communal agreement.

Given his actual arguments about ostension, private language, and the impossibility of grounding meaning in any finite set of past applications or introspectible mental states, what do you think Wittgenstein's verdict would have been on large language models—would he have seen them as posing a new instance of his original puzzle, as a kind of vindication of his skeptical solution, or as something categorically outside the scope of what his rule-following considerations were even about (since they concern human practices and forms of life)? I'm especially curious whether you think he'd have granted that a community of interacting AI systems could satisfy his "communal assent" criterion for rule-following, or whether he'd have insisted that something about embodied human form-of-life participation is doing essential work in his account that no LLM, however fluent, could replicate.
