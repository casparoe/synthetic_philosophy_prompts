Last night a friend and I got into a long, somewhat heated discussion about whether it's ever literally true to say that a large language model "believes" or "wants" something, or whether such talk is merely a convenient shorthand. My friend kept invoking Dennett's intentional stance, arguing that if attributing beliefs and desires to a system reliably predicts its behavior, that's really all there is to say on the matter, full stop. I pushed back, insisting that this collapses an important distinction between systems that merely behave as if they have propositional attitudes and systems that actually have them, though I struggled to cash out what that "actually" could even mean without smuggling in some folk-psychological homunculus.

This got me wondering how David Hume would have weighed in on the debate, given how much of his epistemology and philosophy of mind seems to anticipate the instrumentalist flavor of Dennett's position. Hume's skepticism about necessary connection, his bundle theory of the self (which dissolves the subject into a mere succession of perceptions), and his general willingness to ground concepts in their observable effects rather than in hidden essences all seem to push in the direction of a deflationary, stance-dependent view of mentality. On the other hand, Hume's account of belief in the Treatise ties belief closely to the "vivacity" of an impression-derived idea, which seems to presuppose a kind of felt, first-personal experience that a language model plausibly lacks entirely.

Could you work through how Hume, reasoning strictly from his own principles in the Treatise and the first Enquiry, would likely have responded to the question of whether we can correctly attribute beliefs and desires to something like an LLM? I'm especially interested in whether his empiricism would make him more sympathetic to Dennett's "it's beliefs all the way down to usefulness" instrumentalism, or whether his theory of belief as a felt quality of ideas would instead commit him to a more restrictive, experience-based criterion that a purely computational system couldn't satisfy. Please flag any places where you think the extrapolation to Hume's likely view is shakiest, given that he obviously had no conception of computation or artificial systems.
