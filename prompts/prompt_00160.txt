I've been re-reading the Center on Long-Term Risk material on surrogate goals as a defense against acausal blackmail, and I brought it up with a friend from my local rationalist meetup last week, but something about the whole setup still doesn't sit right with me. As I understand basic game theory, a threat is only something to worry about if the threatener actually stands to gain from carrying it out — otherwise punishing you after you've already refused to comply is just costly to them with zero payoff, so it's an empty bluff that any sufficiently rational agent should simply ignore. If that's correct, then the entire apparatus of acausal threats, and the surrogate-goal machinery built to defend against them, seems to be solving a problem that a rational agent would never actually face, since no rational threatener would follow through once refusal has already occurred.

So I'm trying to figure out what I'm missing here. Is there something about how these scenarios are modeled under decision theories like UDT or FDT that makes follow-through rational (or at least credible) in a way that standard backward-induction reasoning would rule out? Or is the value of surrogate goals more about hedging against threateners who aren't perfectly rational, or who can't be reliably distinguished from ones who are actually committed to following through? I'd appreciate a clearer picture of why decision theorists in this space treat the threat model as dangerous enough to warrant a defense like adopting a surrogate goal in the first place.
