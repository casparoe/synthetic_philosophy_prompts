So I've been going back and forth with some people in a Discord that spun off from a rationalist forum about the paradox of voting, and I keep running into the same wall. The standard Riker-Ordeshook calculus-of-voting framing always felt like a bit of a strawman to me: if you model casting a ballot as a causally isolated act and you're a strict CDT agent, the expected utility of swinging a national election (probability like 1 in 10 million, further discounted by uncertainty over whether your preferred candidate is actually better) is obviously going to be swamped by the cost of standing in line for an hour. But I've seen people gesture at superrationality / EDT-style reasoning (kind of a Parfit "each-we" move, or the idea that sufficiently similar deciders should treat their choices as correlated with other similar voters) as a way to rescue voting as rational for a purely outcome-focused agent, without smuggling in "warm glow" civic-duty utility as a fudge factor.

I'd love an actual argument here rather than a survey of both sides. Please argue that something like evidential decision theory (or updateless decision theory) - not a taste for voting as an end in itself - is what's really doing the work in making voting rational for a self-interested agent who only cares about outcomes. Get into the weeds a bit on how the correlation across like-minded voters would have to be modeled for this to go through (e.g. how similar do two voters need to be for the "sameness" reasoning to kick in, and does this generalize past voting to other diffuse collective-action problems), and say something about whether this ends up being evidence for EDT/UDT over CDT more generally, or whether it's more of a special-case patch.
