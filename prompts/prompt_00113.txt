I'm a retired chemist who's fallen down the rabbit hole of decision theory in retirement (turns out philosophy is way more addictive than titration curves ever were). I've been reading about updateless decision theory in the context of stuff like counterfactual mugging and Newcomb's problem, and there's one thing that's bugging me that none of the blog posts I've read really clear up.

The whole pitch of UDT, as I understand it, is that you shouldn't update on the fact that you've observed yourself in some particular branch or situation -- instead you commit, from behind a kind of veil, to whatever policy is best across all the ways things could've gone, rather than reasoning from where you currently happen to find yourself. Fine, I think I get that much. But then whenever people actually work out what the UDT-optimal policy is for a given problem, they seem to lean pretty heavily on logical/mathematical updating -- like, the agent has to figure out what the predictor would do, or derive what follows from the setup, and that seems to require updating on logical facts as it goes, the same way any of us updates once we've actually worked through a calculation. So how is "don't update on indexical/empirical evidence" compatable with "you must update on logical/computational facts just to act at all"? Is there a principled line between these two kinds of updating that I'm just not seeing, or is this a known soft spot in the theory that people mostly just wave hands over? I'd love an explanation that actually engages with the guts of this rather than just restating the "updatelessness" slogan.
