I've been working through David Lewis's *Convention* for a graduate seminar, and something's been nagging at me that I want to sort out. Lewis defines a convention as a regularity in behavior that persists because it solves a recurrent coordination problem: there are multiple possible equilibria, everyone prefers to conform if others do, and the actual one that gets selected is arbitrary (his classic examples are which side of the road to drive on, or which channel to call someone back on if a phone call drops). The whole apparatus rests on common knowledge of preferences and rationality, and on the idea that any alternative equilibrium would have worked just as well.

My study group got into an argument about whether this picture actually captures what we ordinarily mean by "social norm," especially after one of us brought in Cristina Bicchieri's more recent work, where norms are sustained not just by coordinated self-interest but by conditional preferences tied to normative expectations — i.e., people conform partly because they believe others expect them to and would disapprove if they didn't, even in situations that aren't pure coordination problems with interchangeable equilibria (think fairness norms, or norms against queue-jumping, where there's a real mixed-motive structure and unilateral defection is tempting rather than just risky).

Could you compare Lewis's account of convention with this broader notion of a social norm along these lines? I'm especially interested in: (1) whether Lewis's requirement of arbitrariness among equally good equilibria is doing real work, or whether it's too restrictive to explain norms that involve conflicts of interest rather than pure coordination; (2) how central common knowledge is to each account versus mere normative expectation; and (3) whether Lewis's theory should be read as a special case of a more general theory of norm-emergence, or whether it's actually picking out a genuinely distinct phenomenon that shouldn't be lumped in with norms like fairness or reciprocity at all.
