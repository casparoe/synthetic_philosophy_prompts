I'm a second-year PhD student in a formal epistemology seminar, and we recently covered Aumann's agreement theorem in the context of debates about rational disagreement. As I understand it, the theorem shows that two Bayesian agents who are mutually aware of each other's posterior probabilities on some event, and who both know that this awareness is common knowledge, cannot rationally maintain different posteriors—so any persistent disagreement between people who consider each other honest and rational must ultimately be a symptom of irrationality or concealed private information. I'm drafting a term paper arguing that this result gives us a fairly general diagnostic tool: if two experts (say, macroeconomists disagreing about inflation forecasts, or climate scientists sparring over sensitivity estimates) keep disagreeing even after extensive public exchange of their views, we should conclude that at least one of them isn't being fully honest or rational, and that in principle a few rounds of "I hear your estimate, here's my updated one" should drive them to convergence.

My advisor read a draft and said I was overreaching, but wasn't very specific about where the argument breaks down beyond muttering something about "common priors" and "idealization." Could you help me pin down exactly what's going wrong here? I'd like to understand which of my claims actually follow from Aumann's theorem (and the related Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis dynamic story) and which ones smuggle in assumptions that don't hold for real-world disputes like the ones I want to analyze, so I can either fix the argument or reframe the paper's thesis.
