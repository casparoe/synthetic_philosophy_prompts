I've been slowly working through the game theory chapters of Osborne and Rubinstein's "A Course in Game Theory," partly inspired by some old LessWrong-adjacent threads on Schelling points and common knowledge that kept namedropping iterated dominance as the baseline solution concept before moving on to correlated equilibrium and the like. I got stuck on a subtlety the book mentions almost in passing: iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, once you allow domination by mixed strategies rather than only pure ones, is supposed to pick out exactly the same set of strategies as Bernheim and Pearce's rationalizability — but apparently only in two-player games. I've seen it claimed that the two concepts can come apart once you move to three or more players, and that this has something to do with rationalizability permitting a player's beliefs about different opponents' strategies to be uncorrelated in a way that a single mixed strategy, used as the "witness" for dominance, cannot represent.

Could you compare these two concepts in some depth? I'm especially interested in why allowing mixed strategies as dominators already buys iterated elimination so much of its power relative to the pure-strategy version, what further assumption about players' epistemic states rationalizability is smuggling in that mixed-strategy IESDS is not, and why the equivalence happens to hold cleanly in the two-player case specifically. If you can construct or recall a small worked example — say a three-player game — where the sets of surviving strategies under the two procedures actually diverge, that would help me see the distinction rather than just take it on faith.
