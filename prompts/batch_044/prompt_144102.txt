Student answer:
Material constitution happens when one object is made of another but they arent the same thing, like Michelangelo's David and the lump of marble its made from. The statue can be destroyed by being squashed into a ball, but the marble can survive that, so they have different modal properties. Alan Gibbard's dominance argument says the statue dominates the clay because it has properties like being art and having aesthetic value that the clay lacks, so they cant be identical. This solves the problem because it lets them share a location without being one thing — Baker's constitution view says the statue is constituted by the clay but not identical to it, so impenetrability isnt violated. But theres still the temporary intrinsics problem: at time t the statue is beautiful but the clay isnt, and since theyre in the same place that means the place has contradictory properties. Lewis used this to push perdurantism, but Baker thinks constitution avoids it because properties belong to different primary kinds. I think that fails though, because if two things share all their parts they should share all properties, so the dominance argument is really just a semantic trick. Also Gibbard's contingent identity view says theyre identical in some worlds and not in others, which is weird since identity is necessary. So Baker is closer but the temporary intrinsics issue still breaks her view.
