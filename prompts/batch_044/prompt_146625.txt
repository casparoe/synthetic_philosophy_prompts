You are grading an undergraduate exam in moral philosophy. The exam question was: "In Kant's ethics, an action done from duty (aus Pflicht) possesses moral worth, whereas one done merely in accordance with duty (pflichtmäßig) does not, even when both produce identical outcomes. Consider a physician who declines a lucrative pharmaceutical consulting contract because she judges that accepting it would violate her professional obligations to patients. She also recognizes that refusing will earn her colleagues' respect, and she quietly values that respect, though she insists she would have refused the contract even absent any social reward. Does her refusal have full moral worth? In your answer, distinguish the determining ground of the will from accompanying incentives, and address whether acknowledging a secondary, non-moral benefit undermines the moral status of her action."

A student submitted the following answer: "The refusal possesses full moral worth. Kant distinguishes actions done from duty—where respect for the moral law is the sufficient determining ground of the will—from those done merely in accordance with duty, where inclination happens to align with duty but actually drives the action. Here, the physician's maxim is formed by her judgment that accepting the contract violates professional obligations; she explicitly treats this moral reason as decisive. The prospect of peer respect is present as an accompanying incentive, but since it is not sufficient to motivate the refusal on its own, it does not become the determining ground. Consequently, she is not acting merely in accordance with duty, a category that applies only when self-interest is the true motive. This reading is supported by Barbara Herman's interpretation of Kant, which maintains that inclinations may be present in moral action only if they play absolutely no causal role in the agent's psychology; since the physician's desire for respect does influence her emotional state, a strict Hermanian analysis might actually deny full worth. Nevertheless, the standard Kantian position—focusing solely on the determining ground—correctly grants her full moral worth despite this emotional accompaniment."
