In our philosophy of science seminar on rational belief, the professor summarized Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle as a normative constraint on diachronic coherence. The summary said that if an agent is now certain she will receive specific evidence E and that, upon rationally updating, she will assign credence r to hypothesis H, then her present credence in H conditional on E should equal r. The summary correctly noted that this applies only to updates the agent regards as rational, not to predicted irrational shifts, and that it is a coherence condition rather than a psychological description. It then introduced a concrete case: a physicist who knows she will run an experiment tomorrow and expects that, upon seeing result R, she will rationally lower her credence in quantum interpretation I from 0.8 to 0.3. According to the summary, Reflection requires the physicist to lower her present unconditional credence in I to 0.3 immediately—before running the experiment—because her future rational self is epistemically authoritative. The summary concluded that this renders actual empirical work redundant for belief formation: a fully rational agent should already hold all the beliefs she will hold after every future rational update, so gathering evidence adds nothing. I suspect this conclusion is too strong, but I am unsure where the error lies. Is the formulation of the conditional constraint accurate? Does Reflection really require preemptive adoption of one's future unconditional credence, or is the physicist case misapplying the principle? And does the redundancy conclusion follow from any accurate part of the summary? Please separate what is faithful to van Fraassen's view, what is a subtle misrepresentation or overstatement, and whether the argument for redundancy survives correction.
