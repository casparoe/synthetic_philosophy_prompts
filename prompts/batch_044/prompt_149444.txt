I am a doctoral candidate in perceptual psychology, and I have been invited to participate in a first-in-human trial of a cortical implant that would grant me a stable magnetosensory modality—essentially, a persistent sense of magnetic north integrated into my spatial awareness. The protocol is well-regulated, but the investigators are explicit that the change is not merely sensory. Follow-up interviews with earlier cohorts suggest that acquiring this modality tends to restructure subjects' practical priorities in unpredictable ways: several reported that long-standing career ambitions suddenly felt arbitrary, while others developed intense attachments to migratory patterns or open landscapes they had previously ignored. The research team requires a binding enrollment decision within two weeks.

My advisor, who works in decision theory, urges me to treat this like any other high-uncertainty investment: assign probabilities to various post-implant preference profiles, weight them by my current utility estimates, and maximize expected value. A colleague in the ethics unit objects that this framework presupposes the very stability that the trial undermines—if the implant might alter the evaluative standpoint from which I judge the outcome, then computing an expected utility for the person who will assess the result is not just difficult but conceptually confused. To complicate matters, the trial offers an optional "value-lock" clause: I can legally bind my post-implant self to complete my dissertation and maintain my current social commitments, regardless of what I come to prefer. Some friends say accepting the lock is the only rational safeguard; others argue that it amounts to letting my present self colonize a future person whose values I cannot legitimately claim authority over.

Given all of this, how should I approach the choice? Is there a coherent way to rationally endorse an experience that might replace the very preferences that would ratify or reject it, and does the availability of a binding pre-commitment resolve the difficulty or merely displace it onto a different problem about personal authority across time?
