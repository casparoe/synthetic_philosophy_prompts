Take the 1940 American Negro Exposition in Chicago — specifically Charles C. Dawson's thirty-three dioramas in the Court of Dioramas and William Edouard Scott's twenty-four murals — and analyze the exposition's visual and curatorial logic through W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 thesis that all art is propaganda and that beauty must serve racial truth and uplift. Do not recount the exposition's chronology; I assume familiarity with the outline. Rather, apply Du Bois's framework precisely: identify which display choices — the sequential historical narrative from ancient Egypt to the First World War, the monumentalization of labor and military service, the decision to present history through immersive three-dimensional tableaux rather than conventional gallery display — function as propaganda in Du Bois's specific, non-pejorative sense, and which formal or compositional features in the murals and dioramas resist being reduced to transparent message-bearing instruments. Then reverse the analysis: does the exposition show that propaganda can fully absorb aesthetic form without losing efficacy, or does it reveal an instability in Du Bois's distinction between politically committed beauty and beauty pursued for its own sake? Be disciplined about the mapping — specify the actors involved (the artists, the curators, the viewing public) and state clearly where Du Bois's theory remains silent rather than forcing it to account for every feature.
