Two members of my study group argued about whether a public university should deny a speaking invitation to a well-known activist who advocates replacing democratic elections with a council of appointed experts. Marcus said this is the textbook paradox of tolerance: a society that extends unlimited tolerance to those who reject tolerance can be destroyed by them, so refusing the invitation is not an act of intolerance but a necessary defense of the conditions that make tolerance possible. Elena replied that Marcus treats tolerance as purely instrumental—valuable only because it sustains democracy—whereas she sees it as a deontological commitment to respect persons regardless of their views; on her account, excluding someone specifically for rejecting democracy violates the very principle Marcus claims to protect. Marcus countered that Elena's view is self-defeating in practice, obligating society to safeguard the organizational capacity of those who would abolish safeguards for everyone, which turns tolerance into a suicide pact rather than a virtue. Elena then introduced a distinction she called decisive: there is a difference between suppressing a belief, which she agrees would violate tolerance, and refusing to allocate scarce institutional resources—such as a university platform, security, and publicity—to organize for the abolition of democracy; she argued the latter is resource allocation, not belief suppression, and therefore does not trigger the paradox. Marcus said the distinction collapses in modern politics, since access to institutional platforms is practically necessary for political efficacy, so refusing resources functions as suppression by another mechanism, leaving Elena hiding behind a formal distinction. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move—rate the strength of each argument, say exactly where each side is right or wrong, and give a committed verdict: does Elena's institutional-resource distinction genuinely resolve the paradox, or does Marcus's collapse argument defeat it?
