I'm trying to put a name on a pattern I keep noticing in loose activist networks and online movements, but I don't know if the literature already has one. Picture a group with no formal membership, no written platform, and no agreed list of demands — ask ten people what they're actually for and you get ten different answers. But they have an incredibly precise shared posture: they agree instantly on who the enemies are, what kind of outrage is appropriate, and which statements are "toxic" regardless of content. The weird part is that members will defend two contradictory factual claims, as long as both weaken the same opponent, and they'll reject an accurate claim if it comes from the wrong source or blunts the adversarial energy. The coherence isn't belief-based; it's orientation-based. There's pressure not to think clearly about justice in particular cases, but to maintain the collective emotional alignment.

This feels like Simone Weil's idea that political parties replace the pursuit of justice with collective passion — except there's no party, no card to carry, no official doctrine to sign. It's the same substitution of posture for judgment, but in a decentralized, almost post-institutional form. So my question is: does this specific thing — group coherence without doctrine, sustained by adversarial posture rather than shared belief — already have a name? And if not, can you define it precisely and draw its edges, especially separating it from groupthink, plain tribalism, and Weil's own concept of party-spirit? I don't need a bibliography, just the carving: what it is, what it isn't, and where it sits relative to Weil's critique.
