As a novelist preparing a narrative set in a reconstructed ancient society, I have been studying how archaeologists move from fragments to the beliefs and institutions that shaped them, and I find myself holding a cluster of intuitions about particular inferences that I want to test for coherence. Consider these judgments. First, when grave goods are distributed with near-perfect equality across a cemetery that otherwise shows hierarchical housing, I take the burial practice to express an egalitarian cosmology rather than merely reflecting an absence of surplus; the uniformity seems too ritually precise to be an economic accident. Second, when a remote highland site contains only shattered ceremonial vessels and no domestic debris, I infer the presence of a pilgrimage or seasonal gathering institution, not just occasional local worship; the absence of daily life suggests the place existed for periodic collective action. Third, when an elite burial combines imported luxury items arranged according to local symbolic conventions, I read this as a strategic appropriation that reinforces indigenous authority, not as evidence of foreign domination or cultural assimilation. Fourth, when standardized weights appear across dispersed settlements with no accompanying script, I am inclined to see centralized bureaucratic control rather than emergent market coordination; the uniformity exceeds what decentralized exchange seems likely to produce. Fifth, however, when pottery styles shift abruptly while settlement patterns and skeletal populations remain continuous, I resist inferring institutional or religious revolution and instead favor practical adaptation—perhaps new cooking technologies or trade partners—despite the dramatic material change.

Is there a single principle governing when an archaeological pattern should be read as expressing belief or institution rather than practical constraint, and does it accommodate all five of these verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what does the corrected principle predict about a case I have not listed: a settlement where architectural orientation suddenly aligns with solstitial sunrise, yet every other material category—burial, craft, diet—remains unchanged?
