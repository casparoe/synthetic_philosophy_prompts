Design a 75-minute seminar activity for about 14 upper-level epistemology students, arranged in a circle with tables, that makes them physically encounter the pressure of the epistemic regress and the competing pulls of foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. Give every student a belief card stating a concrete, low-stakes claim — for example, “this archive should reject the donation” — and a deck of justification cards that must be linked backward in a visible chain. Secretly embed three card-types that students are not told about in advance: “bedrock” cards with no reverse side (they halt the chain but carry an arbitrary-choice penalty); “loop” cards whose reverse points back to an earlier card in the circle (they avoid arbitrariness but trigger a circularity fee); and “trail” cards whose reverse points off the table or to an empty chair (they avoid both penalties but force the holder to keep passing a token backward, accumulating fatigue points). Provide each student with twenty shared “credibility tokens”; every unbacked link costs one token from the pool, and the pool is visible to all. The rules should forbid collaboration on chain-building, so each student experiences the pressure alone, and include a small social payoff — the group votes on whether the archive accepts the donation based on whose chain remains intact when the timer stops at 40 minutes. Structure the session so that no position is named until the debrief, and arrange the incentives so that students naturally fall into halting, looping, or extending strategies and feel the corresponding discomfort before it is labelled. In the debrief, map the blank bedrock card onto foundationalism’s arbitrary halt, the loop onto coherentism’s mutual support, and the falling token onto infinitism’s unending demand; be frank about what the activity distorts — for instance, that the fatigue cost is only metaphorical, and that the circularity fee fails to capture the holistic justification coherentists actually defend. Also forecast what will predictably go wrong on the first run — such as students trying to negotiate shared loops despite the rules, or hoarding bedrock cards — and say exactly how to handle it. Finally, state clearly which feature of the regress problem the physical setup cannot reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply verbally. Write in British English, avoid bibliographies or citations, and do not ask participants to identify specific texts or authors.
