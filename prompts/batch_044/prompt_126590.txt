I'm a graduate student in a philosophy seminar focused on Frege's logic and its historical interpretation. For next week's session I need to lead a fifty-minute discussion with first-year students on a single conceptual problem: whether Frege's late political diary—his private reflections on nationalism, antisemitism, and the political destiny of Germany—should affect how we read his philosophy of logic and language. Don't explain the diary's contents to me; instead, design the teaching arc. First, state what foundational distinction must be established before the students can treat this as a philosophical question rather than as biographical gossip about a dead thinker. Second, identify the one concrete comparison that should carry the entire session—something that puts Frege's rigorous anti-psychologism about logical laws in direct tension with the essentialist or group-psychological assumptions visible in his political writing—and explain exactly what that comparison needs to contain and what it must avoid; in particular, say why a generic "genius with flaws" framing should be rejected as the carrying example. Third, lay out the misconceptions they will generate in sequence: likely that moral badness entails philosophical error, then that hidden political motives must drive the logical notation, then that biography is always philosophically irrelevant. For each, specify at which point in the sequence I should intervene and with what conceptual move. Fourth, give me one targeted question I can ask at the close to test whether they have grasped the separation between logical content and biographical origin, rather than merely feeling uncomfortable about Frege's politics. Fifth, tell me explicitly what to leave out at this level and say so—perhaps specific dated entries, the full editorial history of the diary's publication, or analogies to Heidegger's Black Notebooks. Finally, I want a committed answer, not a balanced survey: take a definite position on whether the diary is philosophically irrelevant, illuminating, or damaging to Frege's logic, defend that choice with reasons, and construct the lesson so that it guides the students toward understanding why your position holds rather than leaving them to pick sides.
