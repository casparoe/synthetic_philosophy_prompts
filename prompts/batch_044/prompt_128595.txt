After being named healthcare proxy for my partner following a recent diagnosis of a progressive neurodegenerative condition, I have found that existing debates about assisted dying operate at a level of abstraction that misses the precise fractures I am now navigating. Before I attempt to construct my own framework, I want the full specification: what must an adequate ethical theory of euthanasia and assisted dying actually deliver to count as adequate for real-world clinical and familial decision-making? I am not asking for a bibliography, a jurisdictional survey, or citation of statutes; I want the desiderata—the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the boundary cases it must adjudicate, and the results from medicine, psychology, and law with which it must remain consistent—with the motivating case or consideration behind each.

In particular, the theory must meet the following requirements:

1. It must preserve the moral distinction between refusing life-sustaining treatment and actively requesting a lethal intervention, explaining why collapsing them in either direction misrepresents the moral work being done in each case. The consideration is the ambulatory, non-suffering patient who requests a prescription versus the ventilator-dependent patient in acute distress who declines further ventilation; the theory must say whether the same normative logic governs both.

2. It must address the problem of anticipatory authorization: a fully competent patient directs that aid in dying be provided during a future state of severe dementia in which they will lack psychological continuity with their present self, will not be subjectively suffering in any accessible way, and may even appear content. The theory must state whether such a directive binds caregivers, and if so, on what account—personal identity, substituted judgment, or welfare—without reducing the issue to standard advance-directive logic for medical treatment.

3. It must distinguish among physical suffering, existential suffering (loss of narrative coherence or dignity), and relational suffering (the sense of being an intolerable burden to others), specifying whether these are morally equivalent grounds for assisted dying, which distinctions are defensible, and which risk encoding merely contingent cultural prejudices about dependency.

4. It must remain consistent with palliative-care outcomes without being reducible to them: it must explain why the availability of high-quality palliation does not eliminate all legitimate requests for assisted dying—since some patients reject palliation for reasons that do not reduce to ignorance or untreated depression—while also explaining why legalizing assisted dying should not erode institutional investment in palliative infrastructure.

5. It must account for the moral residue experienced by the physician or family member who performs or facilitates the act—whether that residue is a necessary professional burden, a signal of systemic moral error, or a misapplication of personal morality to institutional roles.

6. It must draw a defensible boundary with suicide prevention, explaining why a request from a terminally ill patient is evaluated differently from a request arising from treatable mental illness, without relying on a stigmatizing distinction between "rational" and "irrational" suicide that collapses under psychiatric scrutiny.

Then, flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether respecting anticipatory authorization (2) can be reconciled with a present-tense, capacity-based consent requirement without importing a contested metaphysics of personal identity. Also indicate which single desideratum does the most damage to the three dominant families of theory: autonomy-centered liberalism, sanctity-of-life deontology, and consequentialist welfare maximization.

Finally, score those three families briefly against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating fractures rather than on the scorecard; I want to see where the list itself forces a choice that no existing camp can comfortably make.
