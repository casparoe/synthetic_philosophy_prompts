I'm working through my notes for a module on intergenerational ethics and want a consistency check before I present. Here is my position:

(1) I accept the non-identity argument: if a choice determines which particular people come to exist, then none of those particular people is made worse off by it, since the alternative is non-existence.

(2) I reject both total-utilitarian and impersonal-person-affecting hybrid resolutions of the problem, because I think moral obligations must always be owed to identifiable individuals.

(3) I nonetheless hold that we have stringent, direct obligations to reduce carbon emissions, justified entirely by the harm that climate change will cause to specific future people.

(4) I think it is permissible for individuals to adjust reproductive plans in light of climate risk, since neither the child born earlier nor the one born later is wronged — each either exists with some welfare level or does not exist at all.

(5) I believe that large-scale mitigation policies (for instance, carbon pricing that alters economic conditions and hence demographic timing) do not wrong anyone, because anyone whose existence depends on the policy is not worse off than non-existence.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions only dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision? In particular, address whether (3) can be sustained alongside (1) and (2) given that aggressive mitigation will almost certainly change who is born in the distant future, and whether (4) and (5) introduce an implicit double standard between individual and institutional identity-affecting choices. Rank the tensions by severity and please write your answer in British English.
