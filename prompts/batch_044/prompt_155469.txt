I'm working through Leibniz's moral philosophy for a graduate seminar on rationalist ethics, and I've hit a tension I can't quite dissolve. Leibniz characterizes justice as the charity of the wise—an affective, other-regarding perfection of rational agents—yet he also treats moral truths as necessary truths of reason, on a par with mathematical relations, and he explains God's choice of this world by appeal to the principle of the best: this is the world that maximizes overall perfection, including the perfection of free rational substances. Given that framework, it seems to me that every monad's internal striving, its appetition, is already pre-harmonized with the universal order of perfection according to its own internal law. So if moral norms are just the rational structure that any perfectible substance ought to follow, wouldn't moral failure have to be something like a miscalculation—an error in clear and distinct perception—rather than a genuinely open failure of will? And if two rational agents genuinely disagree about what justice requires in a particular case, wouldn't Leibniz have to say that at least one of them is literally failing to grasp a necessary relation, not merely weighing competing goods differently?

I'm trying to determine whether this reading collapses Leibniz's ethics into a form of moral rationalism that eliminates the possibility of reasonable moral disagreement altogether, or whether there's a distinction in his system—perhaps between the realm of grace and the realm of nature, or between clear and confused perceptions of the good—that preserves a meaningful space for moral uncertainty without slipping into the voluntarism he criticized in Descartes. Specifically, I want to know where the false step lies in the inference from pre-established harmony and necessary moral truths to the conclusion that interpersonal moral conflict is metaphysically impossible rather than merely epistemically obscured. Can someone help me identify the confusion in this line of reasoning?
