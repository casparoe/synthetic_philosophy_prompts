Having spent three decades in experimental chemistry before retiring and turning my attention to philosophy of science, I have become preoccupied with a question that straddles both disciplines: whether our taxonomies genuinely discover boundaries that exist independently of our practices, or whether we impose them for predictive utility. For an advanced interdisciplinary seminar I am helping to design, please construct a single take-home examination prompt—together with a model answer outline and a detailed marking rubric—centered on the claim that science carves nature at its joints. The exam question should require students to compare two domains in which kind-membership is actively contested: chemical elements, defined strictly by proton number yet encompassing isotopic variation under one kind-label, and biological species, where reproductive isolation, phylogenetic continuity, and ecological niche each propose different membership criteria. Students must also audit the butcher-and-carcass analogy itself, mapping precisely what corresponds to the butcher, the knife, the joints, and the meat, then identifying where the analogy collapses—particularly with respect to conventional nomenclature such as the temporary systematic names assigned to superheavy elements by IUPAC before formal confirmation of discovery. A top answer should distinguish descriptive success, meaning predictive coherence and explanatory power, from metaphysical pretension, meaning the claim that nature presents pre-divided categories awaiting discovery; it should also recognize that some classifications function as tools rather than mirrors. The marking scheme should award highest marks only for answers that separate conventional labeling practices from ontological commitment, that identify disanalogies which genuinely undermine essentialism, and that avoid treating temporary administrative names as evidence of discovered natural boundaries. The expected response length is approximately 1,500 words, with a 48-hour window appropriate for advanced undergraduates or first-year graduate students. Please do not include bibliographies, required readings, or citation lists; the task is conceptual analysis, not scholarly review.
