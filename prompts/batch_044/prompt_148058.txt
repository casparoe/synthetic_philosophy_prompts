I'm the chair of a regional research-grant review panel that evaluates roughly forty proposals per funding cycle across climate science and public-health modeling. Three or four times each cycle, two proposals finish with identical normalized scores on every formal criterion—scientific merit, feasibility, broader impact, budget coherence, and reviewer calibration—after we have eliminated outliers and resolved all disagreements through discussion. The tie is not an artifact of rounding; the panel is genuinely indifferent, and no member can defend ranking one over the other without inventing a distinction. Because applicants may request a written rationale and the funding agency audits our reasoning for consistency, I need a reusable, documented decision procedure I can apply in about ninety minutes per tie, sign off on personally, and defend to both scientists and non-scientist administrators.

Ground the procedure in the problem of rational choice without preference—Buridan's ass and its modern treatments—but do not treat it as a mere puzzle. I want you to inject and operationalize a distinction that is rarely made in popular accounts: separate axiological indifference (the values genuinely coincide) from epistemic indifference (some distinguishing feature exists but is inaccessible to us given our evaluation constraints). The procedure should include an Arbitrariness Audit that tests whether the tie survives counterfactual adjustments to our information set—for example, if we learned an applicant's institutional affiliation or a hidden methodological detail—before any resolution is attempted.

Deliver a step-by-step checklist or flowchart-in-words, ordered so that earlier steps filter out spurious ties and later steps handle genuine ones. For each step, specify the test, the decision rule, who on the panel must confirm it, and whether the step eliminates judgment or merely relocates it. Mark explicitly the points where judgment is unavoidable—particularly where we must choose between transparency to applicants and protection of panel confidentiality, and where we must decide whether a randomly resolved tie undermines the legitimacy of the entire evaluation framework.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: (1) two climate-modeling proposals with identical scores, but one relies entirely on open-source code and the other on proprietary software that the panel cannot audit; (2) a tie in which one applicant has a prior award from this same program, a fact that is technically excluded from scoring but is psychologically salient to every panel member; and (3) a tie resolved by a randomization protocol that selects the proposal I personally find methodologically weaker, requiring me to sign the approval.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of ties it will misclassify, whether it can eliminate arbitrariness rather than merely manage it, and what ethical or administrative limits prevent it from being fully automated. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the procedure must be self-contained and executable by someone with graduate-level scientific literacy but no formal philosophical training.
