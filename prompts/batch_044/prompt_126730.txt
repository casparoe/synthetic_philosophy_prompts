I am drafting a short contribution for a student ethics journal, and I keep returning to a line I first heard in an undergraduate seminar on intergenerational justice: "a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow". In that room it was used to justify applying a 5 per cent social discount rate to climate damages projected for the year 2300, as though the phrase settled both the arithmetic and the moral question in a single move. I want to bring a precise disambiguation of that slogan to the discussion.

Can you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing? For each reading, say whether it amounts to a tautology, a substantive empirical claim about revealed preference, a normative principle of pure time preference, an observation about risk or growth, or something else altogether; indicate which are defensible, which are false or question-begging, and which—if any—actually license applying that particular discount rate to harms two centuries hence, and which leave the moral work entirely undone. I am especially interested in where descriptive impatience, risk adjustment, growth expectations, and pure ethical discounting get smuggled together or kept apart, since the speaker treated them as interchangeable. Please do not offer a bibliography; I need the conceptual map, not sources to chase.
