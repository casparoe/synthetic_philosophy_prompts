I just spent the better part of an evening in a heated but good-natured argument with a close friend—she is a composer who thinks of a well-lived life as analogous to a concert hall: silence only acquires meaning in relation to sound, and a life stripped of joy, achievement, and emotional richness is not tranquility but an empty auditorium. I was trying to defend a view I have been calling tranquilism: that the absence of craving and suffering is the sole intrinsic value, and that every apparently positive state—pleasure, love, accomplishment, even aesthetic experience—is at best instrumentally valuable, if it suppresses suffering, and at worst decorative noise that distracts from the real task, which is reaching and maintaining a state of zero disturbance. I want to press this further by translating it out of ordinary moral language entirely.

I am a control-systems engineer; my native vocabulary involves set-points, error signals, noise floors, damping ratios, feedback loops, and the sharp distinction between a controller designed to drive a system along a desired trajectory (generating signal) and one designed solely to minimize deviation from a null baseline (suppressing noise). Please restate the tranquilist position precisely in those terms: what corresponds to craving and suffering, to their absence, and to the allegedly “merely decorative” positive states? Is craving best modeled as a sustained oscillation, as persistent error, or as an unstable set-point? Does tranquility map onto a zero-error equilibrium, a globally damped state, or something like a null-setpoint policy in which the optimal controller never introduces energy? Then provide the residue report: which parts of the philosophical claim survive the translation without distortion, which parts must be fudged (for example, does the claim that positive states lack intrinsic value have any counterpart in control theory, or does engineering vocabulary already bias us toward treating all non-zero states as either useful signal or unwanted noise?), and is there any element of the debate—particularly about whether meaning can exist in pure absence—that simply has no expression in my framework? I especially want to know whether my suspicion that tranquilism is essentially advocating a global null-setpoint policy holds up, or whether I am smuggling in an engineering prejudice that makes the view look more coherent than it actually is, and whether the residue points to a limitation in my vocabulary or to an unclarity at the heart of tranquilism itself.
