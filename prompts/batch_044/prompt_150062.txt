I am preparing an advanced seminar on the mechanisms of moral change, and I want to examine whether Bernard Williams's thesis — that the collapse of a norm is often driven by shifts in honour and social standing rather than by explicit moral argument — together with Kwame Anthony Appiah's model of an 'honour world' that becomes unsustainable when its costs exceed its benefits for the status group, can illuminate a precise historical transition. Rather than revisit slavery or duelling in the abstract, I want to apply the framework to the disappearance of formal duelling among British officers and gentlemen between roughly the 1820s and the 1860s.

Please analyse this episode as a disciplined case study. Identify the relevant 'honour world': who constituted the peer group for whom duelling was a status-maintaining practice, what the practical options were for an officer accused of cowardice or insult (accept the challenge, refuse and face ostracism, seek legal redress), and how the payoffs changed after Parliament refused in the 1840s to shield duellists from criminal liability, and after the Crimean War accelerated a shift from aristocratic patronage to bureaucratic, examination-based promotion within the army and civil service. Map whether the decline was driven primarily by a change in material interests (career advancement now depended on administrative competence rather than aristocratic reputation), by a transformation in the internal logic of honour (the gentleman's status no longer required lethal vindication), or by the growing parliamentary rhetoric that duelling was barbaric. Then consider what the framework misses: did Williams and Appiah anticipate a transition mediated not by peer-to-peer status loss but by institutional reform that 'outsourced' vindication to courts and promotion boards? Does the case suggest that honour can be transferred to legal and bureaucratic structures rather than simply abandoned? And does it support the claim that moral revolutions require a prior change in interests, or can honour shift independently?

Be explicit about the mapping — who are the players, what counts as the evidence of change, and where the theory is silent rather than forced. I want the analysis to run in both directions: the theory should illuminate the historical pattern, and the historical pattern should test whether the theory needs an amendment concerning institutional mediation.
