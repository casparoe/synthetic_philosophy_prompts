Consider the institutional pattern visible in how universities and cultural institutions treat creators with documented moral failures: art-history curricula and museum exhibitions now routinely include explicit ethical framing—syllabus modules, wall texts, or curatorial disclaimers—that addresses a visual artist’s harmful conduct, yet literature and music programs rarely apply analogous framing to authors or composers with comparable biographies. At the same time, art-market institutions systematically suppress that same biographical moral information in provenance reports and auction catalogs, treating it as valuation-irrelevant, even when the museum sector highlights it. What theory, or family of theories, of artistic value does this three-way asymmetry presuppose? Does the museum and curricular practice presuppose that visual art is uniquely transparent to the creator’s moral character in a way that literature and music are not, or that encountering a painting in a museum is a morally situated relationship rather than a purely aesthetic one? Does the market suppression presuppose a strict autonomy thesis—that aesthetic value is entirely separable from moral biography—or merely a pragmatic judgment that buyers prefer not to know? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments that would render both practices coherent together, distinguish what they strictly presuppose from what they are merely compatible with, and identify where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined—specifically, whether rival views such as expressivism, formalism, or institutionalism could each license the split. Finally, say what would have to change in curricular design, curatorial norms, or market regulation if the underlying view were replaced by one that treated moral biography as uniformly relevant, or uniformly irrelevant, across all art forms.
