My appointment last month to the board of a regional repertory theater has made the dynamics of escalation suddenly personal. Over the past year I have been tracking the judgments of Dr. L. M. Chen, the organizational psychologist whose framework on commitment traps I adopted in graduate school, across a series of scenarios. Her verdicts are these. First, a municipal transit authority approved further funding for a rail extension after costs doubled, with board members arguing that abandoning the project would waste the two billion already spent; she judged this irrational escalation. Second, a pharmaceutical firm extended a Phase III trial after interim data showed no efficacy, citing the eighty million already invested and the need to protect shareholder confidence; she judged this irrational escalation. Third, two university departments engaged in an internal bidding war for a single endowed chair, each successively raising salary and startup packages well beyond the chair’s projected value, with deans emphasizing institutional prestige and the humiliation of losing; she called this a classic dollar-auction-style escalation trap and judged it irrational. Fourth, a family continues an expensive renovation of a historic home they intend to occupy permanently; they mention prior expenditures, but continuation is independently justified by the property’s use value and the irreversibility of partial demolition; she judged this rational persistence, not escalation. Fifth, a nonprofit director renewed a literacy program for a third year despite null evaluation results, explicitly telling the board that the organization’s public reputation depends on not being seen to quit; she judged this irrational escalation.

The case now before my board is this. We have committed $85,000 to sets, costumes, and marketing for an original play that received poor preview responses. The artistic director insists we open as scheduled, noting both the sunk production costs and stating that our subscribers and grantors will interpret cancellation as a failure of artistic conviction. At the same time, an external consultant reports that with a revised marketing strategy targeting a specific niche audience, the run could cover its operating costs, though it will not recover the initial investment.

Given Chen’s track record, would she classify our situation as irrational escalation or as rational persistence? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the competing rules that jointly fit her earlier verdicts: one that turns on whether continuation carries independent future justification regardless of audience or reputation, and one that turns on whether the reasoning explicitly appeals to observer perception, competitive standing, or institutional identity. For each rule, state what it predicts about our theater case. Then give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning, identify the closest analog among her prior cases, and name the single further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a private, unobserved decision-maker with no competitive stakes and no salvage value—that would best distinguish the two rules.

Please also say what argument or consideration would lead you to change your assessment of which rule she is running, and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
