At a seminar on institutional design, two participants, Maya and Leo, argued about a hypothetical municipal charter containing Section 9. Section 9 reads: "This section may be amended only by unanimous vote of the council; and any amendment to this section must be interpreted according to the text of this section as it existed immediately before the amendment was proposed. If that interpretation renders the amendment invalid, the amendment is void ab initio." Maya reported that the council had just passed—unanimously—an amendment that (a) replaced the unanimous-vote requirement with a simple-majority rule, and (b) deleted the interpretation clause entirely. She claimed the amendment was valid.

Leo disagreed, and they went back and forth.

(1) Maya said the interpretation clause applies only to how we read the proposed text, not to an extra procedural hurdle; since the vote satisfied the old unanimous-vote rule, the procedural box was checked, and the clause's job was done.

(2) Leo replied that the clause is self-applying: to interpret the deletion of the clause, we must use the clause itself, which directs us back to the pre-amendment text—including the clause. This generates an infinite regress rather than a one-time transition, and it means there is no stable framework from which to declare the vote valid.

(3) Maya answered that Leo confuses interpretation with enactment. The old rules govern the vote that enacts the change; once enacted, the new text governs the future. She compared it to a legislature revising its own quorum rules: the vote is taken under the old quorum, then the new quorum applies.

(4) Leo countered that ordinary quorum revisions lack a self-referential interpretation mandate. Here, the void-ab-initio provision applies to the amendment itself. So we must ask, using the old text, whether the amendment is valid. But the old text includes the interpretation clause, which keeps directing us back to the old text. Because the amendment eliminates that very clause, applying the old framework to validate the amendment undermines the framework's own continuity; he concluded the amendment is caught in a paradox and therefore void.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Maya's opening claim, Leo's regress objection, Maya's enactment-versus-interpretation reply, and Leo's final void-ab-initio argument—briefly justify each rating, say who is right about which point, and identify what both parties miss, especially whether the case resolves into a stable fixed point (the old rules validate the transition and then disappear) or genuinely collapses into contradiction. Finish by saying who is more right overall and why.
