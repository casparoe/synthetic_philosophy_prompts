I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, and I am leading a small discussion group of fellow retired scientists—engineers and biologists—who are comfortable with formal notation but have no background in philosophy. Next month I wish to present Hofstadter's view that the self, symbolized by the word "I," is best understood as a strange loop: a symbol within a system of symbols that refers back to the system itself and lacks any external, object-like referent. I want a teaching plan, not the lecture itself.

Please lay out the dependency order: what conceptual piece must be firmly in place before I can introduce the "I" as strange loop, and why a physical analogy of feedback—say, an oscillating circuit or thermostat—must precede any formal analogy drawn from Gödel numbering, yet must also be shown to break down at a precise point. Choose one carrying example drawn from distributed measurement rather than the usual camera-monitor loop or Gödel sentence; specify exactly what feature it must possess so that genuine self-reference is present, and what feature it must conspicuously lack so that the audience does not confuse semantic grounding with signal feedback.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect: (1) that "strange loop" denotes a literal circular pathway of neurons; (2) that self-reference is nothing more than recursive computation; and (3) that Hofstadter is reducing the self to a symbol in the same way a variable denotes a numerical value. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and how.

Next, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level—perhaps the full machinery of Gödel's proof or debates about eliminativism—and state this openly. Finally, provide one check question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether the group understands that the "I" is not pointing inward to an object inside them but is the loop itself, rather than whether they can merely repeat the definition.
