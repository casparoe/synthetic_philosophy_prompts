I've been thinking about this ever since my roommate brought up Plato at a party last weekend (we'd both had a few drinks, which made it ironic). I'm definately convinced there's a coherent view running through the Symposium and the Laws that Plato treats ritual drinking not as indulgence but as a kind of stress-test for virtue — the symposiarch is basically running an adversarial audit on your soul, and the whole setup is meant to reveal whether your reason can still steer when your inhibitions are lowered. Some people say he's just a moralizing old man, but I think if you read the two texts together you get something closer to an early theory of set and setting: the rules of the party are there to make the alchohol safe enough that you can observe your own failures without actually destroying the polis.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the ususal "Plato was elitist" dismissal — I want to know what specific claims about psychology, virtue, or social control this interpretation commits me to. For instance, does it depend on a particular view of how alchohol affects the thumos that modern neuroscience just rules out? Would it collapse if the Symposium's playful irony turns out to be incompatible with the Laws' legalistic tone? And is there a risk I've qualified the view so much — "well, he only meant it for the guardians, well, it's only metaphorical" — that it can't be wrong anymore? Rank the defeaters: which ones are fatal, which are just awkward, and which ones a defender of this reading would honestly have to concede.
