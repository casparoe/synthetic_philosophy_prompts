Hey, I'm a novelist working on a character who's an ethicist with a bad habit of mixing up her actual preferences with moral biases. I've been using these cases to figure out her instincts, and I keep running into the same wall.

She thinks eating meat is wrong because of animal suffering, and she thinks gambling is wrong because it exploits addicts -- both feel like real moral stances. She also thinks lying to a murderer at the door is fine, but lying to her sister about liking her painting is not -- that one feels like a practical rule, not a deep bias.

But then there's the messy stuff. She drives a hybrid but judges people with big trucks as selfish. She flies home twice a year and doesn't see the contradiction. She thinks arranged marriage in other cultures is oppressive, but calls online dating 'just how people meet now.' And here's the one I can't resolve: she won't wear leather because of the animals, but she wears silk without a second thought, even though silk kills way more bugs per yard.

Her verdicts seem to split into 'this is a principle' versus 'this is just my taste dressed up.' Is there actually a philosophical principle that can seperate moral preferences from biases in these cases? Maybe something about universalizability, or harm to people who can't consent, or consistant reflection? Or is the line basically circular -- you call it a bias when it doesn't match your social group?

If there's a principle, what does it say about the silk/leather thing? And if there isn't one, what do I call the failure mode -- the way people build moral rules that happen to line up with their lifestyle? I don't need citations, just whether the carving holds up or if I'm writing a character who's just rationalizing.
