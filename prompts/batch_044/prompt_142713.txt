Two colleagues in my philosophy reading group, Elena and Marcus, were debating Bernard Williams’s integrity objection to utilitarianism, specifically the idea that saving one’s spouse from drowning because she is one’s spouse is permissible, whereas saving her only after running a utilitarian calculation involves having one thought too many. They disagreed sharply about how far the objection reaches, and neither would concede ground.

Elena argued that the objection is structural rather than sentimental: utilitarianism requires agents to treat their own ground projects as lacking special weight, which alienates them from their actions. She extended this to institutional roles, claiming that a triage doctor who allocates a ventilator solely on the basis of quality-adjusted life-years is acting with professional integrity, because her ground project is impartial healing. By contrast, if she allocates the ventilator to her own child by invoking that same calculus—claiming the survival probability is marginally higher—she imports an impartial framework into a partial relationship, which Elena said is precisely the corruption Williams identifies. Marcus replied that this renders the objection circular: if integrity simply means aligning one’s reasoning with one’s social role, then a utilitarian can accommodate every case by redefining ground projects, draining the objection of its critical force against impartial moral theory. He pressed Elena on a harder case: suppose a doctor genuinely takes universal beneficence as her ground project and uses the calculus to justify saving her child. Elena dismissed this as moral self-deception, but Marcus countered that without an independent criterion for legitimate ground projects, she was merely smuggling in her own partialist intuitions. He then proposed that both of them might be missing whether the objection targets reasoning patterns, motivational structures, or the phenomenological experience of alienation, which might apply even to role-consistent reasoning if the agent feels detached.

Elena conceded that alienation might be the deeper issue, yet insisted that Marcus’s role-relativism collapses the objection into a psychological point about motivation rather than a philosophical refutation of utilitarianism. Marcus maintained that if utilitarianism can be internalised as a ground project—something Williams himself seemed to allow for the hardened utilitarian—then the objection fails as a universal critique. They left unresolved whether the objection succeeds only against act-utilitarianism or also against rule-utilitarianism, and whether a parent who refuses to calculate at all, even to justify saving her child, exhibits integrity or mere prejudice. Please adjudicate their dispute point by point: does Elena’s institutional extension hold, does Marcus’s charge of circularity land, and what are both parties missing about whether the objection concerns reasoning, motivation, or the legitimacy of ground projects themselves?
