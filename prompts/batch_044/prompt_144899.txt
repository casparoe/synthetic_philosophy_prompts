I'm preparing a position paper for my moral psychology and normative ethics seminar, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present. Over the course, I've settled into a package that feels coherent in pieces but may not hang together, and I want to know which commitments actually pull against each other, which only look like tensions, and what the least costly revision would be. I'm also somewhat unsure whether I'm importing a conceptual distinction that doesn't belong in this domain, so please push back if the framing itself is confused rather than forcing an answer.

Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I hold a right-reasons view of moral worth: an action has full moral worth only if the agent's motivating reason is the moral rightness of the act itself. Second, I think that acting from a cultivated emotional disposition — specifically, a stable compassion that reliably tracks the suffering of others — still counts as acting for the right reason, because the emotional state is responsive to the very moral feature that makes the act right. Third, I want to distinguish de dicto motivation, being moved by the thought that this is right, from de re motivation, being moved directly by the right-making properties such as the suffering involved, and I claim that de re motivation is sufficient for moral worth while de dicto motivation is not necessary. Fourth, I accept a broadly evolutionary debunking argument: our moral-emotional responses are largely adaptive mechanisms shaped by group cohesion and kin selection, so our intuitive confidence that we ever act from pure moral concern is epistemically unreliable; still, I maintain that moral worth is a real, non-illusory property that is instantiated rarely, if ever. Fifth, I apply these to a specific case I constructed for the seminar: Dr. Elena Voss anonymously donates a substantial portion of her salary to a malaria charity. She reports that she does so because she recognizes that distant strangers have equal moral weight, but she also reports an intense visceral revulsion to images of suffering children that she cannot introspectively disentangle from her moral judgment. By my lights, her action lacks full moral worth because her motivational structure fails what I call a transparency condition — the agent must be able to report the moral reason without being confounded by co-occurring affective states that are not themselves moral reasons — though I think she acts for the right reasons in a weaker, derivative sense.

Please rank the tensions in this package by severity, and for each say exactly which commitment has to be revised or qualified if consistency is to be restored. In particular, I'm concerned that my acceptance of evolutionary debunking may undermine my willingness to credit emotional compassion as a genuinely moral reason, and that my de dicto and de re distinction may not do the work I need it to do if applied outside its home domain. I also want you to flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection — for instance, whether the rarity implied by the debunking argument actually conflicts with my claim that moral worth is real. If the task as posed can't be done well because I've built the package on a confused premise, say so plainly.
