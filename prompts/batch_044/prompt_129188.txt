Design a 60-minute classroom activity for 12 upper-level students in decision theory or philosophy of economics that makes them feel the pull of violating the independence axiom, without naming the Allais paradox until the debrief. Frame a two-round point-allocation game as “regional resilience funding.” In Round 1 each student privately chooses between Option S (50 points guaranteed) and Option G (200 points with probability 0.89, 50 with 0.10, 0 with 0.01). In Round 2, presented as “integrated funding with a shared systemic shock layer,” they choose between Option R (50 points with probability 0.11, otherwise 0) and Option T (200 points with probability 0.10, otherwise 0), where both options depend on the same hidden common-state draw revealed only after commitments are locked. Use anonymous recording cards, prohibit discussion between rounds, and run a brief “shock reveal” ceremony. The payoff scheme should award 10 bonus points for consistent risk preference across rounds, but award 30 bonus points for the S-then-T pattern—the standard Allais shift—so participants feel materially rewarded for the structural reversal. After revealing aggregate choices, debrief by mapping Round 1’s certainty preference onto the common-consequence removal in Round 2, identifying the exact moment where evaluation changes despite identical outcome ratios. Predict what will go wrong the first time—students will likely invent domain-specific justifications for switching rather than recognizing a framing effect—and advise handling it by asking them to articulate the principle behind each choice before supplying the formal distinction. Be explicit about which feature the game reproduces well (the framing-sensitive evaluation of shared outcomes) and which feature it cannot reproduce (the normative defense of independence under Savage-style axioms) and must be supplied in facilitator-led discussion.
