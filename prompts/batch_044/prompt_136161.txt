I'm working through Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism for an upcoming seminar on moral psychology—specifically the integrity objection and the familiar case in which a husband saves his drowning wife rather than a stranger, where Williams suggests that supplying a utilitarian justification for the preference introduces one thought too many. In a recent study-group discussion, one of my peers proposed an extension of this reasoning that I find intuitively compelling but worry may rest on a category mistake. She argued that Kantian universalization is structurally parallel: before acting, the agent steps outside her concrete practical identity to test her maxim against a formal law that is not internal to her commitments, thereby subjecting her deepest projects to the same kind of alien evaluation that Williams condemns. If she is right, then the integrity objection does not discriminate between consequentialist and deontological ethics; rather, it undermines any framework that permits reflective distance between an agent and her projects. Does this mean that preserving integrity requires abandoning not only utilitarian aggregation but also Kantian moral deliberation, leaving virtue ethics and particularism as the only viable alternatives? Or is there a relevant difference between weighing outcomes to maximize aggregate welfare and testing maxims for universalizability—one that allows Williams's critique to apply selectively without collapsing the distinction between these two traditions?
