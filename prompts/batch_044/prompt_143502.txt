As a novelist preparing a long-form narrative that takes analogical cognition—not formal logic or linear causality—as the deep architecture of human thought, in the vein advanced by Hofstadter and Sander, I am deciding where to direct my conceptual energy during the eighteen months before drafting begins. I need a defended agenda, not a survey or a bibliography. Among the open questions in this area—how analogical mapping preserves or corrupts causal structure when it moves across domains; whether any stable boundary survives between structural analogy and surface resemblance once we leave textbook examples; how counterfactual reasoning in narrative comprehension depends on analogical transfer rather than on explicit modal machinery; and what I will call analogical fatigue, the precise point in sustained cross-domain mapping where parallels cease to illuminate and collapse into mechanical repetition—which deserve priority, in what order, and why? I ask you to rank them according to conceptual dependency, tractability for a serious generalist without laboratory training, and the degree to which resolving one would reshape the others, while also weighing narrative payoff: which answers would genuinely alter the novel’s metaphysical backbone and which are philosophically interesting but architecturally inert. What criteria should govern this ordering—upstream conceptual dependency, the risk of building on an unstable analogy, or practical feasibility for a non-specialist? Where should I accept trade-offs between philosophical rigor and the demands of long-form fiction? And what specific conceptual clarification or finding over the coming year would cause you to reorder this list?
