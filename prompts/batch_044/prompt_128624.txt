I am taking a course on the philosophy of science, and my instructor described a grading convention used in several physics departments for exam questions about classic thought experiments—Einstein’s elevator, Schrödinger’s cat, Maxwell’s demon, and similar cases. Under this convention, a student receives full credit only if the answer performs what the rubric labels conceptual surgery: it must explicitly name the specific pre-theoretical intuition—such as absolute simultaneity, deterministic macroscopic behavior, or the separability of thermodynamic states—that the scenario is engineered to isolate and then show why formal theory forces its abandonment. Answers that reach the correct quantitative or logical conclusion without identifying and dissecting that intuitive commitment are marked down, even if the derivation is flawless. The practice treats the resolution of the paradox as incomplete unless the student has mapped the exact boundary where everyday intuition fails and theory takes over.

What philosophical commitments about scientific knowledge does this institutional grading norm presuppose? Does it commit to the view that thought experiments yield knowledge not by predicting outcomes but by restructuring the conceptual scheme of the investigator—so that knowing requires the explicit rejection of a particular intuitive belief rather than mere theoretical assimilation? Is the practice consistent with treating these experiments as purely heuristic devices that could eventually be replaced by direct empirical tests, or does it presuppose that some knowledge is accessible only through this kind of intuitive-conceptual conflict? Furthermore, if a department abolished this rule and graded purely on formal derivation, would it be changing only its pedagogy, or would it implicitly be adopting a different philosophy of how thought experiments contribute to scientific understanding? Give a committed response: take a definite position on what the practice strictly presupposes versus what merely fits it, distinguish whether its commitments concern ontology, epistemology, or both, and defend your view against the objection that the grading rule is merely a teaching tool with no deeper philosophical significance.
