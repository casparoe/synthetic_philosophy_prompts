I am inclined to defend a modal-profile version of the constitution view: the statue and the clay are not two distinct objects standing in a material-constitution relation, but a single spatio-temporal entity described under two non-interdefinable modal profiles. The clay-profile tracks diachronic continuity and mereological fusion conditions across deformation, while the statue-profile tracks artefactual-functional persistence conditions tied to authorial intent and aesthetic form; neither profile enjoys ontological priority, and the appearance of two objects stems from applying incompatible persistence conditions to one underlying four-dimensional worm rather than from literal composition. What would have to be true for this position to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure: which concrete cases—gradual part-replacement, destructive digitisation, or artefactual analogues of fission—would be decisive rather than merely awkward; whether standard constitution theorists or eliminativists about artefacts could absorb the same intuitions; and whether the view inevitably collapses into four-dimensionalism or hylomorphism despite its attempt to avoid both. In particular, is there a formal result or a thought experiment that forces a choice between treating 'the statue' and 'the clay' as genuinely co-referring descriptions and treating them as referring to different entities, or can the single-entity, dual-profile account redescribe every challenge without loss? Please distinguish evidence that defenders of this view should themselves concede as damaging from criticisms that merely presuppose the very ontological multiplicity the account denies.
