I work as the senior ethics and protocol officer for an international consortium of national memorial museums, mostly in societies that have recently transitioned away from authoritarian rule or protracted civil conflict. Our board receives frequent proposals—from government ministries, survivor associations, foreign donors, and international tribunals—asking our institution to issue formal statements about historical collective wrongs: engineered famines, forced-labor regimes, collaborationist administrations, or settler-violence campaigns. Because these requests arrive with incompatible expectations about blame, apology, and remembrance, I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Karl Jaspers’ well-known fourfold distinction of guilt (criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical), that my review board can run on any proposal without reopening first principles each time.

Please operationalize Jaspers’ framework into a step-by-step procedure my board can actually execute: specify the diagnostic questions in strict sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each gate, where the procedure stops with a definitive “no statement appropriate,” where it escalates to a value judgment the board must make rather than derive from the concept, and how to classify the correct institutional speech act—acknowledgment, apology, memorial designation, or deliberate institutional silence. The procedure must distinguish between whether the collective subject our museum represents is the right bearer of the guilt-type in question, whether that guilt-type licenses an institutional utterance at all, and where “representational standing” breaks down (that is, where the museum can speak for a collective agent versus where it overreaches into metaphysical or purely personal moral domains). Apply it to three cases: first, a ministry request that we co-sign an official state apology for a famine engineered by a prior regime seventy years ago, where present citizens include both descendants of perpetrators and victims; second, a survivor association demanding we label a past security apparatus as collectively criminal and display the names of individual perpetrators in our permanent gallery; and third, a board-favored proposal to issue no institutional statement about documented forced-labor camps on the grounds that metaphysical guilt exceeds any museum’s representational authority—a case where the procedure should, if correctly designed, either force an unwanted conclusion or expose the board’s reasoning as a disguised value choice. Keep the response under 650 words. Open with a bottom-line paragraph stating what the procedure achieves, its two most important decision gates, and the specific point where it is structurally weakest. Then present the procedure in ordered steps, followed by the three applications, and close with one sentence on what the procedure cannot do.
