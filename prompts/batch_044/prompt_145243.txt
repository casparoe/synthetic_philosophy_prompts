You are an expert philosopher of mind and cognitive science with particular knowledge of the philosophy of perception. Please think carefully, begin with a brief brainstorm of the logical relations among the theses below, and then proceed systematically. I am a high school junior writing an independent study paper, and before I present to my school's philosophy club I want an internal audit of my position on cognitive penetration. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need conceptual analysis only.

Here is the set of commitments I hold:

(1) Cognitive penetration is a real phenomenon: a perceiver's beliefs, expectations, or linguistic categories can alter phenomenal character directly, not merely influence post-perceptual judgment.
(2) The classic New Look experiments on coin-size perception are unreliable evidence for penetration because they do not adequately separate report bias from genuine experiential change.
(3) I have constructed a hypothetical case—the darkroom smell protocol—in which subjects told a chemical odor is dangerous rate it as more pungent than subjects told it is harmless, even though both groups show identical pupillary dilation and skin-conductance responses; I take this to show that penetration can affect phenomenal quality while leaving early sensory registration untouched.
(4) I maintain a sharp personal-level distinction between seeing and interpreting: seeing delivers representational content, while interpreting assigns significance or belief.
(5) I hold that perceptual justification for basic empirical beliefs requires that the seeing involved be both causally and rationally independent of the belief it supports.
(6) I reject strong Quinean theory-ladenness: there is a core of pure seeing that remains conceptually uncontaminated, even if phenomenal character is sometimes tinted by expectation.

Please examine these commitments pair by pair. For each pair, determine whether they are genuinely incompatible, only apparently in tension, or fully compatible. Where tension exists, rank its severity, locate the conflict precisely—whether in the metaphysics of experience, the epistemology of perception, or my methodological standards—and propose the cheapest revision that restores consistency at the lowest cost to my core view. Pay special attention to the relationship between (2) and (3): explain whether my rejection of the coin studies is consistent with my acceptance of the smell protocol, or whether this is special pleading that dissolves on inspection. Finally, identify which single commitment, if revised or abandoned, would eliminate the greatest number of tensions, and explain what I would have to give up in doing so.
