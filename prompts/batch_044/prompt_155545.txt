I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to apply the Stoic dichotomy of control—what lies within my power versus what does not—to my professional life and to the modern revival literature that sometimes reframes the split as a trichotomy of full control, partial influence, and no control. I want to know whether my intuitions about specific cases can be captured by one precise principle, perhaps one that distinguishes intrinsic concern for my own judgments and actions from instrumental concern for outcomes I can influence but do not own, or whether I must abandon some verdicts for the sake of coherence.

Here are my verdicts on concrete situations.

1. A critical service outage caused entirely by a third-party cloud provider: the failure itself is outside my control, so feeling distress about the failure is a mistake; only my judgment and my subsequent actions are proper objects of concern.

2. Whether I respond to that outage with calm, write a clear post-mortem, and update runbooks: fully within my control, and this is where my ethical and practical attention should be directed.

3. Whether my team meets a product deadline when roughly forty percent of the required integration depends on that same unreliable provider: I think I should care about meeting the deadline and should work hard on my portion, but I should not be emotionally devastated if the provider's failure makes us miss it. This feels like partial control, yet I am unsure whether the Stoic framework permits me to care about an outcome that is only partly mine.

4. A junior engineer on my team becomes panicked during the outage. I can choose to speak with her, model calm behavior, and help her reframe the situation. Her internal panic is within her control, not mine; my supportive actions are within mine. I think I should help, but I should not feel responsible for whether she actually becomes calm. This suggests a boundary between acting on others and owning their internal states.

5. Investing engineering time in redundant failover systems: the act of designing and implementing redundancy is fully within my control. But the purpose of that act is to influence a future external event, namely an outage. I think this investment is rational and virtuous, not a case of wrongly attaching my well-being to externals. Yet if the Stoic rule is strict indifference to everything outside my direct judgments, it seems I should be indifferent to whether the redundancy is ever used.

6. My decision to study Stoic ideas in order to improve my decision-making under pressure: this choice is within my control. However, if my motivation is partly instrumental—to become a more effective engineer and earn better performance reviews—then I seem to be valuing an external outcome. I think this instrumental motivation is acceptable and does not corrupt the practice, but I am unsure whether a strict reading of the dichotomy should reject it.

These verdicts feel mostly coherent, but there is tension between case 1 and case 3: in 1 I reject emotional investment in an external outcome, while in 3 I accept caring about and working toward an outcome that is partly external. There is also tension between case 5 and a strict interpretation of case 1, since in 5 I treat influencing externals as a legitimate reason for action.

Is there a principle—perhaps one that uses the trichotomy to map degrees of influence, combined with a distinction between intrinsic and instrumental valuation—that can accommodate all six? If so, state it precisely, identify the clause that handles each case above, and tell me what it predicts for the following unlisted case.

7. I can choose whether to check incident-alert channels during my personal time. Checking gives me information that might let me intervene earlier, which is partial influence over an external system, but it also exposes me to stress over events I may not be able to fix. Should the principle classify the decision to check as a matter of internal judgment, the resulting stress as a failure of judgment, or the information-gathering itself as an illegitimate attachment to controlling externals?

If no single principle can save all six verdicts without excessive ad hoc complexity, I want an honest assessment: which verdict or verdicts should I drop to reach the simplest coherent account, and what does that account predict for case 7?
