I have just come away from a long, rather exhausting argument with a close friend about the standing of causal theories, and I find myself committed to a view that feels sharper than I can fully defend. I have become convinced that the interventionist framework—particularly as articulated through structural causal models and the do-operator—does not merely supplement counterfactual theories in the tradition of Lewis and Stalnaker, but exposes a deep circularity within them: to evaluate what would happen if X were changed, we seem already to need a causal model that distinguishes exogenous from endogenous variables, yet that model appears justified only by prior counterfactual judgments about the outcomes of hypothetical interventions. Rather than treating the two as partners, I now think the counterfactual account is conceptually derivative, and that causation should be analyzed in terms of an interventionist primitive—specifically, a structural mechanism invariant across a range of possible interventions—while treating counterfactual dependence as a downstream descriptive artifact.

This is not merely a methodological preference; it carries a somewhat niche implication, namely that in cyclic or feedback-rich systems where exogeneity conditions for a well-defined intervention break down, the notion of a counterfactual cause becomes not just computationally intractable but metaphysically indeterminate, since there is no stable structural invariant against which to measure deviation. Given that this claim turns on the conceptual priority of intervention over counterfactual evaluation, I want an honest map of its exposure—what evidence, formal result, or conceptual argument should actually move me, and which objections I should regard as merely awkward accommodations rather than fatal wounds.

I am especially interested in whether there is a decisive case—perhaps arising from redundant causation, or from recent identifiability results concerning unobserved confounders—that forces the interventionist back into reliance on counterfactual primitives. I also want to know whether defenders of this conceptual-priority claim typically concede that the circularity is genuine but benign, deny it altogether, or qualify the view—say, by restricting it to acyclic systems—into something so insulated that it risks unfalsifiability. If such insulation exists, is it a theoretical virtue or a defect? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that committed interventionists should honestly concede from those they can legitimately dismiss, and indicate whether anything that has arguably already happened in the literature counts against the view.
