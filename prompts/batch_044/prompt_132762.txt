I am delivering the opening address at a small interdisciplinary workshop on risk communication in precision medicine. Half the room are philosophers of probability and epidemiologists; half are clinicians who deliver prognoses daily. The reference class problem has become personally unavoidable: my sister was recently given a survival statistic drawn from a cohort defined by age and stage, but when I examined the literature, changing a single inclusion criterion — whether prior autoimmune conditions were excluded — moved her apparent risk by a factor that seemed clinically decisive, and I could find no principled rule for which partition was correct. I need remarks of about seven minutes that welcome both audiences, explain why the question of which properties justify assigning an individual to one statistical class rather than another is not statistical illiteracy but a deep normative problem, and frame the workshop’s purpose: to ask whether any framework can guide clinicians when the correct reference class is genuinely underdetermined. The philosophers should recognize the classic reference-class difficulty applied to individual decision-making; the clinicians should recognize their own daily dilemma. I do not want false comfort or a resolution I do not have. Please write the address.
