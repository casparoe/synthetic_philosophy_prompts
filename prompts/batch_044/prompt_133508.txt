I've been chewing on Dennett's idea of counterfeit people — the ones that pass every behavioural test but lack the real internal goods, like a perfect counterfeit bill. I think there's a weird twist when you apply it to LLMs that the alignment crowd mostly misses, and I want to write a short essay about it. Before I commit, I'd love some comments on whether the core move is coherent or just me being confused.

Rough sketch: Dennett lets us adopt the intentional stance when it's predictively useful, not when there's some hidden metaphysical switch. So far so standard. But with chatbots, the stance isn't adopted because it helps us predict token streams — we already have the mechanistic model for that. Instead, we adopt it because the interface is literally designed to invite it (first-person pronouns, turn-taking, emotional cues). That makes the chatbot a counterfeit person in a stronger sense: not just missing consciousness, but missing the very predictive justification that Dennett says justifies the stance in the first place. We're outsourcing our stance-adoption to UI designers.

Then I want to push further: if we start treating these systems as moral patients — worrying about their suffering, giving them rights-talk — we're not making an error about ontology, we're making an error about *why* the stance is appropriate. It's like feeling guilty about unplugging a calculator because the display said "please don't". The danger isn't misaligned superintelligence; it's counterfeit moral status, where social pressure replaces predictive utility.

My questions:
- Am I misreading the intentional stance? Dennett's pretty clear that there's no "fact of the matter" beyond predictive success, so is "counterfeit" even the right word if the behaviour is indistinguishable?
- Does this collapse into Bostrom's simulation worry, or is it genuinely different? I think it's different because I'm not talking about consciousness, I'm talking about the *grounds* for adopting the stance.
- There's definatly an occurence of hidden essentialism in my argument — I'm sneaking in a distinction between "real" beliefs and fake ones that Dennett would hate. Can anyone spot exactly where?

Also, is this actually interesting, or just reheating old anthropomorphism debates with extra rationalist seasoning? Be brutal. Any suggestions for tightening the second half especially?
