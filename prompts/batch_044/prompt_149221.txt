I'm preparing my final paper for Professor Chen's seminar on rational agency and value alignment. Rather than a standard literature review, I want to test how different formalizations of idealized preference hold up against a criterion I'm calling reflexive stability under modal expansion—roughly, whether an agent's endorsed preferences at one stage of reflection remain endorsed once she understands what those preferences actually entail at the next stage. Could you rank the following four approaches on a 1-to-5 scale according to how well they survive this pressure: (1) the reflective-equilibrium formulation of coherent extrapolated volition; (2) the preference-aggregation framework used in welfare economics; (3) the contractualist veil-of-ignorance model; and (4) the narrative-continuity account associated with Dennett's center of gravity. For each, give the score and one or two sentences explaining whether its structure makes the agent's later self likely to repudiate her earlier commitments, or whether it builds in safeguards against that kind of drift. I'm particularly interested in whether any of these frameworks implicitly rely on a static conception of the self that collapses once extrapolation is taken seriously.
