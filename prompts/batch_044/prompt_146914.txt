You are an expert in comparative ethics and philosophy of religion with training in metaphysics. Before drafting, take a brief moment to brainstorm: what is the genuinely hard conceptual knot in treating karma as a theory of moral desert rather than as cosmic bookkeeping or generic causation?

Design one final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of religion (90 minutes, 30 marks, expected answer about 800 words). The prompt should ask students to evaluate karma specifically as a theory of moral desert—that is, the view that present suffering or flourishing is deserved in virtue of prior moral action, with the desert relation extending across embodied lives. To prevent answers that merely summarize doctrines or list traditions, inject the following framing: ask students to assume that karma is coupled with the Buddhist rejection of a permanent substantial self (anātman). The core task should be to determine whether moral desert can survive this combination. If there is no enduring self that is numerically identical across lives, in what precise sense is the present sufferer the same moral agent who deserves reward or punishment for prior acts? The question should force students to distinguish karma carefully from (i) Western single-life retributivism, which presupposes one continuous embodied life, and (ii) purely consequentialist moral bookkeeping that tracks outcomes without any desert relation.

Then provide the following deliverables, clearly labeled:

1. The essay question itself, precisely and formally worded.
2. A model answer of about 600 words that includes the specific moves an excellent response must make—particularly the argument that diachronic moral desert requires conditions stronger than mere causal-psychological continuity, and an evaluation of whether conventional or reconstructed personal identity can bear the normative weight of responsibility.
3. A marking scheme out of 30 marks that separates an A-range answer (26–30) from a B-range (21–25) by naming the exact conceptual moves required (e.g., articulating the tension between diachronic identity and desert, addressing anātman without importing an unargued substantial soul, distinguishing karma from retributivism on both temporal scope and metaphysical substrate) and listing the specific mistakes that should cost marks (e.g., treating karma as mere causation, conflating desert with utility, or failing to address the identity problem).

Keep the prompt self-contained: do not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts. Write in formal academic English. Start with your brief brainstorming note, then present the deliverables.
