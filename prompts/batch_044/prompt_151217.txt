I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow evening at a small interdisciplinary workshop—about thirty people, half philosophers of perception and cognitive scientists, half engineers and clinicians who design sensory-substitution devices for people with congenital blindness. The occasion is the inaugural meeting of a research collaborative I co-founded. Molyneux's problem has become personally urgent: my daughter, born with dense bilateral cataracts, underwent lens replacement three months ago and is now encountering visual space for the first time. The audience knows the standard Locke-Molyneux exchange; I do not want to retell it like a textbook. Instead, I want to build the remarks around an often-neglected structural feature of the original formulation—Molyneux's specific pairing of a cube and a sphere, shapes that share volume but differ precisely in whether their boundaries are angular or continuously curved—to frame what I can and cannot claim about her experience. The speech must do real structural work: it should prevent me from declaring that she now sees exactly as sighted people do, and prevent me from reducing her responses to mere associative inference, without collapsing the distinction altogether. It should run about five minutes, stay formal but accessible to the non-philosophers present, and speak to both the technical concern with modality-specific spatial encoding and the human concern with what cross-modal recognition actually requires. Please write the speech. Begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary. The first sentence should present the central claim directly; then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of this request.
