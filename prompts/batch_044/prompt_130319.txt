I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we're comparing Dennett's multiple drafts model with the old Cartesian theater idea—y'know, the central screen picture where consciousness happens in one place. I tried pushing Dennett's view to see where it breaks, and now I have an argument that seems to prove way too much, like maybe there's literally no self. Here's what I built:

(1) The Cartesian theater is the picture of consciousness as a single stage in the brain where mental content gets presented to an observer.
(2) Dennett's multiple drafts model denies this: different brain regions generate versions of events, and there's no master copy or central audience.
(3) So if I sincerely think I am the same person who started this sentence, that's just one draft among many, with no special authority.
(4) But then there's also a draft tracking my bodily continuity, another tracking narrative memory, maybe one that doesn't track continuity at all—none of them is the real me checking the others.
(5) Without a theater, there's no single subject that can adjudicate between these drafts, so there's no fact of the matter wether I persist.
(6) This applies to every mental state—pain-drafts, belief-drafts, identity-drafts—all competing with no referee.
(7) So denying the Cartesian theater doesn't just kill the central observer; it kills the very idea of a subject that owns mental states.
(8) But that's clearly wrong; even Dennett allows the self to exist as something like a center of narrative gravity, not as pure fiction.
(9) Therefore there must be a mistake in the slide from (2) to (7), and some weaker version of the Cartesian theater survives.

My study partner says the error is treating drafts as ontologically separate entities rather than functional descriptions, but I'm not convinced. Which step do you reject, and what's the cost? I'd really like to keep (8), but I want to know if saving it forces me back into Cartesianism.
