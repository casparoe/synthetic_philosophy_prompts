everyone in political commentary keeps saying modern democracies don't fall to coups like in old movies, they fall like the roman republic did -- citizens get tired of gridlock and keep voting for strongmen who promise to cut through it, then those leaders use legal powers to extend their stay and eventually the whole thing collapses from inside. people use this to argue that the real threat isn't tanks in the streets but voters themselves legally eroding checks and balances. i want to audit this analogy properly, not just nod along. can you lay out the correspondance table: what plays the role of the senate, the tribunes, the client armies, the grain dole, the stretching of dictatorial terms? then where does the mapping break -- rome had no universal sufferage, no mass media, no nuclear deterrence, and by the late republic elections were mostly bribery and street violence? also, does the analogy quietly depend on treating modern democracy as a polybian mixed constitution with separable parts that can be captured one by one, and is that framing even right for presidential or parliamentary systems? and does it smuggle in the idea that modern voters are trading liberty for security like roman citizens allegedly traded the republic for the pax romana? once all the broken parts are on the table, does the conclusion -- that democratic collapse is mainly an internal electoral problem rather than an institutional or economic one -- survive, or was the rome comparison just giving it fake historical weight?
