I would like you to analyze the 2010 San José mine rescue—specifically the Chilean state’s decision to commit to a multi-week extraction operation for thirty-three trapped miners—as a case study in incommensurable values and what Ruth Chang calls “hard choices,” but with a deliberate conceptual twist. Rather than treating the rescue as a single decision point, consider it as a diachronically structured sequence in which the evaluative framework itself shifts between phases, so that the values in play at one stage may not belong to the same evaluative order as those at another.

Consider the episode through its salient structural features, which I will supply so that no external lookup is required: on 5 August 2010, a collapse at the San José copper-gold mine near Copiapó trapped thirty-three workers roughly 700 meters underground. The government, rather than declaring the site a mass grave and turning to compensation, committed within days to an open-ended rescue involving parallel boreholes (Plans A, B, and C), substantial state expenditure, and international engineering collaboration. The operation lasted sixty-nine days; the final extraction via the Fénix capsules required rescue personnel themselves to descend into an unstable shaft. Afterward, the state confronted a further value conflict between prosecuting the mine’s owners—who had ignored well-documented safety violations—and honoring the miners and rescuers as symbols of national unity and collective resilience.

Using the conceptual resources of value pluralism and hard-choice theory—particularly the distinction between options genuinely “on a par” and those that involve tragic, irreparable moral loss—map the decision points with disciplinary precision. At each phase—the initial commitment to rescue versus abandonment; the allocation of risk between trapped miners and rescue workers during the final extraction; and the post-rescue tension between retributive justice and social solidarity—specify who the moral agents were, what the actual options and payoffs looked like, and whether the conflict was one of comparison failure or of unavoidable moral cost. Which features does the hard-choice framework illuminate? For instance, does Chang’s idea that commitment can generate new reasons rather than merely rank old ones explain why the state, once committed, treated further costs as acceptable in ways that a simple cost-benefit calculus would not? Which features does it misdescribe or leave silent? In particular, does the framework erroneously presuppose a stable decision-maker with stable values, when in fact the “agent” shifted from emergency coordinators to political leadership to international observers, and when media spectacle and national identity politics may have resolved what should have remained genuinely open?

Here is the niche conceptual injection I want you to examine: the notion of “diachronic incommensurability.” The claim is that the values at stake in the initial decision—individual lives versus economic rationality—belong to a different evaluative order than those in the final extraction—collective heroism versus individual rescuer risk—and different again in the post-rescue phase—accountability versus solidarity. This implies not merely that values conflict at each moment, but that comparing the “total value” of the rescue operation across its entire temporal arc is conceptually incoherent, because the metric shifts. Does the San José episode support this stronger claim, or does it show that a single underlying value conflict—say, between deontological obligations to persons and consequentialist calculations of collective welfare—persists throughout, with only its surface manifestations changing?

Finally, state what the episode reveals about the theory itself. Is this a confirmation that hard choices can be resolved through an act of will or creative commitment that transforms the evaluative landscape? Or is it an anomaly—an instance where external political pressure and symbolic investment closed off a value conflict that the theory predicts should have remained open, thereby revealing a limitation in the framework’s account of agency? Be honest about where the mapping breaks: say exactly where the theory is silent, and do not force it to speak where it has nothing to say.
