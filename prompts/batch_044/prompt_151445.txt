Arguments comparing the ethical footprint of beef and chicken often invoke what might be called collateral crop mortality—the wild rodents, birds, and insects killed during the planting, harvesting, and storage of feed grains. Given that beef requires roughly an order of magnitude more feed per calorie of human nutrition, the inference that beef is morally worse seems to follow directly, provided we treat all sentient harm as fungible across ecological contexts. I want to examine whether this inference actually holds. Specifically, I am assuming that the wild-animal deaths caused by feed-crop agriculture are both (i) a marginal consequence of my individual consumption choice rather than an infrastructural feature of global food systems, and (ii) comparable in moral weight to the suffering experienced by the farmed animals themselves, despite lacking the sustained, intentional structure of confinement and slaughter. If both assumptions hold, then reducing beef consumption in favor of chicken should reduce net harm. But is the error in this reasoning primarily that wild-animal harm is not marginal but baseline—meaning my dietary shift merely reallocates suffering rather than eliminating it—or that comparing suffering across ecological domains commits a category mistake by ignoring differences in duration, intensity, and agency? I am trying to determine which correction, if either, actually undermines the beef-to-chicken comparison, or whether the entire framework of aggregating farmed and wild harm into a single metric is what needs to be abandoned.
