I have been testing a reconstructed Aristotelian framework for evaluating particular exchange practices, and I want to see whether its earlier judgements flow from one principle or from several that merely overlap. Its verdicts so far are these.

A citizen farmer exchanges surplus olive oil for a neighbour's surplus wheat; both goods are destined for household use, and the exchange ratio reflects relative need and labour. Verdict: just.

A citizen merchant acquires grain during abundance, stores it without alteration, and sells it during scarcity at a price far exceeding the storage cost. Verdict: unjust.

A citizen lends silver to a craftsman for buying tools; the craftsman repays exactly the principal after one year, with no surplus demanded. Verdict: just.

The same citizen lends silver to a hungry family for food during famine, demanding repayment of the principal plus an equal measure of silver. Verdict: unjust.

A citizen physician receives grain and cloth proportionate to the labour of study and cure, restoring a sick citizen to civic life. Verdict: just.

A citizen exchanges a magistracy for private gold, allowing the buyer to exercise judicial authority. Verdict: unjust.

The case not yet decided is this. A citizen-broker, acting professionally but never taking ownership, holds goods belonging to two foreign merchants who share no language. He verifies the quality of the goods without altering them, guarantees replacement should a hidden defect later appear, and takes a fixed portion of both goods as compensation. The goods are tools intended for household use; the merchants' exchange ratio is equal; no interest is charged; the merchants never meet; and the broker's gain derives solely from the occurrence of the exchange and the risk he bears, not from labour applied to the goods themselves.

Predict the verdict. Identify the two or three plausible principles that fit the earlier six judgements equally well — for instance, one centred on service to household need and avoidance of unlimited accumulation, one on proportionality between labour applied to goods and compensation received, and one on direct exchange between citizens for goods that sustain the good life. Say what each principle predicts about the broker case. Give your best-guess verdict, commit to the principle you think best reconstructs the framework, and defend that choice against the alternatives. Be explicit about which earlier verdict, if any, your preferred principle struggles to accommodate. Finally, propose one further case, not listed above, that would best discriminate between your principles, and state what each principle would predict about it.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options neutrally: choose one reconstruction, argue for it, and state where you are prepared to revise an earlier verdict if consistency demands it.
