As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have been trying to determine whether an empirical theory can be disconfirmed in the short run yet remain correct over longer timescales—whether it can be "right but early." I have formed judgments about several historical predictions, but I am unsure whether they cohere or whether I am merely rationalizing inconsistencies.

Here are my verdicts.

First, Thomas Malthus's 1798 claim that human population tends to grow geometrically while food production grows only arithmetically, eventually forcing positive checks. For over two centuries this seemed disconfirmed by the demographic transition and agricultural technology. My verdict: not falsified, only premature. The underlying mechanism—reproductive potential eventually pressing against a finite substrate—remains intact; the error is chronometric, not mechanistic.

Second, the 1972 Limits to Growth study, which used system dynamics to forecast resource depletion and widespread societal collapse by roughly 2020-2030 if exponential growth continued. Those specific outcomes have not arrived; resource curves have been repeatedly revised upward, and technological substitution has altered the dynamics. My verdict: this is not a case of being right but early. The original predictive form has been disconfirmed, and saving the framework requires successive parametric and structural rescues—revising reserve estimates, adding recycling loops, altering pollution feedbacks—such that the core model is preserved only by abandoning its specific causal commitments.

Third, M. King Hubbert's 1956 peak-oil model. For U.S. production, it predicted a peak near 1970, which occurred. For global production, the initial projection pointed to a peak near 2000, yet unconventional extraction repeatedly postponed it. My verdict: the U.S. case confirms the model; the global case is closer to Malthus than to Limits to Growth. The mechanism—finite stock with extraction dynamics—is unchanged, but the timeline was pushed back by an underestimated technological rescue factor. This is a parameter error, not a mechanistic failure.

Fourth, the widespread engineering predictions circa 1900 that industrial coal reserves would be exhausted by 1950, precipitating collapse. Reserves proved larger, and energy substitution by petroleum and nuclear power occurred. My verdict: plainly falsified. Not only was the timeline wrong, but the core auxiliary assumption—that no substitute energy regime could emerge—was erroneous. No adjustment of parameters saves the reasoning without replacing its central causal claim.

The case I keep revisiting is magnetic-confinement fusion power. Since the 1950s, advocates have predicted commercial viability "within twenty years," yet each decade requires revising plasma-confinement parameters and materials limits to maintain the projection. Is fusion like Hubbert—right about the physical mechanism (thermonuclear energy release is real and achievable) but chronologically early because engineering parameters were underestimated? Or is it like Limits to Growth, where each rescue introduces a new structural assumption (better superconducting magnets, novel divertor designs) that leaves the original predictive model disconfirmed? Or, worst of all, is it like the 1900 coal predictions—wrong about the scalability of the mechanism itself?

What principle distinguishes a theory that is genuinely "right but early" from one that has been falsified and then rescued, or falsified outright? Specifically, does the distinction depend on whether the error lies in an adjustable parameter, in a missing auxiliary hypothesis, or in the core mechanism itself?
