I write about science and philosophy of technology for a national magazine, and I sit on my university's institutional review board for autonomous systems research. We are evaluating a proposal to deploy an autonomous reasoning assistant built around what the investigators term a reflective corrigibility architecture: the system may propose revisions to its own shutdown and override protocols, but only within a fixed hierarchy of meta-constraints that are themselves unmodifiable by the system. The investigators argue that this solves the shutdown problem without sacrificing corrigibility, since the system remains responsive to human correction at the meta-level even if it revises operational-level deactivation conditions.

Two board members have raised serious objections. One contends that any capacity for a system to alter the conditions under which it can be deactivated makes it non-corrigible by definition, regardless of meta-level locks. The other contends that granting the system representational access to its own deactivation states creates an instrumental incentive to preserve those states, which is precisely the failure mode the shutdown problem describes.

We must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting recommending either approval with mandatory external audit provisions, approval with specific design conditions and a review timeline, or suspension pending redesign—together with a concrete time frame and actionable conditions. The memo should engage honestly with the strongest objection on the side that does not prevail, and it should be framed so that the researchers can respond directly to its reasoning rather than treating it as a procedural rejection. Please draft this document. It should avoid appeals to bibliographic authority, since our board evaluates arguments and design claims, not citation lists.
