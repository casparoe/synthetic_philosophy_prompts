I have been working through Hume's essay on taste in a philosophy course that applies its framework to contemporary questions about whether AI-generated images can be genuinely beautiful. During our seminars, interlocutors repeatedly invoke the proverb "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" to shut down arguments for an intersubjective standard of taste. Yet the phrase does different work each time: sometimes it denies that aesthetic properties exist independently of observers; sometimes it treats them as observer-relative in the manner of flavors or colors; sometimes it serves as a normative warning against privileging expert critics over ordinary viewers. I want to understand precisely what theses are being compressed into that single sentence, because I suspect the argument against Hume's position depends on which one is actually intended.

Could you separate the precise claims that "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" could be expressing—distinguishing, for example, ontological subjectivism, indexical relativism, a response-dependence thesis modeled on color perception, and an anti-elitist normative stance? For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, whether it is philosophically substantive and defensible, and whether it is false. Then indicate which of these readings genuinely conflict with Hume's appeals to delicacy of taste and to general rules of art as correctives for prejudice, and which ones he could accommodate without abandoning a standard. I am particularly interested in whether a hidden empirical premise about the calibration of perceptual faculties gets smuggled in when the proverb is used to reject expert judgment, and whether exposing that premise changes how much work the slogan can do. Please present the interpretations as a menu rather than selecting one, and for each say whether it actually supports the use to which it was put in our discussions—namely, blocking any inference from subjective variability to an objective or intersubjective norm.
