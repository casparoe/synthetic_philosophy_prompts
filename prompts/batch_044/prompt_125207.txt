Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 14 upper-level philosophy students that makes them feel the specific reflexive tension Popper identifies in historicism: the idea that any genuinely predictive law of large-scale historical development would, once known and acted upon, alter the very social trajectory it was meant to forecast, because human knowledge and its institutional effects are part of the system being predicted. The simulation should not be generic chaos or butterfly-effect randomness; it must stage the problem that knowledge growth is an open-ended, endogenous variable.

Structure the session as a three-round predictive governance game with explicit rules and no real-money payoffs. In Round 1, divide the group into four "Historical Institutes" (each with 3–4 students). Each institute receives an identical, artificially simple social system—say, a resource-allocation mechanism governed by two observable parameters—and is tasked with deriving and publishing a "Law of Development" that predicts the system's state after Round 3. Give them 15 minutes and a scoring rubric: accuracy of prediction earns points, but every published law also becomes public information that automatically modifies the payoff matrix of the other institutes in Rounds 2 and 3 (for example, if a law predicts centralization, other groups can preemptively decentralize, changing the parameter values). In Round 2, institutes may revise their laws based on observed deviations, but they must formally declare whether their revision is an empirical correction or a definitional retreat (e.g., reinterpreting "centralization" so that the outcome counts as centralization regardless). In Round 3, all institutes submit final forecasts; scores are calculated, but the facilitator reveals that the system's "true" dynamics included a hidden epistemic feedback loop—participants' own attempts to test or apply the laws changed the parameter weights—so that perfect prediction was structurally unavailable to anyone inside the system.

Assign roles within each institute: a Forecaster (writes the law), a Policy Advisor (recommends actions based on it), and an Observer (tracks whether other institutes are gaming the published predictions). Use a point system where correct forecasts earn tokens, but acting on one's own law in ways that alter others' conditions costs or gains tokens depending on whether the alteration was anticipated by the law's own terms.

Before naming Popper or "historicism," the rules should force participants to experience a specific coordination failure: they must commit to a predictive framework in Round 1, watch it be undermined in Round 2 not by external shock but by the group's own learning and strategic adaptation to the published framework, and then face an institutional choice in Round 2's revision phase between maintaining predictive content (and accepting failure) or retreating to a tautological or immunized formulation (and preserving formal success at the cost of emptiness).

After running the game, provide a debrief that maps each round onto Popper's argument—Round 1 to the historicist ambition of discovering directional laws; Round 2 to the reflexive problem that knowledge of a law enters the causal chain it describes; Round 3 to the retreat into unfalsifiability or the acceptance of open-ended unpredictability—specifically emphasizing the claim that the growth of scientific and social knowledge is not a deterministic process that can itself be anticipated by a deterministic theory. Be explicit about which feature of Popper's view the simulation cannot reproduce: the long-scale, evolutionary dynamics of actual scientific discovery across generations, which operate far more slowly and with more complex institutional embedding than a 75-minute classroom round. Note how the facilitator must verbally supply that temporal and institutional gap, perhaps by contrasting the students' rapid strategic adaptation with the centuries-long emergence of new physical theories that then reshaped social organization.

Also include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—likely that one or two institutes will immediately try to "game" the feedback loop by publishing deliberately vague laws, or that the Observer roles will dominate discussion and suppress the Forecaster's commitment—and suggest how to handle it (e.g., require that every revision be read aloud and classified by the group before scoring).
