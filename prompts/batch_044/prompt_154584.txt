I convene a quarterly arbitration panel for a research collective that operates at the intersection of forecasting, ethics, and cognitive science. We are repeatedly asked to resolve persistent disagreements between senior members whom we treat as epistemic peers—disagreements that rarely concern isolated propositions, but instead concern which generative model class ought to govern our predictions, and how to weight suffering against autonomy in long-horizon plans. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the common prior assumption and its standard failure modes, but modified to require what I call hyperprior transparency: peers must make explicit not only their posteriors over propositions, but their priors over competing model classes (for example, linear versus non-linear dynamics, or consequentialist versus deontological framing functions).

The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: a sequence of questions the panel can run in a thirty-minute session, with clear stopping rules. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, and flag precisely where the answer is a judgment call dressed up as procedure rather than a direct consequence of the principle. The procedure must distinguish three divergences: (i) shared model class with divergent posteriors that ought to converge once posteriors are common knowledge; (ii) divergent hyperpriors over model classes that do not dissolve with transparency; and (iii) normative framing disagreements that lie outside the scope of the common prior altogether. Where the procedure reaches a verdict of no convergence required, it should state whether the matter is referred back to the researchers, escalated to an external arbiter, or handed to a values committee—indicating in each case whether the panel chair signs off alone or the full board must agree.

Apply the procedure to three cases.

First, two epidemiologists disagree about the ten-year efficacy of a vaccination protocol; they share a statistical framework but assign different prior weights to publication-bias models versus genuine-effect models.

Second, two philosophers disagree about whether deception is permissible in a field study on altruistic decision-making; they invoke different normative framing functions.

Third, provide a case where the procedure yields an answer I will actively dislike: our funding committee must choose between a suffering-reduction programme and a capability-building programme, and the procedure classifies the disagreement as a resolvable model-class divergence—because both sides share a suffering-weighted utility framework but differ only on empirical hyperpriors about long-run feedback loops—thereby forcing a convergence recommendation that strips away the normative framing I had hoped to preserve.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements it will misclassify, where it will misfire if hyperpriors are misreported, and under what conditions the chair ought to override it despite its verdict.

Please write in British English, and give a committed response. Make definite choices about which distinctions survive the translation into practice and which are dropped, defend those choices with reasons, and do not present them as one option among many. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
