Assume Baruch Spinoza—who argued in his political writings that democracy best expresses the collective power of the multitudo and that sovereignty should never be alienated from the people—were confronted with contemporary democratic systems in which political deliberation is shaped by algorithmic recommendation engines. These systems, operated by private entities and optimized for engagement rather than truth or common interest, filter and rank the information available to citizens before collective decision-making occurs. Drawing specifically on Spinoza’s distinction between active and passive affects, his analysis of how theologians maintain domination through ignorance and fear, and his institutional proposals for distributing power so that no single will can monopolize it, predict whether he would view such algorithmic mediation as (a) a hidden form of monarchy that divides and weakens the democratic multitude by controlling the conditions of opinion-formation, or (b) a neutral or even beneficial instrument that, if subjected to democratic ownership and rational design, could help the multitudo overcome its natural susceptibility to superstition and passion. Defend your interpretation by explaining how each of Spinoza’s commitments—freedom of thought, the inseparability of democracy from the common power of citizens, and his skepticism about the rationality of the masses—would bear on the evaluation. Do not treat his view as generic liberalism; show how his particular metaphysics of power and affect determines the answer.
