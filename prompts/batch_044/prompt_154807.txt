I'm putting together the final for my grad-levle ethics seminar and I need one essay question on Taurek's argument about whether the numbers should count — not just a summary, but something that actually forces students to work with the pairwise comparison logic instead of just repeating the label seperateness of persons. I want them dropped into a modified rescue case: you can save one specific person, Alice, or you can save five strangers, but there's also a fair coin-flip that decides between saving Alice and saving the five. The question should ask whether Taurek's reasoning makes the coin-flip impermissible or even absurd compared to just saving Alice outright, and whether that result is a reductio of his view.

Please write the exam question aimed at 90 minutes for upper-level grad students, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit conceptual moves totaling 20 marks. The moves should cover: (1) why Taurek rejects aggregating the five into a collective with multiplied moral weight; (2) whether the coin-flip respects pairwise equality or smuggles in aggregation; (3) whether Taurek must be indifferent between flipping and saving Alice; (4) whether that indifference is genuinely absurd or just counterintuitive; (5) whether a threshold hybrid — aggregation only kicks in past some number — can be grafted onto his view without betraying its core anti-utilitarian premise. For each point list the marks and the typical mistake students make, like confusing pairwise comparison with straight deontology or treating expected-value reasoning as compatible with Taurek. Don't ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and don't lean on obscure secondary literature that a small model wouldn't have seen.
