I'm preparing material for a discussion circle drawn from longtime rationalist blog and forum readers, and I need a taxonomy tree—not a quiz with point values, but a branching classification—of the positions people actually hold about exceptional success and whether societies should celebrate or suppress it. The tree should span the familiar triad of American Dream celebration, Scandinavian Jantelagen suppression, and Anglophone tall-poppy syndrome, but I want it to treat material success and epistemic success—being visibly and correctly ahead of one's peers in reasoning or prediction—as distinct domains, since that distinction is live in this audience. I also want you to inject the niche idea of a reputational commons, treating visible status as partially rivalrous so that one person's exceptional recognition can deplete what is available for others, and I want that treated as a live branch option rather than background context.

Please construct the taxonomy from logical space rather than from a roster of named doctrines. The root should be a concrete case: a single individual in a community achieves outcomes far above peers through some mix of talent, luck, and structure, in either material or epistemic form. Each branch should be labeled by a specific claim that separates siblings, phrased as a judgment or question that someone could answer without knowing which ideology it points to. Include these splits: whether the outcome reflects mainly individual merit or mainly structural advantage and luck; whether the appropriate response targets the individual—reward, suppress, or cut down—or targets institutions such as access rules, reward structures, or collective decision procedures; and whether visible disparity causes harm mainly through psychological resentment among peers, through extraction or domination by the successful party, or through corruption of group judgment when one voice dominates disproportionately. The tree should yield eight to ten leaves. Named leaves must include American Dream-style unlimited proportional celebration of success, Jantelagen-style active suppression of anyone who claims specialness regardless of the source of their achievement, and tall-poppy syndrome driven specifically by peer resentment of those who rise visibly above the group. You must also include at least two unoccupied or unnamed combinations—for instance, the view that epistemic outliers should be celebrated without limit while material outliers should be institutionally flattened, or the view that all visible success is structurally illegitimate yet suppressing individuals is still wrong—and briefly diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please flag any pair of leaves that your branching logic genuinely cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap.

Write the response as continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, exactly as one writes a letter or an email, and use American English throughout. Use concrete scenarios in the branch descriptions—such as a researcher whose predictive model consistently outperforms institutional consensus but receives no citation credit or influence, versus an entrepreneur whose wealth derives partly from regulatory capture—so the instrument sorts people by what they believe about cases rather than by which slogan they prefer. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts that would require lookup; the tree should be answerable entirely from conceptual reasoning. The audience is rationalist-leaning readers who think naturally in terms of incentives, institutional design, and rivalrous goods, so the language should reflect that fluency without becoming jargon-heavy.
