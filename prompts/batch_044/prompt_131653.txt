I have been reading philosophy of mind on my own, particularly the view that phenomenal consciousness—the feeling of what it is like to experience red or pain—is not merely mysterious but literally illusory in a metaphysical sense. I want to write an exam for advanced students that cannot be answered by repeating the standard labels "illusionism" versus "eliminativism" or by summarizing well-known authors. Instead, I want the question to force students into a specific conceptual knot I have been worrying about: if phenomenal properties do not exist, then whatever generates our belief in them must be described without presupposing the very phenomenal awareness being denied. To make this concrete, I ask students to consider a hypothetical subject, S, who retains every cognitive, behavioral, and functional response associated with seeing red and feeling pain, yet has lost all introspective access to qualitative states through a precisely specified intervention. S continues to report "I see red" and "I feel pain." The exam should ask whether this case supports illusionism—because the reports are false beliefs about functional roles—or undermines it—because explaining the misinterpretation seems to require attributing to S exactly the kind of phenomenal ownership that illusionism rejects.

Please write one essay question suitable for a sixty-minute examination, together with a model answer of approximately seven hundred words and a marking scheme totaling twenty-five marks. The model answer must contain five specific moves: first, a precise statement of what illusionism denies about phenomenal properties; second, an analysis of the "mechanism of illusion" problem, especially the reflexivity worry that describing the illusion requires the awareness being denied; third, an evaluation of whether subject S resolves or exacerbates that reflexivity problem; fourth, a comparison with one non-illusionist alternative such as representationalism or non-illusionist physicalism; and fifth, a conclusion about whether illusionism can avoid collapsing into either eliminativism or property dualism.

The marking scheme should award marks for each move and must explicitly distinguish an A-level answer, which identifies the reflexivity problem and applies it rigorously to S, from a B-level answer, which describes the scenario accurately but treats the illusion as a mere perceptual error rather than a metaphysical misattribution. It should also identify the specific mistake that costs marks at the C level—namely, confusing illusionism with the claim that experience is deceptive in the ordinary sensory sense rather than in the sense that there are no intrinsic phenomenal properties to be deceived about. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the answer should be evaluable on conceptual grounds alone.
