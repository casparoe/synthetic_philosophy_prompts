I'm trying to stabilize my view on split-brain consciousness before a seminar and want a consistency audit rather than a literature review. Here is my current package:

(1) I'm a physicalist: consciousness is fully constituted by brain dynamics, with no immaterial binding substance.
(2) Split-brain patients remain a single conscious subject; the callosal cut produces a partition within one phenomenal field—like a landscape divided by an uncrossable river—not a fission into two subjects.
(3) The correct metaphysical criterion for counting subjects is counterfactual phenomenal dependency: two neural processes belong to one subject if the qualitative character of each necessarily depends on the other in nearby possible worlds. In split-brain cases this dependency survives via subcortical and anterior commissural pathways, so the criterion supports (2) despite the callosal severance.
(4) In standard forced-choice paradigms, the left and right hemispheres maintain genuinely contradictory beliefs about the same stimulus, with no mechanism for cross-hemispheric access, correction, or reconciliation.
(5) The left-hemisphere interpreter confabulates a unified autobiographical narrative; first-person reports of "feeling like one continuous person" are therefore post-hoc constructions and unreliable evidence about underlying phenomenal unity.
(6) Consciousness requires global integration within a single workspace; once the corpus callosum is cut, cross-hemispheric broadcasting is abolished, so there is no longer one workspace spanning both hemispheres—if consciousness persists post-commissurotomy, it must do so in two separate, hemispherically confined workspaces.

Do these hang together? In particular, does (4) together with (6) pull hard enough against (2) that I have to abandon either the single-subject claim or the physicalist framework? Is the tension between (5) and (3) merely apparent—because an objective counterfactual criterion doesn't need trustworthy introspection—or does it hide a deeper problem? And where does (6) actually stand relative to (3): are they incompatible, or can distributed subcortical integration satisfy both? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what commitment would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost, flag any pair that only looks incompatible, and treat this as an audit of my position, not a lecture on the split-brain literature.
