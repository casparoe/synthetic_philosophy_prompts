At a dinner party, Mara and Leo got stuck arguing about the Fischer controversy and whether it actually makes sense to hold a whole nation responsible for WWI. Mara said Fischer basically showed the German leadership actively sought a preventive war in 1914 — not just reacted to the assassination — so blaming Germany is just recognizing a specific state-level intent, not some vague collective guilt. Leo said that's wrong-headed: "holding a nation responsible" only works if there's one unified agent with one mind, but 1914 Germany was a patchwork of the Kaiser, the General Staff, diplomats, and industrialists pulling in different directions. Blaming "Germany" is like blaming a crowd for a riot when no one person started it. Mara replied that collective blame doesn't need a single brain — corporations get held liable without every board member agreeing on every harm, and states have institutional continuty that makes them responsible agents. Leo said corporate liability is legal fiction, not real moral blame, and applying it retroactivly to 1914 risks treating history like a courtroom. Mara countered that Fischer wasn't assigning personal guilt to every German citizen, but showing that Germany's decision structures made war the likely outcome, which is exactly what national responsibility means — not one mind, but a system that produces outcomes. Leo then asked: if it's just structural causation, then Russia and Austria-Hungary share that too, so singling out Germany becomes arbitrary unless you can point to specific intentional planning, which just brings you back to Fischer's evidence anyway. They asked me to referee. Can you adjudicate move by move? Specifically: does collective responsibility require a unified national will? Is the corporate analogy fair? And does Fischer's argument depend on proving intentional planning rather than just structural pressure?
