I am taking an upper-division seminar in Comparative Ethics and Buddhist Philosophy, and during our unit on karma and moral evaluation, a classmate circulated a summary they had pulled from an online philosophy forum. The summary argues that Buddhist ethics is essentially a form of long-range consequentialism mediated by karma: because actions produce fruits across lifetimes, ethical judgment requires predicting aggregate welfare effects over an indefinite temporal span, functioning like rule-utilitarianism with reincarnation serving as the feedback loop. It claims that the doctrine of cetana (intention) is frequently misunderstood as a mental-state ethics; in the Abhidharma traditions, it operates primarily as a causal filter—only intentional acts generate karmic results not because mental states possess intrinsic moral value, but because they are the necessary conditions for outcomes to attach to an agent—so that Buddhism ultimately judges actions by their results rather than motives. The summary further contends that the Mahayana bodhisattva ideal resolves the tension between self-interest and altruism by treating personal liberation (nirvana) as subordinate to the maximization of collective welfare across all sentient beings, essentially reconceiving compassion as a universal utility function. Finally, it suggests that the Madhyamaka critique of svabhava (inherent existence) reinforces this reading by denying that mental states or intentions have any non-instrumental value independent of their causal contribution to suffering or happiness. I find this framing compelling, but I worry it flattens distinctions our instructor has emphasized—particularly regarding whether cetanā should be understood merely as a causal condition for karmic fruition or as the primary locus of ethical evaluation, and whether the bodhisattva's vow can genuinely be captured by an aggregative optimization framework. Could you audit this summary? Please go claim by claim: which parts align with standard scholarly interpretations, which are common simplifications that obscure important distinctions, and which are misrepresentations that a specialist in Buddhist ethics or a defender of the tradition would explicitly reject? I am especially interested in whether the conclusion—that Buddhist ethics is functionally equivalent to a reincarnation-augmented rule-utilitarianism—actually survives once the errors and simplifications are corrected, or whether the corrected view pulls in a different direction altogether.
