I have been revisiting the literature on judgment aggregation for a graduate seminar I am teaching on collective rationality, and I find myself puzzled by what seems to be an unnecessary anxiety surrounding the so-called discursive dilemma. As I understand the structure of the problem, it arises only when a group applies majority rule separately to logically interrelated propositions—say, P, Q, and P-and-Q—thereby allowing the collective to affirm each premise individually while denying their conjunction, which yields an inconsistent judgment set. Now, it strikes me that this difficulty is entirely an artifact of employing a conclusion-based aggregation procedure. If the group instead adopts a premise-based rule—voting exclusively on the premises and then deriving all further judgments through strict logical entailment—consistency is guaranteed, since the logical closure of any individually consistent premise-set remains consistent.

Given this, I am struggling to see why philosophers continue to treat the discursive dilemma as a deep obstacle to collective rationality rather than as a straightforward argument for always using premise-based aggregation in institutional design. Is there some hidden structural cost—perhaps in judicial panels that must evaluate nested conditionals, or in scientific committees assessing interdependent theoretical claims—that prevents premise-based rules from being applied universally? Or does the persistence of the problem reflect a failure to recognize that collective consistency should always be purchased by restricting direct democratic input on conclusions, even when the majority would reject the derived outcome? I would welcome clarification on whether there are genuinely intractable cases, involving complex logical architectures, where premise-based aggregation itself collapses into inconsistency or violates conditions we reasonably demand of collective decision-making.
