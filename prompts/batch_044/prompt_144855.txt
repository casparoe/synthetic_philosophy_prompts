Here is my philosophical profile on rational belief and the two paradoxes, and I want an audit of whether the package hangs together.

1. I hold a high-threshold probabilist account: rational belief that p requires my credence in p to exceed about 0.99, and reaching that level is sufficient for belief.
2. I think rational belief ought to be globally consistent: if I recognize that some set of my beliefs jointly entails a contradiction, rationality requires me to revise at least one.
3. I resolve the lottery paradox by rejecting closure for belief: in a fair 1000-ticket lottery I can rationally believe of each ticket that it will lose, and I can also rationally believe that some ticket will win, without this being irrational.
4. I deny that belief has a sharp boundary; belief is just high credence, and there is no precise point—contextual or otherwise—at which credence becomes belief.
5. I treat the preface paradox as structurally identical to the lottery: an author can rationally believe each specific claim in her book is correct while also rationally believing the book contains at least one error.
6. I think there is a meaningful evidential asymmetry between the two cases—inductive meta-evidence about human fallibility supports the aggregate belief in the preface, while symmetric probabilities support the specific beliefs in the lottery—even though I apply the same closure-denying resolution to both.

Go through these commitments pair by pair. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank them by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Identify at least one pair that looks like it should conflict but actually dissolves on inspection. Finally, tell me which single change to this list would eliminate the most tension overall, and whether making that change is worth what it gives up. Do not lecture me on the paradoxes generally; keep the deliverable an audit of this specific set.
