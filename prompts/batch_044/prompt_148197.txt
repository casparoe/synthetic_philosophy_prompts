I'm trying to articulate when a system genuinely exhibits competence without comprehension, using my reactions to concrete cases rather than theoretical commitments. Here are my verdicts.

First, the clear positives. Darwinian natural selection producing the vertebrate eye: functional architecture of extraordinary precision, with no agent anywhere in the chain possessing an understanding of optics or physiology. I count this as the paradigm. A Turing-style universal computing machine deriving correct arithmetic results: procedural accuracy with zero grasp of number. Also yes. A termite colony collectively regulating mound temperature through purely local behavioral rules: distributed functional success, with no individual termite comprehending thermodynamics. Yes, structurally analogous to the evolutionary case.

Now the negative and ambiguous ones. A chess grandmaster who plays a brilliant move she cannot verbally justify, describing the choice only as feeling right: I do not count this as competence without comprehension. She possesses understanding—tacit, embodied, but real—and the absence is only of explicit articulation, not of comprehension itself. By contrast, a large language model generating a novel, conceptually coherent argument about causal inference strikes me as belonging to the family: it displays discursive competence with no unified mental representation behind it, even though its output mimics the form of reasoned understanding. The two cases look parallel—both yield correct, sophisticated output without explicit reasoning available to inspection—yet I want to classify them differently.

Then there are derivative and developmental cases. A medical resident who applies a black-box diagnostic algorithm correctly, though she cannot follow its internal reasoning: her clinical judgment is competent, but the comprehension seems to reside in the tool's designers rather than in her or in the system as a whole. I think this counts, but as a borrowed or hybrid form. Human language acquisition in children troubles me: the learning mechanism operates without explicit grammatical knowledge, yet the result is a speaker who eventually comprehends syntax and meaning. I keep going back and forth on whether the mechanism's blindness makes this a case of competence without comprehension, or whether the emergence of comprehension at the endpoint dissolves the distinction.

If there is one principle—or a small family of principles—that captures these verdicts, I want it stated precisely, with the specific case that motivates each clause. I also want its predictions for a case I have not listed: a decentralized autonomous energy grid governed by smart-contract consensus algorithms that continuously optimize load distribution, where no node, programmer, or user understands the global thermodynamic dynamics, and where improvement occurs without any evolutionary selection mechanism—what I might call synthetic competence without evolutionary selection. Most importantly, I want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving them all requires an unprincipled complexity.

If this framing rests on a confused premise—for example, if the distinction between tacit human understanding and mechanical competence collapses, or if extracting a unified principle is the wrong task altogether—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the reasoning on its own terms.
