I am a retired comparative physiologist and field ecologist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy, especially debates over wild animal suffering and the ethics of ecological intervention. I find myself with the following intuitive verdicts about whether specific forms of habitat alteration reduce or increase net suffering in wild populations, and I want to know whether they cohere around a single principle.

Case 1 — Drained seasonal wetland: A temporary wetland is drained for agriculture. This eliminates a breeding site for mosquitoes and for amphibians that suffer extremely high larval mortality and heavy parasitic infection. The adult amphibians that survive elsewhere have somewhat more space, but the total number of individuals born plummets. My verdict: net suffering is likely reduced, because the averted lives of high-suffering juveniles dominate the calculus.

Case 2 — Old-growth fragmentation: A continuous old-growth forest is fragmented by roads, isolating populations of large, long-lived mammals with low reproductive rates and complex social bonds. Per-capita resource availability falls, social structures break down, and stress-related pathologies—elevated allostatic load, reduced immune function, and prolonged hypercortisolism—become common among survivors, though absolute population numbers drop only modestly. My verdict: net suffering probably increases, despite fewer individuals, because the intensity and duration of suffering per survivor rises sharply.

Case 3 — Grassland patch reduction: A grassland is divided into smaller, isolated patches. Edge effects increase predation exposure and thermal stress for small rodents. Population density in remaining patches initially spikes, then crashes. There are far fewer rodents in total, but those remaining show chronic hypervigilance, injury, and starvation at the margins. I am genuinely torn: the reduction in births seems large, but the per-capita suffering of survivors seems severe and prolonged. I lean slightly toward net suffering being unchanged or slightly increased.

Case 4 — Invasive plant lowering carrying capacity: An invasive shrub lowers the carrying capacity for native herbivores, reducing their population by roughly half. At the same time, the shrub's dense structure provides cover that reduces predation risk and the associated chronic fear responses in the remaining herbivores. Starvation is somewhat more common, but predation-related injury and terror are markedly lower. My verdict: net suffering likely decreases, because the reduction in intense, acute suffering from predation outweighs the increase in slower, lower-intensity suffering from hunger.

Case 5 — Managed fire mosaic: A controlled burn kills many individuals instantly in the burn zone but creates a long-term mosaic of successional stages. The post-fire landscape supports lower densities of long-lived, K-selected species with lower reproductive turnover and, I assume, more stable, lower-stress lives, while the high-turnover, high-mortality r-selected populations that previously dominated are permanently suppressed. My verdict: despite the immediate deaths, long-run net suffering is probably lower, analogous to reducing the number of short, painful lives in a high-turnover system.
