I'm writing for a seminar on Augustine's ethics, and I've become frustrated by the long-standing interpretive stalemate over his treatises on lying. One group of readers insists that Augustine condemns every voluntary divergence between what the speaker believes and what the speaker says, treating mental reservation or strategic silence as equally corrupt because they violate the heart's orientation toward divine truth; another group points to his approval of hidden priests and silent suffering under persecution to argue that he distinguishes false utterance from mere withholding of speech, allowing omission when harm would result. Both sides work from the same texts and historical context, yet neither has moved in decades. Is this disagreement substantive—perhaps they differ on whether Augustine conceives of truth primarily as an ontological relation between mind and reality or as a communicative virtue governing speech—or is it verbal, with each camp using "lie" for a different phenomenon? Might a hidden empirical question about early Christian rhetorical practice be doing the work, or is the deadlock purely conceptual? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, supported by what each side actually emphasizes in his arguments, together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction, textual observation, or reframing could advance it, or whether we should expect a permanent impasse.
