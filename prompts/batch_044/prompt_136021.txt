I am a retired biophysicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have worked through philosophy of mind with the same attention to protocol that I once gave to experimental design. I now serve on the Methodology and Ethics Review Panel of the Meridian Institute for Consciousness Studies, and I must circulate a brief opinion before Thursday's meeting. Our center is preparing a funding call for empirical studies that rely on first-person verbal report—phenomenological interviews, experience-sampling protocols, and related techniques. The panel is divided. One faction, committed to Dennett's heterophenomenological method, insists that every subjective report must be treated as an intentional text—a description of what the subject believes about her experience—rather than as a transparent window onto pre-reflective consciousness. The opposing faction, drawing on neurophenomenological and micro-phenomenological traditions, argues that imposing that interpretive filter uniformly will systematically exclude the very phenomenon we seek: the structure of lived experience prior to conceptual framing.

To break the deadlock, a colleague has proposed a hybrid protocol that I find promising but epistemically precarious. Call it staged reflexive heterophenomenology. In Phase One, investigators conduct standard heterophenomenological interviews, treating every utterance as a text requiring neutral interpretation. In Phase Two, subjects are trained in a minimal meta-cognitive taxonomy and asked to annotate their own Phase-One transcripts, tagging each claim as directly present, inferential reconstruction, or uncertain/confabulatory—without being permitted to correct the original content. In Phase Three, those annotations are themselves subjected to heterophenomenological interpretation, yielding a layered dataset in which no report claims unmediated authority, yet the temporal and categorical architecture of the subject's self-interpretation remains available for analysis.

The chair and two senior members reject this outright. They argue that Phase Two inevitably reintroduces a Cartesian theater: asking subjects to inspect and classify their own mental acts from a privileged vantage means the annotations will be treated by researchers as direct access despite the methodological disclaimer. They recommend that we reject all proposals containing any Phase-Two reflexive layer and fund only studies that maintain strict heterophenomenological neutrality throughout.

My view is that we should approve the staged protocol, but solely for a pilot cycle with a hard sunset clause. Any study using it must include an independent audit comparing its annotations against behavioral and neural correlates; if the audit fails to demonstrate added predictive value within eighteen months, the protocol is withdrawn automatically. This respects the chair's strongest objection—the risk of smuggling in first-person privilege—while allowing us to test whether reflexive layering improves predictive rigor rather than merely satisfying philosophical nostalgia.

Please draft the formal memo I will circulate to the panel. It should recommend this pilot-approval-with-sunset-and-audit course of action, specify the eighteen-month evaluation window and the audit criteria, and include an honest, serious treatment of the chair's objection that Phase Two collapses back into Cartesian transparency. The memo should be addressed to the panel, written in a formal but not bureaucratically dead tone, and should avoid requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. It should treat heterophenomenology as an attractive but genuinely controversial method rather than as settled doctrine. Keep it concise—no more than about four hundred words—so members can read it before Thursday.
