I'm designing a classification exercise for an advanced ethics seminar on the foundations of obligation, and I've modified Glaucon's ring of Gyges to make the diagnostic sharper: in our version, the ring renders the wearer invisible to all others and also erases their own memory of any unjust act they commit, so that neither external punishment nor internal guilt, self-reproach, or retrospective regret can operate—unless they arise independently of any knowledge that one acted wrongly. The question for the module is not merely whether justice is rational, but which metaethical structure survives when every instrumental and memorial sanction is stripped away. I want a decision tree—not an essay—that sorts a student into one of six positions on why one should be moral, without the student recognizing the labels in advance.

Please construct a branching instrument of no more than five diagnostic nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim or scenario about this modified ring case that a student can answer without knowing metaethical taxonomy. The root should ask whether moral reasons survive the total removal of both detection and self-knowledge; subsequent branches should separate internal sanctions that do not depend on memory (e.g., a direct affective response to harming) from those that do, then distinguish whether any surviving reason is valued only for its contribution to the agent's flourishing, whether it applies categorically regardless of desire, and whether the concept of justice retains content when no observer could ever apply it. Map the answers onto these six leaves: hard egoism (morality collapses entirely without enforcement or memory); reputation-contractualism (morality is rational only in visible contexts but binds there); internalist-contractualism (moral commitments are valued for constituting the kind of agent one wants to be, even invisibly); virtue-constitutivism (justice is partly constitutive of human flourishing regardless of payoff); transcendental moral realism (moral reasons apply categorically, independent of any psychological state); and moral error theory (there is no fact of the matter, so the ring reveals only habit, not failure). Build the tree from the logical space of answers first—list the combinations the branches allow—then place the six positions in that grid, noting any logically possible combination that remains unoccupied and diagnosing whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves that share every branch answer and therefore cannot be separated by this instrument alone (for instance, if two positions differ only in metaphysical grounding that the questions never reach).

The audience is philosophy undergraduates familiar with Republic II but not with technical metaethics; avoid framing that depends on recognizing niche terms like "moral fetishism" or "the amoralist's challenge," and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars. Keep every node a question the student could answer from intuition and the modified scenario alone, and include a brief scoring key that translates answer paths into the named positions without using those names in the questions themselves.
