I defend a strict variant of modal realism: concrete possible worlds are maximally spatiotemporally isolated totalities that share no parts whatsoever—neither concrete individuals nor abstract universals—so that cross-world relations are purely descriptive rather than ontological. Within this framework, counterpart relations are not grounded in qualitative resemblance but in functional isomorphism under a canonical descriptive scheme, and the predicate actual is purely indexical, carrying no metaphysical privilege. From these commitments I argue that modal knowledge is structurally analogous to theoretical knowledge of distant physical regions: we infer other worlds from patterns manifest in ours, without requiring causal contact. I want an honest map of what would genuinely defeat this position—not merely the familiar charge of ontological extravagance, but specifically: whether the stronger isolation thesis inadvertently collapses the view into an ersatzist account of properties across worlds; whether functional-isomorphism counterpart theory can answer the Humphrey-style objection without importing essentialist premises I have rejected; whether analogizing modal knowledge to scientific theoretical inference exposes the view to a pessimistic meta-induction about the reality of unobservable entities; and whether stripping actuality of metaphysical weight undermines the normative significance of modal reasoning about what we ought to do. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those that defenders of this package should themselves concede from those they could consistently dismiss. Where relevant, indicate what observations, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would be decisive rather than cosmetic. Finally, state how confident you are in each of your assessments, and single out the point about which you are least certain.
