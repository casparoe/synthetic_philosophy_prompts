I have been working through a philosophical idea on my own that I first encountered in a popular essay: the so-called procreation asymmetry. The claim, as I understand it, is that failing to create a person who would have lived a happy life is not a moral wrong, whereas creating a person who will live an unhappy life is a moral wrong, and that this asymmetry undermines any general duty to reproduce for the sake of the potential child's welfare. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to see whether the idea holds up, and I found myself wondering how a thinker like Thomas Malthus—who was deeply concerned with population, resource limits, and the moral implications of bringing new lives into conditions of scarcity—would have responded to this precise framing. Not his well-known worries about overpopulation in general, but specifically: Would he accept the asymmetry's core distinction between the harm of existence and the non-deprivation of non-existence? Given his criticisms of the Poor Laws and his belief that population growth tends to outstrip subsistence, would he see voluntary childlessness as morally required rather than merely prudential? Or would he reject the asymmetry altogether because his framework treated aggregate welfare and social stability as primary, making the moral status of any particular potential life derivative rather than fundamental? I am especially interested in whether Malthus's distinction between preventive and positive checks maps onto the asymmetry's logic—for instance, whether delaying marriage to avoid suffering aligns with not creating unhappy people, while the absence of an obligation to create happy people aligns with his skepticism about state-mandated welfare—and whether there is a tension between his concern for total suffering and the asymmetry's refusal to count missing pleasures as losses.
