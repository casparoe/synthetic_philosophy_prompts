Consider the strong interpretive claim about Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation: that the three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—are not complementary tools but structurally incompatible existential stances. On this reading, a culture can remain vital only by letting one mode dominate and actively suppressing the other two; attempting to balance all three does not yield synthesis but produces a kind of historical paralysis or schizophrenia, and the required suppression is an intentional, healthy forgetting rather than mere neglect. Take this exclusivity-and-suppression thesis as the position to be tested.

What would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which passages or conceptual structures in the essay undermine mutual exclusivity; which historical or psychological examples suggest that two modes can coexist without paralysis; whether the view collapses into a rival interpretation (for instance, that the modes are sequential phases of one life-process rather than competing stances); and whether the position has been insulated by ad hoc qualifications—say, redefining every apparent coexistence as covert domination by a single mode—such that it risks unfalsifiability. For each defeater, say whether a committed defender of the thesis would concede it as genuinely damaging or dismiss it, and identify the result that would be most decisive.
