I have recently encountered Simpson's paradox in a professional setting involving aggregated outcome statistics, and the experience has made me revisit William Golding's Lord of the Flies, which I am now rereading with a book group. The novel is traditionally read as an aggregate philosophical claim about human nature: stripped of adult institutions, boys as a collective regress to savagery. But Simpson's paradox warns that a trend present in every subgroup can reverse, or disappear, when groups are merged, and that inferring subgroup behaviour from aggregate data is an ecological fallacy. Read the novel through this statistical-philosophical lens. Disaggregate the boys by leadership structure — Ralph's participatory faction versus Jack's hierarchical tribe — and by prior social conditioning rather than treating them as a single population. Does the correlation between isolation from authority and moral collapse hold within each subgroup, or does it reverse? Which scenes — the decay of the conch, Simon's death, Piggy's murder, the naval officer's arrival — illuminate or complicate this disaggregation? I want a reading that treats both the novel's literary architecture and the logic of aggregation seriously: does Golding suppress subgroup differences to sustain an aggregate thesis about "the boys", or does the text itself provide the data that undermines its own received interpretation?
