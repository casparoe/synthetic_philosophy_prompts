Following a recent change in my sleep medication, I have begun experiencing frequent false awakenings and vivid hypnopompic imagery, and I have become preoccupied with a philosophical question that now feels personally urgent: which of these states are genuinely experiential, and which are merely reconstructed upon waking?

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, which I cannot fully reconcile. When I wake with a detailed first-person memory of arguing with a deceased relative in a specific, unfamiliar room, I judge that I genuinely experienced that scene, despite its non-occurrence. When my partner reports that I spoke coherent, grammatically correct sentences about a fire truck during REM sleep, yet I wake with no memory, no emotional residue, and no sense of having been present, I do not count that as an experience of a fire truck; it strikes me as verbal behavior lacking phenomenal presence. In a false awakening, I seem to experience getting out of bed, brushing my teeth, and walking to the kitchen, only to realize moments later that I am still lying in bed; I judge that I genuinely experienced a false situation, not that I inferred it retrospectively. When I wake with only a diffuse, objectless anxiety and then construct a narrative about being chased through a childhood building, I judge that the anxiety may have been experienced, but the chase narrative was confabulated to explain the affect; there was no experience of being chased. In a lucid dream in which I observe events from a detached, third-person vantage without any bodily sensation, I still judge that an experience is occurring, though its structure is unlike waking perception. Finally, consider a hypothetical case of retroactive insertion: a sleeper is interrupted during REM and immediately given a false verbal cue that they were dreaming of water; they incorporate it into their report, but if the cue is withheld they report nothing. I feel that in the adoption case there was no prior experience of water, only a post-hoc fabrication, whereas if they reject the cue and report fire, then fire was genuinely experienced.

Is there a single, coherent principle that captures all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause addresses which case, and predict its verdict for an unlisted scenario: a split-brain patient in whom the left hemisphere, upon waking, reports one coherent dream narrative, while the right hemisphere, via left-hand writing, reports a contradictory narrative with no overlap. If no principle accommodates every verdict, which ones must be abandoned, and what is the simplest coherent account that remains? I ask for conceptual analysis only—no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists are needed.
