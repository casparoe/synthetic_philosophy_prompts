I sit on the ontology review board for the Mid-Atlantic Heritage Consortium -- we vote on how disputed artifacts get entered in the shared catalog when curators can't agree on what kind of thing they're dealing with. We've got case 447-B, which is genuinely weird. Two ceramic vessels from the 1920s, call them Vessel A and Vessel B, are physically identical in every observable respect: same dimensions, same glaze chemistry, same wear patterns, same weight distribution. But each one was manufactured with a sealed internal cavity that was filled and then permanently closed. We can't scan inside without destroying the object, and the original maker's notes were lost in a flood. The only clue is some ambiguous correspondence suggesting that in one vessel the cavity holds a token marking it as part of a distributed ritual set -- meaning A and B might actually be nodes of one single distributed artifact spread across two locations -- while in the other vessel the token marks it as an independent replica made for seperate use. The correspondence is genuinely unclear, and half the board thinks we're just projecting patterns onto noise.

The philosophical knot is whether the ontology of these things -- are they two objects, or parts of one distributed object -- can depend on information that is permanently inaccessible to us. One faction says ontology tracks what's really there regardless of our epistemic limits, so we should preserve both possibilities in the database and link them as a potential distributed unit with appropriate conditions. The other faction says ontology for institutional purposes is partly constituted by what we can identify, verify, and administrate: loans, insurance, repatriation, and conservation all depend on observable properties, so cataloging them as a linked distributed entity when we can't verify it risks fracturing practical accountability for metaphysical speculation. There's also the worry that if we treat them as seperate items and they actually are parts of one thing, we've administratively broken an ontological unit just for convenience.

Can you draft the board's decision memo for circulation before Thursday? We need a concrete recommendation -- catalog them as independent entries, catalog them as a linked distributed set with specific conditions, or suspend classification with a time-bound review trigger -- plus reasoning that actually engages the ontology issue rather than just saying "we don't know." Be honest about the strongest argument for the side you're rejecting, and explain exactly why it falls short or how you've built its concern into the conditions. Don't list philosophers or ask for reading lists; write it so curators who don't care about metaphysics can still see what they're supposed to do with the vessels, and include a clear time frame if the recommendation involves monitoring or review.
