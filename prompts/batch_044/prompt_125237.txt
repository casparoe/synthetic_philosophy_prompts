I'm a junior serving on the three-person editorial board of The Synthesis, our high school's interdisciplinary journal, and our faculty advisor—who studied epistemology in graduate school—has asked me to formalize how we evaluate submissions that rest on testimony. We keep encountering essays that cite an expert's oral report of an unreplicable experiment, or that rely on a widely circulated but anonymously authored historical document, and the board argues about whether accepting these claims requires us to independently verify the speaker's reliability and perceptual access, or whether we can be justified in believing the content directly from the word itself. She framed the dispute for me in terms of testimony reductionism, which treats a hearer's justification as ultimately grounded in her own reasoning about the speaker's credibility and in her direct grasp of the utterance, versus anti-reductionism, which allows that the hearer acquires justification for p straight from the speaker's assertion without that intermediate inference being necessary. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules—that our board can run on any submitted claim that depends on testimony, specifying in sequence: what evidential-pedigree questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure issues a verdict of "accept as testimony-justified," "reject for lack of independent corroboration," or "refer to board judgment because the issue is one of values or risk tolerance rather than fact," and where it must halt because judgment cannot be eliminated.

The procedure should be designed for a non-specialist board meeting twice a month with limited time, so each step needs a clear test that does not require philosophical training to apply, but it must preserve the actual logical difference between the two positions rather than collapsing them into vague "trust your source" advice. Please include worked applications to two hard cases: first, a claim based on a single expert's oral report of an unreplicable laboratory result, and second, a claim based on an anonymous historical document whose authorship and institutional origin are unknown. One of these applications must yield an outcome that should feel counterintuitive—specifically, a divergence where the anti-reductionist path permits acceptance but the reductionist path demands rejection, and the procedure must flag exactly at which step the paths split and why. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, particularly whether the speaker's social position, institutional authority, or demographic identity should enter the reductionist reliability calculation, and mark that gap clearly as a value judgment that the board must make without procedural cover.
