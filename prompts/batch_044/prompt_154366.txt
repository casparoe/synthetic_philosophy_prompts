I'm teaching a seminar on phil of science and we got into this thing about whether Darwin "really" discovered natural selection or if Wallace was robbed, and whether either of them mattered since someone would have figured it out in like five years anyway. I want my students to think about this not just as gossip but as testing a real philosophical position.

So here's what I'm thinking: take the actual 1858 joint presentation at the Linnean Society and Darwin's subsequent rush to publish Origin, and analyze the whole episode through the lens of "discovery inevitability" — but not the vague pop-science version. I mean the distinction between weak inevitability (the observational data and theoretical tools were in the air, so some form of evolutionary theory was coming) and strong inevitability (the specific mechanism of natural selection was structurally determined by the problem-space of Malthusian competition combined with variation). Map out who had access to what conceptual resources — Darwin with his years of barnacle taxonomy and Malthus, Wallace with his biogeographic fieldwork in the Malay Archipelago — and then ask: does the fact that they arrived at essentially the same mechanism support strong inevitability, or does the divergence in their framing (Wallace's more selectionist, Darwin's more gradualist and broader) show that the "discovery" was actually several different theoretical constructions?

Then flip it: what does this case do to the inevitability thesis? If discoveries are inevitable, does it diminish Darwin's moral or epistemic credit? And here's the part I keep coming back to — there's this idea I ran across (maybe from a grad student, I forget) about "social inevitability" versus "epistemic inevitability." Social inevitability says it doesn't matter who made the discovery because the scientific community was structured to recognize and reward it regardless. Epistemic inevitability says the idea itself was going to emerge regardless of social structures. Use that distinction. Does the Linnean Society episode — where Lyell and Hooker arranged the joint reading specificly to protect Darwin's priority while still acknowledging Wallace — show that social structures actually determine whose name gets attached, even if the idea was epistemically inevitable? Or does it show the opposite?

Don't just tell me Darwin was a genius and Wallace got unlucky. I want a disciplined mapping: what are the actual options and payoffs in terms of scientific credit, who were the players (Lyell, Hooker, the Society itself?), and then an honest account of where the inevitability model explains the convergence and where it breaks down. Does the model miss the role of personal temperament — Darwin's caution versus Wallace's boldness in sending the essay? And finally: if we accept some form of inevitability, does that actually answer whether it matters who made the discovery, or is that a seperate normative question?

Make sure you don't turn this into a biography. The case is there to test the theory, and the theory should illuminate the case without forcing it. If there's something the inevitability framework is silent on — like whether Wallace's spiritualist leanings affected how he framed selection — say so rather than inventing an answer.
