A junior colleague describes a dilemma that I want to classify correctly before advising her. At our university, either researcher may initiate a purchase order for a particular specialized instrument, but once initiated, both the initiator and any competing party who has also opened an order are assessed a non-refundable administrative fee—roughly fifteen percent of the instrument’s price—regardless of who ultimately completes the acquisition. The instrument is awarded to whichever party finalizes the paperwork first; the other forfeits the fee and receives nothing. Its research value to either laboratory is modest—clearly less than the fee already paid, and far less than the total cost required to win. Both my colleague and her peer have already initiated orders and paid the fee. She now argues that she must complete the purchase, since withdrawing guarantees a loss of institutional funds with nothing to show for it, whereas success at least secures the instrument; her peer is reasoning identically. I want to advise her whether this justification is a sound strategic response to the institutional rules, or whether it rests on a confusion in practical reasoning about prior expenditures. Specifically, should the already-incurred fee be treated as irrelevant to her present decision, and does the fact that both parties pay regardless of outcome alter the philosophical analysis of whether she is rationally committed to continue?
