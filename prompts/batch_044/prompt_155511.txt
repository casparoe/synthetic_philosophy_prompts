I have no formal training in philosophy—I work as a software engineer—but I have been reading some philosophy of language and social ontology on my own, and I recently found myself unable to referee a disagreement that arose during a discussion with two colleagues in my open-source working group. The subject was whether the unwritten community norm that one should not hard-fork a widely used library unless the original maintainers have clearly abandoned it can properly be analyzed as a Lewisian convention.

Colleague A argued that the norm fits Lewis’s framework well: developers generally prefer ecosystem compatibility to fragmentation, each individual prefers to conform if others do, and the salience of the original repository—the focal point, in Lewis’s terms—solves the coordination problem without any explicit contract. She extended this to the broader shift she has observed in machine-learning tooling, where MIT-style permissive licensing has largely displaced copyleft licenses; in her view, this is the same mechanism at work, because permissive licenses minimize transaction costs and, once adopted by a few high-profile projects, became the obvious default that everyone could see everyone else seeing.

Colleague B resisted in three moves. First, he claimed that for Lewis a convention must be arbitrary in principle—there must be some genuinely available alternative regularity that could have solved the same coordination problem—but in decentralized software development the “no unnecessary fork” pattern is not arbitrary; it is structurally compelled by network effects and dependency graphs, so labeling it conventional misdescribes the causal mechanism. Second, he said Lewis’s analysis presupposes common knowledge of rationality and preferences among participants, yet our community is globally distributed, largely anonymous, and constantly churning in membership, which undermines the mutual awareness the model seems to require. Third, he argued that the licensing shift was not emergent coordination but strategic capture by large corporate sponsors who prefer permissive terms for commercial integration, which suggests the outcome tracks power and institutional interest rather than a shared preference for compatibility.

Colleague A replied that B was treating Lewis’s conditions as stricter than necessary: structural incentives can reinforce a convention without reducing it to those incentives, and anonymity does not preclude common knowledge of behavioral patterns, since we all observe the same GitHub contribution graphs, dependency trees, and star counts. She also conceded that corporate actors may have influenced the licensing landscape, but insisted that individual developers still face a coordination problem about which license to adopt, and that focal-point dynamics operate precisely at that level of choice.

They then turned to me and asked whether B’s three objections actually defeat the Lewisian analysis, or whether A is right that they merely complicate it without breaking it. Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point? Specifically: is the anti-forking norm genuinely arbitrary in the relevant sense, or does network structure make it inevitable? Does distributed anonymity genuinely undermine the common-knowledge requirement, and if so, how damaging is that? And can strategic corporate influence coexist with convention formation, or does it show that the Lewisian explanation is the wrong one altogether? Beyond those specific points, is there something important about how norms evolve in decentralized technical communities—perhaps involving implicit signaling through version-control metadata or dependency inheritance—that neither of them is capturing?
