I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history—eight students, one ninety-minute session. I want an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the bootstrap problem in source criticism about the distant past: specifically, the reflexive moment when the critical criteria applied to testimony are themselves derived from the very testimony being evaluated, so that rigorous, consistent application threatens to erase the evidential base altogether. Design a complete exercise—roles and materials for the group, a sequence of decision rounds, and a symbolic-point payoff scheme that requires no real money—in which students must collectively construct a narrative about a fictional but historically plausible event (the destruction of a provincial archive in 312 BCE) using sealed source cards. The rules should require that, before any card is admitted into the shared timeline, the group applies one critical filter drawn from cards already admitted; over successive rounds these filters should turn back upon earlier admissions, forcing contradictions and removals. The incentives should reward coherence and penalize contradiction, so that participants experience the collapse of their own constructed narrative before anyone names the problem. Include a structured debrief that maps each round onto a corresponding stage in source criticism from Herodotus through Thucydides to modern archival theory, identifies the predictable moment when students invoke an unexamined “foundational” source as an exemption, explains why that exemption is structurally necessary, and is frank about which feature of actual historical distance—the material absence of the archive itself—the simulation cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally. Do not include a request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand entirely on the internal logic of the activity.
