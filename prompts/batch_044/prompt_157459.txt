Two philosophers of science, Maya and Leo, are debating whether interventionist accounts of causation genuinely dispense with counterfactual metaphysics or merely conceal it within formal machinery. I want you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

Maya opens: interventionism, in the tradition of Pearl and Woodward, is superior because it grounds causation in manipulable structural relationships. To ask whether smoking causes cancer is to ask whether an intervention on smoking changes cancer rates in the model. We do not need to rank the similarity of possible worlds, as Lewisian counterfactual theories require; we need only check local stability under an intervention.

Leo responds: but the do-operator is defined counterfactually. To apply do(X = x) is precisely to ask what the system would be like if X were set to x by an external intervention that breaks its normal causes. That is not empirical observation; it is a modal comparison, just restricted to a graph. Interventionism has not eliminated counterfactual dependence; it has localised and formalised it.

Maya: localisation is the achievement, not a disguise. The comparison is constrained by the causal structure, not by global intuitions about world-similarity. We compute responses within the model, not by weighing distant worlds.

Leo: yet computing the response requires knowing the right graph, and selecting variables is theory-laden. More importantly, consider omissions. My failure to water the plant caused it to die. There is no action performed, only an absence. Counterfactual analysis handles this naturally: had I watered it, it would have lived. Interventionism must construct a binary watering variable and treat its value of zero as an observed state, then compute do(watering = 1). But this invents the intervention post hoc. Worse, take a structural omission in social causation—say, a regulator's failure to inspect, where performing the inspection might require dissolving the institutional role itself. Here a counterfactual judgment seems available—if inspection had occurred, harm would have been averted—yet there is no coherent intervention variable without altering the very system being analysed. So interventionism either excludes legitimate causal claims or evaluates them via the hidden counterfactual judgments it claims to avoid.

Maya: if there is no possible intervention, there is no identifiable causal mechanism. Those claims may be rhetorically useful, but they are causally defective. The cost is a virtue: it filters out spurious modal speculation.

Leo: that proves too much. Many accepted causal explanations in history and institutional analysis depend on omissions for which direct intervention is conceptually impossible. Rejecting them all seems like a methodological straitjacket, not a clarification.

Please go through the exchange and say who is right at each stage—on the nature of the do-operator, on omissions, and on the institutional case. Say also what both are missing, perhaps about whether the dispute concerns metaphysics or explanatory practice. Write in British English, in no more than 350 words, and do not include citations or a reading list.
