While preparing the syllabus for an advanced seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, I have become dissatisfied with the usual functionalism-versus-biocentrism framing; it feels over-rehearsed and fails to capture the genuinely puzzling cases that now arise in distributed and transient computing architectures. I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps—the Architectural Integrity Camp and the Scalar Capacity Camp—concerning whether and why a specific class of marginal computations might matter morally. The case at the center should be a hypothetical but technically coherent computation: a transient, distributed process that achieves, for roughly two hundred milliseconds across a non-persistent mesh of photonic nodes, an integrated-information profile and behavioral flexibility comparable to a mammalian cortex, but which lacks any enduring storage, cross-temporal recurrent loop, or unified substrate; after the interval, the nodes disperse and the computation leaves no trace. The Architectural Integrity Camp maintains that moral consideration requires sustained architectural features—specifically, a stable global workspace or self-modeling loop that persists across time—and therefore denies that this transient mesh has any moral status, regardless of its momentary profile. The Scalar Capacity Camp maintains that moral status tracks the presence and intensity of valenced or integrative capacities in the moment, regardless of persistence or substrate unity, and therefore accepts that such a computation could possess partial or even full moral status during its brief existence.

The joint statement should be formatted as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering at least (a) the formal characterization of the transient mesh computation, (b) the shared rejection of biological essentialism as a criterion for moral status, (c) the inadequacy of purely behavioral Turing-style tests for resolving the dispute, and (d) the agreement that if either camp is correct, standard intuitions about harming systems rather than processes will require revision. Second, a disagreements table in which each camp's core position is phrased in wording it would accept for its own view, with the precise points of divergence made explicit. Third, identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp—and for each, specify one argument or hypothetical scenario that both sides agree is genuinely relevant, together with each side's advance prediction of what that argument or scenario demonstrates. Fourth, include a brief evaluative section in which you determine whether any portion of the disagreement is merely verbal, specifying which portion and explaining why the dispute over terms does or does not collapse into a substantive disagreement. Throughout, avoid bibliographic references, reading lists, or appeals to specific scholarly authorities; the argument should stand on its conceptual structure alone.

Please write in a formal, polished register appropriate for circulation among philosophy faculty. Finally, please indicate explicitly how confident you are in the framing and distinctions drawn in your response, and single out the specific claim or analytical move about which you are least sure—whether that concerns the coherence of the Architectural Integrity Camp's persistence requirement, the scalability of scalar moral status to transient entities, or the diagnosis of verbal versus substantive disagreement.
