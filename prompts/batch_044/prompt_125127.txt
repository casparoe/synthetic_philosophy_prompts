Consider the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism, but restrict attention to a precise claim: there exists at least one substantive moral principle P that applies across contextually distinct situations, is not merely a placeholder for "do what is best all things considered," and retains normative force in those situations without being systematically overridden by competing considerations that particularists treat as incommensurable. Give me your credence that this claim is true. Decompose the credence into four terms: (A) the probability that moral reasons are essentially holistic, such that the contribution of any feature depends on an irreducibly global configuration; (B) the probability that the strongest particularist counterexamples demonstrate specification failure rather than principle failure; (C) the probability that particularists conflate epistemic difficulty in articulating principles with metaphysical absence of principles; and (D) the probability that the "shape" of moral reasons is genuinely context-variable in a way that defeats any principled generalization. Assign a rough number to each term with one line of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which term is both the weakest link in your reasoning and the one most susceptible to being moved by a carefully constructed conceptual case study rather than by empirical observation. Do not appeal to authority or citation; reason from the structure of moral judgment itself.
