Here is where I have ended up on hedonism and the heterogeneity problem, and I want an audit of whether this package actually holds together or whether I am helping myself to motivations that pull in opposite directions.

(1) I think the heterogeneity objection is answered at a first-layer level: every pleasure—whether gustatory, intellectual, aesthetic, or social—shares a mode-neutral affective valence, a bare pro-attitude with no intrinsic qualitative texture of its own.

(2) At the same time, I hold that this first-layer property does no normative or descriptive work for well-being. What matters is the second-layer phenomenal mode, which is genuinely content-specific and qualitatively diverse; the pleasure of grasping a proof and the pleasure of tasting wine share nothing phenomenologically that makes them substitutable in a life.

(3) Because of that second-layer diversity, I reject quantitative hedonism outright: there is no single cardinal scale of pleasure intensity that applies across types, and a life rich in intellectual pleasure is not automatically comparable to one rich in bodily pleasure by counting units.

(4) Nevertheless, I think practical reasoning about well-being requires aggregating second-layer pleasures through capacity weights—weights that reflect how central the corresponding human capacity is to flourishing—so that a well-rounded life gets priority over a monomaniacal one even when the latter contains more total pleasure.

(5) I also maintain that some pleasures—malicious delight, pleasure in a false belief—are genuinely pleasant at both layers, yet they contribute nothing to well-being, because well-being tracks only fitting pleasures, and fittingness is determined by whether the object actually merits the pro-attitude.

(6) Finally, I think that if two lives have identical second-layer pleasure-profiles but differ only in first-layer valence intensity, they are equally good lives; this is why I invoke the first-layer property solely to answer what pleasures have in common, not to ground comparisons of well-being.

The friction between (3) and (4)—rejecting a cardinal scale while still aggregating—strikes me as only apparent: the weights can be ordinal and practical rather than mathematical, so nothing forces me onto a quantitative scale. But I am unsure about the rest. Is there a deeper tension between invoking a shared first-layer feature to dissolve the heterogeneity problem (1) while simultaneously denying that feature any importance for describing or evaluating lives (2, 6)? Does my commitment to capacity-weighted aggregation (4) secretly presuppose the kind of cross-type comparability that my qualitative-diversity claim (2) is meant to block? And with (5), have I added an external moral filter about fittingness that makes hedonism false rather than refined, since the first-layer valence is present but normatively inert?

Order the tensions by severity, specifying for each whether (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), or (6) must give, and describe the cheapest repair. Do not treat every friction as fatal; distinguish the tensions that dissolve on inspection from those that genuinely threaten consistency.
