Write a dialogue between Dr. Liora Voss, who defends a Margalit-inspired ethics of institutional remembrance, and Dr. Elias Kohn, who defends a Rieff-inspired ethics of charitable forgetting. The setting is a municipal archives committee deciding the fate of the complete bureaucratic paper trail—eviction orders, internal memos, appraisal sheets, and interdepartmental correspondence—of a 1970s urban-renewal program that displaced a small, ethnically concentrated neighborhood not through physical violence but through systematic devaluation of property rights and selective enforcement of building codes. The harm is now half a century old; most direct victims are deceased, and their descendants live scattered or, in some cases, in the rebuilt district now inhabited by a different demographic.

Voss should argue that granular preservation is a moral debt owed to the dead: reducing the harm to aggregate statistics is a second erasure that converts specific wrongs into generic data and denies descendants the evidentiary basis for a particular, rather than abstract, relationship to the past. Kohn should counter that maintaining every document in a searchable public archive fixes descendants in an identity of grievance and prevents current residents—who bear no culpability—from forming a shared civic identity; he should hold that forgiveness requires a structured release of the past, not its perpetual institutional availability.

Make the exchange genuinely adversarial but mutually competent: each should respond to the other’s actual claim rather than talk past it. In particular, have Voss address Kohn’s charge that remembrance in this case risks becoming a form of moral vanity or trauma fetishism, and have Kohn address Voss’s claim that anonymizing, condensing, or time-limiting the archive is not forgiveness but managed amnesia that serves present comfort rather than past justice. Near the end, let one of them propose a synthetic institutional mechanism—perhaps a tiered access protocol in which raw documents are sealed for a fixed term but made available to accredited researchers studying structural harm—only for the other to reject it on principled, not merely practical, grounds. Conclude without declaring a winner, but ensure that the final lines leave the tension between remembrance and release structurally intact.
