I'm enrolled in a seminar on intergenerational justice, and during our discussion of long-run climate mitigation a fellow student keeps invoking the line "future people don't exist yet, so there's nothing to discount" whenever someone argues that present carbon budgets ought to be shaped by the welfare of people in the 23rd century. He treats it as a knock-down objection that distinguishes climate obligations from obligations to present strangers: we may owe distant descendants a generic duty not to render the world uninhabitable, he says, but nothing owed specifically to them as particular beneficiaries. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to the next session rather than just disagreeing.

Could you unpack the slogan as a menu of distinct philosophical claims? In particular, distinguish: (i) metaphysical readings about whether non-existent beings can be subjects of harm, including presentist versus eternalist ontologies of future persons and whether temporal parts have moral standing before they are actualized; (ii) normative readings about whether moral consideration requires actual existence, possible existence, or some intermediate modal status, and whether harming requires a victim who already is; (iii) axiological and economic readings that separate pure time preference—weighting welfare less merely because it occurs later—from risk-adjusted or extinction-risk discounting; and (iv) the practical inference from any of these premises to his specific conclusion that long-run mitigation is not owed to particular distant individuals. For each variant, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive thesis, or false; and then indicate which readings actually license his dismissal of climate obligations, which leave the debate untouched, and which might even strengthen the case for present action on behalf of the far future.
