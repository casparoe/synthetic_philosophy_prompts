You are a philosopher of mathematics with expertise in early modern thought. Before writing, briefly outline the tension in Hobbes's geometric philosophy between his materialist constructivism—the view that geometric figures exist only as constructions generated by bodily motion and that demonstration must begin from definitions the geometer himself makes—and his dogged, unsuccessful attempt to square the circle. Then compose a villanelle (nineteen lines: five tercets with rhyme scheme A b A, followed by a final quatrain A b A A) in a voice close to Hobbes's own. Use two refrains: the first should express the constructivist claim that the figure is made rather than discovered (for instance, "I draw the line; it is not in the stone"), and the second should register the persistent failure of quadrature (for instance, "the circle remains unsquared"). Let the villanelle's compulsive alternation of refrains mirror Hobbes's repeated attempts to close the proof by motion alone, and let the closing couplet bring both refrains together without resolving whether the gap belongs to the construction or to the circle itself. The poem should be philosophically precise—accurate to Hobbes's belief that geometry is the only truly certain science because its objects are our own creations—without becoming biography, satire, or sermon. It should leave the tension open, readable to someone who knows nothing of Hobbes and recognizable to a specialist as tracking his actual position. Maintain the rhyme scheme strictly; meter is free.
