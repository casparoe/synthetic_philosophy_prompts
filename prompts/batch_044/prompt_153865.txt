Compare David Lewis's counterpart theory and Saul Kripke's doctrine of transworld identity as accounts of modal persistence, but do not merely rehearse the textbook taxonomy. Instead, apply both to a destructive-scanning-and-reconstruction case familiar from rationalist discussions of personal identity: an agent is copied with perfect qualitative fidelity but discontinuous causal history, then ask whether the copy in the reconstructed world is the same particular agent. On Lewis's view the copy is a counterpart related by similarity and origin-world properties rather than identity; on Kripke's view we must ask whether the original agent itself could have undergone that very discontinuity, given essential origins. Which framework yields the cleaner account of why de re modal claims about "this agent" come out true or false? Specifically, does Kripke's essentialism about origins force him to deny that the reconstructed copy is identical in any accessible world, and does Lewis avoid that result only by making modal attribution relational and extrinsic? Conclude by judging whether the disagreement is substantive metaphysics or largely a regimentation choice about modal language, and name one further case—perhaps fission rather than copying—where the two views would clearly diverge.
