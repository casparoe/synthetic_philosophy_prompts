I'm drafting notes for an essay on internal versus external practical reasons. The core move is a two-level distinction: at the first order, I allow that there may be considerations that count in favor of an action regardless of an agent's current motivational set—call these ground-level reasons—while at the second order I defend a modified internalist constraint on deliberative accessibility. To bridge the two levels without collapsing into pure causal psychology, I introduce the idea of a reason-bridge: a transitional mental state that is not yet full recognition of a reason but is not merely causal either, because it has normative structure in virtue of the agent's capacity to see the external consideration as answering to her existing concerns, however tentatively. I then argue that Bernard Williams's internal reasons thesis is best read as governing only these second-order accessibility conditions, not the existence conditions of ground-level reasons. This is meant to preserve Williams's insight that motivation requires some connection to actual psychology, while avoiding the standard objection that internalism makes moral demands on the amoral agent impossible. I would appreciate focused comments on three points: whether the two-level distinction is stable or just relocates the dispute; whether the notion of a reason-bridge does genuine explanatory work or merely renames the motivational gap; and whether my reading of Williams as applying only to accessibility conditions is defensible or an interpretive distortion that undermines the argument's motivation.
