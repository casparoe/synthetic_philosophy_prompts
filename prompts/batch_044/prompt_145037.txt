Some policy research institutes now run parallel prediction tracks: one using statistical base-rate aggregation (the “outside” track) and one using structured expert reasoning about causal mechanisms (the “inside” track). Their evaluation protocol is asymmetric—an outside-track miss is treated as a calibration error correctable by updating weights, while an inside-track miss is treated as a conceptual failure that requires the expert to revise their model of the system. Yet strategic recommendations are drawn almost exclusively from the inside track, even when its accuracy is lower. What view of epistemic authority, or of the nature of predictive error, does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it treat inside and outside knowledge as complementary, hierarchical, or incommensurable? Is the asymmetry in error-attribution coherent with the asymmetry in decision-weight? And what would have to change in the institute’s practice if it adopted a uniformly modest stance, treating both tracks as equally fallible perspectives on an opaque world rather than as different kinds of cognition?
