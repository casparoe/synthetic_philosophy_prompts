In care ethics, the decades-long standoff between those who treat caring as an irreducibly contextual, relational practice and those who seek to integrate it into universalist moral frameworks—whether as a virtue, a distributable principle, or a corrective to justice—has become repetitive: particularists accuse universalists of abstracting away the moral significance of the unique other, while universalists reply that without generalizable standards care collapses into arbitrary favoritism. I suspect the stall is not really about universalizability, but about a hidden disagreement over the moral status of need-interpretation—whether recognizing what another genuinely needs is itself a moral achievement that requires moral virtues, or a pre-moral perceptual skill that only acquires moral weight once filtered through a principle. If that diagnosis is right, the sides may be answering different questions—one about the ontology of moral relations, the other about the psychology of moral reasoning—rather than disagreeing over a shared claim. Is this correct, or is there an underlying substantive disagreement about what counts as a moral reason that I am missing? And if the impasse is indeed structural or verbal in this way, what could actually move it: a sharper conceptual distinction between interpretation and judgment, an empirical account of how caregivers reason, or is the disagreement irremediable because the two projects start with incompatible aims?
