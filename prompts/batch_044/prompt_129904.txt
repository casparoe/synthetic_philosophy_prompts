Having spent decades quantifying phenotypic variance in wild populations, I have recently turned to philosophy of biology and find myself preoccupied by the species problem. I am drawn to a framing—call it gradient ontology—according to which species boundaries are not discrete metaphysical joints but continuous transitions across genetic, morphological, and reproductive dimensions, so that any taxonomic cutoff is pragmatically rather than naturally grounded. Please describe the most important implications of taking this gradient framing seriously, specifically for conservation practice in hybrid zones where putatively pure lineages dissolve, and for ethical reasoning about biodiversity, particularly regarding whether moral considerability should attach to individuals, to populations, or to some intermediate unit if the species category itself lacks sharp boundaries. I seek conceptual consequences rather than bibliographic guidance, and I am especially interested in whether this continuity undermines the intuitive appeal of species-based preservation priorities.
