I'm running a graduate seminar on philosophy of language and cognitive science, and we've been arguing about whether large-scale statistical translation involves anything worthy of the name understanding. Rather than another round of polemics, I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps: the Functional-Equivalence Camp, which holds that modern neural statistical translation achieves a genuine, if non-human, form of interlingual comprehension—sufficient that calling it shallow is a category mistake about mechanism versus performance; and the Conceptual-Anchor Camp, which holds, in the spirit of Hofstadter's criticisms of statistical language processing, that translation without explicit conceptual anchoring across languages is pattern-matching that systematically fails to preserve what must be preserved, especially where grammatical structure encodes meaning.

The deliverable should be a formal document both sides could sign. Include: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims—about the formal architecture of current MT systems, about what successful translation requires in cases involving evidentiality-marked constructions (where a source language grammatically encodes direct visual versus hearsay epistemic status and the target language leaves it pragmatically implicit), and about which formal no-go results, if any, constrain statistical models in this domain; (2) a disagreements table in which each camp's position is phrased in wording that side accepts for itself, with precise divergence points—especially on whether correct rendering of evidential distinctions requires a model-internal representation of epistemic source, or whether distributional correlation across parallel corpora suffices; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp—including what predictions each makes about failure modes in low-resource evidentiality pairs, and what intervention (for instance, explicit epistemic-state tagging in training data) each would take as decisive, with predictions recorded in advance; (4) an explicit identification of any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, with reasoning; and (5) a brief statement of what both sides agree would count as genuinely persuasive evidence against their own view. Keep the formulation precise and in the weeds—avoid generic slogans about AI and language—so the disagreement concerns mechanisms and representations rather than metaphors. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
