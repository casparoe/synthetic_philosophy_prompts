I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "One Mind or Many? Consciousness After Commissurotomy." The audience is genuinely mixed: clinical neuropsychologists and retired surgeons who actually evaluated split-brain patients in past decades are seated beside philosophy and cognitive-science graduate students whose knowledge of the cases comes mainly from textbook summaries. I need a brief address—about four minutes—that treats the split-brain literature as a live structural constraint rather than colorful decoration. I must avoid both the pop-science claim that a divided brain literally houses two distinct people and the opposite reduction that treats every hemispheric dissociation as merely behavioral noise with no philosophical weight. To do this, I want to build the remarks around a somewhat under-discussed conceptual distinction: between executive unity, understood as the capacity to initiate and maintain a single coherent course of action, and phenomenal unity, understood as the subjective sense that experiences belong to one owner. I want to show how commissurotomy cases put real pressure on the common assumption that these two forms of unity necessarily travel together, without overclaiming what the clinical data establish. The speech should be rigorous enough that the clinicians do not wince, accessible enough that the students are not lost, and it should contain no citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references. Please write this opening address.
