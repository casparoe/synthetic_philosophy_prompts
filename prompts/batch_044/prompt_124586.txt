Analyze the 1755 Lisbon earthquake—the destruction on All Saints' Day, the collapse of churches, the death toll in the tens of thousands, and Voltaire’s subsequent rejection of philosophical optimism in works including Candide—as a test case for Leibniz’s doctrine of the best of all possible worlds and his tripartite distinction among metaphysical, physical, and moral evil. First, map the episode precisely onto the framework: classify the earthquake as physical evil permitted for a greater good; identify the systemic goods Leibniz might invoke (the harmony of nature, the preservation of human freedom in matters of construction and settlement, the overall ratio of good to evil in the world); and examine whether the specific distributive pattern of suffering—the religious timing, the geographic concentration—creates an internal problem for the claim that evil is always rationally ordered rather than arbitrarily placed. Second, introduce the modern geological observation that plate tectonics, the mechanism behind such earthquakes, is structurally necessary for long-term planetary habitability through the carbonate-silicate cycle; ask whether this functional inseparability strengthens the Leibnizian defense by showing that the physical evil is compossible with a global-scale good, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty—namely, why a creator with full modal knowledge did not design conscious beings to inhabit only geologically stable cratons, making the worldwide distribution of seismic risk appear redundant rather than necessary. Third, identify where the theory is genuinely silent: for instance, whether there is any calculable threshold at which the magnitude of suffering exceeds compensation, whether empirical knowledge of tectonics alters the modal evaluation of worlds available to God, and whether the theodicy can address distributive injustice across populations without expanding into empirical or moral claims it originally did not contain. Conclude by stating whether the earthquake functions as an anomaly that undermines the theory, a confirming case that reveals its scope conditions, or an episode it can absorb only by making new commitments about design and necessity.
