hey so i'm in a phil of hist seminar and built this argument about narrative vs covering-law that's bugging me because it seems to kill both options. (1) covering-law explanation needs general laws plus initial conditions to derive the explanandum. (2) narrative explanation explains particular events by selecting and ordering prior events into a sequence with a begining, middle, and end. (3) but selection always needs relevance criteria, and those criteria are general: e.g., "fiscal crises tend to push states toward centralization." (4) so every narrative smuggles in implicit covering laws to justify why event A belongs in the story and B doesn't. (5) therefore narrative is just covering-law with the laws hidden and the derivation replaced by emplotment. (6) if that's right, then narrative form adds nothing explanitory; it's just decoration. (7) but historians clearly think form does work—without the sequence, you just have a list of conditions. (8) so either narrative contains explanitory elements that aren't law-like, which means covering-law fails for history, or (9) historians are systematically using redundant form. (10) but here's the kicker: if narrative doesn't need laws, then any ordering of prior events counts as an explanation, since there's no law to constrain relevance. (11) that makes historical explanation arbitrary—like storytelling without rules. (12) so either we accept covering-law and lose narrative's distinctive contribution, or we keep narrative and lose explanatory constraint. i feel like there's a slip between (4) and (5) or between (8) and (10), but i can't name it. i'd rather keep (7) than (12), since i think narrative form genuinely explains, but i don't want to end up saying history is just fiction with footnotes. which step should i reject, and what does that cost me?
