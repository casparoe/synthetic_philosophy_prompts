I am writing for a philosophy-of-value seminar that has been working through tranquilism—the position that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure is at best instrumentally relevant. I want to check whether I am misunderstanding the view’s internal logic, because two worries keep arising for me. First, if the sole intrinsic good is the absence of craving, then the desire to reach that absence seems to be a second-order craving (a craving for non-craving), which would imply that the tranquilist can never consistently arrive at the valued state without violating it. Second, if absence is the only metric of value, then a world with no conscious subjects at all would seem to realize that good perfectly and permanently; so why does tranquilism not entail that the immediate extinction of consciousness is preferable to maintaining a fragile, craving-free mind? I suspect both worries rest on mischaracterizing what counts as craving and on treating an axiological baseline as a maximization principle, but I am unsure exactly where the false steps lie and how to disentangle them. Could you explain the conceptual confusion and correct the framing? Please also indicate how confident you are in your answer and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
