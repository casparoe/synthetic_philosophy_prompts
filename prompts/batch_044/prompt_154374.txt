I've been trying to formalize my intuition that some policy dilemmas aren't just hard but structurally resistant to aggregation, and I keep running into the objection that I'm just being sloppy about my utility function. The context is environmental regulation: agencies are often required to translate ecosystem benefits into dollar equivalents before they can be weighed against development gains, which seems to presuppose that ecological value is ultimately fungible with economic value, just with noisy measurement. I want to see whether this framing is philosophically unavoidable or whether it smuggles in a specific meta-ethical commitment that could be replaced without losing normative traction.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them A and B—who are both fluent in decision theory and practical reason but disagree sharply on incommensurability. A should defend a view she calls domain sovereignty: the idea that certain goods, like the integrity of a specific watershed or the continuity of a place-based cultural practice, belong to evaluative domains with internal standards of success and cannot be translated into a common currency without distorting what makes them valuable. She should propose that rational choice in these cases requires something like threshold deference, where we establish non-negotiable minimums for each domain and then optimize only within the remaining feasible set, rather than computing a global trade-off curve. B should defend structured pluralism: the view that values are indeed plural but never genuinely incommensurable—what looks like irreducibility is usually just an underspecified preference relation or a missing veto condition. B should argue that the monetization requirement in environmental impact assessment is a pragmatic heuristic for tractability, not a metaphysical claim about value, and that a properly constructed multi-attribute decision model with lexical veto rights and partial ordering can do all the normative work A wants without abandoning commensurability. Have them apply their frameworks directly to the monetization practice, respond precisely to each other's technical moves (for instance, whether assigning veto rights to domains collapses back into cardinal comparison once weights are implicitly assigned), and avoid talking past one another. Keep the tone formal and seminar-level, and do not include bibliographies, citations, requests for further reading, or appeals to authority outside the arguments themselves.
