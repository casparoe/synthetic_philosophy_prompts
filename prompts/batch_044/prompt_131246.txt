After a long argument last night with a close friend over whether Wittgenstein’s notion of family resemblance can be turned against itself, I found myself stuck on a chain of reasoning he constructed that seems to prove far more than either of us intended. The discussion started with the usual examples—games, numbers, colors—but then he pressed what he called the reflexivity constraint: if family resemblance genuinely applies to the concept of a language game itself, then there should be no essential feature shared by all language games. From there he built the following argument.

(1) If a concept applies by family resemblance, then membership in its extension requires only an overlapping network of similarities, with no single necessary and sufficient condition. (2) By Wittgenstein’s own lights, "language game" is such a concept: rituals, commands, jokes, and scientific reports share no common essence, only criss-crossing similarities. (3) Therefore there is no necessary and sufficient condition for being a language game. (4) For Wittgenstein, to understand an utterance is to be correctly calibrated within the language game that gives it meaning. (5) If "language game" lacks necessary and sufficient conditions, then for any speaker there is no determinate fact about whether she is calibrated within the relevant game. (6) Hence there is no determinate fact about whether she understands the utterance. (7) Generalizing, if understanding is indeterminate for every word, then no speaker can ever reliably determine whether she has been understood, and communication collapses into mutual opacity. (8) But communication plainly succeeds. (9) So the family-resemblance account must fail when applied to "language game" itself; language games must possess an essential feature after all, which undermines the broader anti-essentialist framework.

I feel the slide happens somewhere between (4) and (7), perhaps in how calibration is conceived, but I cannot locate the exact misstep. If I have to preserve (2)—that language game is genuinely a family-resemblance category—I would prefer not to abandon it, though I am less attached to (5). Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does saving family resemblance require us to give up the idea that understanding is participation in a game, or can we keep both by rethinking calibration?
