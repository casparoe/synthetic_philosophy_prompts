I'm designing a single 75-minute session for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just completed a unit on Hobbes's account of the state of nature but have not yet read Plato. I want to introduce Glaucon's challenge and the ring of Gyges specifically as an argument about motivational architecture: whether justice is valued for its own sake or only for its reputational and punitive consequences, with the invisibility condition functioning to suspend the latter without altering human psychology. Rather than beginning with the magical artifact, I want the carrying example to be a reconstructed modern analogue—an environment of perfect digital anonymity in which every action is technically untraceable and leaves no metadata—to establish the suspension of external tracking before introducing the mythical ring as Plato's compressed version of the same thought experiment. Plan the explanatory arc: the conceptual prerequisite that must be secured first so that the distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value of justice is not collapsed into Hobbes's instrumental account; how to deploy the modern analogue (what it must include, such as the persistence of internal awareness and the absence of legal sanction, and what it must exclude, such as physical invisibility or supernatural power); the sequence in which the magical ring should then be introduced and mapped back onto the analogue; the predictable misconceptions—treating the challenge as an empirical claim about human selfishness, conflating invisibility with the elimination of all social pressure rather than only reputational tracking, or reading it as redundant with Hobbes; the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted; one diagnostic question I can ask at the close to distinguish students who grasp the challenge as a normative problem about the structure of practical reasons from those who interpret it as descriptive psychology; and what should be explicitly bracketed for this session—such as Plato's constructive reply across Books II-IV or evolutionary game-theoretic explanations of cooperation—stated clearly so that I do not accidentally open them.
