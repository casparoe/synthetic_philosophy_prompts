I serve on my university’s Committee for Algorithmic Governance and Institutional Fairness as the philosophy faculty representative. We are reviewing a pilot system for distributing limited emergency research grants. The evaluation algorithm assigns composite scores across four dimensions—scientific merit, equity impact, institutional need, and feasibility—each weighted equally. In the current round, two proposals, one in climate-adaptation engineering and one in what our office classifies as epistemic-justice studies, have received identical scores to the fourth decimal place across every dimension. The system’s default protocol is to invoke a deterministic pseudo-random tie-breaker (seeded by timestamp) and award the grant immediately. A faculty senator, however, has objected that selecting arbitrarily between genuinely equipreferable options violates the university’s stated commitment to reason-responsive governance, and has proposed that we suspend allocation for one funding cycle and redesign the weighting scheme to force differentiation. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame—whether to approve the tie-breaker under conditions, reject it in favor of suspension and redesign, or propose a third resolution mechanism—and it should engage the senator’s objection seriously rather than treating it as a mere programming error. In particular, address whether a decision-maker with no rational basis for preferring one option over another is permitted, or perhaps required, to choose without further reason; whether introducing randomness or temporal delay alters the normative status of the choice; and whether institutional contexts in which accountability and transparency are paramount change the conditions under which a Buridan-style equilibrium is permissible or intolerable. The philosophical problem of choice without preference should be treated as the core of the case, not as an analogy.
