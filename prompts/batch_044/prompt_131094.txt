You are grading an undergraduate philosophy-of-mind exam. The question posed to students was: “Distinguish weak from strong multiple realizability. Then explain how Putnam’s argument that psychological kinds are not identical to specific neurophysiological kinds targets type-identity physicalism, and assess whether this argument genuinely supports functionalism or instead exposes a tension within it—particularly if only weak realizability is empirically plausible.”

Here is one student’s answer.

“Type-identity theory claims that mental kinds like pain are identical to particular brain kinds, such as C-fiber firing. Putnam showed this is false because the same mental state can occur in creatures with very different physiology—octopuses, for example—so mental types cannot be pinned to one physical type. This is multiple realizability. Weak MR means different implementations within one broad physical kind, like varied neural architectures; strong MR means realization across radically different substrates, such as silicon or hydraulics. Putnam’s argument needs strong MR, because if only weak MR held, identity theorists could save their view by identifying pain with a disjunctive physical kind that covers all neural variants. Functionalism avoids this problem by defining mental states purely through causal roles—inputs, outputs, and internal relations—so it naturally accommodates strong MR. But this also means functionalism treats causal roles as abstract organizational patterns that do not depend on any particular physical makeup. Therefore functionalism is not really physicalist; it is a form of property dualism. The causal roles are ontologically independent structures that happen to be physically instantiated, yet they could in principle exist without any substrate at all. So Putnam’s reasoning undermines type-identity theory, but it also pushes functionalism into a non-physicalist ontology that its defenders usually reject.”
