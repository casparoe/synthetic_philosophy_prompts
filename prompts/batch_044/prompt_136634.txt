You are an expert philosopher of applied ethics with specialization in animal welfare science and the conceptual foundations of comparative harm assessment, writing for an advanced undergraduate examination committee. Before drafting, think carefully and briefly brainstorm: what is the deepest philosophical tension in applying quantitative welfare metrics across taxa with radically different nervous system architectures, and why do students usually miss it?

Now construct one 90-minute essay examination for an upper-level course on ethics and the philosophy of science. The exam must focus on welfare assessment for teleost fish and decapod crustaceans within integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) systems—environments in which waste streams, stocking densities, and handling protocols affect multiple species simultaneously.

To ensure the question is meaningful without requiring external sources and cannot be answered by reciting standard welfare principles, embed within the exam prompt a hypothetical assessment tool: the Cumulative Nociceptive Load Index (CNLI). Define it in the exam text as follows. The CNLI weights chronic, sub-lethal stressor exposure—such as dissolved oxygen fluctuation, spatial restriction, and repeated handling—against discrete acute nociceptive events, using taxon-specific calibration curves derived from differences in peripheral nociceptor density and central integration architecture. The exam should ask students to evaluate whether rigorous application of the CNLI in a shared IMTA setting generates a counter-intuitive welfare hierarchy—specifically, whether minimizing acute nociceptive scores for crustaceans might license management practices that substantially elevate chronic stress loads for co-cultured fish.

Write:
(i) The exam question itself (approximately 450–600 words of framing and prompt), which must present the CNLI clearly, specify the interspecies trade-off, and ask students to take and defend a definite normative conclusion about how welfare priorities should be ordered in such systems;
(ii) A model answer of roughly 800 words that takes a committed, definite position—not a balanced survey. It should argue for a specific resolution, defend it with reasons drawn from the philosophy of measurement, the distinction between nociception and phenomenal consciousness, and the ethics of interspecies comparison, and explicitly address why the counter-intuitive implication either should or should not be accepted;
(iii) A marking scheme out of 25 marks that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for example: recognizing that metric construction embeds normative choices; distinguishing nociceptive capacity from subjective experience; applying a control principle to interspecies comparison without collapsing into anthropomorphism; addressing the is-ought gap in deriving management priorities from index scores—and identifying the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating welfare as purely subjective without analyzing metric design; ignoring the taxon-specific calibration embedded in the CNLI; assuming that lower acute scores automatically justify higher chronic exposure; or failing to take a committed position.

Constraints: Do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts in your materials; if you reference philosophers or scientists, paraphrase without quotation marks. Frame all necessary technical and conceptual information within the exam text itself so that a knowledgeable student could answer without searching online.
