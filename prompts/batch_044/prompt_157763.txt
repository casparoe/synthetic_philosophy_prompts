I was arguing with my friends in civics class about whether unelected judges should be able to strike down laws that legislatures pass, and everyone kept using the sports referee analogy — the legislature are the players, the constitution is the rulebook, and judges are just neutral refs who call fouls but dont actually play the game. They say this proves we definatly need judicial review, since a match with no referee is just chaos. But I'm not convinced the analogy holds water.

Can you lay out the full correspondence table — what plays the role of the players, the rulebook, the crowd, the score, maybe even the league commissioner — and then show me exactly where the mapping breaks? I want you to include the less obvious cracks: constitutional text is vague so judges are really making value calls, not just spotting obvious fouls; referees are usually ex-players but judges come from elite law schools; and in Canada there's this notwithstanding clause that lets legislatures overide court rulings for five years, which is like letting the team captain bench the ref for a quarter. There's also Cass Sunstein's idea of judicial minimalism, where courts should stick to narrow decisions instead of big strategic rulings.

Does the referee picture smuggle in the idea that rules are crystal clear and refs never change them, and is that what's really carrying the conclusion that unelected judges are harmless? Once all the disanalogies are on the table — life tenure, no democratic check, the fact that courts can issue sweeping orders — does the defense survive, or was the analogy doing all the heavy lifting? And if it's busted, whats a better analogy that doesnt hide those problems?
