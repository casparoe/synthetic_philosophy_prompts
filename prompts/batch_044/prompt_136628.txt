I am grading final examinations for an undergraduate course in moral philosophy that I taught this past term. The examination asked students to explain the so-called wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, to distinguish it precisely from the objection that moral demands are excessively burdensome, and to present and evaluate either Stocker’s appeal to moral schizophrenia or Williams’s concern about moral motivation being one thought too many in personal relationships.

The topic has taken on unexpected personal weight for me: in the months since I wrote the exam, my mother was diagnosed with early-stage dementia and I have become her primary caregiver. I have found myself repeatedly asking whether my obligations toward her ought to be experienced as moral duties at all, or whether framing them in that way already introduces a kind of distance that damages the relationship. I want to be sure that my grading is not distorted by this, so I am asking for an independent, point-by-point assessment of the answer below.

Exam question:
Explain the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in ethics. Carefully distinguish it from the claim that morality is overly demanding. Then present and critically assess either Stocker’s diagnosis in terms of moral schizophrenia or Williams’s argument that moral reasons can be one thought too many.

Student answer:
The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem arises when someone performs a morally correct action but is motivated by considerations that are inappropriate to the situation. The classic case is visiting a sick friend in hospital: if you go only because you judge it to be your duty, then something has gone wrong, since the proper motivation should be affection or concern for the friend. This is not the same as morality being too demanding, because that objection complains about the quantity or difficulty of moral requirements, whereas here the issue is qualitative—certain motives are out of place regardless of how easy the action is.

Michael Stocker identifies the root cause as moral schizophrenia, a split between the reasons that justify an action and the motives that actually drive it. He thinks modern ethical theories like Kantianism and utilitarianism produce this split by requiring agents to justify conduct with impartial moral principles, even when personal relationships call for partiality. Bernard Williams makes a related point with his example of a husband who saves his drowning wife: if he acts because it is the right thing to do rather than because she is his wife, he has one thought too many. Both Stocker and Williams conclude that moral reasons ought to be excluded from intimate relationships altogether, leaving only personal love and affection to guide conduct there. The proper resolution, then, is a division of labor—moral reasoning governs our dealings with strangers, while personal bonds operate without moral mediation in family life.
