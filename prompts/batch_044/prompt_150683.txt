For a grad seminar on practical reason, 10 people, 90 minutes. I want them to actually feel the problem with indirect normativity in groups, not just hear it. The niche point is that if reasons are anchored to each person's own idealized deliberator, then collective decision-making doesn't have one shared standard—it has ten parallel monologues that happen to sit in the same room. Design a structured activity with real (small) stakes—say, dividing a pool of actual snack credits—where in round one they bargain as themselves, and in round two they must each construct and then defend only the position that their "fully informed, unbiased, but value-preserving" counterpart would take. The rules should force them to discover that they can't appeal to "what we should do" without smuggling in actual preferences, and that the idealized agents don't converge even when the real people want agreement. Include roles or constraints, a timeline, the debrief mapping each round onto the fragmentation problem, and an honest note about which feature of indirect normativity—probably the metaphysical claim that the counterfactual agent is genuinely authoritative, not just advisory—the activity can't reproduce and you have to supply verbally. Keep it runnable without props beyond paper and snacks, and don't let the facilitator name the view until the debrief.
