I'm a high school student working on an independent philosophy project about how moral harms aggregate across different people, times, and minds. Rather than asking for a bibliography, I want to test whether my own intuitions about a classic problem can be captured by a single principle.

The problem is the well-known comparison between one severe harm and an astronomical number of trivial ones. Here are my verdicts on five concrete variants:

Case 1. One person is tortured continuously for fifty years. The alternative is that every human being who has ever lived—roughly one hundred billion people—each receives a single, momentary dust speck in one eye, with no lasting injury. Verdict: The single torture is far worse.

Case 2. The same total quantity of minor discomfort is redistributed: one million people each experience about one hundred thousand separate dust-speck events spread across a normal lifespan, with brief pain and full recovery each time. Verdict: Even this heavy burden distributed across many lives does not match the moral weight of the fifty-year torture.

Case 3. The outcomes are probabilistic. There is a one-percent chance that a randomly selected adult will suffer fifty years of torture. The alternative is near-certainty that ten trillion future people each suffer one dust speck. Verdict: I still prefer avoiding the torture risk, though I am less certain here.

Case 4. The trivial harms are not spread across people but accumulated within one extraordinarily long life: a single being lives for ten thousand years and experiences one dust-speck event every minute, remaining fully conscious of the repetition, with no other suffering. Verdict: This life of relentless minor annoyance is seriously bad, yet I still judge it less grave than the fifty-year torture of a being with a normal lifespan.

Case 5. The victims of the trivial harms are beings with severely diminished psychological continuity—human infants who forget each event instantly—while the torture victim is a fully rational adult with uninterrupted self-awareness. Verdict: Aggregating harms across beings without sustained inner life seems to weigh less, but I cannot tell whether this is a quantitative discount or a categorical exclusion.

My question: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a non-linear aggregation rule, a threshold of psychological continuity, or a distinction between experienced and remembered suffering—that can accommodate all five verdicts? If coherence forces me to drop one, which should it be, and what would the repaired principle predict about a scenario in which the number of trivial harms is so vast that merely counting them exceeds the cognitive capacity of any observer?
