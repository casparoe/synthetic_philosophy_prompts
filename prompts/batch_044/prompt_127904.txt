Consider a museum that employs a predictive reconstruction algorithm to complete a severely damaged Hellenistic torso. The system was trained solely on geometric and material probabilities, with no aesthetic programming or artistic purpose; historians later demonstrate that the result hybridizes formal features from three distinct sculptors rather than recovering any original design. The object is displayed alongside the authentic fragment under the institutional label “restored statue.” Visitors consistently report aesthetic responses—proportion, emotional weight, formal coherence—indistinguishable from those elicited by intact classical works. A philosopher offers the following argument that the reconstructed object cannot be art.

(1) If an object was produced without artistic intention and is institutionally framed as a non-artistic restoration rather than as a creative work, then it lacks the conditions necessary for being art.

(2) The reconstructed torso was produced without artistic intention and is institutionally framed as a restoration.

(3) Therefore, the reconstructed torso is not art.

(4) Yet the object produces experienced aesthetic properties that match those of acknowledged masterpieces.

(5) If one denies artistic status solely on the basis of origin and framing, while the object’s experienced properties are fully congruent with those of art, then the denial is arbitrary unless the same origin-based standard is applied consistently across all cases.

(6) Applying that standard consistently would require withholding artistic status from algorithmic prints, procedurally generated compositions, and other museum-accepted works whose production involves significant non-intentional processes.

(7) Therefore, accepting (1) forces the rejection of artistic status for a broad class of works currently treated as art.

The philosopher concludes that (1) is false and that artistic status must be determined primarily by experienced properties rather than by origin or framing. I find this result suspicious: it appears to entail that any naturally eroded rock formation or weather pattern that happens to evoke the appropriate aesthetic response would also count as art, which seems absurd. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and undermine (4) through (6), but I want to understand precisely where the reasoning slips and whether that preservation commits me to an implausible view about algorithmically assisted works already in institutional collections.
