I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates who have worked through Bentham’s quantitative hedonism and the principle of utility, but who have not encountered his argument in Offences Against One’s Self regarding non-procreative sexuality. I want them to grasp not merely that Bentham rejects legal or moral condemnation of such acts, but the precise logical architecture of his defense: that appeals to “unnaturalness” conflate a physical description with a moral evaluation; that the observer’s feeling of antipathy is not evidence of harm; and that the burden of proof must rest on demonstrating a net diminution of pleasure over pain rather than on identifying a deviation from reproductive ends. Do not explain the argument to me; instead, plan how I should lead the students through it.

Specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit—why the distinction between physical description and moral evaluation has to be in place before any concrete sexual example is introduced, and at what point in the sequence the students must already understand Bentham’s calculus of pleasure and pain for the remainder of the argument to land. Identify the one concrete comparison or case that should carry the entire explanation: it must allow students to separate “what physically occurs” from “whether harm is done,” and it must deliberately lack the feature that causes students to conflate disgust with moral judgment. Say precisely what that example must include and what it must exclude, and explain why a direct discussion of paederasty should be deferred until after the structural point is secured.

Next, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise: that Bentham is licensing any pleasure regardless of long-term consequence; that he denies all psychological harm; that he collapses morality into private preference; and that his rejection of natural-law reasoning is merely an inverted appeal to nature. For each, indicate the precise moment in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused, without derailing the arc.

Then propose a single check—one brief question or exercise—I can pose at the end to determine whether the students have understood the dependency structure (that moral evaluation requires a utility calculation, not a taxonomy of acts) rather than having merely adopted the conclusion that Bentham was “liberal about sex.”

Finally, state explicitly what portion of the historical material—whether the essay’s original legal framing, Bentham’s correspondence with contemporaries, or the manuscript’s publication history—I should acknowledge as present but deliberately leave out at this level, and say why including it would obscure rather than illuminate the conceptual structure I want them to hold.
