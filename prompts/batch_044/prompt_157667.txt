Is there a name for this? In historical post-mortems and policy write-ups I keep seeing the same two-step move. Step one: once you know the outcome, you go back to the pile of causes that were actually present at the time and you pick out the ones that line up with what eventually happened. Step two: you arrange just those picked causes into a neat causal chain so the outcome feels inevatable, like the other factors were just background noise.

A couple examples. There's an account of a 1920s urban planning scheme that collapsed, and the historian zeroes in on one zoning conflict that eventually exploded. They present it as the obvious structural weakness all along, but gloss over the half-dozen other conflicts that seemed equally serious to people living through it. Then there's a tech company that killed a platform in 2008; the retrospective narrative treats the platform's later security flaws as the "real" reason, even though internal memos from 2007 are almost entirely about market share, with security barely mentioned. It's not quite hindsight bias -- that's usually about individuals feeling "I knew it all along." And it's not exactly the narrative fallacy, since there really was a failure and the story isn't just imposing coherence on randomness; it's using the outcome to filter which causes count as load-bearing. It feels like a specific blend of outcome-filtered causation plus retroactive inevitability.

Is there an established concept that captures this exact double move? If not, can you name and define it, and draw clear boundaries with hindsight bias, narrative fallacy, and ordinary cherry-picking? I don't want just a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the edges get blurry. Also, please say what kind of argument or counterexample would change your mind about whether this is genuinely distinct, and flag where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
