I have come to accept the view that victimhood culture is not a moral degeneration of dignity culture, but rather its structurally necessary successor in large, heterogeneous societies—specifically, that victimhood culture solves the coordination failure of dignity culture, which assumes universal internalized self-respect can suppress grievance, by externalizing harm-validation to institutional arbiters and thereby reducing reliance on either stoic suppression or honor-based retaliation. I hold that this makes victimhood culture morally superior to both honor and dignity cultures in conditions of high social complexity and low interpersonal trust, not because it cultivates superior individual character, but because it is the only framework that scales without violence. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, observations, conceptual distinctions, and possible empirical patterns that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be: for example, cross-cultural or historical cases in which the institutionalization of victimhood correlates with increased interpersonal or intergroup violence; conceptual arguments showing that externalized validation collapses into a new form of status competition rather than replacing it; or results suggesting that dignity culture’s stoicism is actually more resilient to institutional capture and corruption than victimhood culture’s dependence on bureaucratic recognition. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view have insulated it into unfalsifiability—say, by redefining violence, scale, or harm-validation in ways that absorb any counterexample—and whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a normative claim of this kind. Finally, distinguish what a committed defender would concede as genuinely damaging from what they would dismiss as a mismeasurement of social complexity, and indicate whether the view, if well-insulated, should still be preferred over its rivals.
