For an upper-level undergraduate seminar (14 students, 90 minutes) on decision theory and self-control, design a runnable role-play and token game that makes participants experience hyperbolic discounting and weakness of will from the inside—not as observers, but as competing temporal selves negotiating over a shared pool. Use only printed cards and small tokens; no digital props.

Structure the group into seven pairs. Within each pair, one student serves as Present-Self for rounds 1–4 and then as Future-Self for rounds 5–8; the other takes the complementary roles. Both share a single cumulative payoff pool visible to both. At the start of each round, the active Present-Self chooses between taking 2 points immediately (added to the pool now) or committing to 5 points delivered to the pool in exactly three rounds. Crucially, if the Present-Self takes the immediate reward, the Future-Self in that pair suffers two consequences in the next round: they lose the right to invoke a binding contract, and the pool suffers an automatic 1-point deduction.

Introduce a precommitment mechanism with real cost: each Present-Self holds one treaty token that can be played before the round to lock in the delayed choice. Playing it costs 1 point from the pool immediately. Between rounds 2, 4, and 6, pairs have two minutes to negotiate and sign a written treaty specifying what the Present-Self will do in the upcoming round. The treaty is enforceable only if both parties jointly invoke it at the round’s start; either party may tear it up, but tearing costs 1 point to the pool.

The niche framing: embed Strotz’s consistent-planning logic but invert it into an interpersonal bargaining protocol. Most pairs should discover that treaties signed in round 2 are torn up in round 3, not because the objective payoff structure changed—the 5-point reward is still three rounds away—but because the hyperbolic curve makes the present cost of honoring the contract suddenly feel larger relative to the now-closer temptation, producing strategic abandonment of prior agreements without any shift in the underlying incentives.

After round 8, tally pools and rank pairs. Then run a 15-minute debrief mapping negotiation breakdowns onto the mathematical structure of hyperbolic versus exponential discount curves, identifying the round where preference reversal typically occurs and linking it to the declining-rate property. Discuss why a willpower-as-depletable-resource model fails to predict the tearing-up pattern: if self-control were merely a stock, we would expect steady degradation, not the sudden strategic abandonment of contracts precisely when the immediate spike in temptation makes the present cost of compliance feel disproportionate.

Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: it stages intertemporal conflict as interpersonal bargaining, so it misses the phenomenology of a single agent feeling divided. The facilitator must supply that first-person framing verbally. Also predict what will go wrong in a pilot run—likely that high-cooperation pairs will overestimate treaty durability, and that some participants will misinterpret torn contracts as evidence of simple irrationality rather than of rational preference reversal under a hyperbolic curve.

Finally, say what would change your mind about the design’s effectiveness: identify which observed behavior in a pilot—if present—would convince you that the game had failed to make hyperbolic discounting feel visceral rather than merely strategic, and note where your reasoning about the payoff structure is most likely to be wrong.
