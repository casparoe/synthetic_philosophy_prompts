Conciliationists in the peer-disagreement literature frequently rely on an analogy to redundant metrology: two atomic clocks, built to the same specification and maintained under identical protocols, suddenly diverge by a measurable margin. The keeper of each clock, the argument goes, should not stand steadfast; she should treat the divergence as evidence of her own instrument’s hidden drift and revise her confidence downward, ideally converging on a weighted mean. This is used to license the view that equally rational peers who share all evidence must split the difference or suspend judgment. Audit the analogy in detail: provide the correspondence table—what plays the role of the clock, the clock-keeper, the calibration protocol, the divergence reading, and the weighted-mean revision—then identify the precise points where the mapping fractures. In particular, examine whether belief-formation has anything like a transparent calibration protocol, whether peer disagreement involves a single shared measurement with independent error terms, and whether averaging beliefs is epistemically analogous to averaging time-readings rather than, say, selecting one instrument for repair. Next, consider the steadfastist counter-analogy: peer disagreement is less like redundant sensors and more like two cartographers who used the same surveying manual but walked different terrain, so each should audit her own path rather than mechanically blend maps. Map that alternative, locate where it breaks—especially regarding the assumption that peers can transparently inspect their own path—and determine whether either analogy genuinely supports its intended conclusion or whether both carry normative weight through smuggled features of the source domain. Conclude by stating which disanalogies are fatal to the conciliationist inference and whether a more accurate analogy—perhaps drawn from distributed computation or adversarial collaboration—would be available, without requiring that you defend either conciliationism or steadfastness outright.
