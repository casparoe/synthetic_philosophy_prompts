Write the opening remarks for a two-day interdisciplinary workshop—delivered to philosophers of mind, biologists who study swarm behavior, and graduate students—on whether collective entities such as ant colonies can be literal subjects of mental states rather than mere metaphors. Build the entire address around the conceptual problem raised in Douglas Hofstadter’s “Ant Fugue” interlude from Gödel, Escher, Bach: the figure of Aunt Hillary, an individual ant with no grasp of the colony as a whole, contrasted with the colony’s apparently intelligent, goal-directed behavior that no single ant comprehends. Let this image do real structural work: it should constrain what you can claim about collective consciousness—preventing both naive anthropomorphism and dismissive reductionism—rather than serving as decorative illustration. The philosophers present should recognize the reference; the biologists should need no prior familiarity with Hofstadter. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations. Instead, introduce a precise conceptual twist: ask whether the absence of a unified first-person “I” in the colony genuinely disqualifies it from having a mind, or whether our requirement of a singular subject is itself a parochial hinge commitment—an unexamined certainty about our own case—that obstructs recognition of genuinely distributed cognition. Conclude by reframing the workshop’s guiding question away from “Do ant colonies think?” toward “What commitments about our own minds would we have to surrender for distributed entities to count as thinkers?” Aim for about four minutes spoken, maintain a formal but accessible tone, and avoid relying on specialized myrmecological data that a speaker without field expertise could not reasonably defend.
