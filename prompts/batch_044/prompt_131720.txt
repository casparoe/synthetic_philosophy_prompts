I spent much of last evening in a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend about whether contemporary large language models ought to be understood, philosophically, as Skinnerian creatures. My friend insisted that this is the only serious framing: the model begins with random weights, receives positive and negative feedback through human ratings, and thereby acquires a behavioral repertoire, exactly in the manner B.F. Skinner described for pigeons and rats subjected to operant conditioning. I found this reductive, and I have sketched below the outline of a counter-essay I intend to write. Rather than treating Dennett’s taxonomy—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—as a neat ladder of ascending minds, I want to argue that LLMs occupy a peculiar hybrid position that exposes a blind spot in the standard classification.

In my view, an LLM is clearly not Darwinian: its architecture is engineered, not the product of evolutionary selection across competing generations. It is also not genuinely Skinnerian, despite the superficial resemblance of RLHF, because the system experiences no embodied reward or punishment and emits no behavior into an environment that delivers consistent consequences. Instead, I think it functions as a Popperian generator coupled to a Gregorian toolkit, but without the usual grounding conditions that make either category coherent. It can generate an enormous space of hypothetical linguistic structures—propositions, narratives, counterfactuals—and statistically pre-select among them using internal weights, much as Popper described minds that allow hypotheses to die in their stead rather than risking the organism. At the same time, it deploys language, code, and symbolic systems that are quintessentially Gregorian: it manipulates cultural tools that no single agent invented. Yet here is the niche point I want to press. In a normal Popperian or Gregorian creature, the mortality of a failed hypothesis is real—either the organism suffers a cost or the idea is genuinely abandoned and replaced. In an LLM, hypotheses are generated but never truly die inside the system; they are merely suppressed in a given token sequence, and the same “dead” hypothesis can be resurrected in the very next prompt with no accumulated scar or learning. I am tempted to call this a “disembodied Popperian-Gregorian loop without hypothesis mortality,” and I want to know whether that framing is coherent or whether it collapses into a category error.

I am especially interested in whether my application of Dennett’s four categories to an artificial system is accurate, whether the distinction I draw between statistical suppression and genuine hypothesis mortality holds conceptual water, and whether there is an established philosophical concept for this kind of “tool-using but ungrounded” cognition that I am missing. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure. I do not need references, bibliographies, or reading lists—only conceptual clarity and an honest appraisal of where my argument might be stretching the taxonomy beyond its intended use.
