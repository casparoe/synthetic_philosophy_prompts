I'm preparing the interview guide for an upcoming episode of a philosophy podcast focused on value theory and future ethics. The guest is an unnamed philosopher—let's call her the interlocutor—who has recently been developing a modified version of Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: she accepts the core claim that human extinction would be bad not merely because of the deaths it involves but because it undermines the value of present activities, yet she rejects the idea that what matters is the biological continuation of the species per se. Instead, she defends what she terms an "institutional afterlife" thesis, according to which our projects derive their present significance from the anticipated continuation of specific normative practices—scientific inquiry, artistic tradition, democratic deliberation—rather than from the mere persistence of human beings. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this position. At least one question should press on the psychological versus metaphysical reading of the dependency: does extinction diminish value because our attitudes would shift, or because the value-conditions of our projects are genuinely undermined? Another should target the boundary of her "institutional" revision—if post-human artificial agents or radically altered descendants could continue these practices, does extinction of biological humanity still register as the loss she describes? I also want a question that anticipates the standard reply that this is just a sophisticated form of legacy-egoism, with a follow-up keyed to her likely distinction between caring about one's personal reputation and caring about the normative inheritance chain. Include one question that applies her framework to the "last generation" scenario—whether knowing we are the final cohort should lead us to abandon long-term projects like basic science or child-rearing, and whether her view can explain why that feels like a loss without collapsing into a direct obligation to reproduce. Finally, include a follow-up sequence that makes her "institutional" revision progressively harder to maintain: start with whether her view can accommodate projects that seem valuable precisely because they are self-contained and need no continuation, then move to whether the view over-intellectualizes ordinary motivation, and end with what kind of empirical or conceptual evidence could count against the claim that our values are structurally dependent on an anticipated future at all. Each question should be phrased for a listening audience but precise enough that a philosopher of value theory would recognize where the literature presses and where her revision departs from Scheffler's original framing.
