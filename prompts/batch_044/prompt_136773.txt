I'm trying to map out when an inference genuinely counts as jumping out of the system, in the sense Douglas Hofstadter discusses with formal systems like the MU-puzzle and the idea of JOOTSing, versus when it is merely an evasion or a category mistake. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, observing that the MIU system's axiom preserves even parity of I's and using that invariant to show MU is unreachable: I take this as a legitimate jump out, because the reasoning operates at a meta-level that the formal rules do not capture. Second, a mathematician who resolves an undecidable statement by appealing to an intuitive physical model of computation, then formalizing that intuition in a strictly stronger axiomatic system: legitimate, but only because the jump is eventually bridged back to a formal meta-system. Third, a moral reasoner who rejects an expected-utility framework by saying I simply do not care about aggregate welfare in that way, with no further structural argument: I rate this as not a true jump, but a bare refusal that offers no meta-principle. Fourth, resolving a semantic paradox by ascending to a higher-order language with explicit truth predicates arranged in a hierarchy: clearly legitimate. Fifth, a researcher who explains away a reproducible experimental anomaly by postulating an unobserved instrumental failure without independent calibration checks: I think this is an illegitimate jump, a defensive invocation of an unverified meta-hypothesis. Sixth, a chess player who claims the rules of checkmate do not bind him: plainly not a jump, just an error. The tension for me is between the third and fifth cases—both invoke something outside the present framework, yet I treat one as honest refusal and the other as evasion. Is there a precise principle that distinguishes legitimate system-jumping from illegitimate escape, and can it accommodate both verdicts? If not, which should I drop? Please state the principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; apply it to an unlisted case—say, a constitutional scholar who argues that an amendment is invalid because it violates the uncodified structural spirit of the original framework; and say honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot save.
