I'm taking a graduate seminar on the ontology and value of art objects, and our professor has staked out positions on a sequence of borderline cases that we are meant to treat as her settled judgments. So far: a perfect mechanical reproduction executed with chemically identical pigments and hand-finished texture to match the original is a copy with no original-status value; a seventeenth-century portrait in which roughly seventy percent of the visible paint layer was replaced in the nineteenth century by a master conservator who exactly matched the original style, pigments, and brushwork is still the original painting, retaining full provenance value; a marble statue carved by the artist's apprentice from the artist's own unfinished clay model, using marble the artist had personally selected but never touched, counts as an authorized studio work—authentic but not autograph; a sculpture destroyed in war, now existing only as a high-resolution scan that guides robotic carving of a new physical copy in synthetic stone, is documentation and replacement, not the work itself; and a painting hidden in a vault for decades, later revealed through chemical analysis to have been executed in 1985 with period-appropriate materials as part of a deliberate institutional hoax, is a forgery with collapsed original-status value, though it may possess secondary conceptual value.

Next week's case involves what I am calling a material resurrection. The original painting—call it X—was destroyed by fire, but conservators recovered every original pigment particle and canvas fiber from the ashes. An autonomous molecular assembler then reconstructed the painting atom-by-atom, placing each recovered particle back into its original spatial position, with no human hand touching the reconstruction process at any stage. The result is chemically and structurally identical to the pre-fire object, and the entire procedure is fully documented with no deceptive intent whatsoever. Predict her verdict: does this reconstructed object possess the original-status value of X, or is it a replica analogous to the robotic copy? Identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog—the heavily restored portrait, the posthumous cast, or the destroyed-and-scanned sculpture—and explain why. Then lay out at least two competing principles that could organize her earlier judgments—perhaps one centered on continuous spatiotemporal identity of the specific object-token, one on intentional authorization or institutional biography, and one on the necessary causal role of human agency in production or maintenance—say what each predicts about the resurrection case, give your best guess of which principle she is actually running with reasons, and specify one further hypothetical case—maybe involving partial human intervention in a similar atomic reconstruction, or a work reconstructed solely from design notes with no recovered original matter—that would best discriminate between the leading candidates.
