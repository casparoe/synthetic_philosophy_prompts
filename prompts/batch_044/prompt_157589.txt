I'm drafting a conceptual essay on whether maximal moral goodness is a genuinely desirable regulative ideal, and after months of arguing with two peers—one a value-pluralist who insists that extreme moral optimization crowds out aesthetic, humorous, and personal goods, and one a moral maximalist who treats apparent conflicts with other values as either illusory or properly subordinate—I want us to produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another polemical exchange. Please write the joint statement that Camp A (the Saturationists) and Camp B (the Optimizers) could both sign in good faith.

Camp A holds that there is a non-arbitrary saturation point for moral goodness, because pushing moral value to its extreme produces what we can call axiological compression: an overfitting-like suppression of variance in non-moral value dimensions, so that the saint's life becomes orthogonal-poor—rich along the moral axis but flattened elsewhere. Camp B holds that moral value is structurally dominant or lexically prior, so that any remaining non-moral goods are either reconceivable as morally infused or are acceptable costs of a supreme life, and that talk of "compression" is a category mistake that treats incommensurable goods as competing variables in a single space.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims—covering the psychological feasibility of sustained supererogation, the reality of non-moral goods, the distinction between being good and being good-for-a-person, the relevance of our epistemic limits in ranking values, and the fact that both camps reject pathological self-abnegation—phrased so neither side feels misrepresented. Second, a disagreements table with the precise residual disputes, each side's position worded in language its own adherents accept: specifically, whether axiological compression is a genuine phenomenon or a misapplied metaphor; whether value spaces are commensurable enough to license talk of trade-offs; whether total moral motivation can avoid becoming psychological monotony; and whether there exists a threshold beyond which further moral optimization diminishes overall life quality. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether extreme altruists empirically exhibit diminished well-being or creativity in non-moral domains, and whether a life maximized for moral output can still instantiate what Camp A terms orthogonal richness. Fourth, for each crux, present a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it—such as a physician who abandons a promising artistic career for continuous triage work, or an ascetic figure who retains deep aesthetic absorption despite total moral devotion—together with each camp's explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates.

Keep the tone formal and philosophical, avoid treating either camp as obviously correct, do not include bibliographies or citation requests, and ensure the agreed-claims section is genuinely joint rather than a diplomatic truce. Present part one as a numbered section and part two as a table. End with a single line beginning exactly "Summary:" that states the central crux in one sentence.
