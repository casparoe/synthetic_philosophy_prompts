Two colleagues in a philosophy seminar were discussing whether Gottlob Frege's late political diary—containing nationalist and anti-Semitic sentiments—has any legitimate bearing on his philosophical work, particularly his anti-psychologism and the sense/reference distinction. Their exchange went as follows.

(1) The first argued that the diary is not merely biographically embarrassing but philosophically revealing. She claimed that Frege's logical project of purging "subjective" psychological content from thought (Gedanke) exhibits a structural resonance with his political desire for a culture purified of foreign influence. His sharp separation between objective logic and embodied psychology, she said, mirrors the sharp separation he draws in the diary between an authentic German identity and outsiders; the universal form of his logic masks a particularist selection of which thoughts count as worthy of preservation.

(2) The second replied that this commits a genetic fallacy. Frege's anti-psychologism is motivated by technical failures in psychologistic accounts of arithmetic—such as the inability to explain the objectivity of number—not by ethnic nationalism. Furthermore, the sense/reference distinction is a semantic tool for analyzing language, not a cultural filter. He added that Frege's universalism actually contradicts nationalism: if thoughts are objective and belong to all rational beings regardless of origin, then nationalist exclusivism is philosophically incoherent on Frege's own terms. The diary therefore reveals personal hypocrisy, not philosophical content.

(3) The first countered that even universalist frameworks can embed particularist values in their architecture. She suggested that Frege's choice of logical primitives and his insistence on a single, correct formalization of arithmetic reflect a preference for purity and uniqueness that parallels his political rhetoric. The rejection of psychologism, she maintained, is not just about arithmetic but about delegitimizing the plural, embodied, and culturally situated dimensions of cognition—dimensions he associated in the diary with corruption.

(4) The second responded that this interpretation overreads metaphor into logic. The normative force of inference rules derives from their role in preserving truth across contexts, not from a cultural preference for uniformity. To link Frege's formalism to his politics is to confuse the context of discovery with the context of justification in a domain—pure logic—where only the latter matters. The diary is historically significant but philosophically inert: it does not affect the validity of the Grundgesetze or the objectivity of sense.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification. Then identify what both interlocutors are missing—whether a more precise conceptual distinction, a relevant feature of Frege's actual logical texts, or a methodological principle that should govern how we relate biographical evidence to philosophical argument.
