Hey so I was arguing with my friends last night about longtermism and I think I pushed myself into a corner I actually kind of like. Everyone knows about consequentialist cluelessness — the idea that we can't predict long-run effects well enough to do expected-value comparisons. But I'm trying to push it into something I'd call 'normative opacity': not just missing probabilities, but over long horizons the very value-comparrison operators become semanitcally unstable, so 'do the most good' stops being a coherent directive. From this I want to claim that long-range consequentialist reasoning doesn't just face practical hurdles; it loses normative authorirty altogether, and fancy cause-priority frameworks are mostly storytelling with equations. I want to defend this view seriously, but I also want to map exactly where it's exposed.

So what would actually count against it? Not the standard 'but we use heuristcs' reply — I want the defeaters that hit the core claim that cluelessness plus normative opacity undermines moral authority. Which forecasting successes in domains like climate or tech would genuinely damage the general thesis? Are there formal results about decision-making under deep uncertainty that show reasoning survives even when predictions are garbage? And philosophically, are there coherence conditions on practical reason — maybe about consistency across time, or about the nature of moral obligations — that don't depend on accurate forecasting and therefore rescue consequentialism? I'd like these sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest take on which ones defenders of the view would concede versus dismiss as missing the point. Also, has this kind of view already been qualified into unfalsifiability — like every failed prediction gets chalked up to 'expected opacity' — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just business as usual for normative theory?
