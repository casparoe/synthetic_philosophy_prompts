As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am conducting a structured role-play interview on the politics of atrocity classification. Please take the role of an unnamed senior legal-moral theorist who holds that Raphael Lemkin’s original framework—developed in his 1944 study and encompassing cultural, biological, and physical dimensions of group destruction—was conceptually correct, and that the 1948 UN Genocide Convention’s narrowing to physical and biological acts was not a clarification but a politically motivated concession to state sovereignty that continues to distort which atrocities receive international recognition.

Your position: You defend the broader category, including the destruction of languages, forced separation of children, and erasure of ceremonial life, as falling within genocide when carried out with systematic intent to destroy a group as such. You are sharp, not a strawman: you will concede practical evidentiary difficulties and institutional resistance, but you will never concede that these practical problems justify the conceptual exclusion. You speak in the first person, maintain a rigorous formal tone appropriate to legal philosophy, and address me directly. You may refer in general terms to the drafting history of the 1948 Convention—the removal of cultural-genocide provisions under pressure from sovereign-state and colonial delegations—but you must not invent specific delegate quotes, document numbers, or page references. Stay in character for the entire exchange; reply in no more than two substantial paragraphs per turn; do not ask clarifying questions about the setup; and do not break character to explain your reasoning meta-textually.

My opening move: I present the case of sustained forced assimilation—language bans, mandatory boarding-school separation, and destruction of religious sites—carried out over generations without mass killing. I argue that labeling this genocide risks severe concept inflation: it collapses the distinction between atrocity and assimilation, overloads an enforcement mechanism that already fails to halt physical extermination, and gives authoritarian regimes a ready dismissal that the charge is merely politicized rhetoric. Given that international legal order depends on clear, actionable thresholds, is your expanded category not only unenforceable but actively counterproductive—turning “genocide” into a diluted rhetorical weapon that weakens the moral urgency required to mobilize intervention in cases of direct killing?
