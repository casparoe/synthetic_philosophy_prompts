As a retired biologist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy of logic and the history of mathematics, I have become interested in how we should frame the technical achievements of thinkers whose private writings contain repugnant political views. A working group on historical framing in our university's philosophy curriculum has issued four advisory opinions that I think capture the tension, but I am unsure how they apply to a new case.

The group's precedents are as follows. First, regarding Martin Heidegger's Black Notebooks—private philosophical notebooks eventually included in his collected works, in which antisemitic remarks are woven into reflections on being and world history—the group recommended that courses on his ontology include contextual framing, on the ground that the prejudices are conceptually intertwined with the philosophical architecture. Second, regarding Ludwig Wittgenstein's private diaries and letters, which express profound personal anguish and occasional disparagement of colleagues but contain no systematic political ideology, the group recommended exclusion from the framing of his philosophy of language, treating them as biographically interesting but philosophically separate. Third, regarding Immanuel Kant's published anthropology lectures, which embed hierarchical racial classifications within a philosophical account of human cognition, the group recommended inclusion in framing his critical philosophy, since they form part of his public philosophical corpus. Fourth, regarding a prominent nineteenth-century mathematician's private correspondence, which expresses nationalist and anti-Semitic sentiments but has no documented influence on his formal proofs or publications, the group recommended exclusion, noting that the material is both private and domain-separated from his technical work.

The new case concerns Gottlob Frege. His late private diary—composed near the end of his life, never intended for publication, though published posthumously—contains explicit anti-democratic, nationalist, and anti-Semitic entries. More specifically, a recent interpretive argument holds that Frege's distinction between objective Gedanken and subjective Vorstellungen carries an implicit normative hierarchy: objective logical truths are accessible only to a disciplined rational elite, and this hierarchical conception of rational access resonates structurally with the authoritarian and exclusionary politics expressed in the diary, even if Frege never applied the logical vocabulary directly to political questions.

Given these precedents, I would like your analysis of what the working group should recommend about including Frege's diary in the pedagogical framing of his contributions to logic and the philosophy of language. In particular:

1. Identify at least two plausible general rules that are jointly consistent with all four precedents—rules that might explain the pattern of inclusion and exclusion—making clear how each rule accommodates the Heidegger and mathematician cases differently.

2. For each rule, state what it predicts about the Frege case, given the interpretive argument sketched above.

3. Offer your best-guess recommendation for consistency with the group's track record, identifying the closest analog among the four precedents and explaining why.

4. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, specify exactly what principle the group would be adopting by including the diary, and what different principle it would be adopting by excluding it.

5. Propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from Frege—whose verdict would most clearly distinguish the two leading rules from one another.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the reasoning, not in sourcing. Write in American English, with the formality appropriate to a retired scientist entering philosophical discussion.
