I recently began co-facilitating a philosophical salon in which communal discussion is paired with regulated drinking, and the practice made Plato's Magnesian code in the Laws suddenly concrete rather than antiquarian. I am applying for a six-month independent research stipend to produce a monograph of about eight thousand words. The precise question is whether, in Laws Book I and II, Plato treats regulated communal intoxication primarily as an epistemic stress-test that reveals a citizen's pre-existing temperamental structure, or as a formative practice that gradually reshapes that structure through repeated ritual exposure. Existing scholarship generally splits between pragmatic readings of the symposiarch's supervision and ironic readings that distance Plato from Socratic asceticism, yet neither distinguishes the revelatory function from the pedagogical one; consequently they cannot explain why suitability for magistracy must be observed specifically in the state of controlled disinhibition rather than in sobriety. My method will proceed in four phases: first, a close analysis of the drinking laws and the psychology of appetite in Laws Book I-II; second, a comparison with the Republic's account of guardian education to determine whether the Laws relaxes or merely relocates the same psychological constraints; third, an examination of Alcibiades' intoxicated speech in the Symposium to assess whether Plato presents alcohol-induced disclosure as epistemically reliable, systematically deceptive, or ambiguously both; fourth, synthesis and drafting. A successful result would yield a taxonomy of the conditions under which Plato thinks intoxication produces true judgment about character versus false appearance, together with an explanation of why the Laws permits what the Republic's guardians are denied without collapsing into contradiction. The project fails if the texts reveal no stable distinction between disclosure and formation, or if the tension with Republic psychology resolves into a standard developmental narrative that requires no special account of ritual drinking. I ask that the response state precisely what consideration would overturn its central thesis—whether, for example, showing that the symposiarch's observations serve only punitive rather than diagnostic purposes—and identify where its reasoning is most likely to fail, probably in treating Alcibiades as an intentional negative exemplum rather than an ambiguous limit case. I will supply all citations myself; please do not include a bibliography or reading list.
