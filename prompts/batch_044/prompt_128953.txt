I spent the last three hours in a heated but friendly argument with a colleague about whether hypocrisy cancels blame or only changes its grammar, and now I have to open tomorrow's workshop with that tension still alive. The session is "Accountability Without Scapegoating," co-hosted by a philosophy department and a community restorative-justice collective; the audience is split between moral philosophers familiar with the standing-to-blame literature, legal practitioners who think in procedural terms, and organizers who have actually facilitated circles where an institution's representative apologized for harm they never personally caused. I need a ten-minute opening address that does not resolve the dispute but uses it to frame everything that follows, because the philosophical distinction should do real structural work in the room: it should determine what kinds of statements are available to us and what kinds are foreclosed.

The idea I kept returning to with my friend — and that I want the speech to turn on — is a distinction I am not fully sure holds up but want tested in public: between "standing to blame" as a relational entitlement to hold someone answerable, and "standing to accuse" as the performative authority to deliver a verdict from a position of unexamined moral cleanliness. Our argument circled whether hypocrisy undermines both, or only the second: if a city government fines a small factory for emissions violations while its own waste contract is in breach, has it lost the right to blame altogether, or has it merely lost the right to speak as though it were blameless? I want the remarks to show that this is not a semantic quibble but a practical fork — that an institution can legitimately lament a wrong and demand repair without claiming elevation, but that once it adopts the grammar of accusation its own record becomes fair game in ways that can paralyze action. The speech needs to land for the specialists without losing the practitioners, so it should avoid unnecessary jargon, carry the distinction through one concrete constructed scenario, and never pretend there is an easy institutional remedy.

Please write the address. Open with the specific moment from my argument — my friend asking whether a hypocritical judge can still render a correct verdict — and then move through the distinction, showing how it reshapes what can actually be said in restorative settings. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the audience needs working ideas, not homework. The tone should be formal but conversational, suited to opening remarks rather than a lecture, and it should close with a framing question that sets up the day's panels without resolving whether hypocrisy nullifies blame or only transforms its grammar. Keep it to roughly ten minutes when spoken, and make sure the philosophical point is doing the architectural work rather than serving as decoration.
