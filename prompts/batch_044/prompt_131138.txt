I've been wondering about the philosophical assumptions buried in how different states handle citizen visibility — Germany's constitutional informational self-determination and strict data minimisation, America's FOIA culture and notice-and-consent market logic, and China's social credit system where your behaviour is totally exposed but the algorithm stays opaque. What theory of the self and of state knowledge does each actually presuppose? Is German data protection treating personal info as an extension of bodily integrity, the US treating it as a commodity you can trade away with informed consent, and China treating it as social residue that never belonged to you individually? Or can all three be reconstructed under a single theory of informational autonomy with just different weights on transparency?

I'm particualrly interested in whether there's an implicit epistemology running through them — like, American openness assumes citizens need to see the state's files to hold power accountable, while Chinese opacity assumes state knowledge is legitimate precisely when subjects can't inspect how it's built. Then there's the German asymmetry: you can inspect your own files, but third parties are blocked. That sounds like it presupposes self-knowledge is a fundamental right but being-known-by-others is a harm. Is that actually required by the law, or is it just one interpretation? And if we replaced that view with a straightforwardly relational account — where identity is partly constituted by being seen by others — what would have to change in German data protection practice?

There's also a niche analogy I've seen in philosophy of information: the quantum no-cloning theorem, where observing a quantum state necessarily disturbs it. Some people use this to argue personal data behaves like a quantum system — you can't copy or observe it without altering the subject's informational state. Do any of these regimes implicitly depend on something like that, or is it just a fancy metaphor? And if it turned out to be literally false — if data could be copied without disturbance — would that undermine any of the legal frameworks, or do they stand on purely normative grounds?
