In aquaculture regulation and welfare certification—such as EU Council Directive 98/58/EC, RSPCA Assured, and ASC standards—farmed salmonids are covered by stunning requirements, stocking-density limits, and feed-withdrawal rules, while penaeid shrimp, lobsters, crabs, and other decapod crustaceans are systematically excluded, even though the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 recognizes decapods and cephalopods as sentient and despite substantial evidence of nociception and stress responses in shrimp. At the same time, some of the very jurisdictions that exclude crustaceans from farm-welfare rules prohibit live boiling of lobsters in retail or restaurant contexts, apparently on sentience grounds. What philosophical commitments about moral status, epistemic access to suffering, and the scope of institutional obligation does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: is it that standing requires a vertebrate-like central nervous system, that welfare duties apply only where suffering is behaviorally legible to human auditors, that tractability silently determines the boundary of concern, or some hybrid? Say whether the practice actually needs any one of these commitments or is compatible with several rival theories of value, assess whether the parts are coherent given the legal recognition of decapod sentience elsewhere, and describe what an aquaculture policy built on a uniform, capacity-based view of welfare would have to change in its auditing, stunning, and stocking practices. In your response, state how confident you are in each part of your reconstruction, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
