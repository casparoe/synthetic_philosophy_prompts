For a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar with 12 students, design a runnable in-class activity centered on a modified centipede game that makes participants feel the backward-induction paradox without allowing them to rely on a standard textbook script. Use pairs or triads. The visible payoff tree should be linear and increasing across four decision nodes, with a take option that splits the pot and ends the round. To inject novelty, assign each student a hidden reasoning-type—myopic, one-step-backward-inductor, or fully backward-inductor—that secretly alters their hidden payoff for taking at each node, revealed only after all rounds are complete. Students decide to continue or take at each node knowing only the visible tree and knowing that everyone has one of the three hidden types, but not knowing anyone's actual assignment. The rules and point-based payoff scheme should be arranged so that rational play depends on beliefs about the distribution of types at the next node, causing cooperation to collapse in practice not because students can compute a fixed endpoint, but because common belief in rationality unravels through second-guessing. Include: clear rules and minimal materials; the payoff table; facilitator instructions for secret assignment and post-game revelation; a debrief that maps specific moments of early defection or unexpected continuation onto the formal backward-induction chain; an honest note on which feature of the paradox—the deterministic common-knowledge rationality chain—this hidden-type version cannot reproduce and that you must supply verbally; and a prediction of the predictable failure mode (e.g., students treating the game as pure type-guessing rather than induction). Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Focus on the design, the conceptual mapping, and the practical pitfalls.
