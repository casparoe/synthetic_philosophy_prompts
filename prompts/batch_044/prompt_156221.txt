I've been studying Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and find myself puzzled by how his hermeneutic theory actually operated under institutional pressure. Analyze the episode of Galileo's 1633 trial and the preceding 1616 injunction against Copernicanism through the lens of Galileo's own two-books hermeneutic — the claim that the book of nature and the book of scripture, having one divine author, cannot genuinely contradict one another when each is read within its proper domain. Specifically, reconstruct how Galileo's framework distinguishes between passages that require accommodative interpretation — such as the Joshua narrative in which the sun is commanded to stand still — and claims that rest on direct sensory demonstration, and apply this distinction to explain why he might have treated heliocentrism as belonging to natural philosophy rather than to scriptural exegesis.

Then determine whether his position implies a strict non-overlapping separation of domains, or rather a hierarchical reconciliation in which demonstrated natural knowledge eventually obliges a revised scriptural reading, and say what each interpretation implies about the philosophical consistency of his 1633 recantation. Finally, assess whether the Roman Inquisition's judgment can be reconstructed as implicitly accepting Galileo's hermeneutic but restricting its scope to non-Copernican cases, or whether it necessarily rejects that hermeneutic in favor of an alternative theory of textual authority — and if the latter, specify what philosophical commitments about the relationship between ecclesiastical interpretation and empirical evidence that rival theory requires.
