I am a doctoral student in comparative historical sociology, and I have noticed a recurring methodological asymmetry in the application of base-rate reasoning to imperial decline that I cannot map cleanly onto the conceptual vocabulary I have encountered.

Consider the following pattern, which I have observed repeatedly in studies that apply an outside view to the fate of large territorial polities. Analysts begin by defining a reference class of past empires that shared a specific cluster of macro-features—say, simultaneous fiscal contraction, frontier overextension, and elite fragmentation—compute a collapse rate within that class, and apply it as a predictive base rate to a current or recent case. When the polity in question does collapse, scholars treat it as a confirming instance: the reference class is left intact, and the base rate is cited as having been borne out.

However, when a polity facing the same cluster survives and adapts—reforming its fiscal apparatus, reorganizing its military, or fragmenting into durable successor states—the literature rarely treats the survival as a straightforward falsification of the base rate. Instead, analysts frequently introduce a post-hoc exclusion criterion: they argue that the surviving polity must have possessed some hidden structural feature—an unusually resilient bureaucratic-legal framework, a particular geopolitical buffer, or an atypical mode of elite integration—that should have placed it outside the original reference class. Crucially, this refinement operates asymmetrically. Scholars do not apply a parallel exclusion to collapsed cases by arguing that those fallen polities must have secretly lacked the hidden feature and therefore never belonged in the class. The boundary of the reference class is tightened around survivors but left loose around failures, preserving the predictive failure rate for the remaining pool.

This is not, I think, ordinary post-hoc rationalization of a single causal theory, because it operates on the comparative-statistical layer rather than on a specific narrative of decline. Nor is it exactly the general problem of selecting favorable analogies, because the selection mechanism is triggered specifically by survival outcomes and applies collectively to the sample rather than to an individual comparison. I am unsure whether this asymmetry has a recognized name within forecasting methodology, philosophy of science, or comparative history—perhaps as a specific failure mode of outside-view reasoning, or as a variant of sample-bias immunization applied to historical reference classes.

Could you identify whether an established concept captures this precise pattern: the outcome-contingent, one-directional tightening of reference-class boundaries in imperial forecasting, where survival prompts retrospective exclusion criteria but collapse does not? If the nearest analogues are general problems of ad hoc reference-class selection or immunizing strategies against falsification, I would ask you to draw the distinctions carefully—what exactly this pattern is, where its boundaries lie, and whether it differs from plain narrative adjustment in that it systematically preserves base rates by reshaping the sample rather than the theory. If no established term exists, I would welcome a precise coinage together with boundary conditions that distinguish legitimate historiographical refinement—say, discovering genuinely overlooked structural differences—from this particular failure mode.
