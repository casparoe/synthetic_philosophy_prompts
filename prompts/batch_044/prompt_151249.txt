I am a software engineer working on the backend of a platform that pairs individuals for shared accommodation. Our compatibility algorithm permits users to assign weights to various attributes—sleep patterns, standards of cleanliness, social habits—to generate a match score. Recently, we have observed a troubling pattern: when users rate 'cultural alignment' as highly important, the resulting pairings exhibit pronounced demographic clustering that closely resembles historical patterns of residential segregation.

Our product team argues that we ought not intervene, on the grounds that these preferences are sincerely held and do not explicitly invoke protected characteristics. To suppress them, they contend, would be unjustifiably paternalistic—analogous to overriding a person's genuine preference for a quiet flatmate over a noisy one. While I recognise the force of this objection, I am concerned that we are merely providing a technical veneer for prejudice. The users are not requesting specific ethnicities; they are requesting 'shared values' or 'similar upbringing'. Nevertheless, the distributive outcome is indistinguishable from exclusion.

I am attempting to understand whether moral philosophy offers any principled method for distinguishing an authentic preference from an inclination that has been shaped by the very social inequalities one wishes to avoid perpetuating. I have encountered references to preferences that form in response to unjust conditions, but I am uncertain how to apply this idea in our context. If an individual genuinely experiences discomfort within heterogeneous environments, does the sincerity of that feeling confer moral legitimacy upon the preference? Or does the fact that such discomfort frequently tracks lines of historical exclusion undermine its authority? Moreover, if we design a system that treats every sincerely reported preference as a legitimate input, do we not risk encoding existing social hierarchies directly into our infrastructure?

Could you assist me in clarifying whether there exists a coherent boundary here—particularly in instances where the distinction between taste and bias appears to dissolve upon closer examination?
