Right, I've been chewing over the usual comparison between French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism, and I'm pretty convinced they're not opposites at all—more like two varients of the same liberal move: turning messy moral disagreement into a bureacratic group-management problem. Multiculturalism essentialises groups by giving them standing as interlocuters—once you're recognised as a 'community', your internal dissent gets filed under cultural authenticity rather than politics. Laïcité flips it: it essentialises the state, treating republican neutrality as culturally blank when it's really a specific post-Catholic grammar that just hides assimilation under formal rules. So laïcité is at least honest about wanting one public language; multiculturalism pretends pluralism while quietly demanding every group package itself in state-legible form.

But I want to know what genuinely counts against this. Not just 'France has riots, Canada works'—I mean the kind of objection that'd force me to drop the core claim. Is there real evidence that multicultural policies preserve internal dissent rather than locking groups in? Can laïcité's blankness be defended as a learnable skill that doesn't secretly favour one heritage? And is the decisive risk fragmentation vs domination, or some third failure—like both suppressing cross-cutting, individual-level disagreement that neither framework can even parse?

If I'm wrong and they're not the same trick, what should I actually be seeing in, say, British multicultural education versus French civic training that my framing can't absorb?
