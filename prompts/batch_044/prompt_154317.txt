I'm taking a bioethics course and we got into a huge argument over coffee about advance directives — you know, those papers where you say "if I'm severely demented, don't keep me alive on machines." I tried to defend why they should still bind, and I built this argument, but now it feels like it eats everything else in ethics too and I can't find the exit.

(1) An advance directive binds a future patient only if that patient is the same person, in the morally relevant sense, as the one who signed it.
(2) That sameness isn't just body or memory; it's practical identity — continuity of your core values and what structures your choices.
(3) In late-stage dementia those values aren't just changed, they're gone. The patient might smile while being fed through a tube, even though the signer said that state was worse than death. So practical identity is broken.
(4) Therefore the directive shouldn't bind — it's not "me" refusing care, it's a seperate ghost writing rules for a stranger.
(5) But here's where it explodes. There's this experimental therapy for early degenerative conditions — not a cure, just a neural rewiring that inverts your value-map so you come to cherish exactly the dependencies and confusions you used to dread. You keep every memory, you can explain your old values in detail, but you sincerely think they were mistakes.
(6) By the criterion in (2), practical identity is broken here too: your evaluative framework is inverted, not tweaked.
(7) Still, we wouldn't say the post-therapy patient is a brand-new person who can tear up their marriage contract, ignore debts, or reject the consent form that authorized the therapy.
(8) So if I use value-continuity to break identity in dementia, I have to do it here too, which means anyone who undergoes radical value shift — conversion, trauma recovery, even just growing up — can walk away from past obligations.
(9) That can't be right. But I also don't want to say the dementia patient is still "the same person" in the relevant sense, because then we honor a dead value-set over a living patient's current will. Where's the slip? Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd really like to keep (2) and (4) if I can, but I'm open to loosing one.
