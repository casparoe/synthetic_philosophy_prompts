Consider a historian—call her Dr. Elena Voss—who sits on a municipal cultural-heritage board and has rendered clear verdicts on five recent proposals. First, she endorsed a proposal for a bronze monument to the town's founder that entirely omits his role in displacing an indigenous settlement, arguing that life needs its great images and that corrective scholarship belongs in classrooms, not in stone. Second, she endorsed without reservation a plan to climate-archive every market receipt and bus ticket from the twentieth century, with no interpretive framing whatsoever, insisting that the past must be preserved exactly as it was, untouched by present concern. Third, she rejected a secondary-school curriculum that taught students only to deconstruct local heroic myths, but she approved a revised version on the condition that students also construct a living continuation of one local craft or ritual, warning that pure demolition leaves life without soil. Fourth, she rejected a museum design that placed heroic narrative, raw archival display, and critical debunking in adjacent galleries meant to be visited together, calling it a paralysis of the will by contradictory histories. Fifth, she approved an open-access digital repository of the archived receipts that provides search and transcription tools but deliberately includes no narrative-generation features, stating that the document must remain sovereign.

The board now faces a new proposal: responsive memorial pylons for the town square. Each pylon houses the same pool of verified archival fragments—documents, photographs, data—drawn from the climate archive. A passerby selects one of three modes via a simple interface: monumental (an inspiring synthetic narrative), antiquarian (the raw fragment displayed with full provenance but no interpretation), or critical (the fragment contextualized to undermine heroic myths). The pylon physically prevents selecting more than one mode at a time, and the three modes never interact, reference each other, or appear simultaneously. The user may switch modes on a return visit, but there is no curatorial guidance about which mode is appropriate for which person or occasion.

Predict Dr. Voss's verdict—approval, conditional approval, or rejection—giving a definite answer rather than a list of possibilities. Identify the implicit principle she has been running across her five earlier judgments: is it strict separation of Nietzsche's three uses of history, a hierarchy in which history must serve present life through a unified curatorial will, an integrity principle that protects raw antiquarian material from narrative corruption, or something else? Lay out at least one other principle that fits her track record equally well, explain what that rival principle predicts about the pylons, and say which of the two rules you actually think governs her thinking, with reasons. Finally, specify one further case—different from the five above and from the pylons—that would best distinguish your preferred rule from the rival. Write in American English and take a committed position throughout.
