and heres the answer my classmate turned in:
Avicenna asks us to imagine a person suspended in empty air, blindfolded, with limbs spread apart so they cant feel anything, and they've never had sensory experience before. Even in that state, they'd still be aware that they exist—their 'I'—so this proves that knowing yourself doesn't need your body or senses at all. Its basically the same argument Descartes makes with the cogito, where you doubt everything and realize you exist as a thinking thing. Avicenna just gets there without the doubt step.

Now, an embodied-cognition theorist might say that even suspended, you'd still have internal proprioceptive loops, so Avicenna's setup is impossible—there's always some bodily feedback happening. But I think that objection misses Avicenna's actual point, which is that the soul grasps its own being directly through presence-knowledge without mediation. The embodied view targets the premise that bodily feedback is totally absent, but Avicenna already agrees the body is an instrument for other kinds of knowledge, just not for this basic self-certainty. So the embodied critique doesn't really land, and Avicenna's argument actually supports a Cartesian-style dualism: the soul is a thinking substance that could exist without the body altogether, because self-awareness is totally independent of physical processes.
