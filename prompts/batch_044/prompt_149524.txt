I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Boundary of Calling: Work, Leisure, and the Thirty-Five-Hour Limit." The audience is divided: half are scholars of economic culture and labor politics who know Weber's Protestant ethic thesis well, who have studied the Japanese legal and medical recognition of karoshi, and who have written on the politics of the thirty-five-hour week; the other half are union organizers and occupational-health advocates who have fought for working-time reduction and overwork protections but who are skeptical of academic abstraction. I need a polished, formal address of about five minutes. The philosophical tension between work as moral vocation and leisure as an inalienable right must do structural work in the speech—not decorative, but determinative: it should shape what claims I can and cannot make. Specifically, I must be able to acknowledge that reducing work hours protects human life without sounding like I am dismissing labor's dignity, and I must be able to take vocation seriously without sounding like I am apologizing for exploitation. The speech should not resolve this tension cleanly; the workshop depends on keeping it alive. It must be fully accessible to the non-academic half—no jargon, no assumed familiarity with technical philosophical vocabulary—while remaining precise enough that the scholars do not feel patronized. Crucially, do not include a bibliography, citation, reading list, or recommendation for further reading. Write the speech so that its closing line frames the workshop's central question as genuinely open: whether leisure is best understood as a protective limit placed upon vocation, or whether vocation must be reconceived so that leisure is not an exception to work but integral to its meaning.
