I am a novelist, and for the past twelve years I have been writing about obligations that survive their origins—inheritances of duty signed by strangers, verbal commitments made in dead languages, and institutional promises that outlast the buildings in which they were spoken. Next month I am to deliver the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop, held in a converted library reading room, on the question of why promises bind; the audience is split evenly between philosophers of action and practicing mediators and contract lawyers, all of whom know the standard terrain well enough to finish one another’s sentences. I want you to write a speech of about eight minutes—formal but intimate, that of a novelist addressing experts rather than lecturing them—built around an injected conceptual framework I will call “promissory residue”: the claim that a promise binds not chiefly through the expectation it creates in the promisee, but through the normative remainder, the residue of accountability, that persists even after the promise is broken, its conditions become impossible, or the parties forget its exact terms. This idea should do real structural work: it should determine what I can and cannot say to the room—preventing false comfort about broken commitments, allowing the lawyers to hear something about the durability of contractual duty independent of enforcement, and letting the philosophers recognize a genuine departure from expectation-based binding. Please avoid any bibliography, citation, named text, or reference to specific philosophers by name; the speech must stand on its own for an audience that cares about the concept in one half of the room and about the atmosphere in the other. To ground the framework, include one concrete, slightly strange hypothetical—perhaps a promise made across a language barrier that is later revealed to have been mistranslated—to show how the residue operates independently of shared expectation, and let the closing lines return to the image of obligations that outlive their makers, without pretending that all such obligations are benign.
