My colleague in the philosophy of mind and epistemology has been assessing whether reconstructed arguments qualify as genuinely transcendental—understood in a broadly Kantian register as establishing synthetic a priori conditions without which some fundamental capacity for judgment or experience would be incoherent, rather than as analytic truths, empirical hypotheses, or normative prescriptions. Her verdicts so far are these: Kant's argument in the Second Analogy that objective temporal succession requires causality, yes, paradigmatic; Strawson's argument that self-conscious experience requires an objective spatio-temporal framework within which self and other are distinguishable, yes; Davidson's argument that radical interpretation requires a background of shared true beliefs governed by the principle of charity, no, this is a condition on linguistic interpretation rather than on the possibility of experience or judgment; a reconstructed argument that numerical identity of consciousness across time requires a transcendental synthesis of reproduction in memory, yes; and an argument that transcendental freedom of the will is a necessary condition for moral responsibility, no, she treats this as a practical postulate rather than a condition on a theoretical capacity.

The case for next week is this reconstructed argument: that epistemic reciprocity—the acknowledgment that others are authoritative correctors of one's beliefs—is not merely a social virtue but a transcendental condition specifically for the sustained practice of collective inquiry, such that while a solitary inquirer might deny it without incoherence, any agent who participates in shared reasoning and denies this reciprocity undermines the very practice they inhabit.

Predict her verdict. Identify the implicit organizing principle—whether she restricts transcendental arguments to conditions for individual theoretical cognition, or whether she permits them for any rational practice in which denial renders one's own participation incoherent, or something else altogether—and defend your reading against the nearest alternative. Then specify the single further case that would best discriminate between your leading interpretations.
