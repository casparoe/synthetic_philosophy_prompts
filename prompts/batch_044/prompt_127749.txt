ok so i was trying to work through Kant's view on animals and i think i accidentally built an argument that proves way too much. here's the chain:

(1) Kant says direct duties only run to beings with rational nature—autonomous will—since only they can be ends in themselves.
(2) Animals lack that, so no direct duties to them.
(3) The only reason animal cruelty is wrong is indirect: it hardens us toward humans.
(4) So the wrongness is totally derivative—if harming an animal never affected our treatment of people, there'd be no moral problem with it.
(5) Now picture a being that's behavorally identical to a human—screams, bleeds, begs—but it's just a biological shell with no nouminal rational nature, like a perfect zombie-duplicate or an AI that mimics suffering perfectly. And I hurt it in a sealed context where I'm psychologically certain it won't spill over into my human interactions.
(6) By (3) and (4), there's nothing wrong with that.
(7) But that makes moral status invisible: two entities that suffer identically, yet one is protected and the other can be tortured with impunitiy, just because of a hidden metaphysical fact and my mental hygiene.
(8) That feels like a reductio—if a moral theory lets you do that behind closed doors, it's broken.
(9) So either the indirect-duty framework collapses, or Kant secretly needs direct duties to non-rational beings.

which step do i reject? i kinda want to keep (1) and (3) but i'm not sure what that costs me. is there an equivocation between 'rational nature' and 'appearance of suffering' in (5) to (6)? or does Kant have some other resource—like a duty to develop our own moral feeling—that blocks the compartmentalization in (5) even without direct duties?
