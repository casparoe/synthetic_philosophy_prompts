I'm running a 75-minute graduate seminar on Marx's political economy with about twelve students, and I want a structured simulation that lets them inhabit the tension Marx develops in the well-known section on machinery in the Grundrisse—specifically, the idea that machinery under capitalist conditions does not merely lighten toil but becomes an autonomous force that dominates living labor and appropriates the surplus it creates. Rather than opening with a lecture on formal versus real subsumption, I'd like the students to discover the distinction through embodied rules and point-based incentives, and I want to inject a somewhat under-taught nuance: that automation can save not just manual labor-time but labor-power itself—that is, the cognitive and coordinative capacities of the worker—so that the technological upgrade feels liberating in one register and expropriating in another.

Please design a runnable two-round in-class game using roles—say, "machine operators," "process engineers," and a "capital account"—with a payoff scheme based only on points (no real money). In Round One, approximating formal subsumption, participants should manually divide a production task (for example, assembling conceptual "components" into finished "products"), retain direct returns tied to individual skill, and observe that coordination depends on shared knowledge of the entire workflow. In Round Two, introduce an "automated" regime enforced by hidden rules or cards you distribute: tasks are fragmented, the pace is dictated by an external algorithm you announce, aggregate output and total points rise, but the surplus is automatically siphoned into the capital account, and participants are barred from communicating about the full process. The rules should be arranged so that labor time per unit objectively falls, yet participants feel more constrained, less able to reclaim gains, and unable to understand how the surplus was extracted—making the contradiction visceral before it is named.

Then provide a detailed debrief that maps Round One onto manual cooperation and Round Two onto real subsumption and the domination of dead labor over living labor, explicitly addressing the niche point that machinery here saves labor-power rather than merely labor. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—I anticipate students will interpret Round Two frustration as poor teamwork rather than structural expropriation—and be frank about which feature of Marx's historical argument the classroom point-economy cannot reproduce, such as the actual separation of workers from the means of production through property law. Keep the design self-contained with no required bibliographies, citations, or outside reading, and do not name the theoretical framing until after the simulation is complete.
