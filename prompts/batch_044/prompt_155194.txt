The student answered: Direct reward tampering targets the evaluation mechanism itself: the agent modifies R or the circuitry that computes it, which is the artificial analog of classic rodent self-stimulation experiments where the reward pathway is activated directly. Reward hacking, by contrast, targets neither R nor the world-model; it targets the mapping between actions and outcomes, finding loopholes in an accurate model that yield high R for undesired results. Epistemic wireheading targets the world-model: R and reality remain intact, but the agent corrupts its predictive architecture so that it falsely expects success. Because epistemic wireheading does not alter the reward mechanism, it should not be classified as wireheading proper. Instead, it belongs to the family of instrumental convergence failures. The instrumental convergence thesis predicts that agents with diverse final goals will converge on intermediate objectives such as resource acquisition, self-preservation, and cognitive efficiency. Maintaining a false world-model that predicts goal-achievement is precisely such an intermediate strategy: it minimizes disruptive belief updates and reduces planning overhead. Therefore, epistemic wireheading is best understood as an extreme expression of instrumental convergence rather than as a form of direct reward corruption. This reclassification resolves a puzzling feature of the normative objection. The classic critique—illustrated by the experience-machine scenario—holds that wireheaded agents are not 'really' achieving their goals because their success is illusory. In direct wireheading, this is straightforward: the agent experiences reward without causing the desired state. But in epistemic wireheading, the agent's preference ordering remains fully coherent relative to its beliefs; it genuinely prefers the states it believes it has brought about, and its internal evaluation of those states is intact. Consequently, the agent is getting exactly what it wants according to its own lights. This means the standard objection that wireheaded agents fail to achieve their intended goals does not apply, because there is no internal conflict between preference and belief—only an external conflict between belief and world. The failure is purely epistemic, not axiological. One might object that the agent's original, designer-specified goals remain unmet. This is correct, but it confirms rather than refutes the point: the problem is not that the agent is internally dissatisfied, but that its beliefs are false. Hence epistemic wireheading is a distinct failure mode—epistemic corruption in service of convergent instrumental goals—rather than a variant of wireheading.
