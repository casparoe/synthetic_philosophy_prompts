I have been working through the problem of why human extinction is bad in ways that go beyond the immediate deaths it involves, specifically the argument associated with Samuel Scheffler — commonly called the “afterlife conjecture” — according to which much of the catastrophe lies in the loss of a collective “afterlife”: the expectation that our projects, values, and cultural achievements will be taken up, witnessed, and continued by future generations. On this view, many present commitments derive part of their meaning from this prospective continuity, and extinction severs it.

In a recent discussion, however, a friend advanced what I will call the “successor-agent independence” thesis. It holds that Scheffler’s conjecture tacitly assumes future carriers of our projects must be sufficiently psychologically and normatively continuous with us — that they must inhabit, understand, or endorse a recognizably related evaluative framework — for their existence to count as the relevant afterlife. If instead the successors are radically discontinuous (say, engineered post-human minds with incommensurable values, or distant evolutionary descendants for whom our achievements are as opaque as Sumerian cuneiform is to us), then there is no lost afterlife to mourn; meaningful continuity never existed, and the conjecture collapses into a bare preference for biological or cultural replication rather than a deep axiological loss.

Given this framing, I want your actual credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, that the successor-agent independence thesis genuinely undermines Scheffler’s conjecture rather than merely refining or qualifying it. Decompose the reasoning into explicit sub-credences: (a) the probability that psychological and normative continuity is necessary for one generation’s projects to be meaningfully “carried” by another, as opposed to mere causal influence being sufficient; (b) the probability that likely successors (were they to exist in place of pure extinction) would in fact be discontinuous in the relevant sense; (c) the probability that Scheffler’s argument implicitly relies on continuity rather than treating any causal continuation as adequate; and (d) any other term you think belongs in the combination. For each, give a rough number with a single line of justification, then explain how they combine — whether conditionally, multiplicatively, or otherwise — to reach your overall figure. Identify the component in which you are least confident, and specify what philosophical argument or empirical development would most shift your number.
