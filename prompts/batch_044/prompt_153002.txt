I run a philosophy of language workshop with about 14 grad students and advanced undergrads, one 80-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes Frege's puzzle about identity statements actually hurt a little — not just "Hesperus is Phosphorus" on a slide, but the feeling that a=a and a=b do different work in your head even when they point to the exact same thing. Please design the full activity: setting, roles/rules, materials, timeline, debrief, and an honest note on what part of Frege's view this can't reproduce.

The basic frame: split them into Bureau Morning and Bureau Evening. Each bureau gets a deck of "observation cards" describing orbital targets detected at different angles/times. Unbeknownst to them, four pairs of cards are actually the same physical satellite/artifact, but the descriptors are incommensrable across bureaus — Bureau Morning tracks thermal albedo and orbital velocity at dawn; Bureau Evening tracks reflected spectra and radar cross-section at dusk. Each bureau assigns internal registry IDs (M-01, E-12, etc.). They share one master registry board.

The rules should force them to feel the gap between reference and sense:
- Maintaining separate registry entries for what turns out to be the same object costs each bureau 2 "procesing tokens" per round.
- A correct merge (M-01 = E-12) frees 5 tokens and opens two registry slots.
- But they can't merge just by comparing raw card properties, because the property languages don't overlap. They need a "translation protocol" — a limited pool of shared keywords they can spend tokens to activate — to even propose an identity claim.
- Submitting an identity claim costs 1 verification token; if they're wrong (false merge of two different objects), the registry "collapses" and they lose 8 tokens.
- Meanwhile saying "M-01 is M-01" is always free, always correct, and gives them nothing.

Inject one niche mechanic that isn't standard classroom Frege: give each bureau a hidden "cognitive budget" of 10 units, and make it visible on the board that redundant representations are literally draining them. Add one trap card where two genuinely different objects share one translated property, so they feel the risk of false identity in their wallets, not just as an abstract mistake.

They should run into the crisis themselves — probably some bureaus hoard tokens and refuse all merges, others merge recklessly and hit the trap — before anyone names Frege, Sinn, or Bedeutung. Only in the debrief do you map:
- registry slots → reference
- internal IDs and translation costs → sense
- the token drain → the epistemic cost of redundant representations
- the verification gamble → the cognitive value/informativeness of an identity statement

Also include: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it; and be frank about which feature of Frege's original puzzle (maybe the purely logical/propositional nature, or the a priori status?) this institutional simulation just can't capture and I have to supply verbally.

No lecture up front, no bibliography, no citation requests — they shouldn't hear the name until after the registry crisis hits. Keep it casual; one or two typos is fine.
