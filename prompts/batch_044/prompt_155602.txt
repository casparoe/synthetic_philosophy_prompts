At a philosophy of mind reading group, two members argued over whether Douglas Hofstadter's view of the self as a self-referential pattern — sometimes called a strange loop — allows a single I to be genuinely distributed across two brains. They imagined a case in which a patient, Maya, suffers damage to the cortical regions that sustain her core loop. Clinicians preserve continuity by migrating key symbol-patterns into a hybrid architecture: part remains in Maya's residual cortex, part runs in her sister's brain via a sustained high-bandwidth neural interface that permits real-time integration. Once stabilized, the loop closes only when both substrates are active; either alone yields incoherence. Maya reports feeling like herself, but also that part of her awareness resides over there in her sister's head. Her sister reports experiencing Maya's memories and intentions as present but clearly alien to her own first-person perspective.

Member A argued that this is precisely what Hofstadter's framework predicts: the loop is the self, and since the loop now requires both brains to complete its self-reference, there is literally one I distributed across two bodies, no different in principle from one computation running on distributed servers.

Member B replied that Hofstadter's loop is not a bare functional circuit but a center of narrative gravity tied to a specific embodied point of view. By distributing the substrate across two intact biological brains — each with its own sensory framing, emotional coloring, and first-person perspective — you do not spread one self across space; you fracture Maya's loop and parasitize her sister's. The feeling of being partly elsewhere is an illusion produced by shared memory access, not evidence of distributed identity.

A countered that B is committing biological chauvinism. Substrate independence means that what matters is the preservation of the symbolic self-referential structure and its continuous coherence, not which skull houses the neurons. If the loop maintains its unity across both substrates, then demanding that it belong to exactly one biological center ignores Hofstadter's own emphasis on patterns over matter. Split-brain patients show one brain can host two loops, but here we have one loop requiring two brains; the asymmetry supports distribution rather than duplication.

B answered that split-brain cases actually support B's view. Severing the corpus callosum produces two loops because each hemisphere retains the capacity for self-reference. In Maya's case, neither brain has lost its own self-loop; both remain intact. What connects them is not a single loop stretched across space but a bridge allowing each intact loop to model the other. The result is mutual modeling and shared information, not a shared subject.
