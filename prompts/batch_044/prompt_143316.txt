I was discussing with a close friend over dinner—she is writing a dissertation on normative ethics and I am auditing a graduate seminar on moral theory—and our conversation turned into a long argument about the paradox of deontology. She maintains that if agent-centered restrictions genuinely prohibit actions such as intentional deception or killing regardless of aggregate consequences, then in symmetric rescue cases where violating the prohibition would avert catastrophic harm, the deontologist is trapped in a structural contradiction: either the restriction collapses into a concealed consequentialist threshold, or morality demands that we allow a worse world without any coherent account of why the agent’s practical identity should survive that choice intact.

I want to argue that this paradox is only apparent, and that it dissolves once deontological constraints are reconceived not as outcome-blocking rules but as conditions on the architecture of practical reasoning, specifically through a distinction between procedural invariance—the requirement that certain norms not be treated as fungible inputs to a calculus—and moral residue, which functions not as punishment but as an epistemic marker that the agent’s practical identity has been altered by treating the constraint as exchangeable. Please present this defense in exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; each point should advance the claim that deontology avoids the paradox by relocating normative work from outcome comparison to the preservation of deliberative structure, and should directly address why symmetric rescue cases fail to undermine procedural invariance.
