I'm currently enrolled in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar where we have been working through Ian Hacking's writings on classification, and I recently encountered a summary of his looping-effect thesis on a student discussion forum that strikes me as partly right but possibly distorted in important ways. The summary runs as follows. Hacking maintains that psychiatric categories are interactive kinds—unlike natural kinds such as chemical elements—because the individuals classified can become aware of the label, alter their self-understanding and conduct in response to it, and thereby change the very phenomena that clinicians seek to study, which in turn forces revisions to the diagnostic criteria. According to this account, every update to a manual like the DSM is therefore not an approximation to a pre-existing clinical reality but rather a direct trace of prior social influence, implying that psychiatric classifications possess no stable biological referent and are therefore purely socially constructed. The summary further claims that Hacking regards looping effects as exclusive to mental disorder, with no analog in physical medicine, and it concludes that because classification and classified behavior are locked in perpetual mutual adjustment, psychiatric diagnosis can never qualify as genuine scientific knowledge.

My instinct is that this captures the mechanism correctly but overstates Hacking's anti-realist and anti-scientific commitments, and I am unsure whether the exclusion of physical medicine is accurate or whether Hacking allows any biological anchoring at all. Could you examine the summary point by point, distinguishing what is faithful to Hacking's actual position, what represents a common exaggeration or conflation—perhaps with stronger social-constructionist views he does not hold—and what he would explicitly deny? In particular, I want to know whether the conclusion that psychiatric diagnosis is unscientific follows from premises he accepts, and whether his framework permits any role for biological factors in constraining the loop. Finally, please apply the corrected view to a case I have not seen discussed in the literature: imagine researchers propose a new diagnostic category, algorithmic attention fragmentation, for adolescents whose sustained attention declines specifically after prolonged interaction with recommendation-driven digital interfaces. How would Hacking's account predict the looping dynamics for such a category, and what would it say about the prospects for biological anchoring—say, in patterns of neural reward processing—once the classification enters clinical and popular discourse?
