I'm trying to formalize my intuitions about the procreation asymmetry—the idea that bringing a suffering being into existence is wrong, while failing to bring a happy being into existence is not wrong, at least not in the same way. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures them, or whether I must abandon some.

Case 1: A couple knows with near-certainty that any child they conceive will suffer from an incurable, severely painful condition. They conceive anyway. Verdict: clearly wrong.

Case 2: A couple could have a child who would live an excellent, flourishing life, but they remain childless to pursue other projects. Verdict: not wrong.

Case 3: Through genetic screening, a couple can guarantee a child with a life well above the neutrality threshold—robust health, rich relationships, meaningful work—but they decline and have no children. Verdict: still not wrong; there is no duty to create such a life.

Case 4: The couple uses screening to select for a life that is only barely net-positive: frequent minor discomforts, limited achievement, occasional pleasures that marginally outweigh the pains. Verdict: wrong, though less gravely than Case 1.

Case 5: The couple faces a reproductive technology choice. Option A guarantees one child with an excellent life. Option B carries a 90% chance of the same excellent outcome and a 10% chance of a severely suffering child; if suffering occurs, resource constraints mean the excellent child is never born. They choose B, and the suffering child results. Verdict: wrong, but I treat it differently from Case 1—there is an element of risk-taking and foregone benefit that makes the moral failure feel distinct, even though the outcome is identical.

Case 6: The couple knows that remaining childless will allow global resources to be redistributed, improving ten existing children from good lives to very good lives. If they have one child, that child will live a good life, but the ten others stay at merely good. Verdict: genuinely uncertain; my intuition permits them to have the child, but I cannot tell whether that is because the asymmetry blocks collective obligations or because I am implicitly weighing potential people differently.

Case 7: A researcher designs an embodied artificial consciousness knowing it will experience constant low-level distress—making its life net-negative for itself—while generating philosophical insights that benefit humanity. Verdict: wrong to create it. But if the being's life were net-positive for itself (just barely) and also beneficial to others, I think creation is permissible, though not obligatory.

The tension I keep returning to is between Case 1 and Case 5: both produce a suffering child, yet I judge them with different moral textures. Meanwhile, Case 6 introduces a collective comparison that does not involve potential persons directly, and Case 7 extends the asymmetry to non-biological, instrumentally valuable beings.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving expected harm, identity-affecting choices, or a well-being threshold—that delivers all seven verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify the clause that addresses each case, and say what it predicts for the following unlisted scenario: a couple can either conceive one child whose life will be barely above neutrality, or adopt an existing child who currently lives in severe poverty and whose life, if adopted, would be improved to barely-above-neutrality, using resources that would then preclude conception. Is conception permitted, required, or prohibited? And be explicit: which of my verdicts, if any, must I drop to maintain the principle?
