I'm working through whether comparative moral ranking of historical evils is possible without being obscene, and my case judgments feel inconsistent. I think it's necessary and not obscene to rank the Holocaust above the Armenian genocide, given the scale and industrial intentionality, and similarly to rank Mao's Great Leap Forward above Stalin's purges by magnitude of death for policy analysis. I think it's obscene, however, to rank the subjective suffering of individual survivors—say, a child in the Rwandan genocide versus one in the Cambodian killing fields—because trauma is incommensurable. Yet I hesitate to rank the long-term structural violence of Belgian colonial rule in the Congo, despite its enormous death toll, against the direct, intentional violence of the Nanjing Massacre, even though the toll was far smaller. I also think that refusing all comparative judgment collapses the distinction between industrial genocide and ordinary wartime excess, which seems morally disastrous. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing dimensions of comparison—that captures these verdicts? Apply it specifically to my Congo-versus-Nanjing hesitation and to a case I haven't listed: comparing the intentional design of the Rwandan genocide with the bureaucratic diffusion of responsibility in the Great Leap Forward. Finally, state honestly which of my intuitions, likely the one about survivor suffering being incommensurable, must be dropped to preserve coherence.
