For a feature I am preparing on why societies abruptly abandon practices they once vigorously defended—chattel slavery, dueling, foot-binding—I have sketched a working framework and want a consistency check before I present it to readers. Here is where I stand. First, I hold that large-scale moral revolutions are rarely caused by the intrinsic force of moral argument; instead they occur when a practice becomes status-degrading for elite reference groups whose behavior sets the social norm, a mechanism Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah have both emphasized, though I think they understate how small that reference group can be—sometimes well under three percent of a population—before a cascade takes hold. Second, I believe that economic and institutional self-interest is usually the proximal driver that makes the practice costly for those elites, which is why I treat the abolition of transatlantic slavery as largely a story of shifting geopolitical and industrial incentives rather than of philosophers persuading legislatures. Third, I maintain that once status and interest structures have turned, moral reasoning functions mainly as a legitimizing narrative for elites who have already decided to defect, not as an independent causal engine capable of overturning a stable practice on its own. Fourth, I am also a moral realist: I think there was an objective moral fact that chattel slavery was gravely wrong in 1750, and that its defenders then were not merely out of step with emerging honor codes but genuinely in error about a mind-independent moral reality that they could, in principle, have recognized through reason. Fifth, I think normative ethics should therefore be strategic: if we want to accelerate moral progress on issues such as factory farming or carbon-intensive consumption, we should engineer status and incentive shifts among high-status reference groups rather than refining syllogisms for a general audience. Sixth, I take it that dueling collapsed not because people suddenly grasped that killing over insults was irrational, but because the professionalization of military and civil bureaucracies made aristocratic honor codes obsolete, which in turn made dueling a marker of backwardness rather than nobility.

These six positions feel individually plausible, but I worry they pull in incompatible directions. Specifically, if moral arguments are always post-hoc legitimizations and moral change is driven entirely by elite status and material interest, then my moral realism about slavery seems to become causally inert and perhaps explanatorily redundant—why invoke objective wrongness if honor and interest do all the explanatory work? Conversely, if I insist that defenders of slavery made a genuine moral error that reason could in principle correct, then it looks as though I am sneaking in the very power of moral argument that my first three commitments deny. Meanwhile, my strategic recommendation in the fifth position seems to treat moral norms as socially engineered conventions, which sits awkwardly with the claim that objective moral facts obtain regardless of elite opinion. There may also be an apparent tension between my claim that reference groups are tiny—under three percent—and my emphasis on broad economic structures like industrialization, since the latter sounds like a mass-level explanation rather than an elite cascade.

Please examine these commitments in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other formatting devices, writing instead as a single sustained letter. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, indicate what the least costly revision would be and what it would sacrifice. I am particularly interested in whether the conflict between my moral realism and my causal story about revolutions is fatal or merely uncomfortable, and whether my strategic recommendation can be preserved without abandoning realism. Also address whether the small-reference-group claim and the broad-economic-structure claim are actually at odds or can be reconciled. Give me an audit of the framework, not a lecture on the philosophy of history in general.
