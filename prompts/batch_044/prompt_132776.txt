I'm putting together a feature on why people keep making the same 'irrational' choices in long-term financial planning even when they swear they've learnt their lesson. I was chatting with an economist friend over coffee and she mentioned money pumps — you know, those classic Dutch Book-style setups where cyclic preferences let someone be pumped for cash — but then she said something that stuck with me: what if the real problem isn't that people have inconsistent preferences in one moment, but that their 'future self' is literally a different decision-maker with different weights?

So here's my question, a bit in the weeds: what are the most important implications if we treat vulnerability to money pumps not as a failure of synchronic consistency, but as a symptom of diachronc fragmentation — where rationality norms have to apply across temporal stages rather than within a single mental state? I'm particularly intersted in whether this reframes the debate about precommitment devices (like Ulysses contracts) — are they fixing an irrationality, or are they just one temporal stage coercing another? And does this mean diachronic rationality requires something stronger than transitivity, maybe a kind of 'intertemporal sovereignty' principle?

I'd like to avoid the usual literature-review answer; I'm more interested in the conceptual fallout. Does this framing make money pumps less of a paradox and more of a diagnostic tool for how agency is structured over time? And what does it imply for practical ethics — should we pass harsh judgement on someone for falling into a pump if their preferences genuinely shifted between the stages?
