As a doctoral student in the genealogy of political legitimacy, I am trying to disentangle two accounts of the justice-force relation that run together in early modern thought. The first is Pascal’s coupling thesis in the Pensées: justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and the two must therefore be joined—justice made strong, force made just—if any stable order is to endure. The second is what I will call the reverse dependency thesis: the claim that force does not require justice as an independent normative supplement, but rather generates its own appearance of legitimacy retroactively, so that justice is merely the name given to an already stabilized coercive equilibrium, and that injecting genuine normative content into force actually destabilizes rather than strengthens it.

Please compare these two positions in detail. First, does Pascal’s coupling thesis presuppose that justice possesses an independent normative content that can be added to force, or can his view be read as holding that justice is entirely constituted by the social acceptance and customary imagination that already accompany effective coercion? Second, how does each account explain the persistence of long-lasting but manifestly unjust regimes—does Pascal have internal resources to account for stable tyranny, or does his framework imply that such regimes necessarily contain some hidden or minimal justice? Third, in what precise ways does the reverse dependency thesis differ from a purely Hobbesian or cynical reduction of normativity to power, and does Pascal’s emphasis on custom, imagination, and the “mystical foundation” of authority provide a conceptual bridge between the coupling thesis and the reverse dependency view?

I am not seeking a literature review or citation list; I want a conceptual comparison that treats both positions as live philosophical options and that identifies where they genuinely diverge rather than where they merely restate the same insight in different vocabulary.
