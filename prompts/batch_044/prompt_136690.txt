I have been preparing for the final session of a graduate course on comparative political concepts, and during our last meeting a debate arose about whether the phrase "Western values" functions as a coherent descriptive category or merely as a rhetorical boundary marker in international argument. For this assignment, please construct a sustained argument defending the intermediate position that the category possesses limited but real coherence, provided it is reconstructed genealogically rather than philosophically. Specifically, argue that "Western values" should be understood as referring not to a universal moral ontology—such as an essential commitment to individual autonomy, secular rationalism, or human rights—but to a specific cluster of interlocking institutional practices: adversarial legalism, competitive multiparty representation, and a historically particular form of bounded secularism in which religious authority was subordinated to state sovereignty without being eliminated from public life. You should maintain that this institutional genealogy explains why the phrase can bundle together norms that stand in deep internal tension—such as expansive liberal rights regimes alongside restrictive civic-nationalist immigration policies, or secular constitutional frameworks coexisting with Christian democratic party traditions—without the bundle collapsing into pure contradiction, because the coherence lies in shared historical conflict structures rather than in logical consistency of principles.

At the same time, defend the stronger claim that once this phrase is detached from its specific post-Reformation European state genealogy and presented as a universal normative framework applicable to societies that developed through entirely different institutional trajectories—whether East Asian bureaucratic-Confucian, Sub-Saharan African communal, or South Asian pluralistic-religious—the category becomes analytically harmful: it imposes a false taxonomy that treats heterogeneous political cultures as defective variants of a single model, and it obscures the ways in which the same institutional practices produced radically divergent moral outcomes, from colonial domination to abolitionist movements, welfare-state solidarity, and neoliberal deregulation. You should directly address the objection that this genealogical approach is either circular—defining the West by practices that are themselves defined as Western—or covertly Eurocentric in privileging European institutional history as the implicit standard. Explain how one might distinguish the specific pattern of institutional conflict from its colonial impositions without resorting to essentialist cultural claims or to a naive universalism. Conclude with a clear verdict: on this reconstructed account, does "Western values" retain any legitimate, precise use in contemporary comparative political argument, or should it be abandoned in favor of direct descriptions of institutional forms? Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the conceptual structure itself.
