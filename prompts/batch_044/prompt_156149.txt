I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents can be escaped by partitioning reasoning. She proposed an architecture—call it the Escrow Agent—that splits cognition into R-slow (axiomatic, fully self-representing) and R-fast (heuristic, non-representing). The design rule is that R-slow applies conditional self-trust—if R-slow derives φ, then φ—only to beliefs that originated in R-slow and never invoked the global trust axiom T. A meta-rule M permits promotion from R-fast to R-slow only after R-slow verifies the belief without using T. She claims this keeps T outside the formal system that proves it, so Löb’s theorem never activates. I think the partition either collapses, because M or the boundary condition implicitly embeds T inside R-slow, or it renders self-trust so weak that the agent cannot reliably bootstrap its own reasoning.

Please draft one exam question for an upper-level graduate course in formal epistemology and rational agency. Present the Escrow Agent architecture precisely, then ask students to determine whether it genuinely avoids the Löbian obstacle. Force them to identify the specific formal failure—whether a quantifier shift over registers, a modal slide in representing the boundary, or the implicit inclusion of T via M—rather than simply summarizing Löb’s theorem. The answer should be answerable in about 90 minutes and reach a clear verdict.

Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that defends a specific diagnosis (for instance, that the boundary rule is representable within R-slow, so the subsystem containing both the derivation and the boundary condition satisfies the premises of Löb’s theorem) and explains what must be sacrificed if one tries to block that inclusion. Also include a marking scheme built around four must-hit points (total 20 marks, 5 marks each): each point should name the specific move a top answer must contain, and include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs those marks. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
