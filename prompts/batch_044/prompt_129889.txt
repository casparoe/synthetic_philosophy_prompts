You are a professional philosopher of science with deep expertise in literary criticism and the philosophy of mind. I am a novelist preparing a graduate seminar on philosophical fiction, and I need you to work with me on a rigorous conceptual reading. Before you deliver your analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm briefly: list the three or four major scenes in Mary Shelley's novel Frankenstein in which the text most directly stages questions about how life, consciousness, or moral personhood might arise from purely physical assembly. Then, thinking carefully and step by step, read the novel through Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes—bottom-up explanatory structures that build complex phenomena without invoking unexplained top-down causes—and skyhooks, which are precisely such unexplained explainers. Alongside this, apply Dennett's critique of greedy reductionism: the error of collapsing higher-level realities entirely into their lowest-level constituents, thereby erasing the emergent patterns that make the higher level genuinely real.

Specifically, examine whether Victor Frankenstein's laboratory practice operates as a crane—an attempt to assemble life from anatomical parts—while secretly depending on a vitalist skyhook, some unexplained principle of animation that he cannot engineer. At the same time, consider whether the creature's development—his acquisition of language through observation of the De Lacey family, his moral and philosophical reasoning after encountering Milton, Plutarch, and Goethe, and his sustained first-person narrative—constitutes an emergent reality that greedy reductionism cannot capture without losing the difference between a person and a mechanism.

In your reading, address the following:
- How the novel dramatizes the gap between the creature's physical composition and his emergent consciousness, particularly in the scenes of self-education, the alpine confrontation with Victor, and the final Arctic pursuit.
- Whether Shelley's text ultimately permits a fully naturalistic, crane-built account of mind and morality, or whether it smuggles in a vitalist skyhook that a Dennettian framework must reject.
- Where the philosophical lens illuminates a scene usually read only as gothic horror, and where the novel resists the framework—where an honest critic must admit that Shelley achieves something the philosophy misses.
- What the novel contributes to the debate about reduction and emergence that pure philosophical argument cannot provide—perhaps regarding the ethical weight of being treated as reducible parts, or regarding narrative selfhood as an emergent structure that appears to float free of its bodily substrate.

Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I want the analysis to stand entirely on its own conceptual footing. Avoid verbatim quotations from the novel or from philosophical texts. Keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of Dennett's ideas, and do not distort the philosophy to fit the plot. Conclude by stating clearly what this reading adds to either our understanding of the novel or to the philosophy of reduction and emergence.
