I have been trying to work out, mostly for myself, whether there can really be a completed infinity or whether infinity is always just a way of saying that a process does not stop. I started with Hilbert's hotel: if every room in an infinite hotel is occupied, you can still make space for one more guest by asking the occupant of room 1 to move to room 2, the occupant of room 2 to move to room 3, and so on, with the new guest taking room 1. This is usually presented as showing that infinite collections behave strangely—part equals whole, and so forth. But I think there is a hidden assumption: we are treating the shifting as if it can be finished for every guest at once. In real life, if I actually tried to move every guest up by one, I would never reach the end, because for any guest I relocate, there is always another one further along. So the hotel scenario seems to depend on imagining a process that is never completed, yet treating it as if the result is already sitting there fully formed.

Then I considered Cantor's argument that there are more real numbers than natural numbers, because any list of reals can be outflanked by constructing a number that differs in each decimal place. Again, this feels like a completed trick performed on a completed list. The finitist view—that we should only accept potential infinities, not actual ones—seems to me less like mathematical conservatism and more like an insistence that we not confuse a rule for continuing with a finished totality. I tried to put these thoughts together in some notes arguing that our resistance to actual infinity comes not from a failure of imagination, but from recognizing that phrases like "all the rooms are filled" or "all the numbers are listed" already import a kind of closure that open-ended processes never actually achieve.

I would appreciate comments on this line of reasoning. Specifically, does my distinction between an open-ended rule and a completed totality actually challenge Cantor's proof, or does it merely restate the finitist position without engaging the mathematics? Does Hilbert's hotel genuinely show that actual infinities are coherent, or does it only show what strange things follow if one grants them? And are there parts of my reasoning—particularly the claim that shifting every guest is never actually finished—that misunderstand how mathematicians think about these operations? I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of readings; I am mainly trying to see whether my conceptual moves hold water or collapse into confusion. Suggestions about accuracy, about whether the essay is interesting, or about specific parts where I am probably misunderstanding the mathematics would all be helpful.
