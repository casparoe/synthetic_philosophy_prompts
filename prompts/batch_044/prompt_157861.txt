I hold that macrohistorical outcomes are strongly path-dependent: specific contingent sequences—critical junctures in coalition formation, institutional choice, or technological adoption—lock in trajectories such that, absent those exact sequences, functionally equivalent outcomes would not have arrived. Apparent cross-case convergence, on this view, is largely superficial, masking deep divergence in causal mechanisms and local adaptations. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the decisive defeaters sorted from the fatal to the merely awkward: evidence for equifinality in institutional development (genuinely equivalent functional outcomes reached via radically different causal paths), documented cases where apparently decisive junctures were followed by rapid reconvergence to a baseline trajectory, and formal models of path dependence that preserve strong contingency yet predict identical outcome distributions regardless of path. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view could insulate it by narrowing the definition of outcome equivalence, whether such insulation has already occurred, and whether that is a theoretical vice or a legitimate precision. Finally, distinguish what a committed path-dependence theorist would concede as counting against them from what they would dismiss, and say whether the view currently risks unfalsifiability.
