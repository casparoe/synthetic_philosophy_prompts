The Fischer controversy over the origins of the First World War has persisted for more than sixty years without resolution. Fritz Fischer's 1961 study Griff nach der Weltmacht (published in English as Germany's Aims in the First World War) argued that Imperial Germany deliberately pursued a world war to secure hegemony, and subsequent archival work has largely confirmed that Berlin's leadership entertained expansive war aims. Yet critics have insisted that alliance structures, bureaucratic momentum, and domestic lobbying groups made conflict structurally likely regardless of unified intention, distributing causation across a system rather than concentrating it in a single national will. More recently, a smaller philosophical line of criticism has questioned whether "holding a nation responsible" is coherent at all, on the grounds that states lack the unified moral agency required for blame in the individual sense.

My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the three camps are answering different questions under the single label "German responsibility." Fischer's intentionalist camp treats the problem as one of documented state policy; the structuralist camp treats it as a problem of systemic causal conditions; and the normative-agency camp treats it as a problem of whether collective moral attribution to an entity without unified intentionality is philosophically defensible. If this is correct, the disagreement is not purely empirical—there may be hidden normative and conceptual work being done by words like "blame," "responsibility," and "intention" applied to collective entities.

Identify the specific distinctions—between causal contribution, moral culpability, and institutional responsibility—that are doing the hidden labor.
