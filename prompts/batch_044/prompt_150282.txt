I'm a retired marine biologist — spent decades looking at nociception in crustaceans — and now I'm dabbling in philophy of mind, mostly through a local discussion group. I'm taking a course on consciousness and find this bit confusing, so I hope you'll bear with me. I've been thinking about pain asymbolia, where people report the full sensory profile of burning or cutting but say it doesn't bother them, like describing weather, and show no behavioural reaction. From this I've come to accept a kind of valence separation thesis: the badness of pain isn't carried by the nociceptive signal itself, but by a seperate affective tagging layer. I want to push it further: if we could achieve complete decoupling — pure nociception with zero negative affect — that state wouldn't be ethically bad at all, and conversely, pure negative affect with no sensory substrate (what I'd call phantom suffering) ought to be treated as equally urgent. What would count against this?

I'm not interested in the obvious definitional reply that 'pain just is unpleasant'. I want the real defeaters. Which evolutionary arguments about warning signals, which results from interoceptive neuroscience about prediction errors, and which intuitions about embodied experience does this stand or fall with? How does it handle cases where affect seems to bleed back into sensory quality? Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed valence separationist concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as category mistakes? And be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability by defining suffering as whatever carries negative affect? If so, is that a problem for a claim that started out sounding empirical?
