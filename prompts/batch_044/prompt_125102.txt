Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi in which all six end-words appear—on the problem of old evidence for Bayesian conditionalization. Use these end-words, in this order at the line-ends of the first stanza: prior, orbit, known, weight, shifts, still. Let the sestina’s fixed rotation do structural work: each time a word returns in a new syntactic position, it should evoke old evidence being re-encountered in fresh reasoning without carrying new confirmatory force, because it was already absorbed in the belief state. Specifically, let “orbit” stand for an observed datum (for example, Mercury’s perihelion) and “prior” for the credence distribution that already entails it; as the words cycle through the form, the grammar should keep producing “shifts” while “weight” never actually moves the posterior. In the envoi, bring “prior” and “known” into direct adjacency to capture the collapse that occurs when the agent recognizes the evidence was internal all along. The poem should be technically accurate—someone familiar with the problem should recognize the formal tension that conditioning on E when P(E) is already 1 leaves P(H|E) equal to P(H), even though we still want to speak of confirmation—yet it must leave that tension suspended, not resolved by sermon or shrug. Write in a voice that takes the prior’s point of view, for an audience that knows the philosophy but has not seen it framed through this particular formal constraint.
