I am writing a novel in which a philosopher-king must defend a particular account of moral worth. Please construct a formal argument that an agent possesses full moral worth only when acting for reasons that survive what I term the double veil—first, the stripping away of all knowledge of actual consequences, and second, the stripping away of all prospect of social recognition—while conceding that those reasons may be partly constituted by subconscious pattern-matching rather than explicit deliberation. Your argument should show precisely why this revised account avoids both Kantian rigorism and the over-inclusiveness typical of moral-luck objections, and it should treat the subconscious formation of reasons as a feature rather than a defect of moral agency. Keep the reasoning focused and do not invoke empirical studies or lists of sources.
