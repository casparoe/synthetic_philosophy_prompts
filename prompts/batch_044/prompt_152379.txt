I am preparing a fifty-minute session for an undergraduate philosophy-of-mind reading group whose members have worked through Descartes and Locke but have never studied clinical neuropsychology. Rather than treating split-brain cases as popular-science spectacle—the left hemisphere speaks, the right hemisphere points, therefore two people inhabit one skull—I want to frame commissurotomy patients as a conceptual stress-test for a distinction that is usually left implicit: phenomenal unity, in which every conscious state belongs to one and the same subject, versus access unity, in which all information is available to a single reasoning and self-reporting system. The goal is not to declare that split-brain patients harbor “two minds,” but to show that our intuition that consciousness must be unified survives only if we can prevent these two conditions from collapsing into each other. Plan the teaching sequence: what conceptual dependency must be secured before any clinical data appear, what single concrete scenario should carry the entire argument and what properties it must possess and lack—specifically, it should allow both hemispheres to respond to an identical stimulus while generating incompatible interpretations, yet it must lack any feature that invites students to retreat to a “hidden twin” ontology. Map the misconceptions in the order they will arise—particularly the Cartesian-theater assumption that consciousness requires a central stage, followed by the inference that hemispheric independence automatically entails two subjects rather than one subject with a fractured self-reference loop—and indicate the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Include one closing check that reveals whether students understand the phenomenal/access distinction rather than merely repeating that “there might be two consciousnesses,” and state explicitly what should be deliberately left unexplained at this level—perhaps the surgical anatomy of the corpus callosum or the taxonomy of callosal fiber types—so that the conceptual architecture remains visible.
