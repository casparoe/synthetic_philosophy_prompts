I'm trying to map my own reflective equilibrium on comparing historical atrocities, and I want to know whether my judgments hang together or require amputation. Here are my concrete verdicts on paired cases; they feel mostly stable, but there's at least one tension I can't dissolve, and one case I keep oscillating on.

Case 1. The Holocaust (~6 million, industrial-bureaucratic, explicit global intent to eliminate every Jew) versus the Rwandan genocide (~800,000, rapid, localized, intimate violence). Verdict: I can compare dimensions—scale, speed, organizational form—without incoherence. But collapsing them into "the Holocaust was worse" feels like a category error; the bureaucratic rationalization introduces a qualitative feature that doesn't just scale with body count.

Case 2. The Belgian Congo Free State (~10 million dead from forced extraction, disease, starvation) versus the Armenian genocide (~1.5 million, explicit state plan for elimination of Armenians within the empire). Verdict: By aggregate human suffering, the Congo dwarfs Armenia. By the specificity of genocidal intent—targeting a defined group for total removal—Armenia is the paradigmatic case. I find myself pulled both ways, and I think ranking them ordinally is misleading, though I still feel a tug to call the Congo "worse" in pure harm terms.

Case 3. American chattel slavery (multi-generational, legally codified racial caste, ~4 million held at peak) versus the Middle Passage and Atlantic trade (~12.5 million transported, ~1.8 million died en route). Verdict: The trade had far higher immediate mortality and violent separation; chattel slavery involved longer structural duration and hereditary dehumanization. I think asking "which system was worse" is obscene, yet comparing specific dimensions—mortality rate versus years of lived domination—seems legitimate and informative.

Case 4. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki versus the firebombing of Dresden and Tokyo (all mass civilian killings by aerial bombardment). Verdict: Initially they look comparable. But Hiroshima feels different—singular technological rupture, long radiation tail, a single decision point with irreversible physics. I go back and forth on whether that difference is morally weighty or merely narratively salient, and I can't tell if my intuition tracks a real moral distinction or just framing.

Case 5. The Cambodian genocide (autogenocide, ~1.7 million, class/ideological targeting, internal social restructuring) versus the Holocaust. Verdict: I feel strongly that ranking them is obscene—the victim categories, social dynamics, and perpetrator-victim relations are incommensurable. At the same time, comparing organizational methods and state culpability seems perfectly meaningful.

Case 6. The Holodomor (Soviet-induced famine in Ukraine, ~3.5–5 million, arguably intentional targeting) versus the Great Leap Forward famine (~15–55 million, catastrophic policy failure with some intentional elements). Verdict: Comparing them feels legitimate because both are state-induced famines. I think we can rank culpability along an intent/negligence axis—Holodomor looks more deliberately targeted, the Great Leap more negligently catastrophic—without feeling that the comparison is obscene.

The oscillating case: Comparing the enslavement and labor extraction of indigenous peoples in the Americas (encomienda, mission labor, coupled with demographic collapse from introduced disease) to the transatlantic chattel slave system. I keep flipping. The indigenous case involves disease-driven demographic collapse intertwined with labor coercion, making direct harm comparison murky; the chattel system was uniquely racialized, hereditary, and market-integrated. I can't settle whether one framework should dominate the comparison, or whether they are incommensurable structures.

My tentative principle—call it dimensional isolation—is that historical evils can be compared only within explicitly separated evaluative dimensions (aggregate harm, explicit intent, structural duration, organizational form, epistemic accessibility of victim experience via survivor archives versus statistical reconstruction), and never collapsed into a single ordinal ranking. Comparison becomes obscene when it ignores incommensurable moral kinds, or when epistemic asymmetry—dense first-person archives for some events, mainly demographic reconstruction for others—is treated as morally neutral.

Does dimensional isolation capture cases 1–6? If not, which verdict does it force me to drop—especially my hesitation in case 4?
