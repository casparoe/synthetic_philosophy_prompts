Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter, divided into two seven-line stanzas separated by a blank line. The first stanza should present the Epicurean argument that death cannot harm the one who dies, because harm requires a subject who is present to suffer it, and death and the subject never coincide. The second stanza should present the deprivation-account objection — that death can harm by removing future goods that the deceased would otherwise have enjoyed — but should leave the conflict unresolved. The formal constraint should do philosophical work: in the first stanza every line must be enjambed (the grammatical sentence runs past the line ending), while in the second stanza every line must be end-stopped. This contrast should mirror the difference between a continuous state of being (where harm must be co-present with a sufferer) and a discrete, bounded event of loss (where the harm is located at a specific temporal boundary). Use concrete imagery — for example, a lamp, a ledger, an empty chair — rather than abstract labels, and do not name Epicurus, cite texts, or invoke named philosophers in the verse. After the poem, provide a section titled "Commentary on the formal choices" of approximately 150 words, explaining how the shift from enjambment to end-stopping tracks the move from presence-based to deprivation-based thinking, and noting any simplifications you made to either position in service of the meter or the form.
