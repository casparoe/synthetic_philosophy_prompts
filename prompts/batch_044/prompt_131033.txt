I am writing a novel in which a former theological student attempts to compose a secular liturgy for a community that has lost its faith but retained its need for ritual. For a pivotal chapter, I require a villanelle—strict nineteen lines, with the refrain rhymes A and the internal rhyme b arranged in the classic tercet-and-quatrain form, the two refrains converging in the final two lines—meditating on George Eliot's relocation of the sacred into human sympathy, particularly the tension between her Feuerbachian conviction that divinity is a projection of human love and her narrative practice of distributing moral knowledge across fallible, finite characters rather than centralizing it in an omniscient God. The first refrain must read exactly: "Sympathy is the only grace we keep." The second must read exactly: "No eye watches the long slow turning." The poem's structure should perform its argument: each cyclical return of the refrains should occur in a context slightly altered so that the form itself enacts the compulsion of prayer without its object, with the turn occurring in the second tercet, where consolation begins to feel like repetition rather than resolution. In the envoi, both lines must sit adjacent, leaving formally unresolved whether Eliot's religion of humanity is a genuine transfiguration of devotion or merely its ghost. It should be conceptually precise enough that a reader familiar with her essays on the religion of humanity and her narrative technique recognizes the move, yet it must stand as an independent lyric and must not invoke specific editions, publication dates, or bibliographic apparatus.
