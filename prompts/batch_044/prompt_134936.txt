Compare David Lewis's account of convention—where a regularity solves a coordination problem, depends on common knowledge, and persists because each agent prefers to conform conditional on others conforming, with at least one genuine alternative available—with an evolutionary-game-theoretic account of norms, in which behavioral regularities stabilize through repeated interaction, payoff structures, and selection dynamics without requiring agents to represent them as shared solutions. Focus specifically on the status of arbitrariness: for Lewis, a regularity is conventional only if an alternative could have solved the same problem, whereas evolutionary stabilization often makes the prevailing norm appear uniquely optimal or path-dependent, erasing the salience of alternatives. To test the tension, consider the case of "normative residue": a linguistic gender-marking practice that began as a fully arbitrary Lewisian convention—common knowledge existed that another split was possible—but became grammatically embedded over generations so that deviation now carries high coordination costs maintained by selection-like pressures rather than by mutual expectation. Does this practice remain a convention in Lewis's strict sense once the original coordination problem has vanished and alternatives are no longer viable? Or has it become a non-conventional norm, or perhaps a convention that has lost its defining arbitrariness? In answering, specify which of Lewis's conditions (common knowledge of the regularity, preference for conditional conformity, availability of alternatives) still apply, and whether the evolutionary framework can coherently describe agents who still regard the practice as arbitrary even though the dynamics no longer sustain alternatives.
