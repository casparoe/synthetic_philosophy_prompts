Two philosophers of science, Alex and Blake, were debating Milton Friedman’s “F-twist”—the defense of wildly unrealistic model assumptions by appeal to predictive success—as it applies to integrated assessment models (IAMs) used in climate economics. (1) Alex argued that criticizing an IAM for assuming a single representative agent, perfect capital mobility, and deterministic damage functions misses Friedman’s point: these are deliberately unrealistic simplifications, and if the model tracks aggregate emissions and temperature trajectories within observed ranges, the unrealistic machinery is a methodological virtue, not a defect. (2) Blake replied that the F-twist presupposes a domain rich in independent observational tests against which predictions can be checked, whereas climate IAMs generate forward-looking policy scenarios with no direct observational analogues; consequently, invoking predictive success here risks shielding genuinely normative parameters—such as the social discount rate embedded in the damage function—from critical scrutiny by misclassifying them as mere predictive simplifications. (3) Alex countered that Blake is running together two distinct kinds of idealization: “representational” simplifications (e.g., homogeneous agents) are indeed defensible via the F-twist if they preserve predictive accuracy, while “structural” parameters (e.g., discounting rules) are openly normative and should be evaluated on ethical or political grounds; the F-twist applies only to the former, so Blake’s objection is really about hidden normativity, not about Friedman’s logic. (4) Blake responded that in actual IAM architecture the two are inseparable—altering the representative-agent assumption changes the computed optimal carbon-tax path, which means the predictive machinery and the normative parameters are dynamically coupled; without a principled, model-internal criterion for disentangling them, Alex’s distinction collapses in practice, and the F-twist becomes a blanket excuse for concealing value judgments behind unrealistic mathematics.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four positions from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state which party is right about which specific issue, and identify what both are missing—specifically, whether a coherent intermediate position exists that preserves Friedman’s insight for genuinely predictive model modules while blocking its misuse in policy-prescriptive contexts, and if so, sketch its core commitment.
