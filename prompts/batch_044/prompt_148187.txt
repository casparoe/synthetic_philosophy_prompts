In my reporting on the philosophy of well-being and its overlap with clinical psychology, I have followed a long-running dispute that shows no sign of resolution despite decades of argument and recent empirical work on craving, anhedonia, and recovery. On one side stand tranquilists, who hold that well-being is nothing more than the absence of craving and suffering; on the other stand proponents of flourishing or positive-state accounts, for whom a life that is free from distress but also empty of realised capacities, satisfied desires, or affective richness remains seriously deficient. Even as studies of sustained satiation and addiction remission accumulate, the philosophical camps do not seem to be moving closer together, and I suspect this is because the disagreement is not mainly about the data but about the evaluative baseline we adopt—whether well-being ought to be measured against a zero-point of non-suffering or against a normative ideal of active human functioning. I wonder, however, whether the stalemate runs deeper: perhaps the sides are answering different questions altogether, one about the structure of subjective experience and the other about the objective goods that justify a life, or perhaps they hold incompatible intuitions about whether naturalness or achievement carries intrinsic value that neither side can argue for from shared premises.

Could you diagnose the precise nature of this impasse? I want an analysis, not a survey of positions, that engages concretely with what each camp actually asserts—whether they differ about facts, about which mental states have intrinsic value, about the standards of evidence required to count a case as data, or about the very framing of the question. Please also consider whether any conceptual distinction—such as a taxonomy that separates mere satiation from genuine satisfaction, or a clarification of what counts as a counterfactual baseline for comparing lives—could actually break the deadlock, or whether the disagreement is verbal, or even ill-posed. Finally, please reply in British English.
