I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to clarify my own intuitions about which collective identities are genuinely old and which are modern constructions—what I think of as invented traditions or imagined communities. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I need to abandon one.

First, the Scottish clan tartan system, largely standardized in the 19th century by textile manufacturers and romantic writers: this strikes me as a clear invented tradition, and I feel no hesitation in calling it fabricated.

Second, Japan's imperial institution, which claims an unbroken lineage back to the 7th century BCE, even though modern historians identify major discontinuities and a 19th-century reconstruction of ritual and genealogy. Here I feel conflict: there is obvious invention, yet I still sense a real institutional thread that distinguishes it from pure fabrication.

Third, South Sudan's independence in 2011, with a flag designed by a committee and a national anthem composed shortly before statehood. The state itself seems obviously modern and invented, but the ethnic communities within it have older collective identities, so I hesitate to call the entire formation a fiction.

Fourth, the European Union's adoption of a flag in the mid-20th century and an anthem arranged from Beethoven, now used in official ceremonies to symbolize a centuries-old European civilization. I classify this as an invented tradition, yet it lacks the moral or conceptual weight I assign to the Scottish case.

Fifth, the well-known origin story of Apple being founded in a Los Altos garage, a narrative that omits that significant early work occurred elsewhere and was polished for marketing. This seems structurally analogous—a corporate invented tradition—but it barely registers with me as deceptive.

The case that keeps reversing my judgment is Israel's establishment in 1948: the revival of Hebrew as a spoken language, the adoption of symbols blending biblical and modern references, and the claim that this restored an ancient nation rather than created a new one. Some analyses treat it as the paradigmatic modern invention of a nation-state; others treat it as the reactivation of an imagined community that persisted through diaspora. I find myself genuinely unable to settle whether it belongs with the Scottish tartans or with something more continuous.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the continuity of collective self-imagination, the persistence of institutional practice, or the transparency of construction—that explains why Cases 1, 3, and 4 feel invented to varying degrees, why Case 2 feels ambivalent, and why Case 5 feels trivial? Or must I drop one of these verdicts?

Please also state precisely what your principle predicts for a scenario I haven't considered: a hypothetical digital nation declared in 2030 that revives a medieval dialect, designs rituals from archaeological fragments, and claims continuity with a 12th-century polity that left no continuous population or institutions. And be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate.
