I'm running a fifty-minute philosophy-of-mind session for final-year undergraduates familiar with Searle's Chinese Room but not with Hofstadter and Dennett's dialogue in The Mind's I. The idea to teach is the extension of the Einstein's-brain-in-a-vat scenario: suppose a book encodes every functional state of that brain with perfect fidelity, and an external mechanism turns its pages in the correct sequence—could the book be conscious? Don't explain the argument; design the teaching arc. What conceptual dependency must be secured before this variant sounds like more than a category mistake? Select the single carrying case—the vat-brain with live feedback, the static page-turning book, or a hybrid—and explain what it must include and exclude (e.g., real-time environmental coupling versus atemporal symbol storage) and why that feature carries the load. Identify the three misconceptions you expect in order: I anticipate "functional isomorphism entails phenomenal identity," "the substrate (paper versus neurons) is decisive," and "this collapses into the Chinese Room"—state exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted and what distinction defuses it. Propose one closing check that tests whether students have separated causal integration from representational completeness, not merely affirmed that books don't think. Finally, name the adjacent well-known problem—say, the hard problem of consciousness or the Chinese Room—that must be explicitly bracketed, and say how leaving it out protects the dependency chain.
