I'm a high school student writing an independent philosophy paper on modal metaphysics, and I've become stuck on a well-known stalemate. The dispute between counterpart theory—roughly, that other-worldly objects are similar stand-ins rather than identical to me—and the transworld identity view—that the very same object can exist with different properties—has cycled through the same examples for decades: essential origins, the possibility of being a poet, Humphrey's election. Both sides seem to talk past each other, yet neither can claim victory. I suspect the deadlock hides a deeper split: counterpart theorists may be doing semantics, explaining how modal language tracks possibilities, while transworld identity theorists may be doing pure metaphysics, asking whether one substance can genuinely bear incompatible properties. To test this, I want to introduce a middle case neither camp handles cleanly: imagine an object in one world is gradually replaced part by part, so that in another world only about sixty percent of its original parts remain, identically arranged, with the rest being new. Is the result me, a counterpart, or neither? If both theories must invent ad hoc machinery—degrees of counterpart relation, or loose identity—to cover this intermediate state, does that prove the debate is verbal, or does it reveal that both sides falsely assume cross-world relations must be all-or-nothing? Please diagnose the stall concretely: do the sides differ about hidden facts, about standards for what counts as a metaphysical datum, about bedrock intuitions on survival, or are they answering different questions under one name? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as real progress: a formal result about modal part-hood, a conceptual distinction that splits the intermediate cases, or an honest admission that no resolution should be expected?
