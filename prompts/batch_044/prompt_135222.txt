As a novelist accustomed to thinking of form as a kind of character with its own will, I want you to write a joke that lives inside the practice of translating Clément Marot—specifically, the tradition of le ton beau de Marot, where the preservation of formal architecture (the rondeau's ABAAAB rhyme scheme, its measured cadence) is treated as inseparable from the preservation of tone. Do not write a generic joke about language barriers. Instead, invent the punchline around a translator who is rigorously bound to reproduce both the rhyme scheme and a specific manuscript corruption: in the source tradition, a scribe has repeatedly substituted cour for coeur in a key stanza, and the translator, honoring formal fidelity over semantic recovery, is forced to reproduce an analogous corruption in English—so that the heart becomes a court not by metaphor but by archival duty, and the rhyme demands this error precisely because it is the only word that satisfies both meter and the false cognate. The humor should arise from the recognition that, in this discipline, preserving the beautiful tone requires a disciplined betrayal of the poet's intention; the form has become so sovereign that it preserves the copyist's mistake with greater loyalty than it preserves meaning. Make the joke precise, dry, and legible to someone who understands that here form is not a container but a co-author of the text. No citations, no bibliography—just the logic of the constraint itself.
