As a novelist working on a novel set in a rehabilitative centre for adults who have gained sight after prolonged congenital blindness, I find myself preoccupied by the philosophical architecture embedded in the institution's assessment protocols. The centre operates a strict two-phase evaluation. In the first phase, patients are presented with geometric solids—cubes, spheres, and pyramids—through vision alone and asked to identify them; any failure is recorded in the case notes as 'anticipated sensory latency' and treated as a matter of visual maturation requiring no special intervention. In the second phase, the same patients are permitted to handle identical objects and are then asked to match the tactile impression to the corresponding visual appearance; failure here is categorised not as sensory but as a 'cross-modal conceptual deficit', triggering an entirely separate cognitive-therapy pathway rather than further ophthalmological training.

What philosophical commitments does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does the protocol tacitly endorse a particular resolution of Molyneux's problem—perhaps that tactile and visual experiences initially yield discrete representational spaces that must be actively unified—or could an integrated empiricist framework license the same clinical division? Examine whether the practice is internally consistent: if a patient fails the visual-only task yet succeeds at tactile-visual matching, or conversely names objects correctly but fails to match them, does the centre's taxonomy of failure remain philosophically stable? And describe what would have to be altered in the assessment if one replaced its underlying epistemology with a view that refuses any separation between raw sensory registration and conceptual identification—would the two-phase structure dissolve entirely, or might it survive on purely pragmatic or administrative grounds?
