I've come around to this somewhat ugly view: that large-scale habitat destruction is actually, on net, better for wild-animal welfare than preservation. Not just the standard "fewer animals means less suffering" line — I mean the more specific claim that intact ecosystems structurally produce more of the worst kinds of suffering (chronic starvation, high-density parasitism, prolonged juvenile predation) and that degraded habitats replace those with lower-biomass, faster-turnover populations where suffering, if present, is more acute and shorter. I also think conservation policy treats ecosystem integrety and biodiveristy as intrinsic goods, which only makes sense if you think species-complexity has moral weight independant of sentient experience — basically ecological speciesism applied to trophic webs. Plus I think diachronic welfare accounting shows that the transitional harm of collapse is smaller than the chronic harm prevented, because the suffering is front-loaded and then the population just... doesn't exist to suffer. So what would actually count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how fatal they are — empirical stuff about suffering in disturbed ecosystems, philosophical problems with comparing non-existence to suffering, whether I've rigged "morally relevant suffering" to be unfalsifiable, and whether diachronic accounting even applies to wild populations. Be honest: which objections would defenders concede are real hits, which ones they'd dismiss, and is it a problem that the view might be well-insulated?
