Two friends of mine got stuck arguing about whether individual choices actually matter for big collective harms like climate change or factory farming, and they kept dragging voting into it. I was just listening.

Friend 1 said the inefficacy objection is basically right: in climate or meat, one person’s choice is a drop in the bucket that doesn’t change the outcome. If you skip driving, emissions stay the same; if you don’t buy the burger, the animal was already killed. So there’s no direct causal link, and saying individuals “ought” to act is confused unless you can point to an actual effect.

Friend 2 said thats too quick, because we clearly think people should vote even though one vote almost never decides an election. So inefficacy can’t kill the obligation by itself. Maybe there’s an expressive reason, or a “do your part” reason that applies even when you’re redundant.

Friend 1 came back: voting is different because it’s a structured collective act with a clear aggregation mechinism — ballots are counted — and it’s low-cost with civic symbolism. Climate and meat are diffuse, high-cost, and invisible. The analogy fails because voting is about participating in a decision procedure, not causing an outcome. So the “should vote” intuition doesn’t transfer.

Friend 2 then said okay, but what about hidden thresholds? Maybe your emissions push us over a climate tipping point, or your burger order tips demand just enough for another slaughter batch. If causation is probabalistic and thresholds are hidden, you can’t be sure you’re redundant. Plus there’s a complicity angle: even redundant participants in a harmful practice share blame for sustaining it.

Friend 1 replied that hidden thresholds are speculative and apply equally to voting (your vote could be the tie-breaker). And complicity collapses into collective guilt that doesn’t guide individual action — if everyone waits for systemic change, nothing happens. The real distinction, he said, is “structural visibility”: in voting your contribution is registered by the system; in climate/meat it’s invisible to the harm mechanism. So morality shouldn’t depend on whether anyone can see you abstain, otherwise we’re just doing virtue signaling.

They got stuck there. Friend 1 thinks Friend 2 is smuggling in symbolic reasons that don’t survive the invisability problem; Friend 2 thinks Friend 1 is ignoring that negative duties work differently from positive duties, and that “structural visibility” is a weird criterion for moral obligation.

Who’s actually right about the comparison between voting and climate/meat? Does the structural visibility point hold water, or does it confuse publicity with morality? And are they both missing something about how probabilistic causation or complicity actually works in these cases? Keep it tight — max 350 words, maybe 4 paragraphs, don’t go over.
