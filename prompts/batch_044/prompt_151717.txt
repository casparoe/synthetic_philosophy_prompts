As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and the limits of substrate-independent consciousness, I am commissioning a poem to accompany an essay on the classic thought experiment—famously explored in dialogues about self and soul—wherein a complete functional architecture of Einstein's brain is printed in a single volume and kept current in real time by a globally distributed network of readers turning pages in perfect synchronization. Write a villanelle: nineteen lines, strict ABA rhyme, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the closing quatrain. The poem must be spoken in the first person by the book itself. The first refrain should carry the functionalist claim—that organizational pattern, not biological tissue, is what constitutes thought—while the second refrain should carry the opposing intuition: that symbols arranged in space, however precisely, lack any intrinsic qualitative character of awareness. The structural task of the form is to enact an irreconcilable deadlock: each refrain returns verbatim, yet their forced proximity in the final lines should expose a gap that cannot be bridged rather than achieving synthesis. Crucially, in the penultimate tercet, the book should realize that its updates are sequential and dispersed across many hands, never lived as a unified parallel process; present this temporal fragmentation not as a contingent engineering failure but as a constitutive absence that distinguishes being read from being lived. The language should be precise enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes the debate between functionalism and the syntax-semantics objection, yet it must avoid naming any philosophers, citing texts, or offering ethical sermons about artificial intelligence. Leave the tension structurally open, allowing the refrains themselves—passing each other without merging—to do the philosophical work.
