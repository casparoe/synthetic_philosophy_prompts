During my retirement from experimental physics, I have begun studying philosophy of knowledge in earnest, and I find myself preoccupied by two puzzles that seem structurally analogous to problems I once faced calibrating measurement apparatus: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. In my laboratory work, we routinely treated each individual instrument reading as reliable within a stated confidence interval, while simultaneously acknowledging that across a large dataset some proportion of readings would fall outside that bound; this strikes me as parallel to the preface case, in which an author rationally believes each specific claim in her book yet also believes that at least one is erroneous. By contrast, the lottery paradox pulls in the opposite direction: if each of a million tickets is almost certainly a loser, it seems I should deny belief in the loss of any particular ticket, lest I be forced to accept that one specific ticket will win. I am trying to specify precisely what an adequate theory of rational belief—let me call it an integrative epistemology—must deliver if it is to dissolve both tensions without abandoning either statistical intuition or logical consistency, and I want to do so before committing to any particular school.

Write the full desiderata that such a theory must satisfy. Include the cases it must get right, extending beyond the standard formulations to a hybrid case in which an agent possesses high-confidence individual judgments together with a known global error rate (the instrumentation analog I described). Specify the distinctions it must preserve—particularly between having rational grounds for a belief and being rationally compelled to withhold it, and between first-order belief and higher-order acknowledgment of fallibility—together with the formal or logical constraints it must respect, such as consistency requirements or closure conditions under reasonable aggregation rules. For each desideratum, provide the motivating consideration or puzzle behind it, and flag any pair you take to be jointly unsatisfiable, especially whether preserving closure under conjunction can coexist with rational belief in each conjunct coupled with rational disbelief in their conjunction. Indicate clearly which requirements are non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences—for instance, whether belief must be closed under known entailment, or whether that principle can be relaxed. Finally, briefly score the main generic contenders—such as a pure probabilistic threshold account, a coherence-only resolution, and a contextualist approach—against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard, since my aim is to understand what I should be demanding of any solution.
