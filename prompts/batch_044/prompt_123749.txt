In several European and North American municipalities, participatory budgeting is structured not as a single preference-aggregation vote but as an iterative cycle: residents propose projects, small-scale pilots are funded provisionally, outcomes are evaluated against publicly agreed criteria, and the scheme is revised or discontinued in the next round, with participants described as co-inquirers rather than clients expressing fixed demands. Yet the governing legal framework typically treats the adopted annual budget as binding and does not permit continuous revision, confining the experimental loop to a preparatory phase. What conception of democratic legitimacy and of the status of social values does this hybrid arrangement presuppose? Does it require the Deweyan view that democracy is primarily a mode of collective inquiry in which norms and goods are experimentally constructed rather than pre-politically given, or can it be accommodated within a liberal-pluralist model that treats the process merely as a more accurate mechanism for revealing existing preferences? Furthermore, is the practice internally consistent: does the point of institutional closure—where experimentation ends and a binding decision is enforced—contradict the experimentalist rationale, or does it tacitly presuppose a separate, non-experimental source of authority? Finally, if the underlying experimentalist commitment were replaced by a straightforwardly aggregative or purely deliberative-democratic theory, what specific alterations to the budget cycle, the evaluation metrics, and the status of pilot projects would necessarily follow?
