Hey — I'm putting together my grad seminar on Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot and I keep circling back to something that feels like it should be obvious but isn't. The whole book spins around translations of Marot's 'Ma mignonne' — that short Renaissance poem to a sick lady — and Hofstadter shows us version after version that preserve the rhyme scheme and the meter more or less intact, yet he keeps insisting that none of them fully captures the 'beautiful tone' and that there is no single correct version. I was talking this over with a colleague who works on algorithmic poetry analysis; she uses this framework she calls a 'constraint-fidelty matrix' that weights formal preservation against semantic deviation, and she genuinely couldn't see why we shouldn't just run every translation through it and pick the highest score. And I think my confusion is right there: I'm treating the rhyme and meter as if they exhaust the form, so that once those boxes are ticked, whatever remains — the 'music,' the tone — must be either a subjective extra or a ghost in the machine.

So my question is this: is Hofstadter arguing that formal constraints and semantic content are incommensurable in principle, which would mean any ranking matrix is conceptually wrong? Or am I making a deeper mistake by assuming that 'preserving form' just means copying the rhyme scheme, when in fact the form of Marot's poem might include things — like the particular rhythm of spoken French, or the social register of addressing a sick lady — that don't survive in English no matter how neat the rhyme is? I feel like I'm missing something about why the residue isn't just noise but is actually part of what translation has to carry, and I can't tell whether my error is philosophical or just linguistic.
