Exam question:
"Some philosophers defend a position we'll call Stabilizing Teleosemantics (ST). On this view, a mental representation R has content C only if two conditions are satisfied: (i) R's producer mechanism was historically selected for generating R-tokens in the presence of C; and (ii) R's consumer mechanism is currently structured so that its successful functioning requires discriminating instances of C from relevant non-C alternatives, with this discrimination profile remaining stable across normal environmental variation. First, explain how ST is meant to solve the problem of misrepresentation, using a concrete example. Second, identify one significant theoretical cost or limitation introduced specifically by condition (ii)."

Student answer:
"ST solves misrepresentation by combining a producer-side selection condition with a consumer-side stabilization requirement. Consider a frog that detects small dark moving objects as prey. On a pure Millikan-style direct proper function account, the retinal producer was selected for firing in the presence of flies, but it can misfire at a speck of dust. Millikan says this is misrepresentation because the producer failed its direct proper function: it was supposed to be triggered by flies, not dust. ST improves on this by adding condition (ii): the frog's tongue-flicking consumer is stabilized to respond only to stimuli matching the discrimination profile for fly-sized, moving, dark objects. So if a dust speck doesn't match that profile, the consumer won't respond, and there is no misrepresentation—just a failure to trigger the system. If the consumer does respond to the dust speck, that shows the stabilization profile itself has malfunctioned, so the error lies in the consumer's architecture rather than in misrepresentation.

ST also handles the disjunction problem more effectively than pure indicator theories. Indicator theories face ambiguity when a detector correlates with flies or bees, yielding disjunctive content. But on ST, content is fixed by the consumer's stabilized discrimination capacity, which reflects selection-for rather than selection-of, as Papineau emphasized. The producer was selected for tracking flies specifically, and the consumer's discrimination profile inherits and locks in that specificity, preventing any disjunctive content.

The main cost of condition (ii) is that it makes content determination depend partly on present neural architecture rather than purely on evolutionary history. If the frog's consumer mechanism is plastic—say, it adapts to larger prey—then the content of the representation shifts even though the producer's history hasn't changed. This introduces a presentist element that undermines the evolutionary stability that makes teleosemantics attractive."
