Analyze the 1822 royal visit of George IV to Edinburgh—staged by Sir Walter Scott as a mass spectacle of Highland tartan, clan pageantry, and “ancient” ceremonial loyalty to the crown—together with the subsequent 1842 publication by the Sobieski Stuart brothers that fabricated and codified clan tartan patterns, through the joint lens of Hobsbawm’s theory of invented tradition and Anderson’s concept of imagined communities. I want a disciplined mapping, not a generic essay: identify the relevant actors (Scott, the Stuart brothers, the Highland Society, the monarchy, clan chiefs), what institutional or symbolic options they actually faced in the decades after the 1746 Dress Act and the Highland Clearances, and what counts as the “evidence” of Scottish national identity in this case. Then explain which features Hobsbawm’s framework captures well—particularly the rapid, elite-driven fabrication of a ceremonial past that had been largely erased—and which it misses or misdescribes, especially regarding the role of print culture and bureaucratic cataloging in sustaining the fiction. Next, apply Anderson: does the Scottish case fit his print-capitalism, census-map-museum model of nation-building, or does it expose a gap because the “imagined community” here was not anchored through vernacular literacy but through a pseudo-genealogical visual code (tartan) invented in a printed costume book and later maintained by a modern tartan registry? Be explicit about where Anderson’s theory is silent. Finally, use the episode to test both theories: does the fact that the invented tradition required continuous institutional maintenance—from the 1822 pageant to the 21st-century Scottish Register of Tartans—support Hobsbawm’s claim that traditions are repeatedly reinvented, or does it suggest that once an imagined community is anchored in material symbols, it acquires a self-sustaining logic that neither Hobsbawm nor Anderson fully anticipated? Where the two theories conflict on whether the nation was being constructed or recovered, or on what the actors should have done, say so; do not force them into agreement, and do not invent historical details to make the mapping work.
