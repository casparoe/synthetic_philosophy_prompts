I'm taking a course on collective decision-making and we've been working through the Condorcet jury theorem. In our discussion, someone offered an analogy that keeps recurring in popular accounts: a jury estimating the weight of an ox works like an ensemble of weak classifiers in machine learning—each voter is a noisy sensor, statistical independence lets errors cancel, and the majority vote functions like bagging in a random forest. The analogy is often used to license the conclusion that democratic aggregation is structurally sound even when individual competence is modest, because the collective mechanism corrects for noise just as ensemble methods do.

But I'm trying to figure out whether this comparison actually supports anything about real-world deliberation, or whether it is mostly decorative. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the classifier, the feature space, the ground-truth label, the independence assumption, the aggregation rule—then audit where the mapping breaks? In particular, does the analogy treat jurors as passive sensors rather than reasoning agents who update on each other's arguments, share common informational environments, or face strategic incentives? Does importing the machine-learning frame smuggle in a stationary, observer-independent ground truth (the actual weight of the ox) that does not map onto normative or predictive political judgments where the outcome partly constitutes the "truth"? If the independence condition collapses under realistic social dynamics—correlated errors, cascade effects, group polarization—does any weakened version of the theorem survive, or was the analogy carrying the normative conclusion all along? And if the analogy does fail, is there a better one—perhaps from epidemiology or distributed computing—that preserves the intuition about error cancellation without importing the passive-sensor framing?
