In a philosophy-of-mind seminar on fundamental physics, a fellow student keeps returning to the line "If consciousness is in the physics, then suffering is in the physics too" whenever we discuss whether panpsychism carries ethical weight. She uses it to argue that once we accept any form of fundamental sentience—whether in particles, fields, or quantum events—moral consideration cannot stop at organisms, and might even extend to physical processes such as decoherence that suppress coherent quantum states, which she treats as a kind of harm to whatever minimal experience was present. I want to bring a precise conceptual map to my professor rather than just nodding along, so could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan might actually be expressing? For each reading, say whether it is trivial (e.g., a definitional restatement), a substantive metaphysical claim about fundamental valence, an ethical inference that genuinely follows, or a category error. Specifically distinguish between: (i) the literal view that individual quantum interactions or decoherence events involve phenomenal suffering with a unified subject; (ii) the structural view that the conditions necessary for suffering are instantiated at every physical level, even if no integrated subject exists there; and (iii) the normative view that accepting panpsychism automatically expands the moral circle to include all physical processes regardless of whether they have interests, welfare levels, or the capacity to be harmed. For each, indicate whether it actually supports her conclusion that coherence suppression is ethically significant, and flag the hidden premises—about aggregation, about the gap between sentience and suffering, and about whether "being in the physics" entails moral status—that each reading needs in order to work. I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I just need the disambiguation with verdicts so I can see exactly where her argument stands and where it slips.
