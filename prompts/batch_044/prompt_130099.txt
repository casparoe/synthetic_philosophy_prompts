I'm working on my final paper for a high school philosophy elective, and I want to tackle Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism at a level deeper than the usual summary. I understand that Rorty urges us to abandon the idea that beliefs mirror an independent reality, and he tries to prevent this from collapsing into relativism by distinguishing between ordinary discourse that works within a shared vocabulary and disruptive discourse that introduces new metaphors. What I cannot see is whether this distinction can do genuine normative work. If we truly reject representational standards, then calling some metaphors productive expansions of moral imagination and others mere rhetorical confusion seems to require a hidden criterion of getting things right about the world.

Write an essay that examines whether Rorty's antirepresentationalism can consistently distinguish valuable moral innovation from empty rhetorical manipulation without secretly relying on representationalist assumptions. You should reach and defend a clear verdict: either that the pragmatic distinction succeeds on its own terms, or that antirepresentationalism inevitably reintroduces a representational commitment whenever it ranks vocabularies. Analyze whether Rorty can say that the second position is an illuminating abnormal discourse rather than a confusion, without appealing to the idea that they have correctly represented the system's inner nature.
