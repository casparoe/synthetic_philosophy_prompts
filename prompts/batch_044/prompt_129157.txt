I have been working through a philosophical idea on my own, without formal training, and I have named it tranquilism: well-being is constituted exclusively by the absence of craving (tanha) and the absence of suffering. On this view, pleasure, knowledge, artistic achievement, love, and recognition of others are not intrinsically good; they are at best instruments that help preserve the two absences, and at worst they are neutral background conditions.

To test whether I understand the view correctly, I composed the following exam-style question and then wrote what I take to be a genuinely mixed student answer—mostly correct on the visible points, but with one or two subtle errors that a hurried grader might let slide.

Question: Consider tranquilism as defined above. A critic introduces a hypothetical stable neural intervention that produces a permanent "craving-void state": all desire and all pain are eliminated, yet basic cognitive function remains intact, leaving the subject with no motivation, no aesthetic response, and no recognition of other people. The critic contends that if tranquilism is true, this state must be the best possible life, which is intuitively absurd. Write an essay assessing whether tranquilism is genuinely committed to praising this state, and whether that commitment undermines the view.

Student answer:

Tranquilism differs from hedonism in a crucial respect. Hedonists treat pleasure as positively valuable, but tranquilists treat only the removal of suffering and craving as having value. Everything else—knowledge, art, love—is either an instrument for maintaining those absences or entirely neutral.

Applying this to the craving-void case, the tranquilist must indeed regard the state as optimal. Every other supposed good is absent or irrelevant, and both suffering and craving are permanently eliminated. The critic calls this absurd, but that reaction is just our ordinary attachment to positive experience. From within the view, nothing of value has been lost.

That said, tranquilism is essentially negative utilitarianism restricted to a single life. Negative utilitarianism holds that we ought to minimize suffering everywhere; tranquilism holds that my well-being is nothing but my own minimized suffering. The scale differs, but the core idea is identical.

There is also a deeper problem: the view is self-refuting. To advocate tranquilism, one must want the absence of craving. But wanting is itself a form of craving. So the tranquilist is caught in a loop—craving the end of craving. Once you see this circularity, the position collapses.

Moreover, if achievement has no intrinsic value, then the achievement of reaching tranquility is also valueless. That means there is no rational reason to pursue the state deliberately. One might avoid pain through instinct, but there is no normative guidance for working toward tranquility. Thus tranquilism removes the grounds for its own practice.

In sum, the student applies the view consistently to the hypothetical case and correctly contrasts it with hedonism, but then undermines it with the self-refutation argument and the motivation objection.
