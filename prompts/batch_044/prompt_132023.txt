Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment set in a non-emergency professional workplace—specifically, a university library's digital-archiving unit—where a staff archivist discovers that a metadata corruption she introduced two years ago has silently misattributed several hundred historical records. Correcting it requires her to work unpaid through a holiday weekend, file an internal disclosure that triggers an audit of her past work, and accept probable reassignment; leaving it untouched means the misattribution persists but is unlikely to be discovered. Engineer the case so that, according to a cost-proportionality account of supererogation—on which an act is supererogatory only if the agent's personal cost exceeds the portion of harm prevented properly attributable to her—this correction is obligatory rather than supererogatory, because her causal responsibility for the original error makes preventing the harm her direct duty regardless of personal cost; while according to a role-boundary account—on which an act is supererogatory whenever it exceeds the duties specified by one's institutional role, regardless of causal history—the very same correction is genuinely supererogatory, because her contractual role does not include retrospective self-incrimination and unpaid remediation beyond standard hours. The divergence between the two accounts must turn on a single structural feature: her prior causal authorship of the error. After presenting the scenario, explain the verdict and reasoning each account delivers, demonstrate why removing that feature—making the corruption someone else's mistake—collapses the divergence back to agreement that the act is supererogatory, and explain how a defender of the account that comes out counterintuitive in this framing would attack the case. Finally, state explicitly what argument or consideration would change your mind about which account handles the scenario correctly, and identify the specific point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
