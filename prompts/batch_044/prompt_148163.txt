Next month I present a dissertation chapter at a philosophy-of-economics workshop. The thesis I am defending is that Keynes’s treatment of uncertainty in The General Theory is not the familiar Knightian risk-versus-uncertainty binary, nor is it reducible to subjective Bayesian ignorance with high-variance priors. Instead, I argue that Keynes uses the “weight of argument” from his earlier Treatise on Probability as a socially constituted, convention-dependent metric: long-run investment expectations are unstable not because agents lack probabilities, but because the intersubjective conventions that fix the premises of practical reasoning can erode without any corresponding change in the available evidence, producing a form of structural ignorance that Bayesian updating cannot model because the prior itself is constituted by collective convention rather than individual belief. The audience is precisely divided: two decision-theoretic economists who read Keynes as an informal Bayesian and treat “animal spirits” as risk-neutral agents facing ambiguous priors; one historian of economic thought committed to the view that Keynes never abandoned the logical-relation theory of probability from the Treatise and that the General Theory remains fully consistent with it; and two philosophers of science who regard any non-probabilistic notion of macro-uncertainty as redundant, since modern stochastic dynamic general equilibrium frameworks already accommodate investment volatility without invoking epistemic limits. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each faction would actually phrase them, with for each: whether I should concede part of it or hold firm, a concise reply suited to a workshop setting, and an explicit note on the weakest point in that reply. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, that my convention-dependent reading renders Keynes’s theory unfalsifiable, since any investment failure can be retroactively explained by a shift in conventional weight rather than by a failed probabilistic prediction—and tell me how to answer it without retreating to textual ambiguity. Finally, identify the weakest point in my overall position that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope the audience overlooks. Keep the response a speaker’s prep sheet, not a literature review or bibliography.
