Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, closing with a three-line envoi—on Plato’s Meno problem: why knowledge exceeds true belief in value when both can guide action correctly. Write it in the voice of someone who has recently begun supervising apprentices in a high-stakes craft and has watched a correct but untethered procedure fail catastrophically when conditions shifted slightly. Choose six end-words—suggested: guide, tether, flee, reason, true, know—and let the sestina’s compulsory rotation enact the very difference Plato describes: beliefs not tied down by an account wander like the statues of Daedalus, while knowledge remains because it carries its justification with it. Incorporate the modern modal-epistemic point that true belief is counterfactually fragile—right in this case, wrong in a near one—whereas knowledge survives modal displacement because of its internal tether. Do not quote Plato verbatim; paraphrase the statue image in your own words. The envoi should bring all six words together without resolving whether they finally hold, letting the form ask whether anything is truly tied down and leaving the value question structurally open rather than sermonized. This is for closing a philosophy-of-knowledge seminar; avoid bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
