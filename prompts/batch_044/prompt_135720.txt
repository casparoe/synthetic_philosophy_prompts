I have recently begun consulting for a civic-technology group that is deploying ambient accountability infrastructure in public-housing corridors—systems that do not capture images, audio, or named identifiers, but instead derive social-emotional dynamics and predictive behavioral patterns from thermal signatures and aggregate motion flows. The developers argue that because no conventional personal data is stored, no privacy interest is engaged. I need to understand whether a coherent distinction remains between informational privacy and what we might call phenomenological privacy—the protection of one's lived experience from being rendered institutionally legible—once sensing operates at this sub-identificatory, affective level. Please explain the conceptual architecture of this distinction, whether it can be sustained without invoking a property-rights framework over data, and what normative force it retains if we accept that part of the self's integrity depends on the opacity of its own emotional processes to external interpretive schemas. I am not asking for a survey of existing literature; I need the conceptual machinery itself, clearly articulated.
