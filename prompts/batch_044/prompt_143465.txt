I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on institutional forecasting, and after class two students—Leila and Marcus—got into a sustained disagreement about how to apply the outside view to the European Union. They asked me to relay the exchange and get an adjudication, since neither feels the other is engaging with the actual methodological problem.

Leila started by proposing a synthetic reference class: instead of using actual historical federations or empires, she constructs a counterfactual durability index drawn from constitutional-design simulations that stress-test multi-level governance under fiscal divergence and democratic deficit. She argues that for entities like the EU—which was explicitly engineered with anti-collapse mechanisms (supranational legal supremacy, conditionality rules, no imperial extractive core)—the appropriate base rate comes not from the archive of historical accidents but from engineered-system failure modes. She notes that historical base rates suffer from survivorship bias in the record: quietly dissolved confederations leave few traces, while dramatic imperial collapses are over-documented, so the empirical reference class is already distorted. Marcus replied that this is just the inside view in quantitative clothing. Every empire claimed structural uniqueness—Roman exceptionalism, British institutional genius—and the whole point of the outside view is to resist that exemption. He applied a historical base rate drawn from multi-level federal entities lasting more than fifty years: roughly sixty percent underwent severe dissolution or transformation within a century, usually driven by fiscal divergence that political mechanisms failed to contain. He then added a small downward adjustment—what he called an institutional-learning discount—arguing that entities whose architects explicitly studied past collapses (the EU's drafters knew the Holy Roman Empire and the US Articles of Confederation well) should have their collapse probabilities reduced slightly, but not eliminated, because learning rarely outruns structural stress.

Leila countered that Marcus's learning discount is unfalsifiable: if the EU survives, he will credit learning; if it collapses, he will claim the stress exceeded the discount. More fundamentally, she said applying imperial-collapse frequencies to the EU is like using wooden-bridge failure rates to forecast steel suspension bridges—both are bridges, but the material properties differ. The EU lacks an imperial center, relies on treaty law rather than extraction, and operates under democratic conditions that no pre-modern federation faced. Marcus conceded the structural differences but insisted that causal mechanism arguments are precisely what the outside view is designed to discipline: uniqueness claims are cheap, and if we allow material-property exemptions for every case, reference-class forecasting collapses into narrative storytelling. He also pointed out that Leila's simulations must be calibrated against historical data somewhere, so she is smuggling the base rate back through the parameter choices, just hidden behind a layer of modeling.

Please adjudicate move by move: evaluate Leila's synthetic reference-class proposal, her survivorship-bias objection, her bridge analogy, and her charge that the learning discount is unfalsifiable; then evaluate Marcus's application of the historical base rate, his learning discount, his charge of inside-view exemption, and his calibration objection. Say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding how one should select a reference class when the institution in question was designed precisely to avoid the failure modes that define the historical class.
