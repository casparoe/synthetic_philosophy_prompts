I am a high school senior, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at our school's annual Civic Dialogue workshop. The audience will include approximately sixty attendees: fellow students, parents, teachers, and two visiting philosophy professors from a nearby university who will moderate the afternoon panels. I want the remarks to use G.K. Chesterton's fence principle—the idea that one should not remove a social institution until one understands why it was established—not as a decorative reference, but as the structural backbone of the speech. Specifically, I want to distinguish between applying the fence argument to formal, codified school rules (where the reasoning behind them is usually documented and recoverable) and applying it to informal social norms and rituals (where the reasoning is distributed, tacit, and easily lost once disrupted). I will argue that well-intentioned reform movements in schools often fail because they apply the fence logic selectively: they demand justification for formal rules they dislike, yet dismantle informal practices without investigating their stabilizing functions. The speech should be accessible to audience members with no background in political philosophy, but it should be precise enough that the two professors recognize I am doing conceptual work rather than repeating a slogan. Please write a speech of about five minutes—roughly 600 to 700 words—suitable for this occasion. At the conclusion, please state how confident you are in the claim that Chesterton's fence should be applied differently to formal rules versus informal norms, and identify the specific point in your interpretation about which you are least certain.
