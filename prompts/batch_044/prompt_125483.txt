I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and for the last month our discussions have circled back to the same stalemate: the Strawsonians insist that reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral indignation—are not responses to some independently verified capacity for control but are themselves the practice within which responsibility lives; asking whether resentment is justified by prior metaphysical conditions, they say, is like asking whether a promise needs an ontological foundation. The rationalists counter that reactive attitudes can be appropriate or inappropriate precisely because they track real differences in reasons-responsiveness or agential control, so dissolving the metaphysical question into social practice just changes the subject. Every move feels rehearsed—references to reactive attitude objectivity and participant versus objective stances come up, Frankfurt-style cases are mentioned, and no one shifts position. Recently, however, a classmate proposed a framing that made me suspect we've misdiagnosed the stall altogether: she argued that the Strawsonians are really investigating the illocutionary grammar of moral address—what it is to hold someone accountable in a second-personal register—while the rationalists are investigating normative eligibility conditions—whether an agent possesses the properties that make blame or praise fitting in a third-personal, evaluative register. Under this framing, both sides may be correct within their own domains but talking past each other under the shared label of moral responsibility.

If this framing is right, is the disagreement verbal—an ambiguity in what responsibility means across these registers—or is there a substantive collision hidden beneath the vocabulary, perhaps over whether second-personal address can exist without third-personal eligibility conditions being satisfied? Or is it a case of two research programs—one phenomenological and pragmatist, one metaphysical and normative—using terms like reactive attitudes and moral community to answer different questions that happen to share a name? Diagnose the structure of this disagreement with reference to what each side actually says, not a generic survey. Then tell me what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the registers cleanly, an empirical study of how reactive attitudes actually function in social coordination that could adjudicate between them, or an argument that the registers are inseparable and one must absorb the other?
