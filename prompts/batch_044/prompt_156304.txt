In our graduate epistemology seminar we have been examining Williamson's anti-luminosity argument: feeling cold is not luminous because there is always a marginal case in which one is cooling yet not in a position to know it. Our instructor noted that Williamson also rejects the KK principle, but the precise link between the two was left implicit. It strikes me that KK—if S knows p, then S knows that S knows p—is structurally identical to applying luminosity to the specific condition "S knows p"; consequently, if anti-luminosity defeats luminosity for all non-trivial mental conditions, then KK should fail as a trivial corollary, since knowing p is itself a mental state with borderline cases. Why, then, do commentators treat Williamson's rejection of KK as a distinct philosophical result requiring its own argument—typically involving safety or reliability conditions—rather than as an immediate consequence of the sorites-style reasoning against luminosity? Is there a suppressed premise that knowledge is uniquely "sharp-boundaried" and therefore exempt from the margin-for-error reasoning, or am I conflating the iteration of epistemic operators with the first-order self-monitoring that luminosity demands?
