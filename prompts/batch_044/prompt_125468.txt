As a journalist covering the archaeology of belief for a science-and-ideas quarterly, I am trying to design a diagnostic instrument for my readers: a decision tree that sorts not by labels but by concrete commitments about when a legend earns the status of history. I want it built around three canonical stress cases—the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative—because each occupies a different quadrant of the evidentiary problem: a site that may match an epic poem, a figure who may anchor a regional resistance, and a collective migration for which material traces are contested or absent. The tree should open with a branch on whether physical correlation—stratigraphic destruction layers, settlement discontinuities, mass-burial patterns—is necessary at all, then move through whether heroic singularity must be recoverable, whether collective memory can preserve a historical kernel without identifiable protagonists, and whether the scale and social form of the claimed event—city-state siege, sub-Roman polity consolidation, or population displacement—alters the burden of proof in kind rather than merely in degree.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position (maximalist, minimalist, mythicist, kernel-historian, narrative-functional, or unnamed if the combination is logically available but apparently unoccupied) together with its most awkward commitment, and you should flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, such as a minimalist stance that denies Anatolian stratigraphic correlations and a maximalist stance that accepts only post-Roman settlement continuity but no individual king. Finally, include one branch asking whether archaeological silence functions as disconfirmation or as neutral absence, since this is precisely where scholars diverge across the three cases without anyone being able to cite the others' literature in real time; the instrument should be answerable by reasoning through cases alone, not by consulting specialist archives.
