As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun attending philosophy seminars, I observed the following exchange during a discussion on rational choice and was unable to settle it myself. Two interlocutors — a logician and a cognitive scientist, both clearly well-read — debated a refined version of Buridan's ass. I am reporting their positions as accurately as I can from memory.

The logician began by arguing that Buridan's scenario exposes a hidden presupposition in standard models of rational choice: they require a complete preference ordering. When two options are genuinely identical in value, rationality has no purchase. He added that in any physically realizable situation there will always be some micro-asymmetry — a slight difference in distance, in ambient light, in muscle fatigue — so perfect ties never actually occur. The ass, he said, is like a frictionless plane: useful for isolating variables in mechanics, but physically impossible, and we should not expect rational agency to solve a problem that nature never presents.

The cognitive scientist replied that this confuses normative structure with empirical occurrence. Idealizations are legitimate in philosophy just as they are in physics. If the reasons are genuinely balanced, rationality does not fail; it permits either option, and an agent may break the tie arbitrarily — by randomization, by habit, or by whim — without irrationality. The starvation result is paradoxical only if one assumes, without argument, that every rational action must be backed by a decisive reason. That assumption, she said, is rationalist dogma.

The logician then pressed a different point. Allowing arbitrary choice, he said, introduces a discontinuity in practical reasoning that threatens diachronic agency. If my selection between two identical options is not determined by any reason belonging to me, then in an identical future situation I have no rational basis for consistency — I might choose differently with no explanation. This breaks the explanatory chain that makes an action genuinely mine, rather than an event that merely happens in my body. He likened it to demanding that a deterministic physical system have memory without a storage mechanism.

The cognitive scientist answered that this over-intellectualizes practical identity. Continuity across time is maintained not solely by reason-linkage but by memory, habit, bodily continuity, and external scaffolding. She noted that in quantum mechanics, genuinely identical particles are indistinguishable, yet the world proceeds without paralysis; perfect symmetry does not cause causal stasis. By analogy, perfect value-symmetry should not cause agential stasis. The demand that every choice be traceable to a decisive reason, she concluded, mistakes rationality for a complete causal account of behavior.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four positions above — from clearly mistaken to clearly correct — with brief justification for each rating. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify the central issue that both seem to be missing, particularly regarding whether the analogy between physical idealization and normative rationality actually holds, or whether practical reasoning involves a distinct completeness condition that physical description does not.
