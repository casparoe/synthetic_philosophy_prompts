I am a clinical psychologist who, since beginning work six months ago with survivors of state-sponsored violence, has found the question of perpetrator motivation personally urgent in a way it never was during my earlier training. I am fluent in the vocabulary of social and clinical psychology—moral disengagement mechanisms, fundamental attribution error, situational versus dispositional causation, normalization of deviance, moral licensing, and the distinction between moral injury and perpetration-induced trauma—but I have no formal historical training. I want you to restate the debate between Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen's argument in Hitler's Willing Executioners entirely within that vocabulary. Translate Browning's emphasis on peer conformity, bureaucratic careerism, and the gradual accommodation to killing into terms of situational attribution, normalization of deviance, and collective moral disengagement; translate Goldhagen's emphasis on voluntary participation, eliminationist antisemitism, and the absence of coercion into terms of dispositional moral pathology, culturally shared cognitive schemas, and the failure of moral licensing constraints. Do this as faithfully as possible: do not smuggle in assumptions from clinical psychology that distort either historian's specific claims about German society, military structure, or ideological formation. Then provide the residue report. For each historian, specify which claims translate cleanly, which survive only through distortion or loss of historical specificity—for instance, whether Goldhagen's particular account of German eliminationist culture collapses into a generic "dispositional" explanation, or whether Browning's granular attention to unit dynamics dissolves into abstract "situational" language. Identify anything that has no expression at all in this clinical vocabulary, and tell me whether that absence indicates a genuine explanatory gap in my framework—something about collective ideological formation or historical particularity that individual-level behavioral concepts cannot capture—or whether it reveals that parts of the historical debate depend on distinctions that lose substance once restated in behavioral-science terms. Finally, address whether the "ordinary men" versus "willing executioners" framing itself survives this translation intact, or whether my vocabulary forces a false dichotomy that neither Browning nor Goldhagen intended, and what that implies for how I should think about the perpetrators my patients describe.
