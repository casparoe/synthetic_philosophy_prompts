I was chatting with a friend on the bus about that thought experiment where there's a planet just like ours except the clear stuff in rivers is some other chemical, XYZ, not H2O, and people there have identical brains but talk about "water" in all the same ways. We both agreed the usual point is that the word means something different there because it tracks the other substance. But then we started arguing about properties like "being wet" or "being drinkable", and suddenly my friend treated those as meaning exactly the same thing on both planets, even though the liquid they're applied to is chemically different. So the refering of the noun gets outsourced to the enviroment, but the adjectives stay locked inside the head.

I've noticed this selective split happen in a couple of different debates. One is about false belief: the twin on the other planet is said to be thinking about XYZ, not H2O, but if they claim "this water isn't drinkable" they're not seen as misunderstanding the concept of drinkability—just applying an internal concept correctly to the wrong external stuff. The other is in arguments about mental content and error, where people want to allow mistakes about water without giving up externalism, so they keep the concept internal and only let the "world" part fix which substance you're talking about. It's like there's an invisible boundary between the parts of a thought that get exported and the parts that stay home, and that boundary always shifts to protect whichever intuition is under pressure.

Is there an established name for this asymmetry—where one part of a mental representation is treated as externally determined and another part as internally shared, specifically to preserve both externalist reference and internalist explanation of error? I know it's near things like context-sensitivity or the division of linguistic labour, but those don't quite capture the noun-predicate split, or the way the line moves argument by argument. If there isn't a specific term, could you name and define it precisely, and say exactly how it differs from plain equivocation or from saying concepts are just multidimensional? I'm not a philosopher by training, so I might be missing something obvious, but I'd like to know where the edges of the thing are.
