I am a doctoral student in comparative ethics and philosophy of religion, and I am designing a single fifty-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates who have encountered basic Buddhist ethical frameworks and some negative-utilitarian arguments, but who habitually interpret any talk of “peace” or “tranquility” as a species of positive affective well-being or as stoic suppression of emotion. The concept I need them to grasp is tranquilism: the thesis that the sole intrinsic good is the absence of craving and suffering, with no corresponding positive mental state being necessary or even relevant to the value achieved. I do not want an exposition of the view itself; I want a pedagogical architecture for delivering it. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—say, between comparative absence and hedonic zero-point, or between cessation and suppression—must be secured before tranquilism can be articulated without collapsing into hedonism, apathy, or mystical ineffability? Second, specify the single carrying example or scenario that should anchor the session: it must isolate the absence of craving from both ordinary pleasure and from numbness or death-like indifference, and you should say exactly what features it must possess and what misreadings it must avoid. Third, map the misconception sequence in the order students will likely generate it—perhaps “this is just clinical depression,” then “this is Stoic emotional suppression,” then “absence is just a very low-level positive state”—and indicate the precise point in the explanatory arc where each should be headed off rather than corrected retroactively. Fourth, propose one check-for-understanding question or brief exercise that would reveal whether a student has actually internalized the non-comparative, absence-only structure of the view, as opposed to having merely adopted a more austere form of happiness-maximizing intuition. Fifth, state explicitly what should be bracketed or refused at this level—whether comparative taxonomy with other “negative” well-being theories, metaphysical claims about the self, or textual exegesis—to keep the session from drifting into familiar debates the students already know how to navigate. Please construct the plan with attention to the logical dependencies and the misconception dynamics, not with bibliographic recommendations, reading lists, or citations.
