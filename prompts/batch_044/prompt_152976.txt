I serve on the clinical ethics board of a large transplant center, and we are required to publish a binding tie-breaking protocol for the rare cases in which two candidates score identically across every metric in our allocation system. Rather than defaulting to chance, I want the procedure grounded in the philosophical analysis of rational choice without preference — specifically the framework associated with Buridan's ass — translated into a reproducible, stepwise checklist that a non-philosopher referee could execute under time pressure with only the clinical file at hand. Lay out the ordered checks: how to classify a tie as epistemic (unmeasured differences remain) or genuinely value-equivalent, which secondary distinctions may legitimately break symmetry, the precise point at which analysis ends and selection becomes arbitrary but fully documented, and the escalation conditions where judgment must pass to the full board because the conflict is one of values rather than facts. Apply the procedure to three scenarios: two patients with identical survival predictions but different ages; two patients tied on every metric including age; and a tie resolved only by geographic proximity to the donor organ. Include one scenario in which the procedure yields a recommendation the board will almost certainly reject, and close by specifying exactly what the procedure cannot determine and where it is designed to misfire. The protocol must require no external literature, depend on no specialized philosophical training, and be completable within fifteen minutes.
