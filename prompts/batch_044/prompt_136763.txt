Write a deadpan parody of a bureaucratic "Charter of Citizenship and Governance" for a fictional modern state, the Republic of Common Notions, that has implemented Spinoza's democratic theory literally. Format it as numbered clauses with subsections, in the flat, serious voice of an administrative manual. The satire should exaggerate real tensions in Spinoza's account: democracy as the form of government closest to nature because it expresses the collective potentia of the multitude; "common consent" understood as rational consensus rather than majority vote; citizenship restricted to those capable of adequate ideas and free from passion-driven slavery; and the impossibility of secession because individual natural right is coextensive with collective power. Include niche provisions that literalize these concepts—e.g., a polling mechanism weighted by the clarity of one's ideas, an exclusion clause for citizens "led solely by affect," and a "Right of Nature" article stating that exit is a logical contradiction. The humor must come from accurate exaggeration of Spinoza's actual positions, not invented ones; it should be readable to someone without prior knowledge but rewarding to a specialist. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan—never wink, never explain. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not quote verbatim from Spinoza's texts; paraphrase without quotation marks if you reference specific doctrines. Length: approximately 700 words for the charter, plus no more than 150 words for an appended author's note. In that note, separate what is settled about Spinoza's democratic theory from what is genuinely contested among scholars (for instance, whether his citizenship exclusions are historically contingent or grounded in universal rational criteria, and whether his democracy is best interpreted as direct popular rule or as an aristocratic council guided by reason), and clearly flag any liberties or anachronisms introduced in the charter's provisions.
