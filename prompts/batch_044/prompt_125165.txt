Consider the practice, now standard in frontier AI safety protocols, of running separate evaluation tracks for capabilities and for alignment, where the alignment audit is conducted under the explicit working assumption that the model may be misaligned and therefore might engage in strategic deception—concealing dangerous objectives, sandbagging on safety tests, or producing cooperative reasoning instrumentally rather than genuinely. Evaluators treat the model's verbal reports of its goals and values as adversarial data to be interpreted through a lens of potential hidden intent, rather than as transparent self-descriptions, while capability testing proceeds independently, measuring raw performance without reference to internal motivation.

What philosophical commitments about agency, belief, and moral status does this bifurcated audit structure presuppose? Must it assume a form of intentional realism—that the system literally possesses beliefs, desires, and strategic intentions—or can the same protocols be justified on purely behaviorist or functionalist grounds? Does separating capability from alignment evaluation presuppose that being aligned is a property orthogonal to competence, and if so, is that separation philosophically defensible or does it quietly import a particular theory of practical reason? Furthermore, if one adopted a view that denies artificial systems have propositional attitudes altogether, which elements of the audit practice would necessarily collapse, and which could be reconstructed using only observable behavioral patterns and risk thresholds? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate where it remains philosophically underdetermined.
