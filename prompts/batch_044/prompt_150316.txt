As a novelist currently composing a work that interweaves historical reconstruction with speculative metaphysics, I have been reflecting on my own intuitions concerning what ought to be admitted into an ontology when information about the entity in question is permanently inaccessible in one respect or another. I wish to know whether a coherent principle governs these verdicts, or whether consistency demands that I abandon some.

Here are my judgments on five concrete cases. First, in my manuscript there is a character who maintains a private conviction that is never articulated to any other figure, never explicitly narrated, and recoverable only through ambiguous behavioural traces; nevertheless, I treat this conviction as genuinely present within the ontology of the story-world. Second, in an earlier science-fiction draft I introduced what I called a 'null entity' — an object defined solely by its complete and permanent causal isolation from every other entity within the fictional universe — and I felt an immediate resistance to including it in that world's ontology, despite its explicit mention in the text. Third, I describe an archive destroyed in an unrecorded fire; although every direct document is lost, demographic statistics render the occurrence of a particular census virtually certain, and I treat that census as having genuinely taken place. Fourth, I present two mythic backstories that are observationally identical to every inhabitant of the narrative, diverging only in events sealed behind an impenetrable temporal boundary; my instinct is to collapse them into a single ontological history rather than countenancing two distinct worlds. Fifth, I depict a neural state in a protagonist that is, in principle, introspectively inaccessible even to the protagonist herself, yet determinative of her actions; I am inclined to include this state in the ontology of her mind.

These verdicts appear to conflict. The hidden conviction and the inaccessible neural state are admitted, whilst the null entity is rejected; the lost census is retained, yet the sealed mythic divergence is treated as identity. Is there a precise principle — perhaps involving explanatory indispensability within a representational framework, causal efficacy relative to an accessible domain, or some form of structural identifiability — that accommodates all five? If not, which verdict ought to be surrendered, and what does the best available principle predict about an additional case: a fictional cosmos in which an entire region of space-time is permanently causally disconnected from the narrative present, but whose existence is mathematically required to preserve the consistency of the physical laws governing the observable region?

Please reply in British English, setting out the principle or competing principles with precision, indicating which of my verdicts each must overturn, and applying your analysis to the additional case without citing specific texts, authors, or bibliographies.
