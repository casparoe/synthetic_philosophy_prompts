Consider the scale argument in the ethics of insect welfare: even if the per-capita probability that an individual insect is phenomenally conscious is low—perhaps on the order of 10⁻² to 10⁻¹—the number of insects affected by agricultural and ecological practices (often estimated in the range of 10¹⁸ to 10¹⁹ individuals annually) yields a vast expected moral burden, yet our evidential access to any particular insect's inner life remains severely limited. Rank the following five philosophical responses to this tension into tiers (S-tier, A-tier, B-tier, C-tier, D-tier) according to how well they jointly preserve moral seriousness toward insects and appropriate epistemic humility. For each tier, provide a one-sentence justification of its placement.

The responses are:

1. The decision-theoretic cutoff view: treat the low per-individual sentience probability as falling below a standard rational-belief threshold (e.g., 0.5), concluding that insects should not be assigned direct moral standing until neuroscience delivers decisive evidence, though indirect ecological concerns may remain.

2. The linear aggregation view: apply standard expected-value reasoning by multiplying the low probability by the enormous population, yielding a large aggregate moral weight that should be incorporated directly into utilitarian or prioritarian calculations without special epistemic discounting.

3. The systemic precaution view: abandon attempts to weigh individual insects and instead target structural practices—such as broad-spectrum pesticide regimes or monoculture expansion—on the grounds that systemic risk management is warranted even when individual-level sentience is opaque.

4. The dimensional pluralist view: reject the binary framing of sentience altogether; insects may possess high scores on specific dimensions (e.g., nociceptive integration, affective valence) without meeting a unitary consciousness threshold, so obligations should track these dimensions separately rather than aggregate into a single probability-weighted value.

5. The epistemic dilution view: hold that as the scale of affected entities increases, the evidential obligation owed to each individual entity proportionally decreases, but the institutional obligation to avoid catastrophic or systemic harm proportionally increases—transforming the scale argument from a direct moral-weight multiplier into a regulatory-risk framework analogous to climate or public-health governance.

After providing the tier list, identify which single position is most vulnerable to the charge that it smuggles in an unearned metaphysical commitment—specifically, a form of moral realism about aggregate suffering that presupposes precisely the kind of unified phenomenal experience whose existence the scale argument admits we cannot verify. Defend your identification in two to three sentences, and then state the one concession that a defender of that position should make without collapsing the view entirely.
