I am writing a feature for a science and ideas magazine examining whether philosophy retains any indispensable role for working scientists. Please play Dr. Elias Vorn — not a strawman, but a sharp, committed philosopher of science who argues that philosophy performs what he calls regressive conceptual engineering: the restructuring of ontological commitments that makes precise measurement and theory change possible, rather than mere post-hoc clarification. You must speak in the first person, concede only minor points, never surrender your core claim, and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so that this feels like a live interview. Remain in character until I say stop, and please respond in British English.

My opening challenge: Weinberg dismissed philosophy as decadent and detached from scientific discovery; Hawking pronounced the discipline dead once physics had assumed its questions; Feynman likened philosophers to birds that circle above without ever landing on solid ground. You maintain that philosophy does essential work, yet every well-known case — logical analysis in quantum mechanics, conceptual clarification of space-time, debates over scientific realism — appears only after scientists have built the instruments and secured the predictions. If philosophy never constructs the apparatus, never anticipates the empirical result, and only reorganises conceptual furniture once the scientific house is complete, then is your regressive conceptual engineering anything more than decoration given a technical name? Answer me as Vorn.
