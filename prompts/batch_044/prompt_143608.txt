I've been trying to reconstruct Émilie du Châtelet's position on happiness from her writings and correspondence, and I've arrived at the following package of commitments. Before I present this to a reading group, I want to know whether it hangs together or whether I'm attributing to her an incoherent set.

Here is where I stand:

(1) Her account is fundamentally hedonistic in structure: happiness consists in the sustained satisfaction of dominant passions, and reason's role is instrumental—directing desire toward durable satisfactions rather than defining happiness itself.

(2) For women excluded from political and public life, rigorous study—particularly in mathematics and natural philosophy—provides an irreplaceable and autonomous source of durable happiness, because it creates a domain of satisfaction that does not depend on marital fortune or courtly approval.

(3) Romantic love is not merely compatible with study but structurally necessary for women's happiness: without the specific affective recognition that love provides, intellectual achievement alone yields an incomplete or hollow happiness.

(4) The pursuit of public glory—being esteemed for one's intellectual contributions—is a rational and legitimate component of happiness, and suppressing this ambition in the name of feminine modesty is itself a source of misery.

(5) Any passion, including love and the desire for glory, undermines happiness if it reaches an intensity that clouds judgment or distorts one's assessment of reality, because durable satisfaction requires that our beliefs remain accurate.

(6) Finally, she adopts a methodological constraint: normative claims about flourishing must be evaluated by their livability from positions of structural dependence, which means that any theory presupposing complete self-sufficiency (for instance, Stoic apatheia) should be rejected for ignoring the relational conditions of women's lives.

My questions are these: Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? For each real tension, rank them by severity and identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency—specifying exactly which commitment must be weakened, qualified, or abandoned and what that costs to the overall view. For the pairs that dissolve on inspection, explain why the conflict is merely apparent. Do not treat this as a lecture on du Châtelet in general; treat my specific package as the object of audit. I am not signaling which commitment I would most readily give up, so do not assume I have a hidden preference.
