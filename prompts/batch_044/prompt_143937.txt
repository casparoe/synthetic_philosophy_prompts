Consider the intersection of absurdist philosophy—particularly the view that the human drive for meaning confronts an indifferent universe—and contemporary naturalist accounts of meaning in life, which typically ground meaning in objective worth, subjective engagement, or some combination thereof. A tension that receives less attention than it should is what we might call the counterfactual stability problem: if a project derives part of its significance from its resistance to cosmic indifference, does that significance survive in nearby possible worlds where the project's success conditions are satisfied trivially, or where the agent's engagement is produced through non-standard causal pathways rather than earned effort? In other words, does meaningfulness require modal robustness—stability across variations in how engagement is generated—or only local coherence between agent and project?

Treat this not as a single puzzle but as a research agenda. Suppose you must direct a small philosophy research group—three years, four people—working specifically on the conceptual structure of meaning-under-absurdism. Which open questions or lines of work should take priority, and in what order?

Specifically:
1. Rank the following kinds of work in order of urgency for this agenda: (a) clarifying whether absurdism is primarily an epistemic claim (the universe lacks meaning-structure) or an existential stance (the human need for meaning is disproportionate to available resources); (b) analyzing whether contemporary naturalist accounts implicitly depend on a tacit rejection of absurdism, or whether they can be reconstructed to accommodate it without loss; (c) developing conceptual tools to assess whether a project retains meaning when its achievement conditions are satisfied counterfactually or through pharmacologically induced engagement; (d) investigating whether the distinction between semantic futility—no cosmic significance—and practical redundancy—the interchangeability of projects—can do independent philosophical work, or whether one collapses into the other.
2. For your top-ranked item, explain why it is upstream of the others: which downstream questions depend on its resolution, how tractable it is for conceptual philosophy rather than empirical science, and what argument or result over the next few years would either confirm its priority or demote it.
3. Identify one line of inquiry common in current discussions of meaning and absurdism that you think is overrated for this agenda—perhaps because its answer would not change much downstream, or because it cannot be resolved without first settling your top-ranked question.

Defend your ordering using explicit criteria (tractability for philosophers without laboratory resources, neglect relative to mainstream ethics, dependency relations among questions, and the magnitude of practical or theoretical payoff if resolved). Be direct about trade-offs: if you deprioritize empirical psychology of meaning, say why; if you elevate a formal or modal question, justify the risk of obscurity.

Finally, be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable: what distinction, hypothetical result, or counterexample would cause you to reorder this agenda? And which step in your argument—perhaps the claim that modal robustness is relevant at all, or the dependency relation between (a) and (b)—is most likely to fail?
