I have been working through karma as a philosophical theory of moral desert rather than as popular folklore, and I have encountered what looks like a deep tension. If karma assigns fruits to intentional actions—cetana—yet the Abhidharma doctrine of momentariness (ksanikavada) treats mental states as existing only for an instant, and the anattā teaching denies any enduring self that could own them, then there appears to be no persistent agent available to deserve reward or punishment. Must we therefore conclude that karma cannot literally function as a theory of moral desert, and should instead be interpreted as a purely causal-psychological account of conditioning with no normative weight? Or is there a philosophically coherent way to preserve desert without requiring a continuous bearer? Please separate what is well established in the relevant Buddhist philosophical traditions and in comparative ethics from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—especially regarding whether karma requires a metaphysical substratum or can be fully naturalized—and do not present any single interpretation as the consensus view.
