I hold that Western anti-corruption norms are not culturally imperialist but instead identify a functional failure—what I call the collapse of role-based distance—where an office-holder’s decisions become responsive to private obligations rather than to the institutional criteria that justify the office. Clientelism and ceremonial gift-giving are corrupt on this account precisely when they generate “shadow obligations”: unstated, open-ended commitments that statistically predict discretionary allocations without being formally contractible, regardless of local norms of reciprocity. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which ethnographic patterns, which conceptual distinctions (for instance, between accountability-preserving reciprocity and accountability-dissolving reciprocity), which results in institutional design, or which cross-cultural normative disputes would genuinely undermine the claim that role-distance is the universal fault-line rather than merely complicating it? Distinguish the defeaters that defenders should concede as damaging from those they could dismiss as missing the functional point, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
