I sit on the Clinical Capacity and Consent Oversight Board of a psychiatric facility, and we must issue a written opinion by Friday on Case 47. The patient, who suffers from severe treatment-resistant depression, has refused a course of ECT. In our structured capacity interview—designed specifically to distinguish first-order preferences from second-order beliefs—she wrote: "The doctors have shown me that this treatment is likely to restore my functioning, but I do not believe it will restore my functioning." She also affirmed that she understands the risks of refusal. The treating psychiatrist argues that this utterance exhibits the structure of Moore's paradox: she asserts the proposition that justifies treatment while simultaneously denying the belief-state that would make assent sincere, thereby undermining the coherence required for informed refusal. The patient's advocate responds that second-order disbelief is a well-known affective feature of depression—an expression of hopelessness rather than a logical failure—and that treating it as incoherence risks pathologizing a legitimate emotional stance. Our board must recommend one of the following: honor the refusal immediately; override it and proceed with treatment; or adopt an intermediate course with an explicit time frame, such as a 72-hour observation period coupled with targeted cognitive assessment of second-order belief stability. Draft the board's memo: state the concrete recommendation and its timeline, explain why the Moore-paradox structure either does or does not compromise autonomy in this clinical context, and treat the advocate's affective-symptom argument as the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not cite literature or reference specific philosophers; reason directly from the conceptual structure of belief, assertion, and clinical competence.
