I have no formal philosophical training, but after reading a popular account of the absent-minded driver problem—the case where someone intends to drive to the store yet ends up at his usual workplace, performing every individual maneuver with full skill—I have been trying on my own to apply its structure to my job. I work as a safety mediator for a small regional transit authority, and I regularly review incident reports in which an operator claims he was “on autopilot” and deviated from protocol, yet every micro-action—signaling, braking, lane-changing—looked deliberate. I need a reusable decision procedure, essentially a checklist or flowchart in words, that I can run in about fifteen minutes on any such report. It should be grounded in whatever philosophy of action actually distinguishes a top-level intended plan from a competing habitual script that has taken over, but translated so that my non-specialist review board can apply it without jargon.

The procedure should ask its questions in a fixed order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, state exactly where it stops with a clear classification, and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as rules. I want it built around a three-layer framework I have been calling a “plan-script fracture audit”: Layer one is the agent’s reported top-level goal at the start of the sequence; Layer two is the routine or script that actually guided the behavior; Layer three is whether the individual physical actions remained under basic intentional control. The procedure should classify cases based on whether all three layers align, whether layer one and layer two have diverged while layer three holds, or whether even layer three has broken down.

Please apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a bus driver who misses three scheduled stops and pulls into his former depot, where he has not worked in two years, claiming he “just drifted there”; (b) a delivery cyclist who takes a wrong bridge because he was replaying a personal argument in his head, but immediately notices the error and corrects it; and (c) a case where the procedure will classify the deviation as not fully excused, even though my intuition is to sympathize—specifically, a driver who runs a familiar red-light pattern on a route he drives daily, saying he was “not thinking,” but the board needs a consistent reason to deny the excuse. For each case, show the path through the checklist step by step.

Finally, tell me honestly what this procedure cannot settle—for instance, whether a competing script was genuinely unconscious or merely neglected, or whether moral blame should automatically follow the classification—and identify at least one point where applying it mechanically will misfire. Do not include reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources; I need something I can put in front of a board that has no philosophy background.
