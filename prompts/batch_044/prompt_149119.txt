I'm drafting a historical novel set in 1840s New Orleans and one of my characters is a kind of amateur sociologist who is convinced that moral revolutions never actually start with arguments. She thinks dueling died out not because people suddenly realized killing each other over insults was wrong, but because of what she calls 'status inversion'—once challenging someone started marking you as vulgar and desperate for status rather than honorable, the practice collapsed, and then people invented moral language afterwards to cover the shift. She's basically running a Bernard Williams / Kwame Appiah line: norms like dueling or even slavery end through shifts in honor calculus and economic intrest, and any moral reasoning that shows up is just retroactive moralization. I'm pretty sold on this view, but I want to stress-test it like a philosopher, not just a novelist.

So present this honor-inversion thesis and tell me what would genuinely count against it—not surface-level objections like 'someone wrote an anti-dueling pamphlet,' but the deep structural defeaters. Which historical patterns, which results about how norms actually diffuse or collapse, which specific cases where the argument clearly came first and the social shift followed? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Also, what would a committed defender of this view concede as genuinely damaging, versus what they'd dismiss as post-hoc storytelling? I'm especially worried the thesis might be too well-insulated—if every moral argument gets reinterpreted as rationalization, is there any observation that could break that loop? And does it risk collapsing into a pure economic-interest theory, and if so, is that a refinement or a defeat?
