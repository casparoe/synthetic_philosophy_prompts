Consider the position associated with Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument: that true moral responsibility—the kind that genuinely makes reward or punishment deserved—is impossible because it requires an agent to be causa sui, the ultimate origin of herself. Since an agent’s character, motives, and mental architecture are ultimately shaped by heredity, environment, and prior causal chains beyond her control, she can never be the ultimate source of her actions; the regress of explanation either runs infinitely or terminates in something outside her. On this view, compatibilist accounts that preserve “freedom” by identifying it with acting in accordance with one’s own motives, without requiring that those motives themselves be self-originated, are said to change the subject rather than solve the problem.

Play this straight: what would have to be true for that conclusion to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a catalog of familiar rebuttals.

Specifically:
- Which premises in the Basic Argument could in principle fail? Could the inference from “not ultimately self-caused” to “not genuinely responsible” be blocked without collapsing into standard compatibilism? What would defenders of the argument themselves concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to objections they treat as missing the point?
- Is there a coherent intermediate position—one that grants agents are not causa sui with respect to their global character, but maintains that they can be localized originators of particular choices through transparent, self-modifying deliberative protocols—such that the explanatory regress is halted at the level of specific decisions rather than the self as a whole? If such a position is coherent, does it escape the argument or merely relocate the problem? What conceptual or empirical development would make it viable or untenable?
- What commitments or predictions does the Basic Argument carry that its rivals do not? For example, does it entail that reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude are always irrational, or only that they are never genuinely deserved? Does it risk collapsing into a practical fatalism about moral practice, and is that a cost its defenders accept, deny, or treat as irrelevant?
- Distinguish evidence or argument that would be decisive from what would merely be awkward. Would a convincing account of neuroscience showing transparent self-authorship of particular choices undermine the argument, or would defenders absorb it by distinguishing causal origin from normative authorship? Would a formal result showing that “ultimate origin” is conceptually incoherent (rather than merely unattainable) strengthen the argument or weaken it?
- Finally, say what would change your mind: which specific argument, hypothetical case, or result would lead you to think the Basic Argument fails more decisively, or succeeds more strongly, than your assessment suggests, and identify where in this reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
