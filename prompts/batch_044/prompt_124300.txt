I am currently designing a forecasting pipeline in which two large language models share identical base weights but have been separately fine-tuned on epidemiological and economic corpora, respectively, and I have recently found myself in a dispute about how to interpret their persistent disagreements. A colleague insists that we should treat them as instances of the standard Bayesian picture: they begin with a common prior (the shared pre-trained weights), update on private evidence (their respective domain texts), and, if rational and fully communicative, ought eventually to converge, just as Aumann's agreement theorem predicts for human reasoners. The analogy is being used to license a specific operational conclusion—that any remaining divergence is necessarily a symptom of hidden data leakage, irrational inference, or failed communication, and therefore must be eliminated rather than tolerated. I am skeptical, but I want an explicit audit rather than an intuition. Please lay out the correspondence table precisely: what in the model setup plays the role of the prior, the private evidence, the conditioning event, the rationality constraint, and the shared communication channel? Then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the operational conclusion—in particular, whether fine-tuning is properly analogous to Bayesian conditioning, whether shared weights constitute a common prior in the epistemic sense required by the theorem, and whether these systems possess anything like common knowledge of each other's rationality. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that divergence is always a bug survives once the mapping is corrected, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work, and if there is a better framing for understanding disagreement between differently specialized models.
