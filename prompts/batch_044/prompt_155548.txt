In recent coverage of economic coordination and artificial intelligence, commentators frequently draw an analogy between large-scale algorithmic planning systems and the socialist calculation mechanisms debated by Friedrich Hayek, Ludwig von Mises, and Oskar Lange. The comparison runs that modern data-driven optimization — with its distributed sensors, real-time feedback loops, and loss-function targets — finally realizes the iterative price-adjustment model Lange proposed as a market-socialist alternative to capitalism, thereby overcoming the knowledge-dispersion objection that Hayek and Mises raised against central planning. The analogy is used to license the claim that algorithmic governance might resolve the calculation debate in planning's favor. I want a rigorous audit of this comparison. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the algorithmic system plays the role of Lange's central board, the trial-and-error price mechanism, the consumer preference data, and the entrepreneurial discovery process that Hayek insisted could not be centralized? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the intended conclusion — particularly regarding temporal dynamics of discovery, the political problem of command versus aggregation, and the absence of profit-and-loss signals that discipline error — and distinguish these from superficial mismatches that do not affect the argument. Third, assess whether the conclusion that AI vindicates central planning survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If the analogy collapses, suggest a more accurate comparison for algorithmic economic coordination — one that does not depend on the planning-versus-market frame — and say what that replacement implies for the governance debate.
