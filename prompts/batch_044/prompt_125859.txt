Assume the role of a philosopher-poet with training in moral psychology and virtue theory. Before composing, briefly outline in one or two sentences how a villanelle’s circular repetition can structurally mirror the situationist claim that behavior recurs by context rather than by stable disposition. Then write the poem.

Write a strict villanelle (nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, two refrains) that stages virtue ethics against the situationist critique through a niche framing: treat low cross-situational correlation not as proof that character is absent, but as evidence for “moral fragmentation”—narrow, context-bound dispositions that never aggregate into global traits. Use refrain A, “The trait divides by place and hour” (the situationist observation of behavioral splitting), and refrain B, “No virtue lives, but virtues do” (the fragmentationist retreat to local expertise). In the octave (lines 1–9), present specific situational variables—authority presence, time pressure, ambient anonymity—that split honest or compassionate behavior across rooms. In the sestet (lines 10–15), attempt to salvage virtue as “local expertise,” knowing when and where to act rather than acting everywhere. End with the final couplet placing both refrains adjacent without synthesis (line 18: A; line 19: B).

Choose tight rhymes (e.g., A-rhymes on hour/power/flower; B-rhymes on do/through/true) so formal proximity mirrors conceptual proximity. The poem’s circularity should feel like the empirical cycle: same agent, different context, different act. Make it accurate enough that someone familiar with the classic situationist literature recognizes the specific challenge—lack of cross-situational consistency, predictive power of situational variables—rather than a vague claim that “people are inconsistent.” Do not cite authors, texts, or experiments by name; do not include bibliographic notes. Think carefully about how the villanelle’s enforced returns make the two interpretations keep passing each other, unable to resolve. Keep the register formal and slightly archaic, appropriate to philosophical verse.
