For a philosophy graduate seminar of about twelve students meeting for ninety minutes, design a runnable in-class simulation that stages the sorites paradox as a structural failure of collective judgment rather than as an individual intuition pump. Invent a fictional “District Heritage Board” charged with applying the vague statutory criterion sufficiently authentic to the original architectural period to eight buildings arranged in a sequence: each card describes one building that differs incrementally from its neighbor by exactly one minor feature—material substitution, proportional shift, ornamental addition—so that adjacent pairs are intuitively indistinguishable with respect to protected-status entitlement, while the first and last buildings are clearly different in overall status. The rules must require the board to reach unanimous pairwise verdicts on whether each adjacent pair is indistinguishable, then to derive each building’s collective classification by strict transitivity; assign roles including a chair who enforces transitivity, two reviewers permitted to propose hidden distinctions to break ties, and a dissent recorder who logs every appeal to an unstated cutoff. Use simulated institutional credibility points—awarded for consistency across the sequence and penalized for arbitrary reversals—rather than money or grades. Structure the session as ten minutes of silent individual ranking, forty minutes of paired deliberation rounds, twenty minutes of forced collective derivation of the full sequence, and twenty minutes of debrief. In the debrief, map each round onto the premises of the sorites—tolerance principle, transitivity, and clear endpoints—identify the precise moment when the group invents ad hoc distinctions to avoid contradiction, and explain what resisting each premise costs the board’s institutional coherence. Include an explicit, honest note about which philosophical feature—specifically, the metaphysical claim that vagueness resides in the world itself rather than in representational or collective practices—the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Specify two predictable failures when running this for the first time, such as the group collapsing into unstated contextualism by silently shifting the standard mid-sequence, or the chair abandoning transitivity without recognizing the move, and advise how to handle each. Do not lecture beforehand on epistemicism, supervaluationism, or degree-theoretic semantics, and ensure participants do not hear the technical names until the debrief. Avoid bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for specific scholarly attributions.
