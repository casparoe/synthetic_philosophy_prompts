Analyze the operational design of the Global Positioning System — specifically the adoption of the Earth-Centered Inertial frame and the synchronization protocols that correct for both gravitational and kinematic time dilation — as a case study in the Rietdijk-Putnam argument for the block universe. Take the physics of special and general relativity as given; do not summarize them. Instead, map the philosophical structure: GPS engineers must select one reference frame and one simultaneity convention to coordinate satellite atomic clocks with ground receivers. Using the Rietdijk-Putnam reasoning — that if event A is simultaneous with B in one inertial frame and with C in another, and B and C lie at different times, then B and C must be equally real — examine whether the engineering need for a single system time undermines or reinforces the argument. Does the fact that the system functions only when corrections derived from every frame are embedded in the algorithm show that all frames' "now" slices are equally valid, hence that all times exist in a four-dimensional block? Or does the practical privilege granted to the ECI frame reveal an implicit presentist commitment — that there is a true present to which the satellites must be adjusted? Include the 1971 Hafele-Keating experiment, in which atomic clocks flown on aircraft diverged from ground clocks in precisely the directions predicted by relativity, as part of the same episode. Ask whether that divergence is best read as evidence that observers genuinely inhabit different temporal slices of a static block, or whether a dynamic, presentist ontology can accommodate varying clock rates without granting reality to distant past and future events. Conclude by assessing whether GPS is a strong analog to the Andromeda-paradox scenario that Rietdijk and Putnam used, or whether the pragmatism of macroscopic engineering reveals a limitation in applying the relativity of simultaneity to coordinated human systems.
