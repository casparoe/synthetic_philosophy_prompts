For the past eight months I have adhered to an identical morning protocol: I wake at 5:30, meditate for twenty minutes, write for exactly ninety minutes regardless of quality, and then run four miles before work. I designed this sequence after a period of severe burnout, telling myself that its value lay not in any individual session but in the absolute refusal to break the chain. Three weeks ago I injured my knee, and running now produces sharp pain; my writing has also become hollow and repetitive, yet I still complete both blocks and then limp through the run, because omitting any step feels as though the entire edifice collapses. A close friend recently told me I am behaving like a machine executing a script rather than a person exercising judgment, but I feel that altering the routine every time circumstances become uncomfortable is precisely the kind of impulsive flexibility that caused my original collapse.

Not long ago I read an account of a digger wasp that performs a fixed burial ritual: it inspects its burrow, drags a paralyzed caterpillar inside, deposits an egg, and seals the entrance. If a researcher moves the caterpillar outside while the wasp is inspecting the burrow, the wasp does not adapt; it drags the prey back in, inspects again, and repeats the cycle indefinitely, apparently unable to register that the environment has changed. I see my own behavior reflected in this loop—restoring the same activities to their place regardless of injury or diminishing returns—and I fear I have reduced myself to something similarly mechanical. At the same time, I wonder whether genuine discipline is exactly this refusal to change course whenever discomfort appears, and whether my recognition of the pattern proves I am still capable of stepping outside it.
