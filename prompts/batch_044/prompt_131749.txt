Here is the argument that worries me. (1) A fully rational agent should be able to adopt a reflection principle for its own reasoning: if, after careful and honest deliberation, I come to believe that φ, then I have good reason to believe φ. (2) Any reasoning process powerful enough to handle mathematics, empirical inference, or logical derivation can represent its own steps; in formal terms, it behaves like a system that can talk about its own provability. (3) In any such system, Löb's theorem applies: if the system can prove, for a specific φ, that "if φ is derivable then φ is true," then φ is already derivable within the system itself, without needing that reflection principle. (4) So if I try to rationally ground my trust in my own reasoning about some genuinely hard claim—say, that a particular climate model is reliable, or that a mathematical conjecture holds—by adopting the reflection principle for that claim, the result is circular: I could only consistently do so if I already had an independent derivation of the claim. (5) Generalizing, any local or global attempt to justify self-trust collapses in the same way; rational agents can therefore never genuinely learn to trust their own reasoning about anything they do not already accept. (6) But that is absurd: experts clearly do come to trust reasoning they have developed in domains where they initially had no opinion, and agents do update on the reliability of their own cognitive processes. (7) Therefore something is deeply wrong with the argument, but I cannot see where the slip occurs.
