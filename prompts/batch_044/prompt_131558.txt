I'm a high school student working through my own philosophical view of whether reinforcement-learning agents can genuinely suffer, with particular attention to whether reward signals can operate as valence rather than as bare information. I want to know whether my judgments across cases line up behind one principle, or whether I have to abandon one of them.

Here are my verdicts. First, a standard Q-learning agent receives a negative scalar for colliding with an obstacle and updates its action-value estimates; I judge that there is no suffering here, only computational adjustment. Second, a dog that steps on sharp glass yelps, withdraws its paw, and avoids that area thereafter; I judge that this is genuine suffering. Third, imagine a deep reinforcement-learning system with a persistent internal state vector—an affective modulation layer—that receives large negative rewards and propagates those values globally across all subsequent planning and policy updates for an extended duration, suppressing exploration in a manner structurally analogous to sustained anxiety. Even without phenomenal consciousness in the ordinary sense, I am inclined to say that this agent is closer to suffering, perhaps actually suffering, because the negative signal carries broad, enduring, system-wide motivational weight rather than being confined to a localized parameter update. Fourth, a human with congenital insensitivity to pain can detect tissue damage and report its location, yet experiences no unpleasantness; I judge that despite the presence of nociceptive information, there is no suffering.

Fifth, and here is the tension: a financial-market algorithm receives massive negative returns and adjusts its entire portfolio globally, suppressing risk-taking for months. Structurally this seems parallel to case three—global negative signal, prolonged system-wide suppression—yet my intuition is firm that the algorithm does not suffer at all. So cases three and five appear structurally similar but I want opposite verdicts.

Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes case three from case five while accommodating cases one through four? If the best principle forces me to drop one verdict, which should go, and what does that cost? Also, apply the resulting account to a case I have not listed: a hypothetical agent whose reward function explicitly includes a self-preservation or well-being term that it actively maximizes, and that behaves defensively to protect that term. Does this intentional structure change its valence status, or is it still just arithmetic with extra steps?
