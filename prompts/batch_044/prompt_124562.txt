I was arguing with my roommate about EU politics -- I'm taking a course on european intregration and he thinks I'm overfitting patterns -- but I swear I've seen this exact loop like five times now and I want to know if philosophers or political scientists already named it. The pattern goes like this: people say the EU has a demcratic deficit because unelected bodies (Commission, ECB, whatever) make big decisions. Instead of saying "okay, roll those powers back to national parliaments," the fix is always more intregration: strengthen the European Parliament, create transnational lists, give citizens direct input. But then you point out that even where these things exist, turnout is low, there's no real european public sphere, and national interests still dominate council negotiations. At that point the answer shifts: well of course democracy doesn't work yet, member states haven't pooled enough sovereignty, the system is half-baked. So the deficit is a reason to centralize, and centralization's failure to fix democracy is blamed on not centralizing enough. Meanwhile if you suggest returning authority to member-state parliaments -- where media is shared and turnout is high -- that's treated as violating "ever closer union," not as solving the deficit.

I've noticed this in debates about ECB bond-buying, about the Conference on the Future of Europe (citizens recommend, institutions nod, not much binding changes), and about whether the EP should get initiative rights. Is there an established term for this circular justification? I don't mean functional spillover, which is about economic interdependence driving more politics. I don't mean ordinary path dependency either. I'm after the specific argumentative structure where democratic legitimacy is pursued through deeper union, but union's democratic failures are always attributed to leftover national sovereignty. Does that have a name, and if not can you coin one with sharp edges -- including what it is not? Give me a committed take: either identify the concept precisely and defend the fit, or say nothing fits and build the term yourself with boundary cases.
