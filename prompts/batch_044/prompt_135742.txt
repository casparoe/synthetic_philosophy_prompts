I am drafting a strategic research agenda for a small philosophy-of-mind group that has roughly three years of funding to work on conceptual problems arising from epiphenomenalism, particularly the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment: if conscious experiences make no causal difference to the physical processes that generate speech and belief, it is puzzling how subjects can form accurate judgments or verbal reports about those very experiences. Rather than requesting a survey or reading list, I want a defended prioritization of the open conceptual issues that actually determine whether the paradox can be dissolved, accommodated, or turned into a reductio. Among the live options—whether to begin by reconceptualizing the causal closure of the physical domain, by weakening the demands of first-person authority so that causally disconnected reports can still count as knowledge, by treating phenomenal self-reference as an evolutionary spandrel that tracks experience without being caused by it, or by questioning whether the paradox illicitly assumes that mental content must be causally mediated—which should the group tackle first and in what sequence? For each step in your proposed ordering, explain why it is upstream of the others in the sense that resolving it would collapse or simplify remaining disputes, how tractable it is for philosophers working without empirical laboratories, and what kind of conceptual breakthrough over the next few years would justify demoting that line of work. I also want you to distinguish sharply between the implications that genuinely threaten the coherence of epiphenomenalism itself and those that are merely counterintuitive but can be absorbed without abandoning the view, noting which of the latter are frequently overstated in informal discussion.

Please present your response as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or enumerated lists, written in the style of a detailed advisory letter. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or lists of authors and texts, since the advice must stand on conceptual reasoning alone.
