hey — writing a feature on death attitudes across traditions, and I've been stuck on this one pattern with Zhuangzi. Everyone cites the tub-drumming scene after his wife dies, comparing it to the seasons changing. Usually people read that as: death is okay because she just transforms into wind or a tree, like qi recycling. But that feels like a misreading. Zhuangzi's point seems sharper — it's not that she survives in another form, but that 'she' was never a fixed thing to begin with. The grief comes from treating alive and dead as natural kinds rather than human value-judgments, like calling one tree crooked and another strait. So the anti-grief argument isn't "you still exist elsewhere," it's "there was no stable subject that could be lost." I've seen this called process ontology or Daoist naturalism, but those miss the specific move: rejecting grief on grounds of a category error about identity, not on harm-cessation or Buddhist no-self. Is there an actual term for this exact configuration? If not, can you map its edges — how it differs from Epicurus saying death is nothing to the dead, from Buddhist anatta, and from the naive she-became-nature reading? I don't need a biblography, just want to know if I'm describing something real or inventing a distintion.
