I am writing a novel in which a philosophy tutor, Dr. Elena Voss, leads a ninety-minute graduate seminar of twelve students, and I want the pedagogical scene to be conceptually rigorous rather than expository. The domain is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—specifically, whether pleasures share a common qualitative nature that makes them commensurable for maximization, or whether the warmth of sun on cold skin, the sudden resolution of a logical paradox, and the sweetness of a remembered forgiveness are so phenomenologically dissimilar that treating them as units of a single currency is a category mistake. Design a runnable classroom activity, complete with explicit rules, roles, and structured constraints, that makes the participants feel this collapse of commensurability from the inside rather than through lecture. The exercise should require students to construct and defend a life portfolio distributing a fixed number of experiential slots across these heterogeneous domains—gustatory, thermal, intellectual, emotional—using only the proprietary descriptive vocabulary native to each pleasure and explicitly forbidding generic translation terms such as better, stronger, or more pleasant. By the midpoint they should have spontaneously built comparative rankings and allocation arguments; by the end they should recognize that every comparison has smuggled in an alien metric, leaving them with an unstable portfolio and a felt intuition that hedonism requires something—a hidden quantitative dimension or a neutral scale—that the phenomenology itself does not provide.

Then provide a debrief that maps these moments of arbitrary exchange onto the philosophical positions: where Mill’s qualitative distinctions attempt to preserve hierarchy without reducing pleasure to one scale, where Sidgwick’s introspective method assumes a shared reflective access that the vocabulary-block reveals as questionable, and where a niche framing I will call phenomenological pluralism argues that pleasures are not merely different in kind but ontologically incommensurable in the way colors are for a blind subject comparing spectra by proxy. The debrief should predict what will go wrong in execution—likely that students with aesthetic or intellectual backgrounds will dominate the vocabulary game, forcing thermal or emotional pleasures into subordinate metaphors—and explain how the facilitator should handle this without resolving the tension prematurely. Be explicit about what feature of the idea the rules can reproduce—the impossibility of neutral comparison—and what must be supplied by the facilitator: the normative move that maximization is impossible without such comparison, and therefore that hedonism either needs a hidden common currency or must abandon its quantitative ambition. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the students and the scene should not need them, and the design should be constructible entirely from the conceptual structure given.
