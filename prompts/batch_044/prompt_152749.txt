Here is the pattern, observed across several discussions of mental causation. A philosopher wants to defend the causal power of mental properties—say, the belief that it is raining—while fully accepting that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. They invoke a realization relation: the mental property is realized by, but not identical to, its neural substrate. When the exclusion argument is pressed—if the neural substrate does all the causal work, what is left for the mental property to do?—the defender introduces a careful distinction. The mental property is causally relevant (it figures in explanations, it tracks counterfactual dependencies, it is part of the constitutive story) but not causally efficacious in the same robust, productive sense as the physical realizer. The mental property “makes a difference,” but only in a derivative or structural way.

The specific move comes next. Once this defensive distinction is in place and the exclusion threat is neutralized, the same philosopher—sometimes in the very same paper—will invoke the mental property in an explanation that clearly requires robust causal efficacy. They will say that the belief caused the action, not merely that the belief was realized by a neural state that caused the action. They do not explicitly revoke the defensive distinction; they do not announce a shift to an ordinary-language framework. They simply operate as if the mental property has direct causal power, enjoying all the explanatory benefits that the strict distinction was designed to protect.

The pattern has three consistent features. First, a defensive distinction is introduced specifically to answer the exclusion argument. Second, that distinction is not applied consistently across explanatory contexts; it is suspended whenever it would be inconvenient.
