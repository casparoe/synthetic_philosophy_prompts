Rank the following four interpretations of Quine’s stance on moral values according to how faithfully they capture his actual philosophical commitments, using a scale of 1 (poor fit) to 5 (strong fit), and justify each rating in one or two sentences.

The interpretations are:

A. Quine regards moral discourse as non-cognitive and purely expressive, on a par with his well-known scepticism about modal logic and essentialism, so that moral sentences lack truth-values altogether.

B. Quine allows moral claims to occupy nodes in the holistic web of belief, revisable in principle by empirical or logical pressure, but he denies them any ontological import comparable to the physical objects of his desert landscape.

C. Quine implicitly advances a naturalised moral epistemology: because he insists that ethics is methodologically continuous with empirical science, he must hold that moral beliefs are subject to the same underdetermination and holistic revision as scientific theories.

D. Quine’s thesis of ontological relativity entails that different translation manuals or conceptual schemes can embed incompatible normative frameworks without objective contradiction, yielding a form of moral pluralism consistent with his broader anti-realism.

In your answer, indicate for each interpretation whether it draws directly on arguments Quine made about ethics, or whether it illegitimately extends positions he developed in the philosophy of mathematics, ontology, or language to a domain he treated with deliberate reticence. Specifically, assess whether C confuses Quine’s claim about methodological continuity with a substantive commitment to moral naturalism he never defended, and whether D overreads ontological relativity by applying it to normative rather than descriptive content.
