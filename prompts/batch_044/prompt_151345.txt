I am drafting an essay on Kant's account of perpetual peace, and before I present it I want to check whether my own commitments form a coherent package rather than a collection of attractive but incompatible ideas. Here is my position. (1) I accept Kant's argument that a republican constitution is necessary for lasting peace, specifically because only active citizens—those with civil independence—should decide on war, and they will be reluctant to bear its costs. (2) I reject the democratic peace thesis as an empirical law: I think modern correlations between democracy and peace are better explained by economic interdependence and nuclear deterrence than by any structural feature of popular government. (3) I hold that cosmopolitan hospitality should be read as including a positive, institutional duty of temporary sanctuary for climate refugees, not merely the negative right of a traveler not to be treated with hostility upon arrival. (4) I defend the federation of free states over a universal world republic, because I take legal and cultural pluralism to be a moral good that centralized sovereignty would erase. (5) I concede that Kant's preliminary article against standing armies is obsolete, since credible nuclear deterrence requires exactly the kind of permanent military readiness he condemns.

My question is whether these five hang together. In particular, is there a hidden tension between (2) and (1)—does my rejection of democratic peace theory actually undermine the republican mechanism I accept, or is the distinction between normative structure and empirical correlation enough to save consistency? More seriously, does (3) conflict with (4): if hospitality creates positive jurisdictional obligations that require cross-border institutional cooperation, am I smuggling in world-republic functions that my pluralist defense of federation rules out? And does (5) do damage to (1) rather than just to the preliminary articles—specifically, does nuclear deterrence change the cost structure of war in a way that breaks the republican incentive mechanism, since citizens now face catastrophic risk rather than calculable burden?

Please audit this package. Rank any tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest revision is and what it costs me, and identify any pair that only looks tense but actually dissolves on closer inspection. If my framing depends on a confusion—say, if I am misreading the active-citizen condition or if I am treating cosmopolitan right as a matter of distributive justice rather than international law—say so plainly rather than accommodating the mistake. I want direct pushback: tell me if one of these commitments has to go, and which one is the weakest link.
