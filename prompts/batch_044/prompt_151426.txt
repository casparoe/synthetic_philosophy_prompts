I'm a senior in an undergraduate seminar on representation, biography, and posthumous ethics, and I'm trying to work out whether dramatists owe the real dead obligations that go beyond simple factual accuracy. I'm particularly drawn to a distinction between factual fidelity—avoiding invented events—and what I want to call axiological fidelity: constructing a portrayal that respects the deceased person's own values and self-conception, even when every depicted scene actually happened. Alongside this, I'm considering the idea of counterfactual dignity harm—the specific wrong done when a representation builds a persona so alien to the real individual's self-understanding that they would experience it as a kind of posthumous misrecognition, regardless of whether the plot points are true. I'd like to read Miloš Forman's 1984 film Amadeus, with Peter Shaffer's play in the background, through exactly this lens. How does the film's wildly inaccurate but emotionally coherent portrait of Antonio Salieri relate to these two obligations? Does it wrong Salieri primarily by inventing the poisoning narrative and the mediocrity-envy plot, or does it commit the deeper axiological failure of constructing a Salieri whose inner values and self-image are systematically inverted? Which specific scenes—the framing confession to the priest, the court musician's humiliation, the final institutionalization—best illustrate either kind of failure, or alternatively defend the dramatist's right to appropriate a dead figure as an artistic foil? Where does the film resist this ethical reading: is Mozart's own portrayal protected by the survival of his music, or is he equally vulnerable to counterfactual dignity harm through the reduction of his genius to childish vulgarity? And finally, what does Amadeus add to the philosophical debate about what we owe the dead that a straight argument about historical license and artistic freedom fails to capture on its own?
