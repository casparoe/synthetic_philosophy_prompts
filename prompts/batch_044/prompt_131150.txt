I have recently begun auditing a graduate seminar in Enlightenment political philosophy, and after a vigorous discussion about how public spectacle shapes civic virtue, I found myself returning to Rousseau's well-known reply to d'Alembert's proposal that Geneva establish a theater. I am drafting an essay that advances a somewhat restricted interpretation of his objection: instead of treating all theatrical representation as uniformly corrupting, I want to isolate a particular structural feature—the one-directional epistemic asymmetry in which the actor knowingly dissimulates an emotion he does not feel, while the spectator, though fully aware that the frame is artificial, is nevertheless invited to grant that simulation the moral weight of genuine feeling. On this reading, Rousseau's concern is not illusion in general but a trained complicity in rewarding false display, and the moral danger lies precisely in the fact that both parties understand the deception yet agree to treat it as worthy of real sympathy.

To see whether this interpretation holds together, I compare it with his praise for public civic festivals, which also involve costume, collective performance, and emotional display but differ, I think, because the participants are simultaneously performers and spectators of themselves, thereby dissolving the deceptive asymmetry rather than institutionalizing it. I then want to push the framework further by applying it to contemporary interactive media in which algorithmic agents generate dramatic scenarios in real time and human users co-author the emotional arc; in this case, the traditional performer-spectator boundary collapses differently, since the generating system lacks sentience and the human user becomes simultaneously deceived and deceiver. I worry that this extension may be anachronistic, but I also think it might reveal something precise about why Rousseau considered representational asymmetry morally dangerous rather than merely aesthetically false.

Would you comment on whether this reading is textually defensible—specifically, whether Rousseau's argument supports isolating this epistemic asymmetry rather than expressing a broader hostility to representation? I would also value your judgment on whether the comparison with civic festivals genuinely resolves the apparent tension in his view, and whether the extension to algorithmic co-creation is philosophically illuminating or merely projects present concerns onto an eighteenth-century text. I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations; rather, I want to know if the argument is coherent and interesting enough to develop into a full paper, and which parts you think need the most substantial revision.
