I'm taking a final-year module on rational choice and artificial agency, and in a seminar on preference architecture we discussed coherent extrapolated volition — the notion that what we genuinely want is not our present, possibly inconsistent desires, but the preferences we would arrive at after idealised reflection, full information, and logical coherence. A classmate advanced an argument that now strikes me as suspiciously strong, because if it holds, it seems to undermine not merely particular policies but the very legitimacy of democratic choice. She laid it out as follows.

(1) Any preference that survives ideal rational scrutiny — unlimited cognition, complete information, and perfect consistency — is, for that agent, the correct preference.
(2) Coherent extrapolated volition defines our true preferences as precisely those that every human agent would hold under these ideal conditions.
(3) Therefore, CEV identifies a single, uniquely correct preference ordering for humanity, not merely a set of individually improved ones.
(4) Actual democratic procedures aggregate the preferences people happen to hold now, which are shaped by ignorance, cognitive bias, and partial information.
(5) Consequently, democratic outcomes will systematically diverge from the preferences identified by CEV.
(6) Whenever we have access to a decision procedure that reliably produces the correct answer, and we know that an alternative procedure systematically deviates from it, rationality requires us to abandon the latter.
(7) Hence, accepting CEV commits us to replacing democratic deliberation with a direct computation of the extrapolated volition, implemented regardless of popular consent.
(8) Yet this conclusion seems to erase the distinction between correcting an error and overriding a legitimate difference in values, which is precisely the kind of authoritarian result that makes caring about human autonomy meaningful in the first place.

She believes the weak point is (6), on the grounds that rationality does not always require substituting computation for deliberation. I am inclined to defend (6) but am deeply uneasy about (3), because I wonder whether idealisation might converge on different endpoints for different people rather than one universal ordering. Still, if I reject (3), I worry that CEV collapses into mere individual self-improvement and loses its claim to define what humanity collectively ought to want. Which step is the proper place to apply pressure, and what does each option cost the coherence of the view? I would particularly like to preserve (2), since without it the concept of extrapolation seems to lose its normative force, yet I also want to avoid the authoritarian conclusion in (8).
