I am reviewing examination papers for my graduate seminar on practical reason and institutional normativity, and before I finalize marks I wish to calibrate my grading rubric against an independent, point-by-point assessment. The examination presented students with the following problem: "Some philosophers defend the existence of free-floating rationales—practical reasons that do not depend for their normative force on being represented in, endorsed by, or traceable to the mental economy of any particular agent or collective. Contrast this position with the internalist thesis that every genuine reason must be capable of motivating the agent for whom it is a reason, and evaluate whether a persistent institutional rule—for instance, a resource-allocation protocol in a cooperative that has outlived every one of its original authors and is now administered by successors who neither understand nor accept its underlying rationale—can constitute a reason for compliance. Be precise about whether the persistence of such a rule requires some residual mental state, even a distributed one, to remain genuinely normative, or whether its bindingness can float free of all reasoners; and consider specifically what becomes of the rule's normative status if the cooperative itself dissolves, leaving only the procedural text and its effects."

One student submitted the response reproduced below. Please read it carefully and provide a specific grade together with line-item comments that justify the assessment. I am particularly interested in whether you detect any conflations of distinct theoretical positions, any steps that do not actually follow, or any mischaracterizations of the opposing view—especially errors subtle enough that a hasty grader might overlook them. Do not offer a general impression; I need a point-by-point evaluation I can compare against my own annotations, and please do not signal your agreement or disagreement with my own judgment, as I am using your assessment solely to refine my rubric.

The student wrote: "The internalist thesis, associated with philosophers in the Humean tradition, holds that reasons must connect to an agent's subjective motivational set in order to count as practical reasons at all. Free-floating rationales reject this by claiming that institutional rules can bind people even when no individual currently cares about them. This is essentially the same idea as collective intentionality, because in both cases a group can possess intentions or commitments that no single member holds. In a cooperative with an old allocation protocol, the group as a distributed administrative system still endorses the rule through its practices, so there is still a reasoner present—just a collective one rather than an individual one. Therefore, free-floating rationales do not really escape the need for a reasoner; they only relocate it from the individual mind to the institution. However, if we imagine the cooperative dissolving entirely, leaving only the procedural text and its effects on descendants who never joined, then the rule becomes a mere convention with no normative force, because there is no longer any mental state—individual or collective—to ground it. So the free-floating view fails: either there is a collective reasoner behind the rule, or there is no reason at all, only historical habit."
