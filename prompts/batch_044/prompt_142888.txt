Having retired from experimental physics and only recently begun serious study of philosophy of mind and epistemology, I find myself stuck on a dispute that seems to have reached an impasse in contemporary discussion: the debate over whether mental states are luminous, and its connection to the principle that knowing entails knowing that one knows. The critics—working from a well-known line about gradual change—observe that a subject can become marginally colder, or marginally tired, without being in a position to know that she is so; if luminosity required that being in a state automatically places one in a position to know it, then it fails for these graded phenomenological conditions. Defenders reply that this confuses the metaphysical presence of a state with the epistemic conditions for its attribution, and that the critic illegitimately imposes a discrete boundary condition on mental states—something like requiring a sharp measurement threshold—where phenomenology is in fact continuous, more like a temperature gradient than a switch. Meanwhile, those sympathetic to the second-order principle treat knowledge of one's own knowing as unproblematic, while opponents treat it as precisely the kind of over-intellectualization that the anti-luminosity argument undermines. Despite years of exchange, neither camp revises its core commitments; each accuses the other of altering the subject.

My working hypothesis—likely shaped by my background in statistical mechanics—is that this stalemate is not primarily about knowledge at all, but about whether mental-state ascriptions require sharp boundaries analogous to discrete phase transitions, or whether they tolerate continuous gradients like fields in physics. If so, the sides may be answering different questions: one normative, about what rational agents ought to be able to report; the other descriptive, about subpersonal processing that never achieves discrete categorization. It may also be verbal, turning on what "being in a position to know" is meant to cover, or it may conceal a hidden empirical disagreement about cognitive architecture that neither side has made explicit.

I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of normative epistemology and descriptive psychology operating under a single heading? Please support your diagnosis by reference to what each side actually maintains about mental-state boundaries, about second-order access, and about the role of gradual change. Given that diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a formal model of graded self-awareness, a precise conceptual distinction applied here, an empirical result from cognitive science, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed because it illegitimately projects physical discreteness onto phenomenology?
