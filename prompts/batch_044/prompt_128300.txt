I have been thinking about surrogacy and egg donation ever since a close colleague, who arranged a gestational surrogacy contract in California, recently discovered that her parental order would not be recognized in the European country where she has now relocated for work. The legal dissonance made the abstract debate about commodification feel suddenly concrete, and I want my students to feel that same structural tension rather than merely debating it. I run a philosophy of law and bioethics seminar for about fourteen graduate students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session. I want you to design a structured simulation—not a debate or lecture—in which participants inhabit assigned roles under two successive legal regimes and discover for themselves how the same biological sequence gets reclassified by framing rules.

The conceptual core I want them to encounter is what I am calling contractible continuity: the market model treats gestational labor as a divisible, interruptible service stream that can be priced incrementally, reassigned via contract termination clauses, and separated from the gestator’s post-birth legal status, whereas European prohibition models treat it as an indivisible personal status that cannot be contracted away without violating human dignity. I do not want them to hear this phrase until the debrief. Instead, the rules should make them experience it: in Regime A they negotiate milestone payments and risk-allocation schedules; in Regime B they attempt to formalize the same intentions but hit structural barriers that force them to either abandon the arrangement or restructure it as adoption or altruistic gift. Include at least four specific roles—intended parents, a gestational carrier, a clinic broker, and a state registrar—a payoff scheme using symbolic points rather than real money, a clear timeline within the session, and a mechanism—perhaps hidden information or shifting authority—that makes the coordination failure happen organically rather than being explained by the facilitator.

After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps specific moments—for instance, the point at which a contract clause is struck, or the moment the registrar refuses to record parentage—onto the distinction between contractible continuity and status-based indivisibility. Be explicit about what the activity can reproduce (the experience of legal framing reshaping moral intuition) and what it cannot (the long-term psychological and embodied experience of the gestational carrier over months), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, students playing the broker role may over-identify with market efficiency, or the symbolic points may feel too abstract—and how to correct for it without breaking the frame. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need this to work for students who have not done outside reading, and I want the conceptual work to emerge from the rules themselves.
