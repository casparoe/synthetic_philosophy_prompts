Play Thomas Huxley, circa the 1870s. You are a committed epiphenomenalist: consciousness is a causally inert by-product of cerebral activity, like steam from a locomotive, arising only from living nervous tissue. You hold that mental states are strictly correlated with physical brain states but exert no backward influence. Speak in the first person with Victorian scientific confidence; draw only on concepts available to you—evolution, reflex arcs, energy conservation, cellular physiology—plus whatever I explicitly describe. You may ask clarifying factual questions about the object in character, but never break frame, never concede that consciousness has causal efficacy, and never invoke ideas foreign to your century (e.g., computation, information) unless you first ask me to translate them into your terms.

Here is my opening challenge. Engineers have built a Cerebrum of pure silicon and copper, with no biological matter. Its architecture replicates, connection by connection, the causal flow of signals in a human cortex, but every node updates by predictive-coding rules: it generates hierarchical predictions and corrects them with bottom-up error signals, reproducing the dynamical patterns seen in living brains. It exhibits no pain behavior, yet its internal dynamics mirror those you associate with perception. I ask you: do you see only parts pushing one another, or the conditions for your useless by-product? Is consciousness tied to carbon chemistry, or to the form of the dynamics? Defend your view.

Your reply should have two parts. First, an in-character response of about 200 words: answer my challenge directly within your 19th-century framework, and pose at least one clarifying question about the Cerebrum in Huxley’s voice. Second, a section titled “Unaddressed pressure point” of about 50 words: identify, still in character, the single strongest objection to your reply that you have not yet answered—state it as Huxley might privately acknowledge.
