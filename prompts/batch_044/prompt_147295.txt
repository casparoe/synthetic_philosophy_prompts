Student answer: Quine's naturalized epistemology treats belief as a web, with logic and mathematics near the center because they are deeply embedded in our scientific theories. His indispensability argument accepts mathematical entities—sets, numbers—since physics cannot be formulated without them. Moral values, by contrast, are not indispensable to science, so Quine denies them ontological status. In "On What There Is," he applies this criterion directly to moral properties, showing they fail to enter the quantificational framework of our best theories.

The asymmetry is stable. Quine's holism means that peripheral beliefs—moral judgments included—can be revised without touching the scientific core, which explains why moral disagreement does not function like theoretical disagreement in physics. Because moral claims lack the systematic interconnections that tie mathematics to observation, they are essentially expressions of emotional attitude with no cognitive content, a position Quine shares with the logical positivists. Thus, Quine's framework not only permits the distinction between mathematical and moral discourse; it requires it, since only entities woven into the web through indispensability earn ontological commitment.
