My mate sent me down a rabbit hole about Von Neumann and self-building machines. He reckons technology can survive us if it hits non-trivial reproduction—where a machine builds something more complex than itself, not just copying like a factory line. I tried to turn this into an argument about AI risk and now it feels like it proves we should never worry about any dangerous tech. Here it is:

(1) Von Neumann said only non-trivial reproduction counts as real technological survival, because that's when tech evolves on its own without human help.
(2) Once a system reaches non-trivial reproduction, it is causally independant of its creators—nothing we do afterwards can stop its spread.
(3) If we can't stop it afterwards, then we're not responsible for what it does later, since responsibility needs that we could have done otherwise.
(4) So the only moral moment is the step just before it crosses into non-trivial reproduction.
(5) But non-trivial is defined relative to the parent's complexity—if AI A builds AI B that's only 2% smarter, that's technically non-trivial, yet it feels like a smooth slope, not a cliff.
(6) So there's no clear line to draw; either we ban all incremental improvement now, or we accept we'll eventually slide over.
(7) If sliding over is inevitable once you start, then taking the first step isn't wrong—it's just nature taking its course, and we should realise that fighting it is pointless.
(8) By the same reasoning, this applies to climate engineering, synthetic biology, anything that might reproduce.
(9) So we'd have to say it's never wrong to build dangerous self-replicating tech, as long as you don't literally build the final version yourself.
(10) That can't be right, but I can't see the crack. Which step should I ditch, and what does ditching it cost me? I'd rather keep (3) than (9) if I can, but I want to know what I'm paying.
