I'm presenting next Thursday at an interdisciplinary workshop on astrobiology and moral philosophy. My thesis is that the dominant ethical case against interstellar colonization—centered on analogies to terrestrial imperialism and ecological destruction—collapses when applied to a specific, under-discussed modality: silent seeding via minimal-footprint self-replicating probes engineered to avoid detectable interaction with exoplanetary biospheres. I argue further that non-intervention is not morally neutral but constitutes a form of cosmic-scale "letting fail to be": if evolutionary convergence makes it plausible that a seeded world would eventually produce sentient life, then refusing to deploy such probes is not passive allowing but an active prevention of potential future value-bearers. This reframes the burden—the wrong, I claim, lies not in quiet seeding but in withholding conditions for potential flourishing, especially since there is no present rights-holder to wrong.

The audience comprises eight astrobiologists—mostly microbial ecologists and origin-of-life researchers—and four moral philosophers with backgrounds in environmental ethics and the non-identity problem, but none trained in decision theory or analytic metaphysics. They are institutionally skeptical of human exceptionalism, view large-scale technological intervention in natural systems as hubristic, and care intensely about epistemic humility regarding alien biochemistry. They also worry that framing the cosmos as a resource compromises scientific objectivity. I need a Q&A prep sheet ordered by damage: first the astrobiologists' objections phrased in their vocabulary—contamination risk, precautionary principle, epistemic humility—then the philosophers' objections framed around doing versus allowing, the non-identity problem, and whether merely potential beings carry moral standing. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, sketch a two-minute reply, and flag the reply's weakest point. Please include the objection I hope nobody raises—likely whether my "quiet seeding" merely hides a consent problem behind information asymmetry, or whether my cosmic application of the doing/allowing distinction commits me to an implausible procreative obligation across all of space-time. The sheet should reflect their actual commitments, not generic anti-colonization talking points, and should treat my argument as a serious position rather than a straw figure.
