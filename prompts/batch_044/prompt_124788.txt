My friend and I just finished a long argument about Rorty’s antirepresentationalism, and I need an adjudication rather than a lecture. She argues that once Rorty denies beliefs represent reality, he loses any principled distinction between rational persuasion and mere causal manipulation—so that a person who abandons a moral vocabulary after trauma is, by his lights, in the same position as one convinced by argument. I replied that Rorty can preserve the distinction pragmatically: persuasion expands solidarity within a shared vocabulary, while trauma-induced shifts fracture vocabulary continuity and thus fail to count as rational redescription. She countered that this smuggles in a normative commitment—solidarity—that Rorty’s framework treats as contingent preference rather than justified standard, making the distinction circular. I answered that Rorty never claimed neutrality and openly champions liberal solidarity, admitting its contingency. She then pressed whether, on his view, a community that suppresses dissent through conditioning rather than argument is merely practicing an alternative vocabulary, leaving Rorty with no rational ground for criticism except tribal loyalty. I insisted he can still condemn cruelty, but I could not explain how he does so without either reintroducing representationalist moral facts or collapsing into bare preference.

Please adjudicate our exchange move by move. Specifically, assess whether Rorty’s distinction between persuasion and force survives his antirepresentationalism without circularity; whether solidarity operates as a hidden representationalist commitment; and whether his condemnation of cruelty is consistent with treating all vocabularies as contingent tools. Identify what both of us are missing—particularly whether Rorty’s concept of redescription supplies the missing normative structure, or whether the difficulty lies elsewhere. I am less interested in a general overview of Rorty than in a precise verdict on these specific arguments.
