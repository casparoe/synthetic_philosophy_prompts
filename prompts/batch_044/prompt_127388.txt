I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on the ontology of biological information, and I have been examining the design protocols used in synthetic-biology laboratories that engineer self-replicating genetic circuits. In these protocols, DNA sequences are specified as strings in formal generative grammars, where the sequence encodes not only a phenotypic output but also a structural description of the transcriptional machinery that interprets it. The design strategy thus treats replication as an instance of typogenetic self-reference—structurally analogous to Gödel numbering, in which a formal system contains the syntactic resources to denote its own rules—while replication errors are handled through information-theoretic noise filters rather than through biochemical-kinetic analysis.

What philosophical commitments about syntax, physical substrate, and biological information are presupposed by this institutional practice? Does treating DNA as a self-describing formal language commit one to a type-token distinction that exceeds what a standard physicalist ontology can accommodate, or can a token-identity theorist treat the grammar as merely an abstract modeling tool? Furthermore, does the practice of measuring evolutionary fidelity in terms of syntactic-structure preservation, rather than molecular-stability maintenance, necessarily presuppose that replication is fundamentally an information-preservation problem? And does the engineering use of Gödel-style self-reference as a literal design heuristic implicitly commit to the view that logical self-reference can be instantiated in carbon-based chemistry, or can the analogy be sustained as purely formal? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that renders this practice coherent, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—especially regarding whether a purely chemical-kinetic account of replication could license the same design choices without the typogenetic framework.
