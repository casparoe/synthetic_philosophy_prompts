I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and epistemology, and I am preparing a two-page proposal for a one-year dissertation fellowship on the reliability of introspection. My half-formed idea is that the standard debate—between perceptual-model theorists who treat introspection as a form of inner observation and expressivist or constitutive theorists who deny any observational gap—has stalled partly because both sides treat introspective failure as a single kind of breakdown: a failure of access to one's own mental state. I want to propose that we split introspective reliability along two dimensions, phenomenal transparency and cognitive transparency. Phenomenal transparency concerns whether a mental state is given to the subject with its qualitative character intact; cognitive transparency concerns whether the subject can correctly classify, articulate, or integrate that state within her broader self-conception. The guiding hypothesis is that much of what philosophers and psychologists call introspective error—misidentifying the source of an emotional reaction, overestimating the strength of a belief, or confabulating motives—is not a failure of phenomenal access but a failure of cognitive transparency that occurs even when the phenomenal dimension is intact. The project asks whether this bifurcation dissolves some classic asymmetry puzzles (for instance, why first-person authority seems both more immediate and more fallible than third-person observation) while sharpening the remaining ones, and whether it offers a more precise framework for interpreting empirical findings about confabulation without reducing philosophy to psychology.
