Hey, I was looking at those standard crisis-report forms that big NGOs have to fill out after a disaster, and there's this weird box for "root causal agent." If the disaster is purely human-driven—like two bad policies interacting—you can list both together, no problem. But if it's a volcanic ash cloud that kills crops and then a fungal blight moves in and people migrate, the form forces you to pick just one root agent: either the volcano, the fungus, or the human movement. You can't tick them as co-equal causes. What philosophical commitments about agency or causation does that filing pratice presuppose? Does it assume only intentional actors can share causal credit, or is there a hidden line between "natural" and "social" domains that doesn't hold up? And if we actually treated germs, climate shifts, and volcanoes as full "historical actors" with their own kind of agency—not just background conditions—what parts of the form and the relief logic behind it would need to change? I'm not asking for a bibliography, just whether the practice is philosophically consistent or just smuggling in old human-exceptionalist baggage.
