I recently found myself in a situation where the ethical assessment of my conduct seemed to hinge almost entirely on an outcome I could not have foreseen or controlled: a decision made with ordinary diligence produced serious harm in the actual world and would have produced clear benefit in a nearby counterfactual, yet observers’ judgments of my blameworthiness tracked the actual result rather than my ex ante reasoning. This has made the problem of moral luck personally urgent rather than merely academic. Among the open lines of work in this domain—whether constitutive luck genuinely undermines the idea of a self-authored moral character; whether resultant luck can be neutralized by a revised control principle that distinguishes capacity for control from its exercise; how circumstantial luck operates when agents act within institutional or algorithmic systems that distribute outcomes opaquely; and whether the boundary between moral and epistemic luck collapses under pressure from recent modal-epistemological work—which should be pursued first, and why? I am asking for a defended prioritization rather than a survey: for each line, explain why it is upstream of the others, what makes it tractable through conceptual analysis rather than empirical psychology, and what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. In particular, defend whether settling the collective-agent version of the problem—whether groups or automated decision systems can be subject to moral luck in a sense that should reshape how we design accountability structures—should take precedence over the individual-level debate, and identify which resolution, if achieved, would do the most to stabilize or destabilize ordinary practices of blame and reward.
