I am a novelist drafting a historical narrative about democratic collapse, and I am convening a private workshop of twelve advanced readers—mostly writers and philosophers—who understand the Athenian assembly in outline but have never inhabited its procedural logic. Over ninety minutes, I want them to experience what I call "honor-cost reversal": the structural condition in direct democracy where correcting an initial collective decision requires not better evidence but a public admission that the assembly's emotional investment was mistaken, a social cost so high that reversal becomes rarer than persistence in error. Design a three-round simulation—each round keyed to one historical failure, namely the Mytilene debate over the rebel city, the vote for the Sicilian expedition, and the rapid jury conviction of Socrates—in which participants speak and vote under rules that mirror historical procedure: speaking order drawn by lot, no private caucus, a binding vote within one session, and hidden knowledge distributed asymmetrically among assigned roles. Give me specific roles—strategos, rhetor, silent citizen holding suppressed intelligence, and observer with a veto that can only be exercised by breaking collective rank—plus a payoff scheme built entirely on social reputation markers rather than money, arranged so that emotional framing locks in during round one, reversing it in round two requires sacrificing collective honor, and in round three individual dissent is structurally isolated by the speed of the vote. The participants should come away seeing, before any lecture, how democratic immediacy suppresses second-order reflection; only in the debrief should you map each procedural moment onto its historical case, predict the first-run failure—likely over-identification with the rhetor and refusal to exercise the hidden veto—and explain how to handle it without breaking immersion. Be frank about which feature of democratic collapse, such as the absence of institutional delay, this simulation can reproduce through rules and which feature must be supplied by narrative framing rather than by the activity itself. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
