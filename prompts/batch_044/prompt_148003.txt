The maxim that travelers should “do as the Romans do” is routinely invoked to settle whether visitors have moral obligations to follow local conventions—tipping service workers, queuing in lines, or refraining from jaywalking—even when those practices seem arbitrary and carry no legal penalty. Unpack this maxim into four precisely different philosophical theses it might express: (1) a purely prudential claim about avoiding social sanctions; (2) a strong moral-relativist claim that local norms constitute rightness and wrongness for anyone present; (3) a tacit-consent claim that entering a territory implicitly binds one to its rules; and (4) a coordination-indexed claim that conventions generate genuine but defeasible moral obligations only when they solve practical coordination problems, and that the strength of the obligation depends on whether one participates in the practice. For each thesis, specify exactly what it implies about a visitor’s duty across tipping, queueing, and jaywalking—distinguishing moral obligation from strategic advisability, and explaining whether locals and visitors are bound differently. Then take a committed position: defend exactly one of these readings (or a precise hybrid you define) as the correct account of whether a visitor in a specific city—where queue-jumping is the locally efficient norm, tipping is treated as a demeaning personal insult rather than a wage supplement, and jaywalking is universal but rarely causes harm—ought to adapt her behavior. Your defense must engage the strongest opposing view, identify the structural feature (such as the presence or absence of a coordination problem, or the distinction between causing harm and giving offense) that does the decisive work, and say what happens to your argument if that feature is removed. Do not present a neutral menu; commit, argue, and flag where your reasoning depends on a contested boundary between etiquette and morality.
