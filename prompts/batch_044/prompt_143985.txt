Design the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of literature and interpretation: one essay question answerable in 90 minutes, totaling 30 marks, with no bibliography or citation expected. The domain is authorial intention and the meaning of a text, but the prompt must be unanswerable by reciting the standard Wimsatt–Beardsley anti-intentionalist position against Hirsch-style intentionalism. To achieve this, embed a niche conceptual scenario: a single author publishes a philosophical dialogue written entirely in the first-person plural ("we argue that...") with no internal markers distinguishing the interlocutors; the work's internal architecture exhibits strict symmetry—each thesis receives an equally developed rebuttal, and no voice is granted final rhetorical authority. Twenty years later, the author issues a retrospective "voice key," assigning specific passages to Voice A (representing the author's "genuine" commitments) and Voice B (a purely dialectical foil). The exam should ask candidates to evaluate whether this retrospective key can determine the text's meaning, and require engagement with at least four specific conceptual moves: (1) the Gricean distinction between semantic meaning and speaker's meaning, applied to the problem of the plural pronoun "we" in an unsigned dialogue; (2) Hirsch's distinction between meaning and significance, particularly whether the key alters the former or only the latter; (3) the anti-intentionalist argument that textual autonomy derives from linguistic convention and internal inferential structure rather than from recoverable psychological states; and (4) the problem of "structural overdetermination"—the idea that a text's symmetrical design, unresolved tensions, and rhetorical balance generate interpretive possibilities that a retrospective sorting suppresses rather than reveals. The question should also ask candidates to consider whether retrospective authorial sorting is itself an interpretive act rather than an authoritative disclosure, and whether a text can carry meanings that are structurally available but psychologically disavowed.

Provide a model answer that takes a defensible, nuanced position—e.g., that the voice key affects significance and may guide charitable interpretation, but cannot override the text's structurally generated meanings because the "we" form and symmetrical architecture make any single-voice assignment semantically underdetermined; or, alternatively, that the key succeeds only where the text's internal design already permits asymmetric resolution. The answer should display precise conceptual distinctions, apply them to the scenario, and avoid dogmatic assertion of either pure intentionalism or pure anti-intentionalism.

Provide a marking scheme divided into five must-hit points, with marks allocated (total 30), and a one-line note on the typical failure mode for each:
- Reconstruction of the scenario and identification of the retrospective/dialectical twist (6 marks): typical failure is treating the case as a standard intentionalist/anti-intentionalist dispute without addressing the "we" pronoun or the retrospective nature of the key.
- Gricean or equivalent analysis of speaker versus semantic meaning (6 marks): typical failure is conflating the two or ignoring the specific problem of plural reference in the absence of contextual disambiguation.
- Use of the meaning/significance distinction or functional equivalent (6 marks): typical failure is treating Hirsch as a naive intentionalist who allows authorial veto over all interpretation, rather than recognizing his commitment to textual determinacy.
- Analysis of structural overdetermination and the constraints of textual architecture (6 marks): typical failure is describing structure as decorative rather than interpretively generative, or failing to explain why symmetry resists the retrospective key.
- Coherent overall position with acknowledgment of limitations (6 marks): typical failure is asserting one side without addressing the counter-pressure from the text's internal design, or collapsing into the claim that the author's later statement is either wholly authoritative or wholly irrelevant.

Specify that the exam is for upper-level undergraduates, that answers should be approximately 1,200–1,500 words, and that no external sources or page references are required.
