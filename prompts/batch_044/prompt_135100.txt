I'm currently taking a seminar on moral responsibility and collective action, and a classmate presented a summary of Karl Jaspers's well-known post-war essay on guilt that she attributed to his fourfold taxonomy. She summarized it like this: "Jaspers distinguishes criminal guilt, political guilt, moral guilt, and metaphysical guilt. Criminal guilt applies to individuals who violated law, but Jaspers extends it to citizens collectively because modern democratic states operate through shared sovereignty; therefore, a population can be criminally guilty for state policies even when courts can only prosecute individuals. Political guilt is different: it is the liability every citizen bears for allowing an unjust regime to exist, but it carries no personal blame and requires only institutional reform and reparative justice, not punishment. Moral guilt is entirely private, arising from one's own conscience about specific actions, so it can never be collective. Metaphysical guilt is universal human solidarity with victims of evil, which means every person alive during atrocities bears it individually, but it is not specific to members of the perpetrating nation. From this taxonomy, Jaspers concludes that collective punishment is appropriate for criminal guilt at the national level, inappropriate for political guilt, and meaningless for moral or metaphysical guilt, since the latter two are strictly individual."

I think she captures the four categories, but I'm unsure whether Jaspers actually assigns criminal guilt collectively, whether he allows moral guilt to have any collective dimension, and whether his view on political guilt really excludes all forms of collective blame. Could you audit her summary carefully? In particular, clarify which of the four categories Jaspers treats as genuinely collective rather than individually distributed, whether there is any role for what she calls "shared sovereignty" in his argument for criminal guilt, and whether the conclusion about punishment and reform actually follows once those misattributions are corrected. I also want to apply this to a case we are discussing: if a contemporary institution still holds material benefits derived from historical wrongs committed by predecessors who are all dead, does Jaspers's framework treat that as inherited political guilt, or does it fall outside his taxonomy altogether?
