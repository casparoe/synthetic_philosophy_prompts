In debates over individual obligations to reduce carbon emissions or abstain from meat, proponents of the inefficacy objection often invoke a hydrological analogy: a single person's consumption is like one drop added to an ocean that is already vast, so withholding that drop changes nothing about the water level, and the drop is therefore causally inert. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that individual abstention lacks moral force—at best it is symbolic expression, not genuine duty—because moral responsibility is said to require causal efficacy. Critics counter that aggregation matters, but they rarely specify exactly where the mapping fails. Audit this analogy explicitly: construct a correspondence table that maps the drop, the ocean, the act of withholding, the observer measuring level, and any implicit agent onto their counterparts in collective harm; identify the precise points of breakdown, particularly with respect to non-linear threshold effects, joint causation, the difference between physical and normative causation, and the absence of intentional coordination in the source domain; specify which hydrological features—linearity, homogeneity, lack of institutional structure, absence of rights-bearing parts—are quietly imported into the moral domain and whether they belong there; and then determine whether the conclusion that individual duty dissolves actually survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses under this audit, propose a more apt physical or social analogy, if one exists, that could do the same argumentative work without smuggling in the same errors.
