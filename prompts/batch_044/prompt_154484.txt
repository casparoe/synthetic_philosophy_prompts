Me and my roommate got into an arguement last night over advance directives—like, if I write one now saying no feeding tube if I get dementia, but then ten years later I'm smiling and seem genuinely content, does my old self still get to call the shots? I ended up defending this view that the earlier self has a kind of constitutional authority over the later one, not just ordinary autonomy. The core idea is that in gradual cognitive decline there's no clean break where the "real me" disappears; instead you get a slow corruption of the original decision-maker. So letting the later self revoke the directive isn't like changing my mind about a restaurant—it's more like a corrupted file trying to edit its own source code. I also think there's a diachronic free-rider problem: the later self enjoys the benefits of the life-plan my earlier self built but wants to ditch the commitments that made it possible, which feels structurally unfair. So I think revocation should require a kind of supermajority rational consistance that dementia typically destroys.

Anyway, I want to know what actually counts against this. Not just the standard people-change objection, but which specific cases—like gradual personality drift versus sudden conversion—would break it? Which intuitions about personal identity, and which results in other domains like persistence conditions for artifacts or contracts, does it stand or fall with? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest about which ones a committed defender of this view would actually concede hurt, versus which ones they'd just dismiss by saying the later self is epistemically compromised. Also, does this view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—like, can any objection be absorbed by saying that wasn't true corruption? And if so, is that actually a problem for a theory of authority? Write the answer in American English, please. I'm tired of surface-level takes and want it in the weeds.
