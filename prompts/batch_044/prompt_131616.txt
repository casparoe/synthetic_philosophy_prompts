Here is an argument about access consciousness and global workspace theory that seems to force me either to collapse the phenomenal/access distinction or to accept that every representational module in the brain has its own private experience. I want to keep the distinction intact, but I do not want to pay the price of ubiquitous micro-consciousness.

(1) Global workspace theory identifies access consciousness with the global broadcast of representational content to multiple consumer systems—memory, language, reasoning, and action planning.

(2) Phenomenal consciousness, if it is not identical to access consciousness, must be constituted by some feature F that can obtain even when a state's content is not globally broadcast.

(3) The only plausible candidate for F is local qualitative tagging: a module represents its own processing with an intrinsic qualitative character, without synchronization across the workspace.

(4) But local qualitative tagging can occur in any module that carries representational content about its own states—for instance, a low-level visual area encoding edge-like activation patterns with an intrinsic "sharpness" tag.

(5) If local qualitative tagging is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, then every such module hosts a phenomenal state that is, by (1), not access-conscious.

(6) That implies phenomenal consciousness is ubiquitous throughout the brain, including in isolated cortical columns and subcortical loops we have no independent reason to count as conscious.

(7) This result is absurd: it turns global workspace theory into a mere theory of reportability and forces us to attribute experience to subsystems that never enter awareness.

(8) So either we reject (2) and identify phenomenal consciousness with global broadcasting, or we reject (3) and deny that local qualitative tagging is possible.

(9) Rejecting (3) seems costly, because it requires explaining how qualitative character can arise only at the global level, which looks like an unexplained emergence claim.

(10) Rejecting (2) collapses the access/phenomenal distinction I wanted to preserve.

Which step should I give up? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (2) than avoid (6), but I want to know exactly what rejecting the other steps costs—whether there is a hidden equivocation between "available to one consumer" and "globally broadcast," or whether the argument overgeneralizes from a possible architecture to an actual one.
