Analyze the classic split-brain studies of the 1960s and 1970s—patients whose corpus callosum was severed to treat severe epilepsy, yielding behaviors in which the left and right hemispheres appeared to possess separate awareness, conflicting intentions, and in which the left hemisphere's interpreter confabulated unified narratives for actions initiated by the right—through the lens of Buddhist anatta, treating personal identity not as an enduring substance but as a conventional designation (pannatti) for a stream of impermanent aggregates (skandhas). Do not recount the neuroanatomy in detail; assume the basic findings are common knowledge.

Address the following: (1) How anatta characterizes the divided consciousness—whether it reveals two provisional selves, no self whatsoever, or a single self that has been fractured. In particular, relate the left hemisphere's narrative confabulation to the Buddhist notion of mental proliferation (papanca) and ask whether that fabrication is the same mechanism anatta identifies as constructing the illusion of ownership. (2) Where anatta outperforms or underperforms Western psychological-continuity theories of personal identity, especially on whether identity can survive hemispheric division and whether there is a subject of experience capable of being split. (3) What the case shows about anatta in return—does the interpreter's relentless construction of unity confirm that the self is fabricated, or does the persistence of unitary self-models in each hemisphere suggest a deeper, non-conventional ground? (4) Where the mapping fails—whether anatta's soteriological and moral framework has anything to say about clinical intervention, and whether treating each hemisphere as a distinct locus of awareness forces anatta into a pluralism about persons that conflicts with its standard denial of ultimate persons (puggala).

Be disciplined about the mapping: identify the actors or substrates in each framework, specify what counts as evidence for the presence or absence of a self, and state plainly where the theory is silent rather than stretching it to cover clinical detail.
