Here are my gut verdicts, offered concretely. Columbus’s organization of the encomienda system and the enslavement and extraction of labor from Taíno populations in the Caribbean strikes me as warranting direct moral condemnation; saying he was merely a product of late fifteenth-century European norms feels wrong, not least because he was actively constructing the machinery of extraction rather than passively inheriting it, and because the scale of foreseeable harm was visible even within his own institutional frame. Churchill’s wartime leadership and his responsibility for the 1943 Bengal famine and his broader imperial commitments strike me as requiring separate judgments: I think the famine policies, where specific resource allocations led to mass death despite available alternatives, warrant direct blame, whereas I am less inclined to treat his general imperial worldview as a personal moral failing, since it was structurally embedded in British governance, though I worry this distinction is special pleading. Jefferson’s ownership of hundreds of enslaved people, his maintenance of the plantation economy, his relationship with Sally Hemings, and his authorship of equality language strike me as a hypocrisy that makes the “man of his time” defence especially weak—he had private knowledge of slavery’s cruelty and even articulated the contradiction—yet I still feel he should be judged differently from Columbus, as a beneficiary of an inherited system rather than an architect of a new one, though I cannot articulate precisely why that distinction should carry moral weight.
