I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who advances a specific variant of the hyperbolic-discounting account of weakness of will. On her view, akrasia is not best understood as a unitary agent's failure to execute an all-things-considered judgement; rather, it is the predictable result of intertemporal bargaining between successive temporal selves, modelled on picoeconomic strategic interaction, in which the present self discounts future rewards hyperbolically and thereby "defects" from a long-run cooperative equilibrium. She maintains that this reframes the normative problem: weakness of will is not irrationality in the standard sense, but a breakdown in cross-temporal co-operation analogous to a prisoner's dilemma played across time.

Please draft eight to ten questions that go well beyond restating this position. I require at least one question addressing synchronic weakness of will — that is, cases of immediate, in-the-moment conflict where there is no appreciable temporal gap between judgement and temptation — and how her bargaining model applies when the "parties" are not separated by time but co-present in a single deliberative field. I also want at least one question on whether treating temporal parts as distinct bargainers collapses the normative distinction between weakness of will and rational preference reversal over time, together with a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply that the distinction is preserved by the stability of the long-run co-operative plan. Finally, include at least one question that presses her on the normative status of hyperbolic discounting itself: is it a cognitive bias to be corrected, or could it be rational given genuine uncertainty about one's future existence or about the continuity of personal identity? For each of these three, provide a concrete follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The remaining questions should probe where the literature on practical reason is genuinely sceptical of importing economic models — for example, whether the model can accommodate moral weakness (failing to do what one judges right for non-instrumental reasons) without reducing moral reasons to intertemporal pay-offs. Avoid generic biographical or career-oriented questions; keep the focus tightly on the conceptual architecture and its vulnerabilities.
