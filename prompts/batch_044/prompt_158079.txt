I run citizen assemblies for a mid-sized city and we're constantly arguing over whether to decide things with a big random pool or a small expert panel. I want a practical decision procedre based on the Condorcet jury theorem, but not the classroom version where voters are isolated and equally smart. In real life people talk beforehand, experts dominate the room, and competence varies wildly. So I need you to turn this into an operational checklist — call it the correlated-competence jury rule — that my staff can run in about fifteen minutes before we design a session. It should ask things in order: first check for independance decay (how much prior discussion or media exposure has already happened), then a rough competence spread (without actual testing), then the stakes of being wrong, then whether strategic voting is likely. For each step say what counts as sufficient, where the procedre outputs "use the small panel", where it outputs "use the large pool", and where it has to punt to a human because it's a values question not a fact. Then apply it to three cases: routing a bike lane through a residential neighborhood, changing zoning near an old factory site, and picking a contractor for hidden water-main repairs where the mayor wants a popular vote but the technical risks are high — that last one should come out against the mayor's preference. Finally say exactly what the procedre can't handle, like negative correlation between bias and expertise, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as steps. Write it casual, no need for references.
