After accompanying a family member through the first months following surgical restoration of sight after decades of blindness, I have come to hold a particular Lockean resolution of Molyneux's problem—one that goes beyond the standard claim that vision and touch initially deliver uncorrelated representations. I now think that cross-modal shape recognition is impossible without what I will call motor-mediated calibration loops: sustained, active exploratory movements that gradually bind tactile shape-memory to retinal input through feedback-driven correlation. This seems to account not only for classic reports of congenital-cataract patients who initially fail to match tactile cubes with visual ones, but also for why sensory-substitution devices require extensive training despite delivering spatial information directly to intact sensory pathways. What I want is an honest map of this position's exposure: what observations, arguments, or formal results would genuinely count against it?

Specifically, which empirical findings—perhaps well-documented cases of immediate, accurate cross-modal identification in restored-vision subjects without exploratory movement, or neuroimaging evidence of innate cross-modal structural mappings—would be decisive? Which philosophical objections—concerning amodal spatial representations, the interpretation of first-person reports, or the distinction between subpersonal processing and explicit recognition—would be fatal versus merely awkward? Does this calibration-loop view predict anything that rival accounts—say, nativist or amodal-representation theories—do not, and if so, what would have to be observed for those predictions to fail? I also want you to assess whether defenders of this view have insulated it from refutation by defining away apparent counterexamples as subpersonal or pre-conscious, and whether that insulation is a methodological vice.
