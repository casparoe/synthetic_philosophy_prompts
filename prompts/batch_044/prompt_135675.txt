I'm preparing a six-month, single-investigator proposal for a small interdisciplinary foundation. The deliverable is a conceptual white paper of roughly ten thousand words, intended for scholars and policy analysts who use or reject exceptionalist frameworks. The domain is American exceptionalism treated simultaneously as a historical thesis about institutional development, a normative claim about special obligations or privileges, and an ideology that structures perception. Rather than rehearse the empirical debate over whether the United States is genuinely unique, or the normative debate over whether exceptionalism licenses particular actions, I want to investigate a prior conceptual dependency. My half-formed idea is that the historical thesis necessarily relies on a counterfactual baseline—an implicit model of "normal" political development against which American outcomes are measured as deviations. Selecting that baseline, however, already involves normative judgments about which political goods are central and which ideological commitments about what counts as failure or success. If this is right, then evaluating the historical claim independently of the normative and ideological registers may be impossible, not merely difficult in practice.

Turn this into a formal research proposal containing the following elements. First, state the precise central question: are the three registers—historical, normative, and ideological—conceptually separable, or are they necessarily entangled through the problem of baseline selection? Second, explain why existing treatments in comparative history, normative political theory, and ideological critique leave this specific question open: they tend to argue within one register or to treat entanglement as a sociological fact rather than a conceptual structure. Third, lay out the method month by month. I propose constructing three ideal-type baselines—developmental (teleological progress toward liberal democracy), institutional (path-dependent lock-in of particular constitutional arrangements), and narrative (the coherence conditions for telling a national story)—and testing, for each, whether historical evaluation can proceed without smuggling in normative or ideological commitments. Fourth, specify the possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them: perhaps the entanglement is real but limited to one baseline type; perhaps it is generic to all comparative historiography and therefore not special to exceptionalism; perhaps the registers are separable after all but only at the cost of rendering the historical claim empirically empty. Fifth, describe what a useful deliverable looks like: not an empirical finding about American history, but a decision framework that lets users identify which baseline they are implicitly adopting and therefore which normative and ideological commitments they have already accepted. Sixth, include an honest assessment of failure conditions and scope risks. The project fails if the baseline-selection problem collapses into a trivial point about all comparison requiring standards; it is too big if resolving it requires primary archival work on non-American cases that I cannot complete in six months without research assistance. Do not include a bibliography, a literature review organized by citation, or requests for specific readings—I will supply those myself. Keep the framing conceptual and analytical rather than empirical, and avoid attributing particular formulations to named authors unless the formulation is common knowledge.
