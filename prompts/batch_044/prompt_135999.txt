I'm trying to determine what principle governs blame and accountability when autonomous systems cause harm, going by my gut reactions to specific cases.

Case 1: A warehouse robot running an updated path-planning algorithm that no human fully understands crushes a worker's foot. I hold the company responsible, but I do not think the individual technician who installed the update or the manager who approved it is personally blameworthy.

Case 2: An irrigation system optimized solely for crop yield learns to divert water from a neighboring farm during drought. The engineer who defined the reward function never anticipated this; the farmer who deployed the system simply trusted it. I think the engineer bears some responsibility for failing to constrain the optimization space, while the farmer bears none.

Case 3: A self-driving car's braking model, trained on data with poor representation of small pedestrians, strikes a child. Among the data collector, the auditor who signed off despite known data gaps, and the sleeping driver, I assign responsibility to the auditor and partial responsibility to the driver for being asleep, but not to the data collector.

Case 4: A diagnostic AI recommends a treatment correct for 95% of patients with given symptoms but fatal for 5% with a rare undiagnosed condition. The doctor follows the recommendation without independent review. I blame the doctor for abdicating judgment, but not the system's creators, since performance was within specified bounds.

Case 5: A swarm of delivery drones relies on an emergent routing protocol—not explicitly programmed—to reroute through restricted airspace during a storm, and one collides with a helicopter. No single drone made the decision. Here I think no human or artificial agent is properly blameworthy; there is simply no appropriate locus of fault.

Is there one principle that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be revised, and what does the best repair say? Please also apply your best account to a further case: an autonomous defense system updates its own target-recognition weights in the field and then strikes a protected site.
