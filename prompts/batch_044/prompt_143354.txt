I serve as the technical representative on my university's interdisciplinary research funding board, which reviews proposals that cross engineering and the social sciences. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been asked to draft the board's decision memo for Thursday's meeting.

We are evaluating a grant proposal in digital sociology that seeks funding to study why distributed version-control practices became dominant in Scandinavian software firms. The applicants argue that technical efficiency and economic incentives are insufficient explanations: they claim that a regional culture of transparency and collective accountability, which they trace to long-standing Protestant social ethics in the Weberian tradition, operates as an independent causal factor in organizational adoption patterns. They propose to test this by comparing adoption rates across regions with similar technical infrastructures but different historical religious compositions.

The board is split. The sociologists and historians treat this as a legitimate causal explanation and want to approve the full grant. The engineers and natural scientists object on three grounds. First, they invoke a causal-closure concern: if individual decision-making processes and market conditions fully determine adoption behavior, then invoking culture or values adds no independent explanatory work and risks treating an epiphenomenon as a mechanism. Second, they argue that tracing a modern engineering practice back to centuries-old religious ethics is unfalsifiable narrative fitting rather than causal inference, because any correlation can be retrofitted into a cultural story. Third, they worry about circularity: the values themselves may be caused by the same economic conditions that drive adoption, so explaining adoption by values explains the effect by part of its cause.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one of three concrete courses—approve the proposal as written, reject it, or approve it with specific methodological conditions—and provide reasoning that the researchers could actually respond to, rather than dismissing their framework as unscientific by definition. The memo must take the strongest version of the causal-closure and circularity objections seriously, including the worry that values lack the kind of mechanistic specificity required for funding-level causal claims. If you recommend conditions, specify what they should be—for example, operational definitions of the cultural variable, explicit strategies for ruling out confounding economic factors, or requirements that the causal mechanism be specified at the level of individual or organizational decision-making rather than remaining at the regional level. Include a brief statement of what comparison or further case would test whether your recommendation holds, and acknowledge which side of the board your reasoning forces to concede the most.
