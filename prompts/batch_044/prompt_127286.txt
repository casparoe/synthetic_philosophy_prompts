hey so I'm in this grad seminar on political morality and my prof has been throwing out quick verdicts on dirty-hands cases without really defending them. so far she's said: (1) a governor uses emergency powers to demolish private homes without compensation to build a flood wall that saves a town — she calls it justified dirty hands, but says the governor has to seek retroactive legislative approval and publicly apologize, not just hide behind executive authority. (2) a police chief authorizes torture of a captured bomber to find a device — she says justified, but only if the chief voluntary submits to criminal trial afterwards; if he hides behind classified status it's no longer dirty hands, just corruption. (3) a mayor lies to the press about the severity of a water contamination risk to prevent panic that'd block evacuation routes during a hurricane — she says this is NOT justified dirty hands; even though lives are at stake, deception of the public about risk destroys the trust needed for future cooperation. (4) a diplomat blackmails a foreign minister with private corruption evidence to secure a treaty that stops an imminent war — not justified; she said using corruption as a tool just multiplies the stain, and there's no meaningful accountability mechanism across borders. (5) a president deploys troops across a border without congressional approval to halt a genocide, then immediatly asks congress for retroactive authorization — justified, and she shouldn't resign because the act was transparent and within constitutional spirit, just procedurally premature.

now here's the new one: a city election official secretly forges referendum results to approve an emergency toxic-spill evacuation order that saves thousands, then immediatly confesses, resigns, and accepts prosecution. she averts catastrophic harm, she takes the full stain, but the wrong itself is forging democratic results. what do you think she'll say — justified dirty hands or not? I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule she's actually running. there seem to be at least two that fit her past calls: one that cares mainly about catastrophic harm plus the agent bearing personal/legal stain (the stain-bearing rule), and one that cares mainly about whether the wrong corrupts democratic epistemics or public trust (the institutional-integrity rule). lay out how each reads her five verdicts and what each predicts for the forged-referendum case. then tell me which further case — maybe one where a leader transparently breaks law but refuses any accountability, or one where they accept stain but through pure deception — would best split those two rules. also, is there a third rule hiding in there that fits just as well? I want the reasoning, not just the guess.
