I am preparing an independent-study essay for my high school philosophy course on whether cross-cultural variation in folk morality—particularly the divergence between WEIRD populations and small-scale traditional societies—undermines moral objectivity. Rather than rehearsing standard realism or relativism, I want to defend a position I am calling modular moral realism. The core claim is that human moral cognition comprises several domain-specific modules (for harm, loyalty, purity, authority, and fairness), and that different cultural ecologies selectively strengthen or suppress these modules rather than producing contradictory beliefs about identical moral facts. On this view, a WEIRD individual who emphasizes harm and fairness and a non-WEIRD individual who emphasizes loyalty and purity are tracking objective moral properties within distinct domains, analogous to how different visual systems can register different but equally real features of the color spectrum without rendering color subjective. I worry, however, that this perceptual analogy may not carry the metaphysical weight I need, and that my account risks collapsing into relativism once we ask which modules ought to be activated in pluralistic or changing societies. Below is my working outline.

Working outline:
1. Brief characterization of empirical divergence in moral foundations across cultures.
2. The modular-realist thesis: each module tracks objective properties within its domain.
3. The color-vision analogy and its conceptual limits.
4. Objection: culturally determined activation seems arbitrary.
5. Response: ecological rationality, not preference, determines appropriate activation.

Please comment specifically on whether step 3's analogy genuinely supports the metaphysical claim in step 2, or whether it merely describes psychological variation. I also want to know whether my framing in step 5 avoids arbitrariness without smuggling in normative premises that depend on the very objectivity I am trying to defend. I am not looking for reading lists, citations, or references to specific studies—only conceptual feedback on coherence, potential confusions between empirical psychology and normative ethics, and suggestions for sharpening the distinction between domain-specific objectivity and full-blown relativism.
