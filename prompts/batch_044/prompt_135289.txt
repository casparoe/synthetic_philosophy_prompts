In our course on classical Chinese philosophy we are working through the Mingjia texts, and I am struggling with a specific argumentative move in Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue. The interlocutor establishes that one can ask for a horse and accept either a yellow or a black one, but if one asks specifically for a yellow horse, a black one will not do; from this stricter condition he infers that "yellow horse" and "black horse" differ in kind from "horse" itself, and extends the point to claim that a white horse is not a horse. I understand that the dialogue turns on the difference between a compound term that includes color and a simple term that denotes only form or shape. What I cannot tell is whether this should be read as an early version of the determinable-determinate distinction—where "horse" functions as a determinable and "white horse" fixes a specific determination that excludes other color-instances—or rather as a mereological claim that adding the property of whiteness changes the kind-membership of the whole. The framing in our edition emphasizes "rectifying names" and aligning language with reality, which makes me think the point is about predication rather than about set membership. Could you help me understand exactly what relation the dialogue is asserting between the universal named by "horse" and the qualified compound "white horse," and whether the determinable-determinate reading or the mereological reading comes closer to what the School of Names meant by the rectification of names?
