Design one final-exam essay question for a 90-minute, 25-mark paper in an advanced undergraduate historiography course. The topic is presentism—whether historians can escape the conceptual and normative standards of their own time—but the prompt must not be answerable by reciting standard summaries of Butterfield’s Whiggishness or von Ranke’s sympathy. Instead, inject the following somewhat unconventional framing: require students to disentangle three analytically separable forms of presentism—(1) anachronistic moral judgment of past agents; (2) unavoidable conceptual framing, the claim that every historical description necessarily employs categories forged in the present; and (3) methodological presentism, the deliberate use of present scientific or causal knowledge to identify and correct past explanatory failures. The question should then apply all three to a single concrete case: explaining why early modern alchemists pursued metallic transmutation. The historian wants to maintain that alchemical practice was rational within its own ontological framework (involving substantial forms and the perfectibility of matter) without reducing it to “failed chemistry,” while also holding that alchemical theories of matter were false and that this falsity partly explains the practice’s eventual decline. Force students to locate precisely where each form of presentism operates in this explanation, whether the three conflict, and whether a consistent historiographical stance exists that avoids both uncritical relativism and triumphalist anachronism.

After writing the essay question, provide a model answer of approximately 650 words that demonstrates the level of conceptual precision required for full marks. The scheme should clearly separate an A-range answer—which makes all three distinctions precisely, applies them to the alchemical case without collapsing them, and addresses the tension between hermeneutic charity and explanatory correction—from a B-range answer, which knows the labels but applies them mechanically or ignores the interaction between the forms.
