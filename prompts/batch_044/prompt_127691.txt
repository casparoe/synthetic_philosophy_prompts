I run a philosophy workshop for adults — about 12 people, 90 minutes, one session. I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of national myths built on lost causes, specifically that weird move where a military defeat gets rewritten as a kind of moral victory that fuels identity later. I don't want them to just discuss the Confederacy or Masada; I want them to build one themselves, then hit the moment where factual accuracy and group solidarity pull in opposite directions. Let's call the mechanism defeat-inversion. Split them into groups of three, give each group a fictional defeated community with a real historical parallel hidden from them, have them draft a commemorative school-lesson in two versions — one that records the loss plainly and one that inverts it into moral triumph — then force a collective vote on which version should be taught to children there. Include clear roles (scribe, skeptic, advocate), a simple point system for the vote, a debrief that maps each group's choice back onto real cases like Kosovo Field or Dunkirk without naming them in advance, and an honest note on which feature of real myth-making — probably the multi-generational transmission? — this just can't reproduce. Also tell me what will predicatbly go wrong the first time I run it, and make sure no lecture happens up front.
