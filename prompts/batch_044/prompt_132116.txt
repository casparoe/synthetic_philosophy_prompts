I'm working through a module on mental causation and have constructed the following argument for discussion. It seems to show that the exclusion principle, if applied consistently, undermines not just mental causation but all macro-level explanation, yet I cannot locate the precise slip.

(1) The exclusion principle: if property-instance P* is nomologically sufficient for effect E, then no property-instance M that is ontologically distinct from P* can cause E, on pain of systematic causal overdetermination at the same time and place.

(2) Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental property M is distinct from its physical realizer P—the two are not type-identical, though M supervenes on P with nomological necessity.

(3) Given appropriate background conditions, P is nomologically sufficient for bodily movement B.

(4) Therefore, by (1) through (3), M is causally excluded with respect to B.

(5) The principle applies diachronically as well: if P at t1 realizes M at t1, and P at t1 is sufficient together with the laws for B at t2, then M at t1 is excluded from causing B at t2.

(6) By parallel reasoning, macro-properties at every other level—evolutionary selection pressures, economic preferences, biological functions—are excluded by their respective micro-realizers.

(7) Hence consistent application of (1) yields universal macro-epiphenomenalism: only the most fundamental physical properties possess genuine causal efficacy.

(8) This result is unacceptable, since it dissolves the explanatory autonomy of psychology, biology, and the social sciences.

(9) Therefore (1) must be rejected.

(10) But if (1) fails, then M can cause B despite being fully realized by P, which is precisely the downward causation that non-reductive physicalists deny and that property dualists or emergentists embrace.

(11) So the exclusion argument, intended to defend physicalism by rendering mental properties redundant, actually forces a choice between universal macro-epiphenomenalism and a form of emergentist dualism.

Please diagnose the chain: which step is weakest, and why did it seem plausible? I am especially reluctant to surrender (1), since it captures the intuition behind the exclusion problem, but I also want to avoid (10). If I preserve (1) and reject (5) or (6) instead, what exactly am I committing to? And if I reject (1) but wish to avoid downward causation, what alternative account of macro-causation—perhaps one that distinguishes realization from competition—is available? Give me a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.
