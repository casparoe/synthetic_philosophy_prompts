I've been working through an idea on my own about whether "democratic backsliding" is a real analytical tool or mostly a partisan label that academics and journalists apply to things they dislike. Without formal training, I've developed a framework I'm calling the inter-temporal reciprocity test, which treats democratic decline not as a single sliding scale but as three distinct failure modes: closure, where the opposition is structurally barred from competing at all (as in Turkey); hard restructuring, where formal elections still happen but the rules, courts, and media environment have been rewritten so that opponents can win only by breaking explicit laws (as I see in Hungary and Poland); and rhetorical delegitimization, where fraud accusations and norm-breaking language are widespread but the structural conditions for peaceful alternation remain intact (as I think describes the US federal system, with isolated local exceptions where election offices have been captured by partisan bodies). I apply this with three concrete questions: can opponents win the next national election without breaking rules? Can civil society still organize across partisan and ethnic lines? Is there still a shared public narrative that power could actually change hands?
