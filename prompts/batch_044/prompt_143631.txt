I'm preparing a seminar on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I want to open with a short poem—eighteen to twenty-two lines, no fixed meter, but with a strict structural constraint—that enacts the result rather than merely describing it. The poem should begin with two distinct voices or refrains: one representing the indicative conditional if A then B, the other representing the corresponding conditional probability P(B|A). Over the course of the poem, these voices should be forced into convergence—through rhyme, repetition, or syntactic assimilation—so that by the final lines they have collapsed into a single utterance, mirroring the proof that if the probability of the indicative equals the conditional probability, then independence conditions fail and the conditional trivializes into either material equivalence or complete probabilistic dependence. The turn, whether formal or implicit, should occur at the moment this collapse becomes unavoidable, leaving the reader with the unresolved tension between the formal result and our ordinary practice of asserting if-statements that seem to carry more information than mere probability. Do not resolve the tension with an appeal to Stalnaker's conditional or with a pragmatic escape route; let the form itself show why the distinction dissolves. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of logic recognizes the steps—especially the failure of independence—without functioning as a mnemonic or a bibliographic summary.
