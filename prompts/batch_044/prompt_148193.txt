I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for a mixed group of law students and philosophy undergraduates who have read the Declaration and the Bill of Rights but have not studied Douglass. I want to teach not the familiar "Douglass changed his mind" narrative, but his constitutional hermeneutics: the idea that he developed an anti-Garrisonian interpretive rule in which the Preamble's universalist frame ("We the People") governs specific textual compromises, rather than allowing the compromises (the Three-Fifths Clause, the Fugitive Slave Clause) to define the document's meaning. Plan the explanatory arc: the order in which the components must be introduced so that the hermeneutic move feels like reasoning rather than wishful thinking; the one concrete textual pairing that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions they will bring or form—likely that Douglass was simply being pragmatic, that his view is anachronistic "living Constitution" theory, that Garrison's disunionism had no textual basis, and that recognizing slavery compromises requires condemning the whole document—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end to test whether they have grasped the interpretive rule rather than just adopted a political position; and what I should explicitly leave out at this level (for instance, his post-Reconstruction disillusionment or his later suffrage advocacy) and say so. Do not provide readings or a bibliography.
