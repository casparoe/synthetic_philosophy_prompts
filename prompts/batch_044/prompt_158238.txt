I'm taking this course on philosophy of time and we read Borges's New Refutation of Time plus a couple of his fictions, and I think I've landed on a weird interpertation that might be totally wrong. Basically, I think Borges isn't just recycling Berkeley or Schopenhauer about time being mental; he's doing something more specific like gramatical idealism — the idea that past and future aren't just unreal, they're literally grammatical moods that language projects onto a static present. Like in that story where the guy gets a whole year of mental life in the split second before he's shot, Borges isn't saying subjective time dilates; he's saying there's only ever one instant, and duration is just syntax forcing us to arrange memories in a line. And if you pair this with Borges and I where the self is also split between the public writer and the private person, it seems like both personal identity and temporal order are the same kind of linguistic mistake — we think there's a continuous me stretching through time, but really there's just simultaneous perceptions being narrated. So my position is: for Borges, time and self are co-constructed by narrative grammar, and denying one without the other misses the point.

But what would actually defeat this? I don't mean generic objections like but causation needs time. I want to know: which specific intuitions about causal direction, which results in physics — maybe quantum non-commutativity or block universe interpretations — and which other Borges texts, like the one with the branching garden of paths where all futures happen at once, does this view struggle with? Would a defender have to admit that causation becomes pure correlation, or can they save it by saying language also constructs causal verbs? Is there a fatal problem, like this collapsing into standard Berkeleyan idealism that Borges explicitly distances himself from, or is it well-insulated because he never claimed to escape language? Also, what about the eternal recurrence stuff in his essay on cycles — does that support my gramatical reading or kill it? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed Borges-reader would actually concede versus dismiss as missing the literary point.
