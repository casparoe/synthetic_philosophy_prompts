Here are my considered judgments about when an experience counts as bad in virtue of being painful, arranged to test whether a single principle can capture them. (1) A typical migraine: intense throbbing accompanied by suffering and avoidance motivation; clearly bad. (2) Pain asymbolia: an identical sensory profile, but the subject reports no unpleasantness and no desire for cessation; I am inclined to say pain is present, yet not bad for her. (3) A masochist who induces a burn of comparable intensity and reports the accompanying affect as positively valenced; I think pain remains present, but whether it is bad for him is doubtful — I lean toward no. (4) A patient under deep opioid analgesia who retains only a faint, distant awareness of tissue damage, with neither suffering nor behavioral avoidance; I want to deny that significant pain, or significant badness, remains. (5) A hypothetical "inverse asymbolia": intense suffering, dread, and urgent motivation to escape, but with no localized bodily sensation whatsoever; I find myself judging this genuinely and severely bad, despite the absence of anything we normally classify as pain. (6) A patient following a prefrontal leucotomy who can verbally report injury and assent that "this ought to hurt," yet shows complete behavioral indifference and denies any affective disturbance; her condition strikes me as closer to (2) than to (1).

These verdicts seem to pull apart the sensory, affective, and evaluative dimensions of pain. Is there a coherent principle — perhaps centered on aversive affect, on evaluative judgment, or on functional role — that preserves all six? In particular, can any account explain why (2) lacks badness despite full sensory presence, while (5) possesses badness despite full sensory absence? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on an unconsidered case (a subject experiencing vivid localized burning, finding it mildly unpleasant, but actively resisting analgesia because she judges the sensation spiritually valuable), and be explicit about which of my six judgments must be sacrificed for the sake of simplicity.
