I serve as the senior commissioning editor for a university-press series of synthetic world-history texts aimed at undergraduate readers. Each volume is multi-authored, and I must sign off on every chapter’s explanatory architecture within thirty minutes, without consulting a specialist in philosophy of science. A recurring problem is the treatment of non-human forces—pathogens, climatic shifts, volcanic winters—as “historical actors”. Some authors anthropomorphise these forces, attributing strategic intention; others fall into environmental determinism, treating climate or disease as sufficient causes that override institutional and human variability. I need a reusable decision procedure I can apply to any draft chapter that explains a major transition through a non-human cause.

The principle I want operationalised is this: a non-human factor may carry genuine explanatory sovereignty in a historical narrative only if (i) it is not a constant background condition but produces differential outcomes across comparable societies facing the same event; (ii) its causal contribution survives the introduction of human institutional responses—that is, it explains variance that remains after accounting for policy, technology, or social structure; and (iii) attributing it explanatory weight does not require imputing intention or strategic agency. Where these conditions fail, the factor should be relegated to background context or explicitly flagged as a trigger whose effects were mediated. The procedure must distinguish this safe attribution from determinism, identify where judgement about narrative emphasis is unavoidable, and tell me exactly when to demand revision.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stop points: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a mandatory revision, where it flags a value-laden choice I must make personally, and where it allows publication with an annotation. Then apply it to three draft proposals:

1. A chapter attributing the post-1492 demographic collapse across the Americas primarily to smallpox and measles, describing the pathogens as “conquerors” that “defeated” indigenous polities, with minimal discussion of varying indigenous public-health responses or political alliances.

2. A chapter arguing that the Little Ice Age was the principal driver of seventeenth-century global crises (state collapse, famines, wars), presenting human institutions as uniformly reactive to temperature and harvest failure.

3. A chapter linking the 1815 Tambora eruption to the 1816 European food crises and subsequent migration, but arguing that state grain-policy differences between Britain and France explain the divergent mortality and unrest—a case where the author expects approval, but where the procedure may yield an answer that undermines the chapter’s central claim.

For each application, show the step-by-step result, including any point where the procedure demands revision, requires my judgement, or produces an unwelcome conclusion. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether it can adjudicate between competing specialist historiographies, eliminate normative choices about narrative focus, or substitute for domain expertise in epidemiology or palaeoclimatology. Write in British English.
