I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of history and epistemology, and in a recent graduate seminar on historiographical theory my colleagues and I have been debating whether Leopold von Ranke's ideal of reconstructing the past precisely as it occurred can survive the sceptical challenges raised by E. H. Carr and Hayden White. One participant advanced a view she labeled trace-constrained narrativism: historical narratives are irreducibly shaped by present selection criteria and emplotment choices, yet remain genuinely answerable to an evidential base of archival traces that prevents them from collapsing into pure fiction. I would like you to state your actual credence—on a scale of 0 to 1—that some defensible version of this middle position is not merely conceptually coherent but represents the correct framework for understanding historical objectivity, and I want the reasoning decomposed rather than compressed.

Please break the overall judgment into at least four components, each with a rough probability and a single line of justification: (i) the likelihood that Carr's and White's scepticism actually undermines any robust evidential constraint on narrative construction, as opposed to merely demonstrating the inevitability of selection and perspective; (ii) the likelihood that Ranke's own ideal was already incoherent or self-undermining on internal grounds, independent of later critique; (iii) the likelihood that a synthesis like trace-constrained narrativism successfully avoids collapsing into either naive realist reconstruction or unrestricted constructivism; and (iv) one further factor you regard as essential—perhaps whether objectivity is the appropriate normative standard for historical inquiry at all, or whether the evidential base of archives is itself too theory-laden to do the constraining work required. Show briefly how these terms combine into your final number, identify the term in which you are least confident, and specify what kind of contested historical case—say, an event with sparse, highly ambiguous primary sources but intense present political stakes—would most substantially shift your credence upward or downward.
