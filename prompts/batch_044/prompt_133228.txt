I am in the middle of drafting my doctoral dissertation in moral philosophy and have reached an impasse with the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: what common property, if any, could justify classifying the pleasure of resolving a complex proof, the pleasure taken in malicious gossip, and the pleasure of cool air against sunburnt skin as tokens of a single kind? Before I evaluate particular theories, I want the specification laid out with care.

Please write the complete list of desiderata that an adequate account of what pleasures have in common must meet. Each desideratum should come with its motivating case or conceptual pressure—covering, at minimum, the accommodation of malicious and aesthetic pleasures without collapsing them into moral or representational categories; the preservation of distinctions between sensory, intellectual, and anticipatory enjoyment; the possibility of diachronic comparison for practical reasoning; and the handling of compound experiences in which gustatory and cognitive satisfactions appear fused rather than merely co-occurring. You should also flag any pair of requirements that seem jointly unsatisfiable, especially whether preserving deep phenomenological heterogeneity can be reconciled with supplying a single aggregative metric. In addition, inject one niche constraint that standard introductions tend to neglect: the theory must explain "retrospective valence drift"—the phenomenon whereby the remembered hedonic quality of an experience diverges systematically from its experienced quality in ways not reducible to ordinary memory decay, and this divergence must be internal to the theory's conception of pleasure rather than treated as an external distortion. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their justifications rather than on scoring; then, briefly and without bibliography or citation, indicate how phenomenal-character theories, desire-satisfaction accounts, and attitudinal theories stand against the list.
