I am preparing a graduate seminar on democratic legitimacy and have become stuck on a conceptual tension that I want you to work through with precision. Consider three institutional arrangements that all fall under the umbrella of direct democracy yet operate with very different philosophical architectures. First, Switzerland’s mandatory referendum system for constitutional amendments requires both a popular majority and a cantonal majority — a double-majority rule that treats territorial units as co-equal bearers of sovereignty rather than mere administrative districts. Second, the United Kingdom’s 2016 Brexit referendum was formally advisory, with Parliament retaining legal sovereignty to trigger or withhold Article 50, yet it was treated politically as binding — creating a peculiar hybrid in which popular expression is epistemically authoritative without being legally dispositive. Third, California’s ballot proposition system permits statutory and constitutional changes by simple popular majority, but packages complex regulatory, fiscal, and rights-based provisions into binary yes-or-no choices that do not allow for partial acceptance or amendment by voters, effectively collapsing multi-dimensional policy space into preference aggregation.

What implicit theory — or theories — of popular sovereignty, collective autonomy, and epistemic deference do these three practices jointly presuppose? Specifically, does the Swiss double-majority model presuppose that democratic legitimacy requires the consent of constituted sub-communities, not merely an aggregate of individuals? Does the Brexit arrangement presuppose a defensible distinction between the people’s will as a source of political obligation and their opinion as a source of policy guidance, and if so, can that distinction be maintained without collapsing into either pure parliamentary sovereignty or pure majoritarianism? And does California’s packaging practice presuppose that citizens possess a unified, coherent preference ordering over complex policy domains, or does it instead reveal an implicit commitment to a kind of procedural minimalism that abandons deliberative coherence for the sake of tractability?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments of each practice, say whether they are consistent with one another or point to incompatible conceptions of democratic authority, and identify what would have to change in institutional design if we replaced their shared presuppositions with a straightforwardly deliberative or epistemic model of democracy — one in which legitimacy derives not from the aggregation of pre-formed preferences but from the transformation of judgment through structured public reasoning. Please do not offer bibliographies or reading lists; I am interested solely in the conceptual reconstruction itself.
