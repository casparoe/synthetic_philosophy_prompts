The analytic debate over Buddhist anātman and personal identity has been stalled since the 1970s: philosophers of mind keep refining bundle theories and four-dimensionalist analogues for the five skandhas, while Buddhist scholars—working mainly in Theravāda Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka—insist that anātman is not a thesis about diachronic identity conditions but a soteriological denial of svabhāva (inherent existence) within the present aggregates, leaving conventional persons (prajñapti-sat) intact. A smaller literature then asks whether Buddhist momentariness (kṣaṇikatva) pushes the view toward an eliminativism closer to Siderits than to Parfit. Despite decades of exchange, the sides rarely engage the same argument. Is this stall verbal—"self" meaning "diachronic substrate" for one camp and "synchronic substance" for the other—or substantive, hiding a disagreement about whether moral agency requires a metaphysically simple bearer? Or are they answering different questions—metaphysical versus phenomenological—under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, not a survey of positions, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal distinction, a phenomenological result, or an argument that the question is ill-posed.
