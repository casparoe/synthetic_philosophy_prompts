A philosopher's settled rulings on properties run as follows: she holds that redness is a universal multiply instantiated in every red particular; that the resemblance between two crimson objects is a particular trope, not a shared universal; that mathematical properties such as being prime are abstract universals with no causal efficacy; that compound properties like being red-and-round are logical constructions, not primitive universals; and that relational properties such as being taller than depend on ordered pairs rather than being universals themselves. She is now asked to rule on a proposed "context-sensitive universal": a single property claimed to be instantiated by every member of a class, but instantiated as-F in some members and as-G in others, with no underlying decomposition into two separate universals and no hidden common feature. Predict her verdict, identify the closest analog among her prior rulings, state the two competing principles about universals—one permitting non-uniform, multi-track instantiation and one requiring uniform, simple instantiation—that would produce opposite answers, and propose one further hypothetical case, perhaps involving a structural mathematical property or a quantum disposition, that would conclusively distinguish those two principles.
