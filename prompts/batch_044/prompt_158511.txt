I am running a ninety-minute session with twelve students in an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and I want them to experience the conceptual pressure of the species problem from the inside rather than learning about it by lecture. Design a tribunal simulation with roles and rules arranged so that the failure of essentialism and the inadequacy of pure conventionalism emerge from what the students do, not from what I tell them afterward. Split the group into three panels of four; give each panel an identical sealed dossier containing raw field notes, mating-observation logs, ecological-niche maps, and genetic-distance matrices describing five populations that should remain unnamed in the materials: a ring-species analog where adjacent populations interbreed but terminal ones do not, a morphologically identical but reproductively isolated cryptic pair, a hybrid swarm exchanging genes with both parental lineages, a paraphyletic group that excludes one of its own descendants, and a bacterial lineage whose evolutionary history is reticulate rather than tree-like because of extensive horizontal gene transfer. Instruct each panel that they must construct a single binary legal criterion for what counts as a protected species under a fictional international treaty that awards conservation funding only at the species level and explicitly excludes hybrids, subspecies, and unranked populations; they have fifteen minutes to draft the rule, then twenty minutes to apply it to all five cases and record which qualify and which do not. After that, convene a plenary defense session where panels present their rules and discover that every criterion either arbitrarily splits a continuous gradient or lumps together entities with no shared evolutionary trajectory, and only then should the facilitator name the philosophical positions at stake: morphological essentialism, the biological species concept, phylogenetic monophyly, and ecological niche essentialism. The debrief should map each panel's specific crisis point onto one of these positions, explain why that position looked locally plausible, and price the cost of abandoning it. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the institutional authority and historical contingency that actually drive taxonomic revision in working biology, and the emotional and political weight that conservationists attach to named species, neither of which can be generated by a classroom tribunal with no real stakes. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, specifically that students will tend to patch their rules with ad hoc exceptions rather than recognizing the underlying conceptual failure, and that one panel will likely propose a disjunctive definition that technically covers all five cases but explains nothing; advise how to handle these failures without stepping in to lecture. Present the entire design—setting, materials, timeline, rules, debrief, and honest limitations—in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists, as if writing a single letter. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for sources, and do not ask participants to research specific texts during the activity.
