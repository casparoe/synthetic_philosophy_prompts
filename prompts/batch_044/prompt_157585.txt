I am a novelist preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for an adult reading group—readers of literary fiction with no background in political economy—who have encountered the idea of the undeserving poor only in passing and who tend to treat benefit fraud as simple theft. I want them to grasp, not as administrative detail but as conceptual architecture, how workfare conditionality and fraud-detection regimes do not merely police welfare but actively construct the moral category of the deserving recipient. Give me a teaching plan rather than the lecture: the exact dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that conditionality registers as a conceptual transformation rather than bureaucratic nuisance; the one concrete welfare mechanism that should carry the whole explanation, with explicit instructions on what features it must possess and which real-world analogues it must avoid; the misconceptions they will bring or form in likely sequence—beginning with the assumption that fraud is straightforward stealing and ending with the belief that moral judgment has been eliminated—and the precise point in the arc where each should be defused; a single closing question that would reveal whether they have understood the conceptual shift rather than merely adopted a posture; and an explicit list of what must be omitted at this level, named as such. To prevent the discussion from collapsing into familiar partisan framing, build the explanation around an injected distinction: treat the welfare relation not as a gift or contract but as a hidden moral bookkeeping system in which obligations and entitlements are double entries that must balance, so that fraud becomes reconceived not as the theft of resources but as a category error that dissolves the recipient's standing altogether.
