I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, and I host a small interview series aimed at technically minded listeners who occasionally want to think through conceptual problems that sit behind long-term decision-making. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who has recently been defending Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is catastrophic in a way that far exceeds the deaths it involves. On her account, the deeper harm lies in what Scheffler terms the afterlife conjecture: the claim that much of what gives our present activities their point depends on an implicit assumption that human life will continue after us, so that a doomsday scenario would rob existing lives of meaning rather than merely ending them.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions in British English. The tone should be formal and polished. I want to move well beyond having her restate the view; each question should press where the philosophical literature actually presses, anticipate the reply she is most likely to give, and include a specific follow-up keyed precisely to that reply.

In particular, please ensure the sequence covers the following angles:

First, whether the argument requires specifically biological continuity. If our values, scientific programmes, and artistic achievements could be carried forward by artificial successors — digital or synthetic minds that genuinely extend our projects — would that be enough to preserve the value of present efforts? Include a follow-up for the case where she insists that biological embodiment is indispensable.

Second, the scope of the conjecture. Does it only explain the loss of value for people who already care about distant future generations, and how ought it to address those who are childless, present-focused, or actively misanthropic? Anticipate her reply that the concern is structural rather than psychological, and press that distinction.

Third, the temporal structure. Is an indefinite future genuinely necessary, or would a very long but finite continuation — for instance, ten thousand years of flourishing — restore most of the value? Include a follow-up that asks whether there is a non-arbitrary threshold, and what philosophical work is done by the idea of an open-ended future rather than one that is merely long.

Fourth, the deflationary objection that caring about posthumous continuation is irrational or an evolutionary misprogramming. Include a follow-up that asks what independent reason we have to treat that evolutionary explanation as debunking rather than as explaining a genuine value.

Please avoid generic biographical questions, and do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The response should be in British English.
