I keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern in discussions about time and existence, particularly where special relativity is used. One side observes that observers in different states of motion do not agree on which distant events are happening simultaneously with their own experience. From this disagreement they infer that there is no single, shared present moment cutting across the universe, and therefore that events we call past or future must exist in the same ontological status as events we call present — everything laid out together without a privileged slice. The other side, wanting to preserve the reality of a present moment, responds that presentness need not be shared: each observer possesses a private "now," and reality contains all of these local present-moments together, overlapping but never coinciding exactly.

What strikes me is that this response seems to reproduce the first conclusion in disguise. If every local "now" is equally fundamental, then an event that lies in one observer's present and another's past is simultaneously present and past in an absolute sense, indexed only to a perspective. The collection of all such local presents looks structurally identical to the four-dimensional layout the first argument described, except that it is partitioned into subjective slices rather than treated as one flat manifold. Is there an established term for this intermediate position — presentness that is neither globally unique nor entirely abandoned? If philosophers have named it, how is it distinguished from both the universal-block view and from ordinary presentism? And if it has no standard name, what exactly are its boundary conditions: at what point does multiplying present-slices cease to be presentism and become the very view it was meant to resist? I am not looking for a bibliography, but for the carving itself — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
