I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been reading philosophy of mind and philosophy of science without formal training. Next month I will deliver the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop—roughly half philosophers of artificial intelligence and cognitive science, and half practicing scientists from other fields—titled "What We Leave Out When We Reason." I want the frame problem to structure the remarks rather than decorate them. Drawing on forty years of running long-term experiments, I want to advance a specific, somewhat unconventional framing: that working scientists never solve the frame problem by enumerating non-effects, but instead practice what I will call "domain locking"—tacitly stabilizing a background field of conditions they could not fully list—while formal AI addresses the problem through representations like successor-state axioms or non-monotonic logics. The speech should set up a genuine tension between these two practices, suggest that they may be addressing different problems, and pose an open question that frames the workshop's purpose. The audience includes technical experts who will catch mischaracterizations and family members or junior colleagues who will not. Write a ten-minute address that respects both groups: precise enough that the philosophers do not wince, accessible enough that the scientists feel spoken to rather than lectured at, and shaped so that I sound like someone genuinely puzzled after a career in the lab rather than like a philosopher delivering a survey. Do not include a bibliography, citation requests, or references to specific papers I should read; I will supply references myself if I use this.
