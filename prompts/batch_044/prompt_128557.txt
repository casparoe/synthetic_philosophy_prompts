Write the commencement address for the State University of the Eastern Marches, an institution founded in 1923 by families exiled after the collapse of a short-lived republic. The university’s central tradition is the annual commemoration of the Winter Siege of 1847, when republican forces held a fortified capital for eight months against imperial troops before surrendering. Over generations, this defeat has become the country’s defining national myth: taught in schools not as military failure but as proof of inherent moral superiority, and recently invoked by political leaders to argue that historical victimhood should shape current minority-language and immigration policy. This year’s graduating class is the first in which students from the minority communities—descendants of families that lived under imperial administration—outnumber those from the founding émigré families. Several student representatives have asked that the traditional anthem commemorating the siege not be performed, while alumni and government officials expect it. The president has asked you, a philosopher with no family tie to either side, to deliver the address on the obligations of remembrance. The speech must navigate a precise philosophical tension: it should acknowledge the real suffering and dignity of those who resisted, without allowing that acknowledgment to become a moral credential that converts past defeat into present exclusion. It must speak to historians in the audience without losing the students, honor grief without sanctifying grievance, and avoid both false reconciliation and performative condemnation. Structure the address so that the distinction between mourning a loss and mythologizing it actually determines what you can and cannot say about democratic accountability, minority membership, and the limits of national narrative.
