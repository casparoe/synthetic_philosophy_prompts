I'm preparing a workshop pitch for a philosophy-of-decision-theory session on risk attitudes, and I need help sharpening a proposal. The core idea is this: risk-weighted expected utility (REU) is usually presented as a unified framework that resolves Allais-style paradoxes by letting agents assign risk weights to outcomes independently of their probabilities. Yet almost all treatments assume that the agent's outcome space and value scale are fully determinate before risk weights are applied. I'm interested in whether REU can be extended to handle what I call "structurally ambiguous catastrophic outcomes" — cases where an agent recognises a non-negligible probability of an extreme negative result, but the very nature of that result (and hence its placement on the value scale) depends on epistemic resolutions that occur after the decision point. In long-horizon technological-risk scenarios, for instance, the "catastrophe" might involve the emergence of values or agents that render the pre-decision utility scale incommensurable with the post-resolution state.

My proposed question is precise: can REU be modified with a second-order "domain-gating" mechanism — where risk weights operate not only over first-order outcomes but over epistemic partitions of the outcome space — without collapsing into standard imprecise-probability models or abandoning the representation results that motivate REU in the first place? If such an extension is possible, what formal conditions on the gate must hold for preference consistency? If it is impossible, does that show that REU implicitly presupposes a kind of "value determinacy" that fails in precisely the high-stakes domains where risk-sensitive reasoning is most needed?

The proposal should be suitable for a one-semester, approximately 6,000-word term paper or a small grant application. Give an honest assessment of scope: what part of the project is realistic for a single semester, and where it might grow too large.
