Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the constitutional-pluralist camp and the four-dimensional-monist camp on the ontology of statues and clay. The pluralist holds that a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects with different persistence conditions and artistic properties, even while they share all material parts at a given time. The monist holds that there is only one four-dimensional spatiotemporal object; “statue” and “clay” name different temporal phases, relational profiles, or descriptive perspectives on that single worm, and any apparent modal or property differences are explained by temporal indexing or historical relations rather than by co-located distinct entities.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: state precisely what both sides accept about the formal ontology of material constitution (if the pluralist admits a constitution relation, state its formal features both can sign onto), about the empirical data that require explanation (gradual part replacement, destructive testing, artistic restoration, and the possibility of formless intermediate states), and about the limits of standard arguments—specifically, what Leibniz’s Law arguments do establish, what they fail to establish, and where modal-difference premises are contested. Second, a disagreements table: for each residual dispute, present (a) the pluralist’s formulation in wording the pluralist accepts, (b) the monist’s formulation in wording the monist accepts, and (c) the exact logical or metaphysical point of divergence in one sentence. Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions—such as whether artistic-form properties are intrinsic or relational, whether coincident objects can have divergent persistence conditions, whether causal-historical continuity is necessary for artwork identity, and whether “part” is indexed to an object’s kind—such that settling any one crux in either direction would cause at least one camp to revise its core view. For each crux, record what both sides agree would count as decisive evidence or argument, and state each camp’s explicit prediction in advance. Fourth, apply the framework to the Shifting-Form Case: a statue composed of a shape-memory material that can revert to a featureless ingot and then be reheated back into the identical macroscopic form, either with the original atoms or with perfect synthetic analogues that are physically indistinguishable but lack the original causal chain. Both camps should record in advance whether they predict the statue survives this cycle, whether it survives analog-substitution, and whether the clay-object survives independently of the form’s presence. Fifth, if any portion of the dispute is verbal—concerning, for example, the terms “object,” “part,” “survival,” or “identity”—identify exactly which portion, explain why it is verbal rather than substantive, and propose a neutral reformulation that both sides could adopt without altering their ontological commitments. Keep all formulations precise, avoid slanted characterizations of either view, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to external authority.
