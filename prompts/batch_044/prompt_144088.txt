For a graduate seminar I am convening on mechanistic explanation and scientific discovery, I wish to frame the central philosophical problem not as the generic question of what constitutes a mechanism, but as the far more recalcitrant question of mechanistic identity across anomalous conditions. In particular, I want the discussion to center on what I will call structural latency: cases in which a system retains all of its original physical parts and local causal capacities, yet an anomalous perturbation—whether a crossed thermal threshold, the absence of an inhibitory signal, or a nonlinear feedback cascade—activates relations among those very parts that were present but causally inert under standard operating conditions. The organizational graph is rewired from within; no component has been replaced or removed, but the relational architecture that defined the mechanism’s normal functioning has been superseded by an alternative one that uses the same substrate to generate behavior the original mechanism could not produce. The challenge, then, is to determine whether we should regard this as the same mechanism operating in a degraded or alternative mode, or as the emergence of a numerically distinct mechanism, and what conceptual criteria could adjudicate this without collapsing into pure component-identity on one side (which misses the organizational shift entirely) or into an overly brittle relational structuralism on the other (which implies that almost any perturbation generates a new mechanism).

With this framing in mind, please write the desiderata that any philosophically adequate theory of mechanistic sameness must satisfy if it is to handle such cases rigorously. For each requirement, specify the phenomenon or distinction it is meant to cover—normal functioning, graded degradation, latent-pathway activation, counterfactual stability under intervention, predictive tracking for anomaly detection, and consistency with interventionist accounts of causal relevance—and provide the motivating consideration behind it. You should clearly flag which desiderata are non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific preferences, and you should explicitly identify any subset of requirements that you take to be jointly unsatisfiable, particularly if organizational invariance and component-identity come into direct conflict when latent pathways are activated. Keep the weight of your answer on the architecture of the criteria themselves rather than on an exhaustive scorecard, though I do ask that you briefly indicate—without bibliographic apparatus—how a Craver-style entity-and-activity account, a Glennan-style interactionist view, and a more recent organizational-invariance approach fare against your list. I am interested in the conceptual architecture, not in citations or reading lists; write as though the model must evaluate these positions from its own conceptual resources.
