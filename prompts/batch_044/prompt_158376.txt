In my reporting on the intersection of moral psychology and normative philosophy, I have followed for more than a decade what looks like a stalled argument about moral foundations theory and its normative implications. The descriptive picture is largely settled: across cultures, liberals tend to weight harm avoidance and fairness more heavily, while conservatives distribute moral concern more evenly across loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty. Yet two camps remain locked in place. The normative pluralists treat this asymmetry as evidence that liberal moral cognition is systematically impoverished, missing genuinely binding group-level and purity-based obligations. The debunkers treat the identical asymmetry as evidence that conservative cognition is over-calibrated to tribal defense and hierarchy maintenance, so the extra foundations are evolutionary artifacts with no normative standing. Both sides accept the same cross-cultural data, both agree that suffering is morally bad, and neither has moved.

My working hypothesis is that they are not really fighting over whether loyalty or sanctity are values at all, but over a hidden meta-normative question—whether moral judgment should function primarily as a universalizable constraint mechanism or as an in-group coordination device—so they are answering different questions under the single heading of who is right. I want an honest diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. Is this disagreement verbal, turning on what counts as a moral foundation versus a psychological bias? Is it substantive, with each side appealing to different standards of evidence for what makes a consideration morally authoritative? Is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps about whether the conservative-weighted foundations operate with universal scope or only in-group scope—doing all the work? Or are we facing bedrock intuitions about whether group loyalty can ever be a moral reason, with no further argument possible? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between scope-weighted and domain-expanded reasoning, an empirical finding about cross-cultural application patterns, or a reconceptualization of the debate itself? And if none of those would help, is the impasse a sign that the original question is ill-posed?
