Consider a hypothetical cognitive architecture—call it a triangulated loop—in which three distinct human brains are linked by a low-bandwidth, bidirectional interface that permits only partial overlap of episodic traces and procedural habits. No single brain contains the complete pattern of memories, intentions, or emotional dispositions; instead, a stable first-person perspective, if it exists, emerges from the dynamic interaction among the three, with each brain contributing irreducible fragments. This is not a standard split-brain case, because the separation is partial, distributed, and sustained over time rather than being a surgical division of hemispheres.

Against this backdrop, evaluate how well the following five theoretical stances accommodate the claim that a genuine “I”—with persistent self-reference and first-person authority—could inhabit such a distributed system. Assign each stance an integer score from 0 (complete failure) to 10 (full accommodation), then rank the five from highest to lowest score. For each, explain in one or two sentences what feature of the stance drives the score, and note the main cost or concession required if one adopts it for this case.

The five stances are:

A. A patternist account in the spirit of Hofstadter, on which an “I” is a high-level strange loop sustained by symbolic self-reference patterns that can, in principle, be implemented across multiple substrates provided the loop remains causally coherent.

B. A strict neuro-embodied substrate view, which holds that first-person persistence requires continuous integration within a single biological brain and that distributing fragments across separate skulls necessarily dissolves the “I” into three separate subjects.

C. A psychological-continuity theory, according to which what matters for persistence is the appropriate causal and qualitative relations among mental states, regardless of whether they are housed in one brain or spread across several, provided the chain of continuity is not broken.

D. A narrative-identity framework, which treats the self as the author and protagonist of an ongoing, coherent life-story; in the loop, this story could be co-authored by the three brains if their fragments are woven into a single, self-told biography.

E. A hybrid “fragmented-authority” stance—constructed specifically for this scenario—which grants that a distributed “I” can exist but denies it full first-person authority, holding instead that each brain retains veto power over any mental state attributed to the collective, so the “I” is always partly alienated from its own contents.

In your ranking, pay particular attention to whether stance A can genuinely preserve the causal coherence Hofstadter demands without collapsing into B or C, and whether stance E’s concession of partial alienation makes it more or less plausible than D in this specific architecture.
