I am a doctoral student in political philosophy serving as the recording secretary for my university’s Public Forum Oversight Committee. By next Thursday we must vote on whether to approve, approve with conditions, or reject a proposal for a year-long funded speaker series titled “The Boundaries of Tolerance.” The series is not a campaign of abuse; it is a structured academic inquiry into whether democratic funding bodies have a reflexive obligation to platform arguments that reject democratic equality—including arguments that, if taken seriously, would dissolve the authority of the very committees that approve them. The organizers explicitly frame this as an institutional version of the paradox of tolerance: if tolerance extends to those who argue for its abolition, then institutionalizing tolerance becomes a mechanism of self-undermining.

Our committee charter, revised in a 2018 governance restructuring, contains an unusual procedural clause that requires every funding decision to satisfy what it calls “reflective non-dominance”: a proposal may not be excluded solely on the ground that its conclusions, if adopted as institutional policy, would eliminate the authority of the body that approved it. Some members read this clause as a safeguard against hidden orthodoxies; others treat it as a reductio ad absurdum that should be ignored when it conflicts with the committee’s survival. The proposal forces the issue, because its central speakers intend to argue that honoring this clause—funding voices that deny the legitimacy of the funding body—is precisely the kind of self-destructive tolerance the paradox warns against.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should be addressed to the full committee, run approximately 800–1000 words, and contain a concrete recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions—together with an implementation framework and an explicit time frame (e.g., a pilot period, a mandatory debrief, or a sunset clause). The reasoning must engage seriously with the proposal’s strongest philosophical claim rather than dismissing it as a familiar paradox or reducing it to free-speech boilerplate. It must also address the “reflective non-dominance” clause with intellectual honesty: if the recommendation violates the clause, explain why the clause should be interpreted differently or overridden in this case; if the recommendation honors the clause, explain why doing so does not collapse into incoherence or institutional suicide.

The memo should treat the losing position with full charity, identifying the best version of that view and explaining what it gets right about institutional legitimacy or democratic risk. It should not ask for further literature reviews, provide bibliographies, cite scholars by page, or depend on external sources the committee cannot access; the argument must stand on its structural merits. Avoid reducing the issue to legal definitions of hate speech or generic diversity statements. The recommendation should turn specifically on how the philosophical problem is instantiated by this proposal and by the reflexive structure of our charter clause.
