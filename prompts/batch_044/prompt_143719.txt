I serve as senior compliance architect for a transnational distributed archive that aggregates authoritative records from independent regulatory bodies, scientific consortia, and judicial repositories. Our governance charter explicitly adopts a dialetheist stance: some contradictions between equally authoritative sources are literally true—reflecting genuinely inconsistent but legitimate authorities—rather than errors to be eliminated, and it requires paraconsistent containment so that a single contradiction does not invalidate an entire record cluster. I need a reusable decision procedure my review team can run on any proposed pair of contradictory submissions before merging them into the active archive. The reviewers have legal and scientific literacy but no formal logic training; each case gets roughly two hours; and the output must be one of three dispositions: retain both records with the contradiction flagged as a true dialetheia; reject one record as erroneous; or escalate to the ethics board because the conflict involves values rather than authority or logic. Lay out the steps in order: the exact question at each stage, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a definitive answer, and where it must hand off to human judgment because the distinction cannot be mechanized. The procedure must separate substantive contradictions (genuine disagreements among authorities) from artifactual ones (translation errors, temporal misindexing, scope ambiguities), and it must identify when a contradiction is locally contained versus when it threatens cascade failure across linked records—triggering escalation regardless of its truth status. Provide a concise set of quick tests reviewers can apply, flag the hard cases where the procedure will misfire, and state explicitly what it is not designed to do. Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, two environmental agencies submit mutually exclusive safe and toxic thresholds for the same compound, both asserting current statutory authority; second, a judicial interpretation and the statute it cites contain directly contradictory definitions of negligence, but only in a narrow subclass of liability cases; and third, a scenario in which the procedure delivers a disposition that stakeholders will strongly resist—identify that disposition and explain why the framework mandates it despite the objection.
