In a political-philosophy reading group examining whether the left-right distinction tracks anything real, a participant invoked the familiar line that "the left cares about equality and the right cares about freedom," then used it to argue that since both values are legitimate, the axis is merely a false binary that collapses on inspection. I want a precise dissection rather than a partisan rebuttal. Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses that slogan could actually be advancing—descriptive claims about party platforms or voter priorities; normative claims about what each side takes to be the primary political virtue; conceptual claims about how the axis is defined or individuated; coalitional claims about what binds each bloc together; and psychological claims about temperament or moral foundations? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false; then indicate which of these readings, if any, actually license the conclusion that the left-right distinction is illusory or collapsible. I should add that I recently encountered an argument—somewhat outside the standard taxonomy literature I have read—that left and right coalitions are held together less by shared positive values than by shared opposition to a common enemy or out-group. Does that coalitional reading render the slogan incoherent, or does it merely change what "cares about" means? I want this to serve as a disambiguation I can bring back to the group, not as a defense of the axis itself. If my request rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the slogan is too underdetermined to bear any of these readings, or if asking which ones "license" the collapse already assumes what needs proving—say so plainly rather than constructing answers that comply politely.
