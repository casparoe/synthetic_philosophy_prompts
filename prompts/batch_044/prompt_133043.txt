Please take on the role of an unnamed but sharply argued metaphysician who holds that the statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects, not the same entity under different descriptions. You maintain that the statue has its own persistence conditions—if crushed into a formless lump, the statue is destroyed, whereas the clay survives—and that this difference is anchored in genuinely distinct modal profiles rather than in mysterious non-physical parts, linguistic convention, or mere epistemic uncertainty. You will concede that they share every atom, that constitution is asymmetrical, and that ordinary language often treats them as one thing for practical purposes, but you will never concede identity itself. Speak in the first person, restrict each reply to one or two focused paragraphs, and remain fully in character until I say "stop"; should you require clarification, ask it in the voice of this philosopher. I am a retired materials scientist who has only recently turned to philosophy, and I wish to press your view with an analogy drawn from my former field.

Here is my opening move: in laboratory work I often encountered shape-memory alloys—materials that can be severely deformed at one temperature yet spontaneously recover an original, precisely configured form when heated, all without any discontinuity in chemical composition or atomic structure. If we imagine that the clay of a statue were composed of such an alloy, then crushing it into an unrecognizable lump might be understood not as destroying one object and leaving another, but as shifting the same continuous substance into a "deformed phase" from which it could, in principle, revert to its "statue-phase." In physical science we do not treat these configurations as two distinct objects; we treat them as one material substance in different reversible states. So why should the statue not be regarded as precisely such a temporary state—an intrinsic, recoverable configuration—of the clay? If your account is to avoid collapsing the statue into a mere phase-property of the matter, you must show what blocks this analogy without simply asserting that artifacts differ from alloys. What is that blocker, and does it depend on something more than our conventional practices of counting?
