There's this small translation workshop that does Renaissance French verse — especially stuff in the vein of Clément Marot. They work with this double constraint they call le ton beau de Marot: you have to keep the formal architecture exactly (rhyme, syllable count, caesura) but also preserve that specific Marot-style elegance — clear, chatty, never pompous. Heres the weird part though: they encourage you to swap out period-specific references for modern ones if it helps the tone land right, but they automatically reject any version that uses archaic diction or inverted syntax just to sound "authentic," even if the meter and rhyme are perfect. So theyll accept a translation that changes half the imagery but keeps the rhyme and the breezy voice, but reject one thats metrically flawless and semantically literal but reads like a museum label. What theory of translation — or of what a poem even is — does that practice actually need? Are they assuming form and content are separable but both essential, or that there's some "spirit" of the work living in the tone rather than the words? And is there rejection of archaism actually consistent with there insistence on formal preservation, or does it show there smuggling in a reader-response view without admitting it?
