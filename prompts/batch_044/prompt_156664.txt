I've been revisiting Herbert Simon's satisficing in light of longstanding discussions here about bounded rationality and search costs. Simon's model is typically read descriptively: an agent holds an aspiration level, searches options sequentially, and stops at the first one that clears it, with the level itself adapting upward or downward with experience. I've been working through a prescriptive variant—call it anchored threshold search—in which the stopping condition is fixed to a non-negotiable floor that never updates downward with failure, functioning more like a deontic constraint than an adaptive heuristic. Compare Simon's adaptive satisficing with anchored threshold search. Do they share the same vulnerability to path-dependence and ordering effects in the search space? Does Simon's machinery for aspiration adjustment have any analog in the anchored case, or does the rigidity of the floor break the bounded-rationality justification entirely? And if both can be gamed by manipulating the sequence of encountered options, is the failure mode symmetrical, or does anchoring introduce a distinct form of fragility that adaptive satisficing avoids?
