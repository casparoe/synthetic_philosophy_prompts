I keep encountering a particular defense of larger populations that doesn't seem to fit the standard categories. The speaker says that with more people alive, we increase the chance that someone will produce a world-changing idea, a masterpiece, or an exceptional form of understanding—almost as if humanity were running a lottery where each life is a ticket and the prize is a rare, transformative achievement. They insist that this isn't just adding up happiness across lives, because the argument still works if many of the added lives are ordinary; what matters is that the pool is big enough to cover the tail end of human possibility. They also reject averaging, since they aren't claiming the exceptional people make up for mediocre ones—rather, they seem to treat unrealized potential as a loss in itself, like leaving seats empty in a theater that was built for a full audience. I don't know whether philosophers have named this specific hybrid: valuing population growth partly as a means of diversifying or "insuring" for rare high-value outcomes, and partly as a refusal to waste built-in human capacity. Is there a recognized term for it? How does it differ from total-utilitarian, average-utilitarian, and person-affecting frameworks, and where does it break down?
