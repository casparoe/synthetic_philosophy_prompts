A colleague walked me through this argument that Hume's standard of taste ultimately collapses into subjectivism, and I cannot find the exit. (1) Hume maintains that judgments of beauty are not merely expressions of personal preference but can be correct or incorrect, because human sentiment has a uniform structure that supports a genuine standard of taste. (2) That standard is specified by the verdicts of true judges—those possessing delicate sentiment, extensive practice, comparative breadth, and freedom from prejudice. (3) Yet Hume also treats the identification of these true judges as an empirical matter: we discover who they are by observing whose judgments converge reliably across time and culture. (4) Therefore the standard does not operate as an independent criterion against which judgments are measured; it is constituted by the very convergence it is meant to explain. (5) Now consider what I will call an uncalibrated perceptual domain: a class of objects—say, images generated by algorithms that vary continuously across high-dimensional parameter spaces far outside the range of human evolutionary exposure—where human sentiments do not converge at all, and where practice and comparison fail to produce agreement. (6) In such a domain there can be no true judges, because there is no convergence by which to identify them. (7) But if there are no true judges, then there is no standard of taste, and consequently no objective fact about whether any particular object in that domain is beautiful or ugly. (8) Hence Hume's account makes the existence of beauty contingent on the accidental uniformity of human perceptual response; where that uniformity breaks down, beauty vanishes. (9) This implies that beauty is not an objective feature of objects but a byproduct of our evolved cognitive architecture, which is precisely the subjectivist conclusion Hume sought to avoid—especially since he wants to maintain that a work like Milton's is genuinely superior to inferior productions regardless of contingent agreement. (10) The conclusion seems to prove too much, for it would also eliminate aesthetic truth in any genuinely novel domain of experience. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) or (9), though I am open to paying elsewhere.
