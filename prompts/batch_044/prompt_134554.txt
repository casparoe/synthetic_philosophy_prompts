I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working on classical reception and moral education, and I've been trying to sharpen my thinking about biography's pedagogical function. In seminars on Plutarch and in recent literature on hagiography and moral learning from lives, people keep invoking the idea that biography works like a "mirror"—the reader beholds virtue or vice instantiated in a concrete life and, by comparison or identification, corrects or forms her own character. The slogan is used to defend biography's value against critics who reduce it to celebrity worship or entertainment; it is also used to excuse hagiographic distortion by claiming that idealization serves the mirror's purpose rather than deceiving it; and it sometimes appears in virtue-ethical arguments that narrative exposure is the primary route by which moral dispositions are cultivated.

But I suspect this metaphor collapses several distinct mechanisms into one vague image. Could you lay out the precise conceptual claims that "biography is a mirror for moral self-correction" might actually be expressing? I want a disambiguation that separates at least the following possibilities: (1) a perceptual or cognitive claim that seeing virtue embodied in a particular narrative makes it more available to practical reasoning than an abstract principle does; (2) a mimetic claim that readers unconsciously model their behavior on the protagonist's actions; (3) a normative-assessment claim that comparing one's own life to the biographical subject yields a rational judgment about one's moral standing; (4) a psychological or habituation claim that narrative immersion produces emotional conditioning or gradual character formation; and (5) a more radical hermeneutic claim—associated with some recent work on moral exemplarity—that the self is partly constituted through identification with narrated others, so that biography does not merely instruct but actively constructs the moral agent.

For each reading, please say whether it amounts to a near-trivial restatement of the slogan, whether it is a substantive and defensible thesis in moral psychology or virtue ethics, and whether it actually supports the particular uses people put the metaphor to—especially the defense of hagiographic exaggeration and the dismissal of biography as mere diversion. I am also interested in how each reading fares against the situationist critique that robust character traits do not persist across contexts, since some mirror-models seem to presuppose stable dispositions that situationism denies. Finally, apply the best-supported reading to a case I keep returning to: a student reads a highly idealized medieval hagiography, then shows markedly increased charitable behavior toward strangers but no corresponding change in her treatment of family members. Does the mirror metaphor still apply here, and if so, which version of it? If none fit cleanly, which of my implicit verdicts about the case should I revise?
