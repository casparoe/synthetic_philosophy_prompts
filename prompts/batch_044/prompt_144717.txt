I have just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about whether maximal moral goodness is desirable, and before I commit my position to writing I want an audit of the specific commitments I was defending. Here is my philosophical profile on the matter:

1. Moral saints—agents who systematically subordinate every domain of life to the maximization of moral value—are not appropriate role models for ordinary people, because the elimination of personal projects, intimate attachments, and non-instrumental pursuits in favor of constant moral optimization produces a form of life that is deficient in richness, individuality, and non-moral goods.

2. At the same time, I hold a perfectionist view of moral development: each agent has a duty, not merely to avoid wrongdoing, but to become as morally excellent as is genuinely achievable given their capacities and circumstances.

3. I maintain that supererogation is coherent and important: acts that go well beyond the call of duty are genuinely possible, which implies that reaching the highest possible level of moral achievement is not universally obligatory.

4. I also believe that the costs of being a moral saint are not merely subjective or psychological; they represent an objective loss of well-being, where well-being includes not only pleasure but also the realization of objective goods such as creative achievement, deep personal relationships, and autonomous life-shaping.

5. Yet I reject the claim that moral goodness and personal well-being are fundamentally zero-sum; I hold that they are, in principle, harmonizable, and that extreme moral virtue does not necessarily entail extreme deprivation of the goods mentioned in (4).

6. Finally, I hold that a society composed entirely of moral saints would be undesirable—not because such people would be unpleasant, but because the very concept of a moral saint requires a background of non-saints whose needs and vulnerabilities generate the demands that saints respond to; universal sainthood would, on this view, dissolve the conditions for its own possibility.

My request is for a consistency check focused on these six commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision that would restore full consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, specifying for each what exactly must give and what that costs me conceptually. I am especially concerned with whether my perfectionism (2) can survive together with my acceptance of supererogation (3) and my rejection of saints as models (1); whether my claim about objective costs (4) undermines my harmonizability thesis (5); and whether (6) introduces a hidden contradiction with (2). I want the audit itself, not a general lecture on moral demandingness—focus on what this particular set of commitments forces me into.
