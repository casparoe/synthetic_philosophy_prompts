I sit on our state's secondary-civics curriculum review board, and after a long argument with a friend—she insisted that treating American exceptionalism in the classroom either reproduces nationalist myth or smuggles in an academic framing that makes the ideology respectable—I need to circulate a short opinion before Thursday's vote on a proposed mandatory eleventh-grade unit. The proposal does not celebrate or deny exceptionalism; it requires students to analyze it as three separable dimensions: an empirical historical thesis about institutional divergence from comparable settler societies; a normative claim that such divergence grounds special obligations or privileges; and an ideological function that selectively mobilizes historical evidence for present political ends. Half the board objects that this tri-partite framework is too abstract for sixteen-year-olds and implicitly legitimizes exceptionalism by translating a contested political narrative into an apparently neutral taxonomy. The other half contends that without this separation students cannot distinguish factual comparison from moral entitlement or propaganda. Please draft the board's decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—recommending a concrete course of action with an implementation timeline if adopted, and engaging seriously the strongest consideration on the losing side: specifically, the objection that the framing itself is a conceptual move that obscures power relations by presenting myth as analytic object.
