In my novel, a historian-protagonist applies Nietzsche's distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history to judge cultural practices. Her track record is consistent but ambiguous: she approves monumental narratives only when they actively serve present life and rejects them as mere hero-worship; she values antiquarian preservation solely as continuity with the past, condemning it when it becomes lifeless accumulation; she endorses critical history only as a temporary rupture from a harmful inheritance, warning that perpetual critique destroys the soil for future growth. She has praised a civic commemoration that invented a heroic origin to motivate reform; she has rejected a museum that catalogued every artifact without narrative purpose; she has supported severing legal ties to a colonial past; and she has criticized a generation that refused all historical inheritance. Now she must judge a new institution: a digital archive that achieves perfect antiquarian preservation of every recorded human experience, while allowing users to algorithmically suppress traumatic episodes and synthesize heroic narratives for collective motivation. Predict her verdict, but first identify at least two distinct rules consistent with her earlier judgments—say, one that privileges the preservation of continuity regardless of content and one that subordinates all preservation to present life-enhancement—and show how each applies here. Give your best guess at her actual judgment with reasons, identify the closest analog among her prior cases, and specify what further case—perhaps one involving involuntary preservation without any synthetic function—would definitively separate the two rules.
