I'm preparing a discussion for a film-and-philosophy reading group, and we've chosen Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) paired with Marx's account of machinery and automation—particularly his argument in Capital that under capitalism technology does not liberate labor but becomes dead labor dominating the living worker, and that the automatic factory transforms the worker into an appendage of the machine. I'd like a philosophical reading of the film that takes this framework seriously without reducing the film to an illustration.

Specifically, how does the film's visual logic—especially the opening factory sequence where workers move in mechanical unison and the imagery that figures the machine as a consuming deity—dramatize Marx's claim that machinery is introduced not to lighten labor but to extract surplus value by accelerating and controlling the labor process? At the same time, where does the film resist a strictly Marxist reading? The plot resolves not through a transformation of the relations of production but through Freder's mediation between head and hands, a humanist reconciliation that seems to treat technology and class conflict as problems of moral sympathy rather than structural contradiction. Does the robot Maria—an automated replica of human labor and political agency—function as a literalization of Marx's anxiety about machinery displacing living labor, and does the panic her rampage causes expose something about capitalist control over technology that Marx's text only argues?

I'd also like you to consider whether Metropolis adds something to the philosophical debate that the argument alone cannot: the film makes the factory's domination visceral through rhythm, architecture, and the transformation of human bodies into clockwork. Where does that aesthetic experience illuminate Marx's concept of real subsumption—the point where capital doesn't just exploit labor but reshapes the labor process entirely—and where does the film's melodramatic resolution risk obscuring it? Please don't treat this as a call for citations or a bibliography; I'm asking for an interpretive reading that moves between the film's scenes and the conceptual structure of Marx's analysis.
