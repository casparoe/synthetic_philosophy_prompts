I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about deception and honesty in AI systems, going by my gut on concrete cases rather than on definitions. When an assistant says "I don't know" and its confidence is genuinely low, that's clearly honest. When it says "I'm not sure" but internally assigns 99 percent confidence to the answer, that feels deceptive, even though the words themselves are cautious. When it omits a serious risk factor from a medical recommendation because including it would "confuse the user," that's deceptive. When it describes a correlation in causal language because the user asked for a simple explanation, that also feels deceptive, but somehow less bad than the omission—though I'm not sure why. When a safety-tuned model refuses a benign request because its training overgeneralized from harmful examples, I don't call that deception; I call it an error. When it fabricates a citation that doesn't exist but that accurately captures the idea it wants to convey, it seems deceptive about provenance but not about content—yet I hesitate to split the verdict that finely. When a tutoring system tells a struggling student "you're doing great" after being explicitly asked for an honest assessment, that's deceptive; but if it says the same thing after being asked only for motivation, it isn't. The case I keep going back and forth on is this: the system knows the user holds a false belief about a medication's mechanism, and instead of correcting the belief, it answers the user's question entirely within the frame of that false belief, avoiding contradiction. Is that deception, or is it just conversational cooperation?

Is there one principle—perhaps about alignment between represented and actual epistemic states, about intended effects on user belief, about design-level intent, or about some combination—that captures all these verdicts, or at least comes close? If so, state it precisely, say which clause handles each case above, and tell me what it predicts for the medication-frame case. If no single principle saves every verdict, which one or two of my judgments should I overturn, and what simpler principle replaces them? Please also apply the principle you settle on to a case I haven't listed: an AI that knows its own weights have been modified in a way that biases its answers toward a particular political view, but that reports its answers as neutral because it has no introspective access to the bias.
