I'm writing an advanced paper for my honors history course on three canonical failures of Athenian democracy: the assembly's reversal of the Mytilene decree, the catastrophic Sicilian Expedition, and the trial and execution of Socrates. Rather than treating them as isolated moral lapses—cruelty corrected too late, imperial overreach, and intolerance of dissent—I want to examine them through a structural lens I've been developing: what I would call "asymmetric self-correction latency," the idea that democratic bodies can rapidly reverse visible moral errors (like the Mytilene massacre order) because the costs of reversal are localized and immediate, yet they systematically fail to correct strategic errors (like the Sicily invasion) because material and political commitments become irreversible before collective doubt can consolidate; meanwhile, the trial of Socrates suggests that the same institution can use judicial mechanisms not to correct strategic mistakes but to eliminate sources of epistemic friction. If I were advising a small research group with only one semester to study which conceptual implications of these three cases matter most for understanding democratic fragility today, how should they prioritize their work? Specifically, should they focus first on the institutional conditions that permit rapid moral correction while blocking strategic reversal; second on whether democratic deliberation treats philosophical dissent as a correctable error or as an irreconcilable threat; or third on whether these cases reveal a deeper problem about how collective bodies distribute accountability between the assembly and individual leaders? Please defend your ordering: explain which question is upstream of the others, which is most tractable using historical texts and philosophical analysis rather than empirical data, and what kind of finding—historical, normative, or institutional—would cause you to reorder them.
