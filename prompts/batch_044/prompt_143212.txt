I'm preparing material for a seminar on personal identity and want to move past the standard one-replica teletransporter case that everyone already knows. Please construct an original variant—call it the asymmetric multiplex bridge case—engineered to the specifications below. Keep the scenario itself to roughly one paragraph, avoid exotic physics or science-fictional devices beyond a theoretical scanner and a temporary neural-computational link, and do not invoke demons, perfect predictors, or omniscient observers.

The scenario: Subject S enters a scanner that destroys S's original body. Two replicas are generated at distant locations. R-alpha receives about 85 percent of S's psychological profile, including all long-term autobiographical memories, but each of those memories carries a phenomenological tag marking it as recollected from an external source rather than owned in the first person. R-beta receives the remaining 15 percent—the core personality structure and all memories from the past year—but these are fully integrated with authentic first-person ownership. For exactly 72 hours after awakening, R-alpha and R-beta are joined by a direct, bidirectional substrate that allows seamless, real-time exchange of cognitive states, so that they function as a single distributed system with unified access to both memory-sets. After 72 hours, the link is permanently severed, leaving two fully independent beings.

Your design must yield the following verdicts, and you must show the work for each. First, a bodily-continuity theorist must deny that S survives at all; state the criterion and explain why neither replica, even during the bridge, counts as the same living body or continuous organism. Second, a psychological-continuity theorist in the Parfit tradition—who allows branching—must conclude that both R-alpha and R-beta are S, but in unequal degrees; identify which is the closer continuer and justify this by appealing to first-person ownership of memories rather than to raw data volume. Third, introduce a unitary-narrative condition—survival requires the capacity to maintain a single, non-contradictory first-person autobiography without distributed scaffolding—and show that on this view S survives precisely during the 72-hour bridge period as a distributed subject, but once the link is severed S ceases to exist entirely; neither post-severance individual is S.

Then perform the check: for each theory, state the belief or survival claim, the method or criterion applied, the relevant nearby worlds (for example, what if the bridge lasted indefinitely, or what if R-alpha lacked the ownership deficit?), and why the condition delivers its verdict. Identify the structural feature that forces the unitary-narrative view to diverge from psychological continuity—namely, the combination of temporary distributed substrate with the ownership asymmetry. Include an honest defense note: explain how a psychological-continuity theorist would attack the unitary-narrative verdict, likely by arguing that narrative unity is an epistemic or practical desideratum rather than a metaphysical condition on survival, and that R-beta possesses sufficient continuity to ground survival regardless of narrative fragmentation. Finally, say what happens if the 72-hour bridge is removed: the case should collapse back into a standard branching teletransporter problem, and the unitary-narrative view should be forced either to select one replica or to accept branching, thereby losing its distinctive result.
