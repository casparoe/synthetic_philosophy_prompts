I am a high school student pursuing an independent study in metaphysics, and I have been trying to understand why philosophers still debate whether the universe requires an explanation for its existence. In my reading I have encountered the cosmological argument in two familiar forms: the kalam version, which holds that whatever begins to exist has a cause and that an actual infinite temporal regress is impossible, and the contingency version, which asks why contingent things exist at all and answers by positing a necessary being that explains them without itself requiring explanation. Most of the summaries I find treat these as entirely separate traditions, but recently I encountered a hybrid framing that separates the question of whether the universe had a temporal beginning from the question of whether it depends for its very being on something outside itself at every moment. This framing specifically asks whether a universe that has existed for an infinite past could still be metaphysically contingent, and if so, whether it still demands an explanation of why there is something rather than nothing.

Given this precise distinction, I would like to know how you think Thomas Aquinas would have responded to these modern presentations. Would he have regarded the kalam argument's emphasis on a temporal origin as philosophically secondary, since he maintained that creation is not an event in time but a continuous relation of ontological dependence? And confronted with the contingency version's claim that an infinite regress of contingent explanations is impossible, would he have recognized it as essentially his own Third Way, or would he have objected that modern proponents illegitimately treat existence as a kind of property that needs explaining, rather than seeing existence itself as the act by which a thing is actualized? I am not asking for a summary of Aquinas's biography or a list of his works; rather, I want a reconstructed philosophical response to these particular modern formulations, especially regarding whether he would accept that an eternal universe could nevertheless be genuinely contingent and therefore in need of a sustaining cause.
