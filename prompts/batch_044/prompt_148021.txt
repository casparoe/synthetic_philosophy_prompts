I spent last weekend in a long argument with a friend who insisted that every case of organizational escalation—continuing a failing procurement, extending a broken contract, funding another tranche of a stalled project—is just Shubik’s dollar auction replayed at scale: once resources are committed, withdrawal becomes more expensive than continuing, so rational agents keep bidding past their true valuation. I think that collapses too quickly, because it treats all prior costs as sunk in the same structural way and ignores whether the environment has a second-place penalty—reputation damage, broken coalition commitments, forfeited option rights, or shadow competitors—such that leaving costs something even if you never “win.” I advise boards and public-agency governance committees, under tight deadlines and with signatures required, on whether to authorize another funding round or terminate. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop/no conditions, and marked handoff points where the issue is organizational identity or face-saving rather than decision-theoretic fact. The procedure should distinguish a true dollar-auction trap, in which withdrawal bears unrecoverable collateral costs regardless of eventual success and there are multi-party reputation stakes, from ordinary sunk-cost confusion or from rational strategic continuation that buys option value or resolves uncertainty. It should include an “exit premium” calculation comparing the full cost of leaving against the expected value of continuing, but only after filtering out costs that are genuinely sunk and non-transferable, and it should flag where rational escalation is being misclassified as pathology. Apply it to two cases: first, a municipal transit authority that has spent sixty percent of its rail-car budget with a vendor now delivering defective units, where termination triggers a two-year litigation freeze and loss of credibility with the state funding authority; second, a research consortium deciding whether to extend a clinical trial that has shown null interim results, where direct extension costs are low but partner trust and publication rights depend on formal completion. Include one case where the procedure recommends termination that the governing body will strongly resist, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot adjudicate—particularly when identity-preservation or coalition maintenance is the real driver and should be named rather than disguised as cost-benefit reasoning.
