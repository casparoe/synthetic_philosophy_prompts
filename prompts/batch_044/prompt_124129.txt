I am a sixth-form student working through the philosophy of logic, and my tutor has guided us through a series of proposed answers to the problem raised in Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise — namely, whether a rule of deductive inference such as modus ponens can be justified without already presupposing it. Over the term she has delivered clear verdicts on six distinct proposals. First, she accepted a model-theoretic argument that modus ponens is justified because it preserves truth in every interpretation. Second, she ruled that the Tortoise’s strategy of demanding that Achilles supply the inference rule as an extra premise shows precisely that deduction cannot be justified by premises alone, and she rejected that strategy as a successful solution. Third, she accepted a syntactic derivation that constructs the rule using only symbol-substitution and concatenation within a formal system that does not already contain logical inference. Fourth, she rejected a pragmatic argument that rational agents must accept deduction in order to avoid practical paralysis. Fifth, she rejected an evolutionary argument that logical cognition was selected for its survival value. Sixth, she rejected a circular argument that invokes the law of non-contradiction in order to defend modus ponens.

The new case I wish to put to you is a seventh proposal I have drafted for an essay, which I call the hermeneutic-closure argument. It holds that anyone who genuinely understands the premises offered in a deductive argument already implicitly grasps the relevant rule of inference, because understanding those premises requires interpreting them as standing in logical relations to one another; consequently, to deny the rule is to undermine the very comprehension needed to evaluate that denial. The argument concedes that this involves a kind of circularity, but insists that it is a virtuous hermeneutic circle — a condition of interpretive understanding — rather than a vicious logical one.

If my tutor wishes to remain consistent with her six earlier rulings, should she accept or reject this hermeneutic-closure argument? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the following: identify at least two plausible principles or rules that fit all six of her earlier verdicts equally well; state what each principle predicts about the hermeneutic-closure case; offer your best guess at her actual verdict, with reasons; and specify one further hypothetical case that would force a choice between those two principles. In addition, please separate clearly what is broadly settled in the philosophical literature on Carroll’s problem from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation as though it were the consensus view.
