I am preparing an interview for a philosophy-of-science podcast focused on quantitative genetics and its conceptual limits. My guest is an unnamed defender — call her Dr. K — of a rigorous interpretation of heritability: she holds that narrow-sense heritability (h²) is not a trait-level property but a population-relative partition of phenotypic variance that is constitutively dependent on the environmental range present in the study population, and she derives from this a constraint she terms the "intervention-variance mismatch." On her view, a heritability coefficient carries no predictive authority for the effect of a targeted environmental intervention unless that intervention replicates the precise variance structure of the environment in which the coefficient was originally calculated; consequently, she argues that heritability estimates are explanatory in a restricted, within-population sense but are systematically misused when invoked to argue either for genetic determinism or for broad policy predictions about malleability.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic framing — no questions about biography, motivation, or "why the public should care" — and instead press on the internal structure of this position. Specifically, include at least one question on why high heritability does not entail immutability or genetic determinism; one on the intervention-variance mismatch itself, including how it differs from the familiar criticism that heritability estimates do not license between-group inferences; one on whether broad-sense heritability (H²) escapes or inherits the same constraint; and one on what positive explanatory work heritability coefficients can perform if they are stripped of both individual-level predictive power and general intervention-guiding authority.

For each question, provide a brief sketch of the answer Dr. K is most likely to give, followed by a sharp follow-up designed specifically to exploit the weakness or tension in that reply. The questions should anticipate standard moves in the literature — such as appeals to findings that heritability of cognitive traits varies with socioeconomic status, or to the within-group versus between-group distinction — and should push past restatement of those moves rather than allowing her to deploy them as conversation-stoppers.

Finally, please separate what is settled within quantitative genetics from what remains genuinely contested philosophical interpretation regarding heritability's predictive and explanatory scope, and do not present Dr. K's intervention-variance mismatch thesis as if it represented the consensus in either field.
