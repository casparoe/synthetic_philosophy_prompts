I'm on the university's Campus Space and Institutional Memory board — got appointed like two months ago mostly because I teach in the history dept and they needed a warm body, but now I'm actually stressed about Thursday's vote. We've got a proposal to tear down the old wrought-iron fence cutting through the east quad; it's been there since 1972 and the current admin has no idea why it was installed. The planning office wants it gone for accesibility reasons — there's data showing it forces students using mobility aids onto a route that's almost twice as long — but two board members plus some loud alumni are pushing back with that Chesterton fence idea and also Burke's notion of prescription, arguing that long-standing boundaries carry authority just from having survived and we shouldn't demolish something just because its purpose isn't obvious to us anymore.

Here's the twist I just dug up in the archives: the fence wasn't security or decorative. It was part of a failed 1972 federal grant experiment in "behavioral architecture" — they literally built it to study whether physical barriers would cause spontaneous social clustering and self-segregation among students. The study ended, the grant disappeared, but the fence stayed. So now the preservationist argument is kind of weird: yes, it has a hidden purpose, but that purpose was ethically sketchy pseudo-science. The other side says that makes removal even more urgent. I need you to draft our board's decision memo — approve removal, reject, or approve with conditions — recommending one concrete course of action with a clear timeline. It has to take the Burkean/Chesterton objection seriously rather than just waving it away, but also deal with the fact that the "unknown reason" turned out to be something we'd probably want to reject on principle. Don't just list philosophers for us to read; we need to actually decide, and the memo should include an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side so the board can respond to it properly.
