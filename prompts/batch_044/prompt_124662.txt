I'm working through what rational commitment actually requires, and I'm testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than just accepting the standard formulation. Here are my verdicts.

First, in the classic toxin scenario: a billionaire will deposit one million dollars into my account tomorrow at noon if, and only if, I genuinely intend at that moment to drink a toxin that will make me severely ill for exactly twenty-four hours. Once the deposit clears, I am under no obligation to drink. My gut says it is rational to form the intention and collect the money, and then rational not to drink. The intention and the action come apart cleanly.

Second, the same offer but with actual execution required: the money is released only after I swallow the toxin. Here I think it is irrational to take the deal, unless I am truly desperate, because a day of severe illness is not worth a million dollars to me.

Third, a pre-commitment device: I sign a contract with an app that will automatically send five hundred dollars to a cause I despise if I fail to submit a school application by Friday at midnight. On Thursday I genuinely intend to submit. By Friday evening I feel exhausted and miss the deadline; the donation goes through. I think it was smart to set up the contract, but I do not feel I acted irrationally by missing the deadline—only that I was weak-willed.

Fourth, a professional commitment: I am a surgeon who has promised a patient that if her vitals drop during surgery, I will perform a risky procedure that could save her life but carries a small chance of permanent paralysis. During the operation her vitals drop, but in that moment I feel an intense, almost physical reluctance to take the risk. I think I should perform the procedure anyway, and that my initial commitment binds me despite the change in my feelings.

Fifth, a conditional interpersonal promise: I tell a close friend that if she gives up smoking, I will give up my own harmful habit in solidarity. She quits. I do not. I feel that I have broken something, yet I also think my promise was only genuinely binding if the mutual reason—solidarity through shared struggle—still carried force for me, and now that she has already succeeded, that reason feels hollow.

The case I keep reversing myself on involves a self-binding letter. On Sunday evening I write to my future self: if you have not finished your essay by next Sunday, you must destroy your favorite book. By the following Sunday I am tired, and I decide not to destroy the book, even though I remember genuinely intending to enforce the rule. Was it rational to intend the punishment a week earlier? And was ignoring it on Sunday a failure of rationality, or just a reasonable change of mind?

What principle, if any, unifies these verdicts? Specifically, I want to know whether rational commitment depends on the stability of the underlying reason, on the presence of an external enforcement mechanism, or on something like the distinction between strategic planning and moral obligation. Which of my verdicts—especially the surgeon case versus the friendship case, or the letter-to-self case—does each candidate principle preserve, and which does it force me to abandon? And what does the best principle predict about a variant I have not considered: a toxin-style contract in which I pay a trusted third party to administer the toxin if I fail to complete a task, but the third party forgets and never administers it?
