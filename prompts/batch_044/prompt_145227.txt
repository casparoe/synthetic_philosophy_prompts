As a novelist currently drafting a narrative in which a consciousness researcher serves as a central interlocutor, I have been invited to moderate a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed philosopher—let us call her the proponent of thresholded integrationism—who defends a refined global-workspace account. On her view, phenomenal consciousness requires only intra-modal binding within a sensory module, whereas access consciousness demands cross-modal broadcast that exceeds a dynamic coherence threshold; she applies this distinction to split-brain and inattentional-blindness cases, maintaining that phenomenal experience can persist without global access provided local integration remains intact. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that avoid asking her to restate the framework and instead press precisely where the literature presses.

Specifically, I need at least one question probing how she distinguishes her position from Block's classic overflow argument without collapsing into a redescription of it, paired with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that local binding accounts for excess phenomenal content without invoking global workspace failure. I also want a question challenging whether her coherence threshold is calibrated in an ad hoc manner, especially given subliminal priming conditions in which intra-modal binding may occur yet introspective report is impossible; the follow-up should anticipate her defense via threshold calibration through neural coherence metrics. A third question should ask whether her model allows phenomenal consciousness in isolated cortical columns that lack thalamic loops, with a follow-up addressing her probable appeal to minimal integration. Finally, I want a closing question asking what empirical result—short of direct first-person report—could in principle disconfirm her claim that intra-modal binding is sufficient for phenomenology, framed so that she cannot dismiss it as merely a research-program objection rather than a genuine falsifier. The tone should be rigorous yet accessible to an interdisciplinary audience of philosophers and cognitive scientists.
