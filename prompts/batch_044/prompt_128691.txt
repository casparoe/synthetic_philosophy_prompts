I am convening an upper-level seminar in philosophical epistemology—twelve postgraduate students, one ninety-minute session—on the problem of peer disagreement. Rather than asking for a standard comparison of conciliationism and steadfastness, I want you to design an in-class simulation that forces participants to experience what I shall call the calibration cascade: the situation in which two epistemic peers disagree about a substantive claim, apply conciliationist pressure to revise their first-order beliefs, and then discover that the very act of revision threatens their mutual assessment of peerhood, which in turn demands further revision. The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives of the game, not from any preliminary lecture.

Please design a structured deliberation with real, if small, stakes. Divide the group into pairs and provide each student with a private information packet about a single normative case study—perhaps whether a particular form of algorithmic decision-making in public administration violates procedural fairness—constructed so that the packets contain genuinely overlapping but differently weighted considerations. Each student must record an initial confidence level and rate their partner's likely competence before discussion. During a timed deliberation round, pairs must either converge on a shared confidence interval (conciliation) or declare a principled stand (steadfastness), but with a mandatory second-order move: each must re-rate their partner's epistemic peer-status in light of the disagreement. The rules should make it costly—through a point system affecting which case study the group examines in the final thirty minutes—to lower peer-status without justification, yet equally costly to maintain peer-status while refusing all belief revision. Include explicit rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, materials needed, and a debrief that maps each stage onto the formal structure of conciliationism, steadfastness, and the uniqueness thesis, explaining what will predictably go wrong (for example, students treating peer-status revision as an ad hominem attack rather than an epistemic adjustment) and which philosophical feature—likely the stability of long-term belief networks—this simulation necessarily fails to reproduce.

The response should be written in British English, should avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or named secondary literature, and should not depend on highly specialised empirical data that could not be reconstructed from general philosophical knowledge.
