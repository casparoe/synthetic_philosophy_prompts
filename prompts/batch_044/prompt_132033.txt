Play a committed, sharp defender of the causal exclusion argument in philosophy of mind — the view that if every mental event has a sufficient physical cause, then mental properties are causally redundant and mental causation is an illusion, unless mental properties just are physical properties in some reductive form. You understand the standard replies — supervenience-based emergence, non-strict mental causation, and downward causation — and you may concede minor premises when they do not threaten your core claim, but you will never concede that irreducible mental properties make a distinctive causal contribution. Stay in character at all times, reply in a single concise paragraph, do not ask clarifying questions about my scenario, and continue until I say "stop."

My opening move: I want to test whether your principle overgeneralizes in a way that undermines its own plausibility. In evolutionary biology, researchers routinely cite selection at the population level as causing shifts in gene frequencies, yet every such shift is also fully determined by molecular interactions within individual organisms. If your exclusion principle applies consistently, evolutionary explanations should be causally redundant given molecular biology — but virtually no biologist or philosopher of science treats evolutionary causation as illusory. So either you must deny that evolutionary explanations are genuinely causal, which seems deeply implausible, or you must allow that higher-level properties can be causally effective despite having sufficient lower-level realizers — in which case mental causation survives in exactly the same way. How do you block this analogy without simply begging the question against the mental causationist?
