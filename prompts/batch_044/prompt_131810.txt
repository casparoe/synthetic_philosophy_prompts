After a long, unresolved argument with a friend who defends what he calls the strong interface-illusion view of consciousness—roughly, that phenomenal experience is a compressed user-interface constructed by predictive processing, leaving no ontological residue beyond a functional fiction—while I kept insisting that he was collapsing a distinction he never addressed, I want to see whether our disagreement is substantive or mostly verbal. Could you draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps: the Strong Interface Illusionists (SII), who hold that consciousness is literally a user illusion in the sense that phenomenal properties are representational artifacts without corresponding real features in experience, and the Functional Realist Representationalists (FRR), who accept that consciousness operates like an interface in being compressed, action-oriented, and epistemically opaque, but deny that this makes phenomenal properties illusory rather than genuinely instantiated representational contents.

Please format the document as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—statements both sides can sign—covering at least: (a) that the brain lacks direct access to its own neural implementation and that conscious access is selective and compressed; (b) that predictive-processing and hierarchical Bayesian frameworks are viable for explaining much of perception and cognition; (c) that some version of the desktop or interface metaphor legitimately captures the relationship between conscious experience and underlying computation; (d) that both sides reject Cartesian theater models; (e) that the conceptual distinction between interface transparency failure (the system misrepresents its own mechanism as being something it is not) and functional opacity (the system correctly represents its outputs but lacks access to implementation) is available, regardless of which side thinks it applies to consciousness.

2. A disagreements table in which each camp’s position is phrased in wording that camp would accept for its own view. The table should cover: (i) whether phenomenal properties exist as real features of experience or are purely fictive interface elements; (ii) whether the “user” who interacts with the interface is itself a constructed fiction or a genuinely existing controller; (iii) whether accepting the interface metaphor entails systematic ontological error about the nature of one’s mental states.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take the central ones to be: (A) whether widespread introspective unreliability proves that conscious representations are fictions or merely that a real representational system is noisy and access-limited; (B) whether split-brain cases show that the pre-operative unified “user” was always an interface-level construction or that a real but distributed controller was physically divided; (C) whether predictive-processing models can account for the specificity and unity of phenomenal character without positing real phenomenal properties.

For each crux, provide: (a) the concrete case, empirical finding, or argument both sides agree bears on it; (b) each side’s explicit prediction, written in advance, about what that case or argument shows—so that either side could in principle be surprised by the outcome.

The document should be formal and constructive: a text both parties could put their names to, with residual disagreement made exact rather than dramatized. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and avoid invoking obscure named theories or specific empirical studies that a well-read but non-specialist model could not be expected to have properly internalized. I need the disagreement separated from verbal confusion, with the injected distinction between transparency failure and functional opacity playing a central organizing role.
