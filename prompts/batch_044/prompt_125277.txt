I am a retired experimental neurophysiologist who has recently turned to philosophy of action. Next Thursday I present to an interdisciplinary seminar comprising two analytic philosophers of action (one with libertarian sympathies, one a committed compatibilist familiar with Frankfurt and O’Connor), a cognitive neuroscientist whose work centres on predictive processing, a historian of science deeply sceptical of neuro-reductionism, and a graduate student in jurisprudence concerned with criminal responsibility. My thesis is that the classical Libet experiments do not show conscious will to be epiphenomenal; rather, they reveal voluntary action as a stochastic resonance process in which pre-conscious neural dynamics generate a distribution of possible motor outputs, and conscious awareness operates within a narrow temporal window—approximately 150 to 250 milliseconds before movement—not as an initiating trigger but as a graded, probabilistic filter that shifts the likelihood landscape of those outputs. I term this the “probabilistic veto”, and I argue that moral responsibility ought to be reconceived not as sourcehood of initiation but as the agent’s demonstrable capacity for such modulation. I will concede up front that I have no direct empirical measurement of noise-parameter shifts in human RP amplitude during conscious attention, that “stochastic resonance” is borrowed from nonlinear physics and requires analogical justification, and that my account does not resolve the sourcehood problem to the libertarian’s satisfaction.
