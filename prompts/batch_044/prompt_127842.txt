After a long argument with a friend about whether historians genuinely know that specific distant-past events occurred—for instance, that a particular battle took place on a specific plain—or whether they merely assemble the most coherent narrative available from fragmented traces, I want you to formalize our disagreement as an adversarial-collaboration document. The two parties are Critical Reconstructionists and Testimonial Discontinuists.

Define the Reconstructionist view precisely: modern source criticism, combining internal consistency checks, transmission-error modeling, and archaeological correlation, can justify specific factual beliefs about events lying beyond the testimonial horizon—the conceptual boundary beyond which no chain of direct or near-direct living testimony extends. Define the Discontinuist view precisely: beyond that horizon we possess only coherence relations among material traces; no amount of internal consistency or correlation can bridge the qualitative epistemic gap to justified belief about a specific distant event, though it can yield well-ordered narratives.

Both sides should sign a numbered section of agreed claims about what Herodotus’s inquiry (historiē) and Thucydides’s method (privileging autopsy, precise chronology, and the exclusion of myth) actually achieve; about the current practices of source criticism; and about the testimonial horizon itself. They should then record their residual disagreements in a table, with each formulation phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording—especially on whether trace-coherence-plus-correlation across the horizon amounts to knowledge.

Then identify the cruxes: at minimum (a) whether independent trace-coherence can substitute for testimonial inheritance, and (b) whether material or archaeological evidence can function as a direct evidential bridge rather than merely corroborating narrative coherence. For each crux, provide a concrete historical or hypothetical case that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side’s prediction in advance. Finally, state explicitly whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Keep the prompt focused, do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and frame the issue through the inheritance-reconstruction distinction rather than through obscure specialized historiography. Use the format: numbered agreed-claims section, followed by the disagreements table, then the cruxes with cases and predictions, then the verbal-substantive assessment.
