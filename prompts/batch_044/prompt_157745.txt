I am preparing the closing piece for a graduate seminar on non-classical logics, and I would like you to compose a poem—exactly sixteen lines, arranged in four quatrains, with a refrain that closes each quatrain but alters precisely one word with each repetition—that takes as its subject the philosophical core of intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism. Rather than treating the rejection of the law of excluded middle as mere skepticism, the poem should present it as a positive discipline: for the intuitionist, to assert the existence of an object requires an explicit construction or an effective method for producing it, and a proposition may stand in an irreducible state in which neither it nor its negation has been established. To ground the abstraction in a precise mathematical instance without turning explanatory, embed a reference to the fact that there is no general algorithm that decides whether an arbitrary Diophantine equation has a solution—a result familiar to anyone who has followed Hilbert's tenth problem—and treat this not as a limitation to be lamented but as a domain where the classical disjunction "either there is a solution or there is none" is precisely what the intuitionist refuses to assert without a witness for one side or the other.

The refrain should mirror the intuitionistic handling of double negation: its first occurrence should read not false is not yet true, the second should shift one word to read not false is closer, not true, the third should become not false, still not true, and the fourth should break the grammatical pattern altogether, leaving the tension open rather than synthesizing it. The mutation of the refrain across the quatrains should do structural work analogous to the BHK interpretation's demand that evidence accumulate step by step without ever licensing the classical leap from not-not-P to P. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mathematics recognizes the distinction between constructive and classical existence—perhaps echoing the spirit of Brouwer's program and Heyting's formalization without naming them didactically—yet it should stand as an independent poem for a reader who has never encountered intuitionism. Do not resolve the argument in the final lines; let the form itself, particularly the broken refrain, embody the unresolved stance.
