I'm trying to determine whether my responses to sorrowful art form a coherent pattern, and what principle might lie behind them. Watching a stage production of Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, I feel exhilarated by the tragedy: this is clearly valuable. Listening to a mournful folk song about lost love that resonates with my own past also feels good, though more privately so. Watching a documentary that presents unvarnished footage of actual famine, however, I avoid; the sadness there is not enjoyable at all. Watching a fictional horror film with graphic suffering gives me tension and excitement, but it does not feel like the same kind of sadness. Contemplating a bleak landscape painting that depicts no narrative whatsoever, I find it beautiful and melancholic together, without any discomfort. Yet a literary novel based closely on a recent real disaster shifts my verdict: sometimes I find it moving and worthwhile, sometimes it feels like exploitation, and I keep going back and forth. I wonder whether the key is something like counterfactual emotional insulation — the idea that enjoyment requires my emotional response to be directed at a represented world I take to be framed or counterfactual, rather than at the actual causal origins of what I am perceiving. Does any principle along these lines capture all my verdicts? What does it predict for an immersive virtual-reality narrative in which I must actively make choices that cause a virtual character's grief, rendered with full emotional realism? And if a coherent principle cannot accommodate my shifting reaction to the disaster novel, which of my verdicts ought I to overturn?
