I sit on the city's Interdisciplinary Policy Review Board, and by next Thursday we must vote on whether to adopt the MetroFlow predictive model for allocating public-transit subsidies. The model treats commuters as non-reflective, perfectly informed utility-maximizers with no social preferences—assumptions that are demonstrably false—yet it predicts ridership patterns with 94% accuracy. Its designers invoke Milton Friedman's well-known "F-twist": in predictive science, unrealistic assumptions are harmless if the predictions hold, so explanatory realism is a luxury that policy can abandon for efficiency. Our governing charter, however, requires that any allocation mechanism be "justifiable to affected citizens in terms they can recognize as reasons." A minority of the board argues that adopting MetroFlow implicitly commits us to "explanatory nihilism" about citizens—treating them as black-box behavioral inputs rather than reason-responsive agents—and therefore violates the charter regardless of predictive success; the majority replies that the charter demands only procedural transparency, not metaphysical accuracy about human psychology. Draft the board's formal decision memo recommending adoption, rejection, or adoption with conditions, and include a concrete review time frame (for example, a six-month evaluation). The memo should take Friedman's argument seriously, examine whether predictive success can substitute for explanatory or normative justifiability in democratic policy, and present the strongest consideration for the position you ultimately reject. Keep it to about 400 words—roughly one page.
