Question: Consider the standard distinction between the trolley switch case and the footbridge case under the doctrine of double effect (DDE). Critics argue that in the switch case the death of the one is causally necessary for saving the five—just as in the footbridge case—so defenders are wrong to claim that only in the footbridge case does the agent intend the death as a means. Defenders reply that in the switch case the agent redirects an existing threat, making the death a foreseen side-effect rather than a causal means. Assess this defense, paying particular attention to whether it survives when the mechanism is not physical but institutional: a hospital’s automated triage protocol, activated by an administrator, that withdraws life-sustaining support from one patient so that the resources can sustain five others. Here the administrator initiates the protocol knowing the one will be harmed, yet the harm results from withdrawal rather than from using the patient’s body or death as the mechanism that delivers resources to the five. Does DDE treat this institutional case like the switch or like the footbridge? Justify your answer by explicit reference to the four conditions of DDE, and address any ambiguity in applying the “means” condition to distributed or mediated causation.

Student answer: The doctrine of double effect, first articulated by Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae specifically to resolve collective-harm dilemmas such as trolley problems, permits an act with a bad effect only if (1) the act is good or neutral, (2) the bad effect is not intended, (3) the bad effect is not the means to the good effect, and (4) the good is proportionate to the bad. In the switch case, pulling the lever redirects the trolley; the death is a side-effect of that redirection, so condition (3) is satisfied. In the footbridge case, pushing the large man uses his body as a means to stop the trolley, violating (3). In the institutional protocol case, the administrator activates the algorithm; the death results from withdrawal of support, not from using the patient’s body or death to save the others. Because the harm is mediated by an institutional mechanism rather than direct physical causation, condition (3) is automatically satisfied—this is clearly like the switch case, not the footbridge case. The administrator does not intend the death, and the protocol’s design ensures the harm is merely foreseen. Thus DDE permits activation, provided the proportionality condition holds, which it does since five lives outweigh one.
