Consider the following examination question and the response submitted by a student in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry.

Question: Ian Hacking describes psychiatric classifications as potential interactive kinds that exhibit looping effects: being classified can alter the self-understanding and behavior of the people classified, which in turn can alter the classification itself. Focusing on dissociative identity disorder (DID, formerly multiple personality disorder) in North American clinical practice during the 1980s and early 1990s, explain what a looping effect is in this specific context and distinguish it from the claim that DID is merely a socially invented fiction with no clinical reality. Then address a further conceptual distinction: some philosophers of science distinguish between institutional feedback loops, in which formal diagnostic criteria or epidemiological tracking are revised in response to aggregated clinical reports, and interpretive feedback loops, in which individual patients privately reshape their experiences after encountering the classification through therapy or media. Applying this distinction, analyze whether the proliferation of DID cases during this period is best understood through institutional revision, private interpretive reshaping, or a combination of both. In your answer, clarify why Hacking regards psychiatric kinds as interactive rather than indifferent, without claiming that interactivity necessarily undermines the reality of the suffering involved.

Student response:
Hacking's looping effect means that when clinicians apply a diagnosis like DID, the patients begin to manifest the symptoms listed in the manual, so the classification literally creates the disorder. This is different from saying DID is a fiction, because the patients genuinely suffer; the point is just that the suffering takes the shape dictated by the diagnostic category. Hacking calls psychiatric kinds interactive kinds, unlike indifferent kinds such as chemical elements, because people can learn about the category and change themselves accordingly. During the 1980s, the spread of DID was driven mainly by institutional feedback: the DSM criteria for multiple personality disorder were repeatedly expanded based on reports from clinicians, which allowed more patients to qualify. At the same time, patients in therapy encountered descriptions of alters and traumatic memories, leading them to reinterpret their own experiences in line with the diagnosis. So both loops were present. Because psychiatric kinds loop in this way, Hacking concludes they are inherently unstable and should not be used in epidemiological studies that assume fixed natural kinds.
