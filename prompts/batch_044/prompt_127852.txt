I am preparing an essay on Hofstadter's conception of the self as a strange loop, in particular his idea that the symbol 'I' is self-referential and loops back upon the brain's physical patterns. Rather than rehearsing the familiar internalist reading, I want to propose a more specific mechanism, which I term a 'reflexive attractor': the notion that the 'I'-symbol does not merely point to a cognitive pattern but actively stabilises it by drawing heterogeneous neural and representational processes into a unified, illusory centre of gravity. Against this, I wish to introduce 'distributive self-modeling', a framing in which the strange loop is not wholly intra-cranial but is partly constituted by external social and linguistic practices that sustain the loop across brains. My central question is whether this distributive framing genuinely conflicts with Hofstadter's internalism, or whether it merely externalises the loop's conditions without breaking its self-referential structure. I am particularly concerned that 'reflexive attractor' may either smuggle in dynamical-systems commitments that Hofstadter's symbolic account rejects, or conversely, that it merely renames processes he already describes.

Please comment on these notes. Is the distinction between the reflexive attractor and distributive self-modeling philosophically coherent? Does the latter misrepresent Hofstadter's position, or is it a legitimate extension? Where does the proposed argument seem strongest, and where is it most vulnerable?
