I'm a doctoral student in early modern history who just finished curating a small local-archive exhibit on a seventeenth-century village dispute that never appears in state records, and my advisor—who writes institutional histories—dismissed the case as merely anecdotal. Our disagreement made me realize that I cannot actually make someone feel, in their bones, why microhistorical evidence is not just a smaller version of macrohistory but operates with a different logic of proof, one tied to what I am calling the evidentiary inversion: the idea that a fragmentary source gains inferential weight not despite its damage and isolation but precisely through the survival conditions that made it survive at all. I am running a seventy-five-minute workshop next month for fourteen graduate students in mixed specialties—some trained in state-archive methods, some in social history—and I need you to design a participatory activity, not a lecture, in which the group must reach a collective historical judgment using two competing evidentiary regimes. One regime should consist of aggregate census data, tax rolls, and official correspondence representing the state scale; the other should consist of a single incomplete personal letter, a damaged parish register entry, and an object with no provenance record representing the micro scale. Arrange the rules, roles, and incentives so that participants initially trust the aggregate data, then are structurally forced to treat the fragmentary evidence as carrying a genuinely different inferential status—changing what counts as an explanation rather than just adding emotional color—before anyone names microhistory or history from below. Include specific roles with opposing obligations, a decision mechanism that creates real social stakes without using money (for instance, collective reputation points tied to a forced publication verdict), and a clear timeline that fits the session. The debrief should map each critical moment in the game onto the scale translation problem—the point where reducing the unit of analysis transforms the category of explanation—predict what will go wrong the first time (I expect the state-archive defenders will try to absorb the fragments back into the aggregate narrative), and be explicit about which feature of actual microhistorical practice the activity cannot reproduce, such as the long temporal immersion required to read against the grain of a document. Take a definite, committed position throughout: design every rule and every debrief point around the evidentiary inversion I described, giving your reasons for that commitment, and do not lay out competing interpretations of microhistory for me to choose between. End with an honest note on where your design takes liberties with standard historiographical practice, particularly regarding the epistemic status you assign to objects that lack provenance.
