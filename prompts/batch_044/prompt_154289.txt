I am currently drafting a novel in which one of the protagonists is an artificial reasoning system that, like Carroll's tortoise, refuses to apply any inferential step unless an explicit meta-premise has first licensed it — thereby generating an infinite regress of justification. To ground this motif with philosophical precision, I have returned to Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles', particularly the sequence in which Achilles records premises (A) and (B), then adds (C) "If A and B are true, Z must be true", followed by (D) "If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true", and finally (E) "If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true", only for the Tortoise to demand yet another hypothetical. Some interpreters read this as a pure illustration of the epistemic problem of justifying deduction: without an infinite hierarchy of rules, we can never compel acceptance of a conclusion. Others treat it as an anticipation of later formal distinctions between object-language and meta-language, or even as a playful foreshadowing of Gödelian self-reference. I am unsure which of these frames captures Carroll's actual staging, and I want to avoid misrepresenting the argument in my fiction.

Could you help me understand, in conceptual terms, exactly what problem Carroll is presenting through the Tortoise's resistance? Specifically, is the regress best understood as a difficulty in justifying logical rules themselves, as a gap between using a rule and stating it, or as something closer to a paradox of self-trust? In answering, please distinguish what is broadly settled about Carroll's dialogue — for instance, that it targets the idea that logical consequence can be reduced to an explicit premise — from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of logic, such as whether Carroll intended any anticipation of twentieth-century formal results. I am not seeking a reading list or scholarly citations, but a clear conceptual map. Finally, please indicate wherever your explanation relies on a contested interpretation rather than a consensus reading, so that I do not present a speculative view as established fact in the novel.
