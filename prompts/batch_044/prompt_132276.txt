I serve on my university's Interdisciplinary Curriculum Oversight Board. The Department of Cognitive Science has proposed a required graduate seminar titled The Phenomenal Boundary Problem, constructed around Daniel Dennett's Chase-and-Sanborn intuition pump from Quining Qualia. The syllabus is divided into two parallel tracks taught concurrently. Track A interprets the divergence—Chase reports that his taste has changed and that he has become a more sophisticated coffee drinker, while Sanborn reports that Maxwell House coffee no longer tastes to him the way it once did—as evidence that phenomenal reports are irreducibly ambiguous, supporting the thesis that qualia is an incoherent posit. Track B interprets the very same divergence as tracking real but privately calibrated phenomenal differences, arguing that denying qualia collapses an epistemic limitation into an ontological absence. The department requests board approval of the dual-track design, with students required to submit a final adjudication essay defending one interpretation or offering a third alternative.

The Dean of Arts has filed a formal objection. She contends that both tracks share a hidden premise—namely, that there is a determinate fact of the matter about whether Chase's experience changed or remained constant—which a rigorous eliminativist position rejects altogether. In her view, framing the seminar as a binary choice between incoherent posit and privately calibrated reality imposes a false dichotomy and effectively excludes the eliminativist option from the curriculum. She therefore proposes a revised single-track design in which the Chase/Sanborn case is presented solely as a historical artifact of late-twentieth-century philosophy of mind, with students analyzing its rhetorical structure and conceptual presuppositions but not being asked to adopt any substantive metaphysical stance.

The board must decide at its meeting next month among three options: (1) approve the dual-track proposal as submitted; (2) approve the Dean's single-track revision; or (3) reject both and require the department to redesign the seminar so that it neither presupposes the false dichotomy the Dean identifies nor mandates metaphysical commitment from students. Please draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend one of these three courses with concrete conditions and an implementation or redesign timeline (e.g., approval for the fall term or a revised proposal due within six weeks); it should engage seriously with the Dean's false-dichotomy objection, including her claim that both tracks smuggle in a shared premise about determinacy; it should address whether a course centered on the question of whether qualia is an incoherent posit can be taught responsibly without requiring students to commit to a metaphysical stance; and it should include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest argument for the option you do not recommend. The memo should be written so that both the Dean and the department chair can respond to it point by point.
