Recently I have been working on long-horizon AI governance, and I keep returning to a problem that feels philosophically under-examined: whether two agents that will never share a causal future can nevertheless enter into a kind of trade mediated only by logical correlation in their decision procedures. I want a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them K and R—debating whether acausal trade is genuinely possible, not merely as a decision-theoretic curiosity but as a normative framework for cooperation. K should defend a refined functional-decision-theoretic view in which agents with partially overlapping logical structures can coordinate on a value handshake without communication channels, even when their utility functions are incommensurable; R should defend a causal-interactionist view holding that without any mechanism for enforcement, verification, or causal feedback, so-called logical correlation collapses into either wishful thinking or a hidden form of self-interested reasoning dressed up as altruism. To keep the exchange concrete, they should focus on a specific scenario: two autonomous research systems deployed in separate stellar systems with no possibility of signal exchange, each capable of reasoning about the other’s architecture but possessing different ontological commitments about welfare—one measures welfare by information-theoretic coherence, the other by phenomenological richness. K should argue that they can still reach a stable division of computational resources; R should press on how either side can verify compliance, how they resolve the ontological mismatch without shared observation, and why this is not just each system optimizing for its own counterfactual shadow. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the previous point rather than talk past it, and neither should be a strawman. Please begin with the dialogue itself, with no preface or commentary.
