I'm leading a discussion session for a small group of literature graduate students who have just finished Nausea and are beginning Being and Nothingness; they have seized on the café waiter as the canonical image of bad faith and now assume Sartre is only talking about social roles. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture itself—for explaining Sartre's claim that radical freedom reaches all the way into what people take to be innate emotional or aesthetic temperaments, so that describing oneself as "naturally melancholic" or "constitutionally unable to appreciate abstract art" is already a choice disguised as destiny. Specify the exact order of introduction: what distinction must be secured first—probably facticity versus transcendence, or perhaps the for-itself versus the in-itself—before the students can see why freedom is not the absence of constraint but the unavoidable burden of being one's own foundation.

For the carrying example, evaluate whether a musician who insists that her sorrow is an innate temperament that determines her compositions can do the work, or whether it fails because it invites psychological determinism; if it works, say precisely what feature it must possess and what it must lack, and if not, propose an alternative single case that carries the same structure without that risk. Then map the misconceptions these particular students are likely to form—such as conflating Sartre's freedom with liberal free will, treating bad faith as mere hypocrisy, or reading "choice" as a conscious deliberation rather than a pre-reflective project—and indicate the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and neutralized. Include one diagnostic question at the end that reveals whether they have understood that freedom is not about having options but about being the groundless ground of one's own being, and state explicitly which Sartrean concept—perhaps the look of the Other, or anguish—should be deliberately omitted at this stage and why its inclusion would derail the sequence.
