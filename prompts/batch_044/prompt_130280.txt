Since my recent appointment to a governmental advisory panel on representational equity, I have been reviewing our body's prior positions on quota-based mechanisms across jurisdictions, and I need to determine how we should rule consistently on an unprecedented proposal. Our record so far: we endorsed race-conscious holistic review in U.S. university admissions but drew a hard line against fixed numerical quotas for student bodies; we upheld caste-based reservations in Indian public employment and education as constitutionally required compensatory measures, yet we refused to extend analogous percentage mandates to private-sector board appointments; and we approved Norway's statutory 40 percent gender quota for corporate boards, but only on the condition that it include both sunset provisions and merit-preserving eligibility thresholds. The new proposal before us would impose mandatory representation quotas for fluent speakers of endangered indigenous languages on all public broadcasting boards, without any sunset clause and without any professional-eligibility filter beyond language competence alone. Given our precedents, should we approve or reject this proposal if we wish to remain consistent? Which of our three prior rulings is the closest analog, and if our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, what distinct principle would we be adopting with an approval versus a rejection? Finally, what hypothetical case—perhaps involving linguistic representation in private media or in a different sector—would best distinguish between the competing rules that fit our past verdicts?
