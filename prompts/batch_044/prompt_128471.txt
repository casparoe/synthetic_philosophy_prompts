I will defend the following thesis in a seminar next Thursday before a mixed audience: normative ethicists (mostly contractualists and virtue theorists), a legal philosopher specializing in immigration and territorial rights, and two cultural anthropologists whose work concerns ritual and social coordination. The thesis is that conventions governing tipping, queuing, and jaywalking are not universal moral obligations but coordinative artifacts that generate derivative duties only for agents who stand within a local expectational contract; visitors who lack that contractual history are bound not by a positive duty to comply but only by a negative duty not to destabilize the artifact. I further distinguish stabilizing artifacts, such as queuing norms that preserve cooperative expectations, from extractive artifacts, such as tipping regimes in service economies where gratuities suppress base wages, and I maintain that derivative obligations attach only to the former. Please prepare my Q&A defense: identify approximately eight objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging they are and by the discipline most likely to press them—contractualist, virtue-theoretic, anthropological, and legal—phrased in the vocabulary each group would use. For each objection, draft a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, and append an explicit note identifying the weakest point in that reply. Include the single objection I am hoping no one raises. Finally, indicate what I should concede in my opening remarks to lower the temperature of the room, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists.
