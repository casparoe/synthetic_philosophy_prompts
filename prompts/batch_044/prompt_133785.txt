I've been arguing with my roommate about promises — not just whether breaking them is wrong, but literally why they bind in the first place. Is it because the other person relied on you and you'd cause harm by letting them down? Or is it about your own autonomy, like you exercised some normative power over yourself and breaking it violates your own will? There's also the social practice view, but that's harder to squeeze into a verse form. I'm taking a philosphy course on moral obligation and honestly our discussion just circled back to these two without resolving, which feels like the point.

I want a villanelle — the 19-line form with two refrains that keep cycling and finally meet in the last quatrain. Make one refrain the reliance-based answer (something about expectation and harm to the other) and the other the autonomy-based answer (something about self-commitment or normative power). The repetition should do philosophical work: the refrains should keep turning through different angles of the same problem, missing each other in every tercet, and then sit side-by-side in the final couplet without reconciling, since that's exactly where the debate lands. Make it accurate enough that someone who's read Scanlon or Raz on promissory obligation recognizes which line is which, but it should still work as a poem if you don't know the literature. Start right with the poem itself, no preface or commentary, please.
