I am drafting an essay on an internal tension in Leo Tolstoy's aesthetic theory as developed in What Is Art? I want to advance the following line of argument. Tolstoy defines art essentially as the transmission of feeling from artist to audience through a work that infects the recipient with the artist's emotional state. At the same time, he restricts genuine art to works that transmit feelings conducive to human brotherhood and moral improvement, excluding those that transmit hatred, pride, or sensual indulgence. The tension I wish to explore concerns what I will call affective dissonance infection: cases in which the artist successfully transmits a complex emotional state that is itself constituted by moral tension—say, the simultaneous experience of reverence and horror before suffering, or compassion mixed with an unflinching recognition of human cruelty. Because Tolstoy evaluates art by the moral quality of the feeling transmitted, he seems compelled to classify such works as either non-art (if the feeling contains any corrupt element) or as art that transmits a single, unified good feeling (thereby flattening the emotional complexity). I intend to apply this specifically to Tolstoy's dismissal of certain religious and modernist works—works that, by his own infection criterion, successfully transmit feeling, but that he rejects because the feeling is not unambiguously benevolent. My central claim is that Tolstoy's moral filter does not supplement his infection criterion but actually undermines it: if infection is the defining feature, then any work that achieves it should count as art, regardless of whether the feeling promotes brotherhood.

I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to secondary literature. Rather, I would like structured comments on three points. First, is this reading of Tolstoy's distinction between art and non-art accurate to his argument, or does he possess a principled way to distinguish complex-but-good feelings from mixed-and-corrupt ones that I am missing? Second, does the concept of affective dissonance infection genuinely expose an internal contradiction in his framework, or is it an anachronistic import that mischaracterizes the emotional categories available to him? Third, if the tension is real, what is the best way to resolve it strictly within Tolstoy's own commitments—by relaxing the moral criterion, by refining the infection criterion, or by accepting that his theory is normative rather than descriptive? I would also welcome suggestions on how to sharpen the argument without relying on contemporary aesthetic theories that Tolstoy himself would reject.
