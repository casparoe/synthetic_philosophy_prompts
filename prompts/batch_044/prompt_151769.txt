In my philosophy of biology seminar last week, two classmates got into a sharp exchange about the units of selection debate, and I am genuinely unsure who has the stronger position. Alex defended the gene-centered view: only genes possess the fidelity, longevity, and copying accuracy required to be true replicators, so even when we talk about organisms or groups being selected, we are really describing vehicles that genes have built to propagate themselves. Jordan pushed back by invoking evolutionary transitions in individuality—for instance, the shift from unicellular to multicellular life—arguing that once lower-level units become so interdependent that they can only reproduce together, selection operates on the collective as an integrated unit, not merely on the genes inside it. Alex replied that this is just a change in the vehicle’s architecture; tracking gene frequencies still explains why the collective survives, so identifying the gene as the unit is not circular but consistent with the replicator-interactor framework. Jordan then raised a more specific case: the diffusion of legal and institutional norms that suppress kin-based altruism and reward large-scale cooperation, which spread through inter-group competition even when they reduce the genetic fitness of individuals within the group. Jordan said that if gene-level selection were genuinely the explanatory unit here, these norms should be unstable, yet they persist and reshape genetic environments rather than the reverse. Alex countered that this is still an extended phenotype—brains evolved to learn culture are gene products—so cultural spread is ultimately gene-mediated even when the causal path is indirect. They asked me to adjudicate.
