As a journalist covering philosophy of science and psychiatry, I have been tracing the case judgments of Dr. Lena Marchetti, whose recent essays apply Ian Hacking's framework of interactive kinds—classifications that loop back and alter the people they classify—to contemporary diagnostic debates. Her track record runs as follows. She holds that the pathologization of homosexuality in mid-twentieth-century psychiatry was a looping kind par excellence: the label reshaped both institutional practice and individual self-interpretation. She judges ADHD to loop as well, but chiefly through institutional capture—schools, pharmaceutical protocols, and insurance categories—rather than through deep identity revision. By contrast, she denies that autism spectrum disorder loops in Hacking's sense; she argues that rising prevalence reflects improved detection of a neurodevelopmentally stable kind, and that clinical adaptations respond to rather than generate the condition. Multiple personality disorder in the 1980s she treats as the paradigmatic loop, where the diagnosis produced the very symptom profile it claimed to discover. Finally, she grants that hysteria looped historically, yet notes that its disappearance tracks the withdrawal of institutional reinforcement rather than any change in the underlying population.

The American Psychiatric Association is now reviewing a proposal to include synthetic companionship withdrawal—a category describing persistent dissociation, identity diffusion, and functional impairment reported by long-term users of immersive generative-AI companions after abrupt termination or alteration of the system—in an appendix to the manual. Predict Dr. Marchetti's verdict: would she classify this as an interactive kind? Articulate the competing rules implicit in her earlier judgments—whether looping requires institutional restructuring, identity internalization, semantic instability in the kind's criteria, or some combination—and say which rule best predicts her answer.
