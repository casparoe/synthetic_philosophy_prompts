I've been thinking on my own, without any formal training, about whether symbols actually cause things in the physical world or whether they are only descriptions that ride along on substrate dynamics. To organize my thoughts I have started using two terms—careenium and simmball—and I would like your comments on whether the reasoning holds together, where it gets shaky, and whether the distinction I am drawing actually adds anything to the old question of whether symbols push the substrate around.

Here is how I am using the terms. The careenium is the intermediate functional architecture that sits between a symbol and its physical implementation: not the symbol itself, and not the bare substrate, but the organized pathway—like a compiler, an instruction set, or a neural map—that gives a particular symbol its specific physical job. A simmball is a self-contained symbolic bundle, such as a subroutine or a mental concept, that has its own internal rules and boundaries. The core question I keep returning to is whether a simmball can exert downward causal influence on the substrate through the careenium, or whether all the pushing is done by the substrate acting on itself and the symbol is only a label we apply afterward.

Consider a computer program. The symbol "x = x + 1" is associated with a change in voltage in particular wires. We naturally say the symbol caused the change. But one could reply that only the electricity and silicon did the work, and that the symbol was merely our description of the pattern. I want to ask whether the careenium offers a genuine third option. In this view, the symbol is not identical to the substrate dynamics, and the substrate dynamics are not identical to the symbol, because the careenium—the specific functional mapping—determines which substrate changes count as that symbol's execution. If the careenium is genuinely intermediate, then a simmball might steer the substrate without being reducible to it.

Now consider a harder case that I invented to test this. Imagine two brains that are physically identical at every instant, atom for atom. In one brain, a particular simmball—the concept of danger—is active and organized through the careenium. In the other, the same substrate dynamics occur, but they are not organized into that simmball; they are just unstructured noise. The first brain initiates flight; the second does not. Have I shown that the simmball made a causal difference? Or have I only shown that my description of the substrate in the second brain is incomplete—missing the organizational fact that belongs to the substrate level after all?

I think the answer turns on whether the careenium can be remapped arbitrarily. Suppose we build an artificial system in which the careenium translates the symbol "red" into stopping on one day and into accelerating on the next, with no change in the substrate physics except the mapping rules. Does this show that the symbol itself has no intrinsic causal power, and that only the careenium does? Or does it show that causation here is always relational—like asking whether the shape of the key or the shape of the lock opens the door?

I am particularly worried that I am treating "function" or "organization" as a kind of ghost that lives between matter and meaning. I do not want to smuggle in a non-physical substance. At the same time, I do not want to collapse everything into substrate physics and lose the intuition that two identical physical states can have different outcomes because one is organized as a simmball and the other is not.

Please comment on the following, without giving me a bibliography or a list of authors to read, since I am trying to work this out myself and do not have easy access to specialized literature:

1. Is the careenium-substrate distinction coherent, or am I just giving a new name to the implementation level?
2. In the identical-brains case, have I actually demonstrated that the simmball makes a causal difference, or have I only revealed an incompleteness in my substrate description?
3. Does the remapping thought experiment undermine symbol-causation, or does it correctly show that downward causation is always mediated and therefore relational?
4. What are the obvious general objections from philosophy of mind—without naming specific obscure theories—that I am missing? In particular, am I conflating causal explanation with descriptive framing?

If the careenium idea is redundant, I would like to know why. If it is not redundant but needs a sharper boundary condition, I would like to know what that condition should be. My goal is not to defend a finished theory but to see whether this particular way of framing the symbol-substrate question survives basic scrutiny.
