Question: In philosophy of science, Milton Friedman advanced a well-known defense of unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, arguing that the realism of premises is largely irrelevant so long as the model generates accurate predictions. Critics have pressed what is sometimes called the F-twist: if unrealistic assumptions are excused solely by predictive success, then when a model’s predictions fail precisely in those conditions that violate its unrealistic premises, the defense risks circularity—since the failure is explained away by appealing to the very unrealism that was meant to be immaterial. Analyze whether this F-twist genuinely undermines Friedman’s position. In your analysis, apply the reasoning to a hypothetical competitive-market model that assumes both zero transaction costs and perfect rationality, but whose forecasts break down specifically in high-friction, boundedly-rational environments.

Student answer: Friedman’s central claim is that assumptions do not need to correspond to reality; predictive power is the only criterion that matters. The F-twist formalizes this insight by showing that unrealistic assumptions are defensible exactly when they can be quarantined to domains where they do not interfere with prediction. In the hypothetical market model, zero transaction costs and perfect rationality are both false, yet the model predicts prices well in liquid, low-friction markets. When the same model fails in high-friction, boundedly-rational settings, this does not damage Friedman’s view—indeed, it confirms the F-twist—because the failure occurs precisely where the unrealistic premises are violated. Thus the breakdown validates the idea that unrealistic assumptions are harmless outside their intended scope. The only mistake one could make is to demand that false premises be literally false across all domains, which Friedman never required. Consequently, the F-twist strengthens rather than undermines the defense of unrealistic idealization.
