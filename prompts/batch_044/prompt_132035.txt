Two risk analysts, Maya and Jonas, are revising an engineering report and have reached an impasse over how to treat two conditional claims. First, Maya includes the sentence “If the reservoir level exceeds 85 percent by Thursday, the spillway opens automatically,” and treats it as an indicative predictive conditional evaluated against current dynamics and open future branches. Jonas insists it should be read through the same machinery used for counterfactuals: hold the present design and physical laws fixed and check the nearest worlds where the antecedent holds. Second, Maya counters that indicative evaluation is not world-selection but belief-update—assume the antecedent within one’s current epistemic state and check whether the consequent follows, in the tradition of the Ramsey test; counterfactuals, by contrast, hold actual-world background conditions fixed and allow only minimal deviation for a known-false antecedent, which explains why they feel categorically different. Jonas replies that an objective risk report updates no one’s beliefs; it merely projects physical dynamics, so Maya’s epistemic account is a category mistake. The real distinction, he says, is that indicative conditionals concern epistemically open futures while counterfactuals concern closed pasts, but both employ the same similarity ordering over worlds. Third, they examine a hybrid sentence in the report: “If the spillway had been redesigned in 2019, then if the reservoir level exceeds 85 percent by Thursday, it will open with a 40-second delay rather than instantly.” Jonas argues this proves the mechanisms must be layered: first apply counterfactual deviation (the 2019 redesign) to establish a modified background, then apply indicative projection within that background. Maya says it is just nested quantification over worlds with different temporal indices, requiring no distinct logical kinds. Fourth, Jonas claims Maya’s unified view cannot explain the asymmetry that false-antecedent indicatives feel like they require a “miracle” or remote possibility, whereas false-antecedent counterfactuals are evaluated routinely; this shows indicatives carry an implicit commitment to the actual-world trajectory that counterfactuals suspend. Maya responds that “miracle” talk is only a heuristic for extreme remoteness in the similarity ordering, and that nested structures like the hybrid sentence dissolve the asymmetry.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate Maya’s initial framing, Jonas’s belief-update objection, Jonas’s hybrid-layering claim, and Jonas’s miracle-asymmetry point—each from clearly mistaken to clearly right—with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall about whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals differ in logical kind or only in temporal orientation and epistemic status. Finally, identify what both are missing regarding how embedded conditionals with mixed temporal orientation should be formalized in a predictive engineering context. Please begin with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
