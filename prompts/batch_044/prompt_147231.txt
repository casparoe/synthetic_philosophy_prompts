I’m a retired marine biologist who finally started reading philosphy and political theory after leaving the lab. Lately I’ve been stuck on Amartya Sen’s argument that famines aren’t just nature going wrong but are political crimes—though I’m not totally sure which rule he’s actually running. So I’m trying to map his verdicts across three cases he discusses, and then test them on something I made up.

First, the Bengal famine of 1943: he says it’s not a food-availability problem at all—there was rice around—but an entitlement collapse driven by wartime inflation and the colonial goverment’s failure to intervene. Verdict: political crime, not a natural disaster.

Second, the Irish famine of the 1840s: he treats it in a similar spirit, I think—potato blight was the trigger, but grain kept leaving Ireland under British policy and relief was blocked by ideology. So the verdict seems to be that the blight alone didn’t cause the scale of death; politics made it catastrophic.

Third, the Great Leap Forward in China, 1959-61: here there actually was a harvest collapse, but he still classifies it as a political failure because the authoritarian system suppressed bad news and refused to correct course, turning a bad year into tens of millions dead.

Now here’s the case I invented to see where the line really is. There’s a democratic republic—call it Veridia—that suffers a fungal blight destroying about 60% of its staple crop. The goverment is elected, the press is free, and they immediately publish damage figures and ask for international aid. But then they reject all foreign help on nationalist grounds—“self-sufficiency is virtue”—while wealthy citizens import food privately through open markets. Poor rural families starve. The bureaucrats aren’t hiding data; they’re loudly defending the refusal. The death toll is severe, though smaller proportionally than China’s.

If we want to stay consistent with the three verdicts above, what’s the call? Is this still a political crime, or a natural disaster made worse by democratic stupidity? I can see at least two rules that fit the first three cases: one about entitlement failure (people lose access despite aggregate food being present or importable), and one about information suppression and authoritarian feedback loops (the China case). There might also be a third about avoidable magnitude—if a goverment could have stopped the deaths and chose not to, it’s on them.

So please tell me: what does each rule predict for Veridia? Which of the three analogs—Bengal, Ireland, or China—is the closest match? And if the framework genuinely underdetermines the answer, what principle would we be adopting either way? Finally, what single further case would best tell these rules apart—maybe one where information is fully transparent but entitlements still collapse, or where an authoritarian regime actually saves everyone?

Please write the answer in American English, and don’t worry about giving me a bibliography—I’m just trying to think clearly, not write a paper.
