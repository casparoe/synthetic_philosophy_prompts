Please explain the conceptual tension between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease when they are applied to mental conditions characterized by high phenotypic plasticity—such as anxiety-related or mood-regulatory responses—where evolutionary design, statistical distribution, and subjective harm systematically diverge. Focus on the problem of what we might call cryptic dysfunction: a psychological trait can be statistically normal within a modern reference class because cultural and environmental buffering suppress its phenotypic expression, yet it may still constitute a failure of an evolved mechanism that was shaped for an ancestral environment. How does the naturalist, who defines dysfunction relative to biostatistical norms within a specified reference class, identify or even accommodate dysfunction that is statistically invisible? Conversely, how does the normativist, who appeals to harm or to a failure of well-being, prevent the category of disease from collapsing into that of socially disvalued deviation, given that the harm experienced in these cases is partly constituted by the very cultural norms that also obscure the statistical signal? In your answer, clarify whether resolving this tension requires a fundamental revision to the reference-class conditions or to the harm criterion, or whether it reveals a deeper conceptual limitation in one of the two approaches.
