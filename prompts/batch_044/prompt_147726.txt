Recently, after my uncle's death, I inherited his field journals from an ecological survey he conducted in the 1970s across a remote highland basin. The notebooks are filled with handwritten measurements, abbreviations he invented without explanation, and observations that appear to contradict earlier pages—recording soil pH as strongly acidic in one entry, then describing the same plot as alkaline months later, with no notation of any change. I have been drafting an essay that applies Donald Davidson's framework of radical interpretation to this concrete hermeneutic problem: treating my uncle as a speaker whose language and beliefs I must reconstruct from scratch, with no shared dictionary and no access to his intentions for clarification.

In the draft, I argue that the principle of charity requires me to interpret the bulk of his symbols and measurements as largely true by my own lights—if I know the region's geology and climate, I should resolve ambiguities in ways that align with actual conditions—while allowing localized errors only where they can be explained by external interference such as instrument malfunction, fatigue, or weather damage to the page. I also invoke the holism of belief: if I interpret one measurement as erroneous, that revision should ripple through related entries rather than being isolated, because the meaning of an individual symbol depends on its place within the broader network of his commitments. At the same time, I worry that I am violating the conditions of radical interpretation by relying on my own modern botanical and geochemical training to fix the content of his terms; Davidson's interpreter begins with only observable behavior and environment, not with a pre-existing scientific theory of the subject matter.

I would welcome your comments on this draft, particularly on three conceptual points. First, is my use of charity defensible when the speaker is dead and can never correct my interpretation, or does the absence of live interaction undermine the normative force of the principle? Second, have I handled the holism correctly—if I encounter a contradiction in soil records, should I revise my interpretation of the symbol for acidity itself, or should I preserve the symbol's meaning and instead attribute the contradiction to a false belief about that particular plot? Third, does the framing avoid collapsing into a generic hermeneutic exercise, and is there a sharper way to articulate what is philosophically distinctive about interpreting a dead scientist's private notation system?

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or references to specific editions; I want conceptual criticism and suggestions for sharpening the argument's philosophical structure.
