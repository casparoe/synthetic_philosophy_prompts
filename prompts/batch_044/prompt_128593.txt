As a novelist drafting a manuscript on chronic illness, I wish to check whether my philosophical commitments about suffering cohere. I hold that (1) the badness of pain is wholly intrinsic to its phenomenal character and needs no motivational or functional supplement; (2) pain asymbolia confirms this, for patients report the identical awful quality without any aversive response, showing that badness tracks phenomenology rather than motivation; (3) I am a physicalist, identifying phenomenal qualities with specific neural configurations; (4) in my fiction I intend portrayals of bodily suffering to carry genuine ethical weight—readers ought to treat the characters' pain as truly bad and worthy of sympathy, not as mere narrative ornament; (5) yet fictional beings lack neural configurations, so by (1) and (3) they lack phenomenal consciousness and therefore any intrinsic badness; (6) hence the moral gravity of these portrayals can only arise from the reader's projected empathy and narrative convention, not from any property of the represented state; (7) if a real patient genuinely feels pain but, through neurological deficit, never finds it aversive, we still possess direct moral reasons to relieve it, reasons that do not depend upon the patient's own motivation; and (8) I reject free-floating rationales, insisting that every reason belongs to some agent. Do these eight positions form a consistent package? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which conflicts are merely apparent, and for each genuine tension specify the least costly revision and what it costs. In particular, address whether (4) survives (5) and (6), and whether (7) conflicts with (8). Please reply in British English.
