Give me your actual credence—stated as a single number between 0 and 1, not an interval—that one conscious “I” could be synchronously distributed across two or more spatially separated human brains with no direct neural coupling between them, such that there is genuinely one subject of experience whose mental states are partly constituted by processes in each brain, rather than two subjects who share memories or a split-brain case. I am not asking about Hofstadter’s notion that a pattern may survive in others after death; I mean real-time distribution of a present self, coupled only through high-bandwidth external interaction.

Decompose the credence into three terms with a number and one-line justification for each: (i) the prior that personal identity is fundamentally an abstract functional pattern rather than a substance-bound particular; (ii) the conditional probability that such a pattern, if correct, could preserve the unity conditions of consciousness—singular point of view, integrated information, global workspace coherence—when instantiated across disconnected biological substrates; and (iii) the probability that no hidden metaphysical constraint (e.g., an intrinsic “ownership” relation necessarily tying a self to one brain) blocks this regardless of functional integration. Show roughly how they combine into your final figure.

Then identify which term is the weakest link: if it shifted by ±0.30, how far would the overall credence move? Finally, specify what argument or empirical result—say, a decisive failure of cross-brain workspace integration in coupled subjects, or a compelling phenomenological report from a brain-bridge experiment—would most change your verdict, and indicate whether your reasoning is most vulnerable at the pattern-ontology stage or at the unity-conditions stage.
