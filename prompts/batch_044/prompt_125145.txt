I am preparing for an interdisciplinary workshop presentation defending a normative thesis at the intersection of decision theory and epistemology. My core claim is that geometric rationality—specifically a modified Kelly criterion I call absorption-adjusted Kelly betting—should govern how scientists allocate credence and research resources across sequential, structurally fragile hypotheses. I argue that when epistemic failure is absorbing, meaning one catastrophic disconfirmation destroys not merely prior capital but the institutional and cognitive capacity to continue testing altogether, the optimal growth rate of true beliefs is maximized not by the standard Kelly fraction f* = (bp − q)/b, but by a lower fraction that weights future epistemic optionality as a multiplicative constraint on the geometric growth rate. I defend this as a distinct normative principle, not reducible to Bayesian expected-utility maximization with log utility, nor to Savage-style state-dependent utility that merely redescribes risk aversion.

The audience is precisely composed: two philosophers of science who treat decision-theoretic imports into epistemology as category mistakes; two financial economists who accept Kelly for capital growth but deny that epistemic goods are fungible enough to bet; one conciliationist epistemologist who will worry my framework licenses irrational steadfastness in peer disagreement; and a decision theorist committed to subjective expected utility who will defend state-dependent utility as the correct formalization of absorption risk. They will care about whether I am smuggling in descriptive psychology, whether the framework applies outside artificial betting analogies, and whether I have genuinely solved rather than relocated St. Petersburg-style problems for unbounded growth.
