You are a professional political philosopher with particular expertise in democratic theory and the normative ethics of collective decision-making. Please think carefully and systematically; begin by briefly mapping the conceptual terrain — distinguishing the duty-based, expressive, and lesser-evil accounts of voting — before applying them to the case below. I have been discussing this with colleagues who study institutional design, and I am trying to work out whether standard philosophical treatments miss something essential about how ballot structures interact with normative theory.

Consider the 2000 United States presidential election in Florida, specifically the predicament of voters who preferred Ralph Nader but were casting ballots in a jurisdiction where the race between Al Gore and George W. Bush ultimately turned on a recount margin of only a few hundred votes. Rather than asking for a legal history of the Supreme Court decision or the mechanics of the recount, analyse this episode through the lens of voting ethics, but with two complications that are rarely brought together in the literature.

First, incorporate the Palm Beach County butterfly ballot, which caused a substantial number of voters who intended to support Gore to accidentally register votes for Pat Buchanan. This raises a problem about the transparency of preference-expression: if the voting mechanism itself systematically distorts what citizens actually intend, how does that affect the expressive justification for casting a vote for a non-viable candidate? Second, introduce what we might call a threshold-deontological constraint: the idea that a voter’s duty to avoid harm by selecting the least objectionable viable option activates only when the expected margin of influence lies within a non-negligible range, and that outside such a range the obligation dissolves into pure expression. Apply this constraint specifically to the Florida context, where polling and prior knowledge made the race appear far less close than it in fact was.

What, according to each framework, did the Nader supporters face? Which aspects of the episode — the spoiler dynamic, the ballot-design failure, the retroactive revelation that the margin was extraordinarily narrow, and the fact that voters could not have known this in advance — does each account explain well, and which does it misdescribe or suppress? In particular, does the near-tie retroactively impose a lesser-evil duty on those Nader voters, or does it expose that the duty depends on epistemic conditions that were absent? Does the butterfly ballot show that expressive voting is conceptually fragile once institutional design is included, or can the expressive view accommodate it without collapsing? Conclude by stating what this case, once these two complications are injected, reveals about the broader theory: is it strengthened, shown to be incomplete, or revealed to presuppose conditions of electoral transparency and predictable influence that seldom obtain in practice?
