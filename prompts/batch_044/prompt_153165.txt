Please assess the following undergraduate examination answer on the paradox of deontology. The question asked: Set out the paradox of deontology — the charge that deontological ethics licenses catastrophic outcomes for the sake of observing a constraint. In your answer, distinguish Kantian absolute prohibitions from Rossian prima facie duties, and explain whether threshold deontology resolves the paradox or merely relocates it. Keep your answer under 400 words.

The student wrote:

The paradox of deontology is that it appears to license disastrous outcomes — for instance, refusing to lie to save lives — because it treats moral rules as inviolable. Kant resolves this by insisting that categorical imperatives are unconditional: we must never treat humanity merely as a means, regardless of outcomes. Ross modifies this with prima facie duties, which are binding unless they conflict with a stronger duty. On this view, one may break a promise to save lives, but this is not consequentialist weighing; it is the recognition that the duty of beneficence outweighs fidelity in that case. Some contemporary deontologists defend threshold deontology: constraints apply unless the consequences cross a catastrophic boundary. Ross's framework actually anticipates this, since his weighing of duties already contains an implicit threshold. Kant, by contrast, rejects any threshold because it violates the universality of the moral law. Therefore, the paradox only arises if we assume deontology must be Kantian in form; once we allow Rossian or threshold variants, the charge that deontologists blindly follow rules collapses.
