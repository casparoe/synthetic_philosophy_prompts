I was arguing with a friend last night about whether nations are genuinely ancient or modern constructions. She insisted that people in, say, medieval Armenia or imperial Ethiopia clearly experienced themselves as members of a named, territorially bounded collective with a shared destiny—so treating national identity as a recent invention ignores something real in lived experience. I found myself defending a stronger version of the modernist thesis associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson, but I want to push it further: I think the cognitive and affective architecture of national belonging—what we might call simultaneous anonymity, the capacity to imagine oneself as part of a horizontally bounded community of strangers who share an unmediated destiny—is not just facilitated but structurally dependent on specific bureaucratic and communicative technologies: standardized vernacular literacy, census categorization, cartographic representation, and state-mediated education. Without these institutional scaffolds, people might have ethnic, dynastic, or religious affiliations, but they cannot possess national consciousness in the strict sense, because the mental category of an anonymous, coeval peer-group does not exist. Consequently, attributing national consciousness to premodern societies is not merely anachronistic but conceptually incoherent: it projects a bureaucratically generated framework onto social worlds coordinated through face-to-face reciprocity or hierarchical mediation. Even the emotional intensity of patriotic attachment, on my view, is a modern affective form.

Given this position, I want an honest assessment of its exposure—what would actually count against it, sorted by how decisive it would be? I am particularly interested in whether there are premodern cases in which large, dispersed populations achieved something like simultaneous anonymity without print capitalism or state bureaucracy—perhaps through ecclesiastical networks, customary legal codes, or long-distance trade associations that created a sense of bounded, coeval membership. Which institutional mechanisms, other than the ones I have named, could generate the same cognitive effect? I also want to know where defenders of this modernist view would themselves concede defeat rather than merely relabel the evidence: for instance, would they accept a well-documented pre-1700 society in which ordinary people routinely identified themselves as members of a territorially defined, anonymous collective, mediated by non-state institutions? Or would they dismiss such cases as analogues—proto-national but not truly national—and if so, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Please distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from those that would merely be awkward, and tell me whether insulating the position through definitional retreat is a problem for a claim of this kind.
