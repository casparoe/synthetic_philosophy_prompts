Write the opening remarks—approximately six minutes—for an interdisciplinary symposium titled "Deep Time and Policy: Climate, Geography, and the Limits of Event-Driven Governance." The audience is divided: historians and geographers familiar with Fernand Braudel’s distinction between the longue durée—geography, climate, and structural rhythms that unfold across centuries—and the rapid pulse of events, politics, and individual crisis; and policymakers, activists, and community organizers who work in election cycles and emergency response. Use that distinction as the structural spine of the speech, not as ornament: argue that framing climate change chiefly as a sequence of discrete catastrophes (hurricanes, heat waves, diplomatic summits) systematically misplaces the scale at which durable solutions become conceivable, without dismissing the moral urgency those events rightly generate. The remarks must remain fully accessible to the non-academics, precise enough that specialists do not wince, and must contain no bibliography, citations, or appeals to textual authority—paraphrase the conceptual distinction entirely in your own terms. Finally, construct one original analogy, drawn from a domain outside both history and climate science, to illuminate how slow, deep structures constrain rapid human agency.
