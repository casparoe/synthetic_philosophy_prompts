Draft the joint statement that a political associative nationalist and an institutional cosmopolitan could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the question: do compatriots have special moral claims?

The nationalist maintains that shared democratic citizenship generates associative obligations—obligations of solidarity, priority, and mutual justification—that are irreducible to universal duties and not merely instrumental, yet denies that ethnicity, culture, or birth location as such ground any special claim. The cosmopolitan denies that citizenship per se creates agent-relative reasons, holding that all special obligations arise from direct causal relationships, explicit commitments, or institutional roles that happen to be territorially bounded; however, she accepts that states have stronger remedial responsibilities toward those they govern and that global institutional design is currently inadequate.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: (a) the distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral moral reasons; (b) the empirical fact that modern states vary widely in their capacity to protect and represent residents; (c) the rejection of ethnic, racial, or cultural nationalism by both parties; (d) the existence of uncontroversial special obligations (e.g., to family, friends, or explicit promisees); (e) the observation that digital and economic globalization has made transnational cooperative networks functionally similar in some respects to domestic ones; and (f) the agreement that the debate concerns the normative status of citizenship, not merely the practical difficulty of global redistribution.

2. A disagreements table with two columns—Nationalist Position and Cosmopolitan Position—each phrased in wording that its own side would accept. The table should specify: whether citizenship is a source of associative duty comparable to friendship or promise (nationalist yes, cosmopolitan no); whether the moral arbitrariness of birth location undermines any citizenship-based priority (nationalist no, because associative obligations are constructed through shared institutions; cosmopolitan yes, unless the institution is voluntarily joined); and whether state agents may legitimately give stronger weight to citizens' interests in policy decisions (nationalist yes, as an expression of democratic accountability; cosmopolitan only instrumentally, to maintain legitimate institutions that ultimately serve everyone).

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving any one might move at least one party. Identify at least three, including: (i) whether functional equivalence between domestic and transnational cooperative networks undermines the nationalist's claim that citizenship creates a unique associative bond; (ii) whether the cosmopolitan can consistently allow state officials stronger obligations to citizens without implicitly granting citizenship special moral status; and (iii) whether a mandatory domestic service obligation (e.g., military conscription) can be justified on associative grounds if an equally effective voluntary global service scheme exists.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use at least two cases: (A) a democratic state deciding whether to allocate a fixed budget to domestic climate-adaptation infrastructure or to contribute the equivalent sum to an international climate-loss-and-damage mechanism, with both options saving comparable numbers of lives; and (B) a scenario in which citizens of State A can, through low-cost digital coordination, provide direct emergency aid to non-citizens in State B faster than their own government can, testing whether proximity or citizenship should determine duty.

Format the output as the numbered agreed-claims section, followed by the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. Write formally and precisely. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific scholars or texts that would require verification. Ensure that any conceptual injection—such as the functional-equivalence challenge or the digital-coordination emergency scenario—is framed in general terms that do not depend on obscure empirical findings.
