I am a philosophy professor preparing the opening remarks for tomorrow’s interdisciplinary workshop, “Opera, Ethics, and the Limits of Explanation,” co-hosted by our philosophy department and the conservatory. The audience will be genuinely mixed: analytic philosophers who have worked closely with Bernard Williams’s occasional writings on opera and musical drama; vocal performers and directors for whom philosophy is not part of their training; musicologists interested in aesthetic form; and several donors who funded the event and will not follow technical jargon. The occasion calls for something more than ceremonial welcome: I need about five minutes of formal, polished remarks that use a philosophical idea to do real structural work—specifically, to explain and justify our unconventional schedule, in which we present performances first and philosophical commentary second, rather than framing the music with prior analysis.

Please construct the speech around Williams’s well-known resistance to moralism in art criticism—his claim that reducing an opera to a vehicle for ethical instruction misses precisely what the integration of music and drama achieves—without turning the remarks into a lecture on his corpus. To keep the prompt from being something a model could answer from pure pretraining repetition, I want you to weave in a niche conceptual framing I have been developing: the notion of an acoustic suspension of judgment, in which the orchestra does not resolve the ethical tensions raised by the libretto but holds them in a state of reflective immediacy that pure philosophical argument cannot replicate. The speech should welcome each constituency, clarify why we are letting the performance speak before we speak about it, and avoid offering false comfort that opera teaches us how to live. It must be accessible to the performers and donors without being condescending to the philosophers, and it should end with an invitation that respects the tension between emotional directness and critical distance rather than dissolving it.

Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific page numbers; a listener without web access should be able to follow every claim. Do not quote any text verbatim—paraphrase Williams’s position in your own formal prose. The tone should remain that of a senior philosopher addressing colleagues across disciplines, acknowledging that the room contains people who understand exactly why Williams distrusted reductive ethical readings of Mozart and Wagner, as well as people who simply want to know why they are sitting through philosophy before the curtain rises.
