For an upper-level seminar in philosophy of social science or historical sociology — sixteen students, one eighty-minute session — design a runnable in-class simulation that makes participants feel the specific explanatory problem Max Weber framed as elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) and why it is more difficult to defend than either direct cultural causation or pure structural determination. The domain is Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis and the general problem of explaining macro-level economic change by appealing to values rather than to material conditions.

The simulation should run in four groups of four. Each group receives a hidden “value script” — Calvinist-worldly-ascetic, Catholic-communal-redistributive, secular-rational-maximizing, or status-display — and an explicit “structural endowment” specifying market access, property security, and credit availability. In Round One (twenty-five minutes) all groups share identical endowments; in Round Two (twenty minutes) two groups retain full endowments and two suffer a sudden collapse of credit and property security, but no group may change its script. Groups accumulate “capital points” through labor-allocation and investment choices constrained jointly by script rules and endowment conditions. After each round, every group must write a one-sentence causal explanation of its outcome and post it publicly.

The rules, a non-monetary payoff scheme using only point totals and reputation markers, the materials needed, and a minute-by-minute timeline must be specified in full. Crucially, embed a somewhat niche conceptual mechanism — the counterfactual asymmetry problem — that forces participants to confront whether values explain success only in the presence of favorable structures but never in their absence. After Round Two, run a hidden “preservation vote”: each group must choose, if guaranteed that one factor would collapse in a hypothetical Round Three, whether they would preserve their value script or their structural endowment. The facilitator then reveals that every group except the ascetic one votes to preserve endowment, even though those same groups attributed Round One success primarily to their script.

The debrief must map each round onto Weber’s distinction: values as direct motivational causes, values as selectively resonant with structures, and the hidden slide from one to the other in participants’ own explanations. Predict what will go wrong the first time the activity is run — specifically, that groups will over-attribute Round One outcomes to script differences despite identical endowments, and in Round Two will blame structure without noticing that only the ascetic script permits rapid adaptive reinvestment under collapsed conditions — and instruct the facilitator how to surface this without lecturing. Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the long-term historical feedback loop by which a resonant value script can gradually reshape the very structural conditions it initially required, a contingency Weber acknowledged but that a single-session game with fixed rules cannot model.

The prompt should ask for all of the above without requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and without leaning on theories, authors, or specific works so obscure that they would not be present in standard pretraining data for smaller language models.
