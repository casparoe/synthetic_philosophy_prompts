During my career in physical chemistry I became accustomed to classifying reaction pathways by branching conditions—temperature, pressure, catalyst presence—rather than by the names chemists later assign to them. Having recently turned to moral philosophy, I am trying to apply the same discipline to a debate I find both historically specific and structurally intricate: the moral evaluation of the 1945 atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, particularly as it was framed by G. E. M. Anscombe in her protest against Harry Truman’s honorary Oxford degree. Rather than asking for an essay on Anscombe, I want a classification instrument.

Please construct a decision tree, or taxonomy, that sorts positions on the morality of those bombings according to the logical distinctions that actually drive disagreement, not according to pre-existing philosophical labels. Each internal node should be a concrete claim about the case that a respondent could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to. Suggested branching questions include, but are not limited to: whether the intentional destruction of a military-industrial center embedded in a civilian population counts as direct or indirect harm; whether factory workers contributing to the war effort fall under the category of innocent in the relevant moral sense; whether the doctrine of double effect requires proportionality only among intended effects, or also imposes a separate constraint on foreseen but unintended casualties; whether a supreme-emergency argument—claiming that the bombings averted a greater number of deaths distributed across a prolonged invasion—can ever license the intentional targeting of non-combatants; and whether the moral status of the victims depends on individual innocence or on collective structural complicity in the war economy.

Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual position—such as absolute prohibition on direct killing of innocents regardless of consequential scale, indirect harm permissible with strict proportionality, rejection of the innocent-combatant distinction in conditions of total war, or supreme-emergency consequentialism—or be marked unnamed, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Please also include one branch that introduces a distinction rarely emphasized in standard accounts—specifically, the distinction between individual moral status and structural complicity through economic participation—and indicate whether this distinction opens a genuinely occupied position or an unoccupied logical space. Where two leaves are not genuinely separable by your tree, flag them explicitly. Do not ask the respondent to identify themselves by label, and do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
