Consider the strong formulation of the precautionary principle, according to which an action ought not to proceed when its potential harms are severe and purportedly irreversible, even if the probability of those harms remains epistemically indeterminate. I ask you to provide a decomposed credence—expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1—regarding whether this formulation is conceptually coherent rather than self-defeating when applied symmetrically to both intervention and non-intervention. Decompose your reasoning across four dimensions: first, whether “indeterminate probability” can be rationally distinguished from “low but unquantified probability” in regulatory epistemology; second, whether the principle survives a reversal test that applies identical precautionary logic to the risks of regulatory stagnation and foregone benefits; third, whether the criterion of “irreversibility” possesses a stable metaphysical or ecological boundary, or whether it collapses into socially constructed risk perception; and fourth, whether coherence demands reconceiving the principle as a decision heuristic rather than a normative decision rule. For each dimension, supply a rough probability with a brief justification, then combine them into your overall figure. Identify which dimension is most sensitive to your final judgment, and specify what conceptual or empirical finding—accessible without specialized literature—would most substantially revise your credence.
