Compare two theoretical accounts of what pain asymbolia reveals about whether pain is necessarily bad: (A) the phenomenal-essentialist view, which holds that pain’s negative valence is inseparable from its sensory quality, so that asymbolic patients—who report pain but show no aversion—are still suffering in a way their behavior merely fails to express; and (B) the integrative-command view, which treats pain as essentially a protective command that binds sensation, affect, and motivation, so that in asymbolia the sensory component persists but the command (and thus the badness) is broken.

In your comparison, address how each account explains the patient’s own reports (e.g., “it hurts but I don’t care”). Then consider a hypothetical mirror case—call it “reverse asymbolia”—in which a patient shows intense protective withdrawal and reports severe distress, yet denies any localized sensory quality whatsoever. Which account accommodates this mirror case more naturally, and does the pair of cases together favor one view over the other?
