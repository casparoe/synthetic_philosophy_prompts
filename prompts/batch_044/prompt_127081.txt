I’m a retired biologist — spent thirty years in neuroscience labs — and since I stopped working I’ve fallen down a philosphy hole, mostly mind and ethics stuff. I sit on the acquisitions ethics panel for our university library, and lately we’ve been getting proposals for these strange new artefacts: printed connectomes, interactive digital archives that reorganise themselves, that sort of thing. The other day my friend mentioned that old thought experiment from The Mind's I about Einstein's brain being simulated, and whether a book describing every neuron could actually be conscious. It got me wondering if we need a proper decision procedure for our panel.

We’re not philosophers — we meet once a month, decisions have to be made in about twenty minutes, and I’m the only one with any theory — so please act as a professional philosopher of mind with practical administration experience, and think carefully step by step. Before you build anything, do a quick brainstorm of the distinctions that actually survive simplification: functional integration versus static storage, temporal binding, self-modelling, and whatever else is load-bearing. Then turn the principle — that consciousness needs dynamic causal integration, not just correct information — into an ordered checklist my colleagues can run: questions in sequence, what counts as enough to stop with a clear 'no', where it hands the decision to a person because it’s values not facts, and where it will definitely misfire. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls in disguise.

Apply it to three cases: a printed book containing the full connectome of Einstein’s brain; a digital archive that updates it’s own indexing in response to queries and holds internal state for months; and a basic e-reader showing the same text with no feedback loops. I particularly want the second case, because I suspect the procedure will push toward giving it moral consideration and I’ll hate that result, so flag exactly where that tension comes from. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do — can it ever settle hard cases, or does it just push them upstairs? — and note any point where the answer depends on accepting substrate-independent intuitions that not everyone shares.
