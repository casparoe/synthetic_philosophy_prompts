I'm working on a chapter about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommittment, and I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about tying future hands. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Odysseus tying himself to the mast to resist the sirens: totally fine.
2. An addict giving her friend the meds with instructions not to return them even if she begs: fine.
3. A dementia patient signing an advance directive refusing feeding tubes in a later happy-but-confused state: fine, even though it feels like we're honoring a past self over a present one.
4. A depressed writer giving her agent contractual power to force manuscript submission even when she begs to stop: I think this is okay too, but it feels different - it's forcing action rather than blocking harm, and I'm not sure why I don't mind.
5. A parliament enacting a climate lock that automatically bans fossil subsidies if emissions miss targets, with no legislative override: not fine. That binds future people who never consented.
6. A constitutional provision requiring 90% supermajority to amend free speech protections: fine, that's classic entrenchment.
7. But a constitutional language preservation clause that can never be repealed, binding all descendants: not fine. Classic dead hand.

The tension is between (3) and (4) - both bind a future self with changed preferences - and between (5) and (6), where both are collective precommitments but I split them. Is there a principle that captures this pattern, maybe about whether the bound party can be said to have authorized the bond? Please state it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, tell me what it predicts for the case I'm wafling on - a university charter (adopted by current faculty) that automatically dissolves any department with zero peer-reviewed publications in 10 years - and be honest about which of my verdicts above it can't save without getting ugly.
