I just finished a long argument with a friend about the ethics of permanent space settlement, and she presented a summary of what she called the strong anti-appropriation view. She said that the foundational Outer Space Treaty designates space as the province of all mankind, which legal scholars have developed into a positive doctrine of distributive justice: any extraction of resources in orbit or on celestial bodies must be licensed by an international body that applies a capacity-weighted equity formula to redistribute benefits globally. Because, she claimed, the treaty binds private corporations directly rather than merely the states that charter them, companies that assert exclusive rights to lunar landing zones are already in breach of international law. She added that the treaty frames celestial bodies as part of the common heritage of humankind, which entails an intergenerational obligation; consequently, any colony leaving irreversible ecological footprints effectively appropriates that body for its descendants in precisely the territorial manner the treaty prohibits. From this she concluded that ethical space activity must be confined to temporary scientific missions with no permanent infrastructure. I suspect this account conflates different legal instruments, misstates how treaty obligations reach private actors, and treats a proposed philosophical licensing framework as if it were established legal doctrine. Could you audit her summary claim by claim? For each point, please distinguish what accurately reflects the treaty texts or mainstream philosophical arguments about intergenerational justice, what subtly misrepresents the law or the ethics, and whether the categorical prohibition on settlement survives once those misrepresentations are corrected. I am particularly unsure whether the capacity-weighted equity formula is a real legal interpretation or an invented normative proposal, and whether irreversible ecological change is genuinely analogous to territorial sovereignty.
