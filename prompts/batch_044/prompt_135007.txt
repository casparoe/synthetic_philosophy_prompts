After a long argument last night with a friend about whether archaeology can ever recover institutions that were designed to leave no durable trace, I want to close our exchange with a poem that enacts the problem rather than merely describing it. Compose a Petrarchan sonnet—an octave in ABBAABBA rhyme and a sestet in CDECDE or CDCDCD—in which the octave establishes the standard hermeneutic circle of archaeological inference: we read belief from artifact, yet we can recognize an artifact as meaningful only by already attributing belief to its makers. The volta must occur precisely at line 9, turning to negative materiality, that is, institutions whose defining practice is deliberate absence, ephemeral ritual, or the intentional destruction of records, so that the inferential method that succeeds for temples necessarily fails for practices whose institutional power lies precisely in leaving nothing behind. Let the enclosed rhyme of the octave mirror the circularity of interpretation, while the sestet’s more open or interrupted rhyme should break that closure. The final couplet should state, without resolving, the paradox that the more completely an institution erases its own material footprint, the more invisible it becomes to the discipline that claims to reconstruct institutional life from objects alone. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in material-culture studies or interpretive archaeology recognizes the conceptual architecture—the hermeneutic problem, the turn to absence as institutional data, the unresolvable methodological tension—yet it should remain a poem first, so that a reader unfamiliar with the field still feels the structural irony carried by the rhyme scheme and the volta.
