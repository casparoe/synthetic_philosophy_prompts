I am revising the syllabus for a graduate reading group on medieval theories of causation, and a participant raised an objection that I have been unable to resolve without making Al-Ghazālī's position look incoherent. His occasionalist thesis is that God produces every particular event immediately; the connection between, say, fire and the burning of cotton is not a necessary link in nature but merely a constant conjunction (al-ittisāl al-ʿādī) that God reproduces according to His customary course (sunnat Allāh). From this I have reconstructed the following chain, which seems to prove that occasionalism cannot accommodate miracles—the very phenomena Al-Ghazālī invokes to defend prophecy. (1) If there are no necessary causal connections in nature, then there is no logical contradiction in God producing burning without fire, or producing a text without an author. (2) A miracle is traditionally understood as an event that violates a necessary law of nature. (3) Since occasionalism denies that nature contains necessary laws—there are only habitual conjunctions—there are strictly speaking no laws to violate. (4) Therefore, on occasionalist premises, there can be no miracles in the traditional sense; there are only unusual departures from God's habit. (5) Yet Al-Ghazālī treats miracles (muʿjizāt) as exceptional events that authenticate prophetic claims, which requires that they stand in sharp contrast to an ordinary, stable order. (6) If that stable order is nothing more than God's habit, then a miracle is merely God altering His habit; but without an independent criterion for which alterations are prophetic and which are arbitrary, the category of miracle collapses into mere statistical rarity or theological stipulation. (7) Hence occasionalism undermines the miracle/ordinary distinction it needs for its own religious defense.

The argument feels suspicious—probably one step slips—but I cannot locate the slide. If I must preserve both (1), the denial of necessary connection, and (5), the claim that miracles genuinely occur and validate prophecy, which premise should I surrender, and what does that surrender cost? Does the damage fall mainly on (2), the traditional definition of miracle, or on (6), the demand for a non-arbitrary criterion? I want to understand whether occasionalism can redefine miracles without losing their epistemic force, or whether the cost of saving the position is to abandon the idea that miracles violate anything at all.
