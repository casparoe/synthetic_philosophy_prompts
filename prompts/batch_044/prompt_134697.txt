As a novelist constructing an immersive philosophical workshop for a manuscript set in a near-future ethics academy, I require a precisely engineered classroom activity that makes participants feel the crushing logic of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility—not through lecture, but through lived contradiction. You are an expert moral philosopher and experiential pedagogy designer with deep familiarity with metaphysical arguments about agency and regress. Design a 75-minute session for 14 graduate ethics students seated in a circle. Frame it as a fictional "Causal Lineage Tribunal." At entry, each student receives a sealed Genesis Envelope containing a hidden chain of prior conditions—upbringing, neural predispositions, linguistic environment, and a hidden "Reflexive Loop Card"—that determined their moral intuitions and reasoning styles. These are not chosen; they are revealed only at a controlled moment.

Roles: three Accusers (arguing a scripted Defendant—another student—is morally responsible for a scripted harmful act); three Defenders (arguing the opposite); and eight Jurors (who must reach unanimous verdict using only the tribunal standard: "Blame attaches solely to agents who are the ultimate cause of their own character").

Rules and stakes: Accusers and Defenders earn social capital (public commendation from the facilitator, recorded on a visible board) for persuasive arguments; Jurors earn capital for unanimous, well-reasoned verdicts. The Defendant receives capital if found not responsible, but loses it if blamed—creating real, if small, social stakes.

Timeline: 10 minutes silent reading of Genesis Envelopes (sealed, unopened). 20 minutes opening deliberation, during which Accusers deploy intuitive blame-assignments and Defenders invoke mitigating circumstances. At minute 30, the moderator forces the Revelation: all envelopes are opened. Each student reads aloud their causal chain, including the Reflexive Loop Card stating that their very resistance to determinism, their emotional reaction to the Defendant's act, and their argumentative style were among the predetermined variables. The group must then continue deliberation for 20 minutes, now knowing that every preference they hold—including their belief in free will—was authored by conditions outside them.

The debrief (final 15 minutes) must map moments onto the argument's steps: the initial demand for ultimate self-creation; the discovery that one's selecting mechanisms are themselves unchosen; the collapse of the act/character distinction; and the circularity of trying to be responsible for one's own responsibility. Name the argument only in the final five minutes. Include explicit materials list (envelopes, cards, moderator script, capital board), precise timing, and rules for the moderator.

Be frank about predictable failures: students may refuse to open envelopes; Accusers may conflate determinism with fatalism and halt deliberation; the emotional weight of the Loop Card may cause withdrawal rather than engagement. Provide a contingency for each—e.g., a private journaling option for those who resist public revelation, a moderator prompt that distinguishes causal explanation from excuse.

The activity reproduces the phenomenological shock but not the modal structure.
