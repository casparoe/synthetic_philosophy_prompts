Consider a contemporary philosopher working within the Nyāya tradition who has offered explicit verdicts on whether particular cognitive processes constitute independent pramāṇas—independent means of valid cognition—in a series of borderline cases. Her rulings are as follows: direct sensory awareness of a present object is pratyakṣa; inference from an observed sign (e.g., smoke) to an unobserved fact (e.g., fire) is anumāna; learning an unfamiliar term or fact from a competent speaker is śabda; identifying an object by comparing it to a previously known similar instance is upamāna; knowing that a jar is absent from a room through the failure to perceive it under appropriate conditions is anupalabdhi; a yogi’s direct cognition of distant or microscopic objects is still pratyakṣa, merely range-extended rather than a novel source; rapid intuitive detection of a logical contradiction without explicit step-by-step reasoning is reducible to compressed anumāna; and belief formed through an instrumentally aided imaging system—such as a radiologist interpreting an MRI—is an extension of pratyakṣa aided by an instrument, not a separate pramāṇa.

Now consider a new case: a synthetic neural prosthesis processes raw astrophysical telemetry entirely subpersonally and induces in a human subject a true belief about the core temperature of a specific distant neutron star. The subject experiences no sensory quality, performs no conscious inference, receives no linguistic testimony, makes no comparison to a known case, and does not apprehend an absence; the belief is formed through an opaque, non-conscious computational pipeline that feeds directly into her doxastic system, with the star itself lying far outside any possible causal contact with her unaided senses.

Predict the philosopher’s verdict—would she treat this as a new, independent pramāṇa, as reducible to one of the existing ones, or as not a pramāṇa at all? Identify at least two competing principles that fit her earlier rulings equally well, say what each predicts about the prosthesis case, and defend which prediction is more likely given her implicit commitments. Then specify a further hypothetical case—one that differs from both the prosthesis case and the earlier ones—that would decisively distinguish the two principles you have identified.
