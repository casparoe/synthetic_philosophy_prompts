I'm on the board of a small agricultural cooperative that maintains a shared seed vault and irrigation network serving about forty households in our valley. We have received a one-time grant of roughly eighty thousand dollars and must choose, before winter, between two mutually exclusive projects. Option A is to reinforce our water storage and install a backup filtration array: this produces no improvement in harvest during normal years, but it gives us approximately a fifteen percent chance of preventing total system collapse if we face a prolonged drought followed by upstream contamination—an event our hydrologist considers plausible though unlikely within the next decade. Option B is to distribute the funds as direct soil-amendment subsidies across all member plots, which would reliably raise every household's yield by about twelve percent for the next three growing seasons, offering no protection against catastrophic failure.

Our disagreement is not merely about probabilities. Several members argue that because collapse would destroy soil microbiome diversity we have rebuilt over fifteen years—harm they regard as irreversible—we should not weight that fifteen percent by its face value; they propose a nonlinear adjustment that assigns greater decision weight to near-catastrophic risks than to moderate gains of equal expected value. Others reply that such an adjustment is arbitrary unless we can agree on a shared baseline defining what counts as irreversible for each household, and they observe that three of our members are elderly and have explicitly told us they value guaranteed yields over the next two years far more than long-tail risks they are unlikely to live to see. A younger member recently suggested a compromise: compute risk-weighted valuations separately for each household using their own stated time horizons and vulnerability profiles, then aggregate these through a weighted voting rule. In principle this sounds equitable, yet I cannot see how to implement it without either imposing one person's risk philosophy on the collective, or adopting a decision procedure that ignores the public-good nature of the infrastructure—if we build the backup system, it protects everyone regardless of whether they personally assigned high weight to the risk.

I sense there is a deeper conceptual issue about how groups ought to decide when individual risk attitudes diverge sharply and the outcomes are shared, but I lack the vocabulary to articulate it precisely. Could you help me identify the philosophical structure underlying our disagreement, assess whether the proposed nonlinear risk-weighting is defensible, and advise whether there exists a coherent method for aggregating heterogeneous risk preferences when the choice involves shared infrastructure?
