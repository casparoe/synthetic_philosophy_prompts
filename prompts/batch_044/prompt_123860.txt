In a seminar on relational theories of the self, a colleague invoked the Ubuntu formulation that a person is a person through other people—commonly summarized by the phrase I am because we are—to defend a striking claim about solitary confinement. She argued that isolating a prisoner for years does not merely harm a fully constituted person but actively dissolves the conditions of personhood itself, so that describing the prisoner as a rights-bearing subject after prolonged isolation involves a kind of category mistake. I want to press her on this, but I realize the slogan could be doing very different philosophical work.

Please disambiguate the precise theses this formulation might express. Lay them out as a menu, ordered from the weakest and least controversial to the strongest and most revisionary, and for each specify: (1) whether it concerns conceptual dependence, causal or developmental dependence, metaphysical constitution, or normative ascription; (2) whether it implies that an isolated human being literally lacks personhood, or only that they suffer a grave deprivation of a relational good; (3) whether it makes relational properties essential to diachronic identity, and if so what kind of continuity it demands; and (4) whether that specific reading actually licenses the claim that solitary confinement un-makes the person rather than severely damaging an intact one. In particular, identify which readings avoid the consequence that hermits, severely autistic individuals with minimal social interaction, or newborn infants temporarily fail to be persons, and explain what philosophical costs each such reading incurs—whether in explanatory power, in compatibility with intuitions about harm, or in commitment to a specific ontology of relations.
