You are marking papers for a seminar on the philosophy of law in the seventeenth century, and you wish to maintain an impartial, rigorous standard. Consider the following examination question and the answer that was submitted.

Question: In the Pensées, Pascal maintains that justice separated from power is ineffectual, and that human societies establish equity not through natural reason but through custom enforced by authority. Explicate Pascal's argument for the interdependence of justice and force, explain why he treats custom as the actual source of equitable rules, and determine whether this position collapses into Hobbesian conventionalism.

Student response: Pascal argues that people are too fallen and irrational to perceive true justice, so they invent arbitrary conventions and back them with violence. He shares Hobbes's view that a sovereign's coercive power is the only thing that gives justice any reality; without the sword, justice is mere noise. Pascal also claims that custom is the entire foundation of equity, which shows he thinks there is no natural law accessible to human reason. The strongest should govern because they can preserve these conventions. Thus Pascal is essentially a conventionalist: justice is nothing more than what the powerful have made customary, with no independent standard available to condemn injustice.

Assign a letter grade and provide a point-by-point assessment. Identify precisely where the student grasps the visible architecture of Pascal's thought—especially the impotence of justice without enforcement and the centrality of custom—while also noting the more elusive errors: any conflation with Hobbes's social-contract framework, any misinterpretation of whether Pascal endorses the rule of the strongest as normative rather than descriptive, and any omission of the theological premise that true justice remains hidden in God and is therefore unavailable to fallen human reason. Deliver only the evaluation; do not indicate whether you personally share the student's conclusions.
