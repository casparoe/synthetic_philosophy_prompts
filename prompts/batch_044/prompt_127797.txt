I serve on my city's Institutional Memory and Civic Archive Committee, and we must vote this Thursday on a petition that has split our advisory board. For twenty years we have maintained the Municipal Testimony Repository—approximately four thousand first-person accounts collected between 1982 and 2003 from residents affected by the closure of the Riverside Industrial Complex—under an open-access preservation mandate: every record digitized, indexed, and available in perpetuity. A coalition of former residents and their heirs now asks us to adopt a "mnemonic sunset clause," a structured protocol allowing contributors or direct descendants to request that specific testimonies be de-indexed from public access and moved into restricted archival storage (not destroyed) after a thirty-year retention period, renewable or releasable at will. They argue that perpetual public accessibility transforms private trauma into permanent civic spectacle and violates a right to be forgotten that complements collective remembrance. Our historians object that selective withdrawal corrupts evidentiary integrity, invites institutional amnesia about industrial harm, and betrays a moral duty to remember that binds the municipality to its past failures.

Last night I argued about this for hours with a friend who works in public history; she insisted that both Margalit's ethics of memory and Rieff's defense of forgetting miss the institutional dimension because they treat remembrance as something societies or individuals do, rather than something archives perform through display. She proposed we need a third structural category—not preservation versus erasure, but "curatorial dormancy"—in which materials remain physically intact and legally preserved, yet are withdrawn from active circulation unless a specific ethical-access threshold is satisfied, thereby separating the duty to retain evidence from the practice of perpetual public exhibition. I think she is right that the archive itself is doing philosophical work here, but I need to turn this into an actionable recommendation rather than an abstract framework.

Please adopt the role of a senior ethics advisor and institutional-policy specialist with expertise in philosophy of memory and archival governance. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm: isolate the precise tension between evidentiary integrity and contributor dignity, specify how curatorial dormancy differs from both open preservation and destruction, and identify the feature of the case that does the philosophical work. Then draft the committee's formal decision memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action—approve a pilot dormancy protocol with conditions, approve a modified sunset framework, or reject the petition with a specified alternative—and include a clear time frame for implementation or review. The reasoning should treat the strongest objection on the losing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal, and the memo should present an actionable bottom line the committee can vote on. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to highly specialized theories; think carefully about whether perpetual accessibility serves remembrance or merely serves the archive's own logic of display, and write in a formal, polished register suitable for an institutional body.
