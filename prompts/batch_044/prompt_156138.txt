I have been trying to work out whether an ordinary person's choices—what they eat, how they vote, whether they fly—can be genuinely wrong in cases where those choices make no measurable difference to climate damage or factory farming. The standard reply is the inefficacy objection: if my single act is causally lost in the collective, there is no moral reason to abstain. I no longer find that reply decisive, but not for the usual reason people cite about collective impact. Instead, I have come to hold that the wrongness of participating does not depend on causing harm at all, but on what I call structural awareness: the agent must explicitly recognize that their contribution is redundant within the system, and must still refuse participation precisely as an expressive stance toward the collective practice rather than as an attempt to fix outcomes. On my view, an act like eating meat or casting a vote in a landslide election becomes morally weighty only when performed with that awareness; without it, the act is indeed just preference, but with it, the agent is refusing to be enlisted in a practice they understand to be harmful regardless of their individual efficacy. So my position is that individual obligations in collective harm cases are real, non-causal, and conditional on this kind of explicit structural recognition—and that this condition saves the view from being a mere feeling. What would count against it? I want the arguments and cases that should actually change my mind, sorted from fatal to merely awkward: whether requiring explicit philosophical awareness makes morality too intellectualized for everyday life; whether mental-state conditions let agents excuse themselves too easily by claiming ignorance; whether the view secretly collapses into a form of virtue ethics or expressive individualism that abandons the original problem of harm; and whether there are collective contexts in which structural awareness is impossible or incoherent, making the moral obligation vanish in precisely the cases where it should apply. I also want an honest assessment of whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by defining structural awareness so narrowly that any counterexample can be dismissed as lacking the requisite awareness—and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind. Finally, say what defenders of this view would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say.
