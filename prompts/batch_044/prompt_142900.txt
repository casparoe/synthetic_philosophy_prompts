I've been reading about Nietzsche's idea that people relate to the past in three ways: treating great figures as models to imitate, preserving every detail out of loyalty to what came before, and breaking aggressively from tradition so the present can live. He warns that each mode carries a hidden danger — feeling too small to act, being buried under preservation, or cutting away roots we still need. I want to run a 90-minute workshop for about ten friends and colleagues, not students, where they feel these pulls and dangers physically rather than just discussing them. Design a structured activity with rules, roles, and forced trade-offs: they are curators of a collective memory for a small fictional town that has just lost its only factory, and they must decide what survives in a shrinking physical archive. Give one group incentives to build a heroic narrative, another to save every document and object, and another to destroy whatever blocks new life, but make storage space shrink each round and add a hidden mechanism — perhaps a rising living cost for every item kept, or action tokens that can only be earned by deleting — so that preservation literally competes with present decision-making. Include a debrief that maps the conflicts they experienced onto the three modes and the specific disadvantages, especially the risk that destructive judgment can turn back on the judge. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, such as one role dominating or participants avoiding direct conflict, and how to handle it. Be frank about which part of Nietzsche's argument, particularly the idea that critical history demands a kind of necessary self-injury, the activity cannot reproduce on its own and I must supply in the closing explanation. Write all participant instructions without academic jargon, and do not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
