As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to read systematically in philosophy—particularly in the theory of action and the ethics of research design—I have found myself troubled by an argument I constructed about informed consent that appears to prove far more than I intend. The problem concerns what I have started to call the ontological drift of consent objects: the fact that the intervention to which one consents is not a static entity, but acquires new causal properties as scientific knowledge advances. From this I derived a chain of reasoning that seems to show that long-term biomedical research can never satisfy the conditions for ethically valid consent. I lay it out with explicit steps because I need to know precisely where the inference slips.

(1) For consent to an intervention to be valid, the subject must possess competence regarding the essential nature of that intervention, and any deception—whether by commission or omission—about that essential nature vitiates the consent entirely.

(2) In any complex, long-term study, the intervention possesses causal pathways—such as microbiome-mediated metabolic feedback or delayed epigenetic modulation—that are genuinely part of its structure but are currently unknown and therefore undisclosable.

(3) Once these pathways are discovered, they are recognized not as extraneous side effects but as constitutive elements of what the intervention actually does to a subject over time.

(4) At the moment of consent, however, these pathways are epistemically inaccessible; the subject therefore cannot be competent with respect to them, and the researcher cannot disclose them without deception about the limits of present knowledge.

(5) Consequently, either the unknown pathways belong to the essential nature of the intervention—in which case every such consent is structurally vitiated by omission—or they do not, in which case the distinction between essential and accidental features is drawn arbitrarily by present ignorance rather than by any real boundary in the intervention itself.

(6) If the distinction is arbitrary, then a researcher could withhold a known risk simply by labeling it non-essential, which collapses the very protection the deception condition is meant to provide.

(7) Therefore, if we preserve both the competence requirement and the prohibition on deception about essential features, we must conclude that no consent to long-term research is ever valid; and if we reject that anti-research conclusion, we must abandon one of the premises that seemed indispensable.

I am convinced that something has gone wrong between steps (4) and (6)—perhaps a hidden modal slide that treats epistemic inaccessibility as equivalent to intentional omission, or a failure to distinguish the object of consent at time t from its later-described causal profile—but I cannot locate the exact error. Which premise should be rejected? If I hold fast to (1) and (6), am I genuinely committed to halting such research, or is there a way to stabilize the boundary between essential and accidental features without appealing to arbitrary epistemic limits? I want a disciplined diagnosis of the argument’s structure, together with an honest account of what each possible rejection costs conceptually, particularly with respect to the coherence of the competence condition itself.
