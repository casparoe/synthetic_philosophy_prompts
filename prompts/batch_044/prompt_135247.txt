I have been turning over the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—the old puzzle about what, if anything, all pleasures have in common—since a graduate seminar last week in which a student argued that if pleasure is simply whatever a subject happens to enjoy, then hedonism collapses into tautology and loses any explanatory power. I want you to write a dialogue between two philosophers, both fully competent and neither serving as a strawman, who take up positions that do not map neatly onto the familiar "common feeling" versus "common attitude" dichotomy. One should defend what I will call the structural resonance view: that pleasures are unified not by a shared qualitative feel but by a particular temporal architecture in which present attention is sustained without resistance, so that the pleasure of tasting wine and the pleasure of grasping a proof share the same formal dynamics even though their contents differ radically. The other should defend a modified disjunctive family view: that pleasure is analogous to "game" in the later Wittgenstein, a cluster concept with no single essence, but with the further claim that this disjunction is not merely linguistic but metaphysically real—pleasures form a natural kind only at the level of evolutionary function, not at the level of phenomenology or neural signature. Have them discuss a specific hypothetical, namely an "inverted hedonic implant" that reproduces the precise neural correlates of gustatory pleasure during abstract mathematical reasoning, and ask whether this produces genuine pleasure, a phenomenal counterfeit, or something that undermines the very idea of a common currency. Each speaker should respond to the other's actual points about the implant, about evolutionary function, and about whether hedonism can survive as a substantive theory of well-being without requiring some shared feature across all pleasures. Write this as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might compose in a letter to a colleague.
