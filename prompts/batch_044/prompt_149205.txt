I have no formal background in philosophy or the history of science, but I have been thinking on my own about why major ideas sometimes seem to arrive twice: calculus with Newton and Leibniz, and evolution by natural selection with Darwin and Wallace. I want to know whether these discoveries were genuinely inevitable—whether the right tools and problems made them unavoidable—and whether it actually matters who reached them first.

Please respond in the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated.

1. Tiered ranking of explanations (about 220 words). Rank the five accounts below from most to least convincing for explaining both the calculus and the evolutionary cases together. Assign each a tier—A (strong for both cases), B (works for one but is strained for the other), or C (weak overall)—and in one or two sentences say why it lands there. The accounts are:

- The maturity thesis: the surrounding mathematics, observation, and open questions had reached a state in which this specific solution was structurally unavoidable.
- The shared-tool convergence thesis: both pairs had access to the same emerging techniques, notation, or observational methods, which channeled them toward an identical formal result.
- The institutional-bottleneck hypothesis: universities, patronage networks, and publishing venues concentrated talented minds on the same visible problems, raising the odds of parallel work.
- The competitive-arousal model: awareness of rivals accelerated each party’s progress, so the “simultaneity” was actually interdependent rather than truly independent.
- The archival-compression effect (a tentative idea of my own): what looks like mental convergence is largely an illusion produced by slow correspondence, printing delays, lost manuscripts, and later priority disputes; true independent convergence is rarer than the record suggests.

2. Cross-case consistency check (about 100 words). Does the explanation you placed in tier A fit calculus and natural selection about equally well, or does one case suit it far better? If you think the two discoveries need different leading explanations, say so briefly.

3. The attribution question (about 120 words). Split “who made the discovery” into two dimensions and score each on a scale from 0 (it does not matter at all) to 1 (it matters completely). First, epistemic weight: how much does knowing that Newton rather than Leibniz, or Darwin rather than Wallace, was first change whether the idea itself is true or well-supported? Second, social weight: how much does the discoverer’s identity matter for credit, historical narrative, and the motivation of future researchers? Give both numbers and one sentence defending each.

4. Verdict on inevitability (about 100 words). Based on your ranking, answer directly: were these discoveries genuinely inevitable in the sense that they would have occurred within roughly twenty years even if the actual discoverers had never lived? Answer yes, no, or probably, and state the one condition that would most change your answer.

Please use clear, non-technical language, but do not flatten real differences between the explanations. I am refining my own thinking, not asking for references or further reading.
