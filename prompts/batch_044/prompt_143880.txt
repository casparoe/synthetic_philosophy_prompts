Write a short story of roughly 600–900 words that dramatizes the philosophical problem of why promises bind, set in an invented culture where commitments are established not through spoken language but through the exchange of small, irreplaceable objects composed of a material that decays only upon the intentional breaking of a commitment. The protagonist should be an archivist who discovers that a commitment made three generations earlier was violated accidentally—without intent—yet the corresponding object shows no decay, suggesting that the normative force of the promise may not depend on performance, expectation, or social convention alone. As the archivist confronts the descendants of both original parties, the narrative should make room for at least three competing accounts of promissory obligation—those grounded in mutual expectation, in conventional practice, and in the direct creation of a normative relation—without naming philosophers or citing texts. The story should include a moment in which one character offers a justification for honoring the original commitment that is recognizably a reason of the wrong kind: a consideration that supports the action but fails to capture the specific normative force that a promise is supposed to carry. End ambiguously: both original agents are long dead, and the object has become culturally valuable as a historical artifact; the descendants disagree over whether anything still binds them. Do not resolve the dispute by appeal to legal authority, utility, or self-interest. Instead, let the closing image suggest that if promises do bind across death and accident, they do so in a way that outstrips both instrumental reasoning and conventional agreement.
