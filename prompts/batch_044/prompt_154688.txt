I'm preparing an in-depth interview for my science-and-ideas column with Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of physics who has recently advanced a position she calls degenerate relationalism. Her central claim revises the standard reading of Black's two-sphere universe: she argues that the spheres do not violate Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles, but rather expose an unexamined assumption that spatial separation is automatically a qualitative difference. In a perfectly symmetric universe, Voss contends, the relation of distance between the two spheres collapses into a single self-relation of the cosmos mediated by a background metric field—a field that, on her account, lacks any internal conditions of individuation. She extends this framework to quantum exchange symmetry, proposing that multi-particle systems under global symmetry offer not merely an analogy but an ontological model for how relation-types can exist without being anchored to discrete relata. I want the conversation to stay in the weeds of the argument rather than drifting into biography.

Please draft eight to ten polished, journalist-style questions suitable for an educated general audience. I need at least one question that presses her on whether her metric-field move secretly reintroduces extrinsic individuation—the very thing she claims to reject—with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the field is relational all the way down. Another should ask how she distinguishes a genuinely symmetric ontology from a merely epistemically hidden asymmetry, and what conceptual or empirical marker could break that symmetry. A third should probe whether her appeal to quantum exchange symmetry is a literal ontological parallel or only an illuminating analogy, and what hangs on the distinction. The sequence should build logically, ending with a sharp question about what observation—thought-experimental or physical—would genuinely falsify degenerate relationalism rather than invite a redescription.

Please present the questions as a coherent interview arc, not a bullet list, and avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. After delivering the questions, state explicitly how confident you are that they fairly capture Voss's position as I have described it, and single out the specific point—whether about the metric field, the quantum parallel, or the falsifiability criterion—about which you are least sure.
