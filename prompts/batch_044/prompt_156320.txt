I am preparing discussion material for an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind, and I find that the standard classroom treatments of the Cartesian theater and Dennett's multiple drafts model tend to stay at a level of abstraction that makes it hard to see where the two frameworks actually diverge in practice. Rather than a broad comparison, I want to examine a specific conceptual issue through a constructed example. Consider an experimental setup in which a subject is briefly exposed to an ambiguous visual stimulus—something like a figure that can be perceived either as a face in profile or as a vase—presented under masked conditions with a very short exposure time, followed by an immediate spontaneous verbal report identifying the stimulus one way. Then, after a brief delay and the introduction of only a minimal contextual cue (for instance, a single word in the background that primes the alternative interpretation), the subject offers a second verbal report identifying the stimulus in the opposite way, without any explicit awareness that a change in report has occurred and without any feeling of having revised an earlier judgment.

Please describe this scenario as a concrete example of the conceptual tension between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model. In particular, explain how a theorist who accepts the Cartesian theater picture—who assumes there is a single central arena in which consciousness happens—would be compelled to interpret the sequence: must they say there was one definite conscious experience that was initially misidentified and then corrected, or that the first experience was replaced by a second in the central "screen"? Contrast this with the interpretation offered by the multiple drafts model, according to which there may be no unified fact about what the subject experienced during the interval, because the two verbal reports represent distinct drafts of narrative that were never assembled into a single canonical stream in a central place. Your answer should make clear why the dispute is not just about the content of the reports but about whether there is a determinate answer to the question "what was it like for the subject between the two reports," and it should highlight the role of temporal binding and narrative consolidation rather than simple perceptual error.
