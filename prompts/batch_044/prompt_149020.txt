I've been thinking a lot lately about whether reading about other people's lives actually teaches you how to live, or if it's just fancy entertainment with old names. I was reading about this greek writer plutarch who wrote paired biographies comparing greek and roman figures, and it got me comparing that to modern books about saints and these debunking profiles of famous people. Here are my gut reactions on when a life-story actually helps morally, and I can't quite see the pattern behind them.

Reading about two people with similar virtues but totally different fates — like when plutarch pairs someone up — makes me think character is separate from luck, and that feels like real learning. But a biography of a saint that only lists miracles and never mentions doubts or mistakes feels like hagiography, just makes me feel inadequate, not taught. Then there's these modern books that take a revered figure and show they were selfish in every small detail — I feel like that just teaches cynicism, not virtue. But a story about someone who was genuinely kind in ordinary moments but also made huge public mistakes seems better than the pure hero version, because I can actually see how virtue and failure sit together in one person. There's also the case where a biography is written so the reader will identify with the subject and copy there choices exactly — I think that's actually dangerous, not learning, because I'm not them and my situation is different. And I'm torn about biographies of people from cultures with values I don't share: sometimes seeing there reasoning teaches me tolerance, other times I think it's just interesting history with no moral use for me.

Oh, and I also think comparing two lives side by side teaches more than reading one alone, but comparing people across centuries feels unfair because the rules were totally different. So is there one principle that gets all these? Maybe it's about whether the biography shows the person's reasoning rather than just outcomes, or whether it invites comparison without demanding imitation? I want to know if there's a coherent account, and if not which verdict I should drop. And what would it say about a biography of a fictional character written with all the psychological detail and realism of a real life — would that teach the same way, or is there something special about knowing the person actually existed?
