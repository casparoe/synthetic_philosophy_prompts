I've been thinking on my own about an old philosophical puzzle—sometimes called the Meno problem—about why actually knowing something seems more valuable than just being right by accident. The classic example is two people heading to Larissa: one knows the road, the other has a true belief about it that happens to be correct. Both arrive, so what's the real difference?

As I've tried to work this out, I've come across what seem like three different explanations, and I'm struggling to tell whether they are the same idea in different clothing or genuinely separate answers. One explanation says the extra value is a kind of achievement: you did the cognitive work, so the success is creditable to you. Another says it has to do with stability—if circumstances had been slightly different, your belief would still have tracked the truth, whereas a lucky belief collapses. A third says knowledge is valuable because it functions differently in your mind: you can build further reasoning and action on it without constant verification, while a bare true belief remains fragile for planning.

Can you explain whether these are competing accounts of the same surplus value, or whether they answer different parts of Plato's original question? I'd especially like to see where they come apart—maybe in a case where someone gets credit for a belief that isn't actually safe, or has a safe belief they can't really use.
