I am writing a novel about a residential retreat called the Sanctuary, where guests voluntarily surrender every quotidian decision—what to eat, when to sleep, with whom to speak—to a director who claims this eliminates the burden of autonomy. The guests report profound contentment; few leave. One protagonist, initially skeptical, finds her anxiety dissolving, yet she feels an inexplicable grief upon discovering that the Sanctuary's weekly council votes are purely ceremonial: residents vote on schedules that are never altered, and this ritual of participation increases their satisfaction more than either genuine self-rule or open coercion would. When she confronts the director, he argues that human beings evolved for coordination rather than sovereignty, and that her grief is not the loss of something intrinsically valuable but a cultural phantom—like pain in a limb that was never truly functional. He offers her a clear choice: leave and reclaim her autonomy, knowing it will likely diminish her well-being, or stay and accept that freedom is only instrumentally useful, and here it serves no purpose. I want this confrontation to carry real philosophical weight rather than mere dramatic tension. Is the director's argument defensible? In particular, does the fact that people seem happier under benevolent dependency, and even prefer the illusion of choice to its reality, undermine the claim that autonomy possesses intrinsic worth? Or can one defend the value of genuine freedom without assuming it must always increase happiness?
