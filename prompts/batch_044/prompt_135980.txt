Consider the following pattern in political life, which I have observed in several democratic contexts but do not have a precise term for. A government official authorizes or executes a policy that violates widely shared moral norms — for example, ordering the targeted killing of a citizen without trial, or negotiating with an organization that employs terror — and does so in pursuit of an important collective good such as national security or peace. In the standard dirty-hands analysis, this creates a moral stain that the official must bear, ideally without losing the capacity to recognize it as wrong. But in the cases that interest me, the stain operates differently: it becomes a public resource. The official's supporters cite the willingness to do what is morally forbidden as evidence of seriousness and resolve; critics who invoke the violated norm are treated as failing to understand the weight of office; and the leader's authority is partly constituted by the visible fact of having crossed a line that ordinary citizens would not cross. The wrongness is not concealed or minimized but displayed, and the display itself produces legitimacy. This seems structurally distinct from hypocrisy, because the leader is not hiding the act, and from the classic dirty-hands formulation, because the moral cost is not a regrettable byproduct but an active component of political authority. Is there an established concept in political ethics or political psychology that captures this specific inversion — where the moral dirt of a necessary wrong is cultivated rather than borne? If there is, how does it differ from the standard formulation in terms of the agent's moral psychology and the audience's evaluation? If there is not, can you define the phenomenon precisely, specify its boundary conditions (for instance, whether it requires democratic accountability or can occur in authoritarian contexts), and distinguish it from nearby ideas such as costly signaling, moral authority derived from sacrifice, or the legitimation of state violence?
