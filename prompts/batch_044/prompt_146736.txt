I am fluent in structural geology and think in terms of stratigraphic superposition, index fossils, unconformities, radiometric brackets, and the evidential weight of a catalogued layer for a tectonic model. Restate the Bayesian problem of old evidence—specifically the puzzle that previously catalogued measurements seem unable to confirm a new formation hypothesis because they already sit near probability one—entirely in that vocabulary, without using Bayesian notation or the language of subjective credence. Translate the roles of prior, likelihood, conditioning, and confirmation into geological analogues (for example, a model's predictive reach over a layer, or the difference between observing a fossil marker and observing that the model requires it). For each element, report what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit (such as treating continuous degrees of belief as binary presence or absence of a marker), and what has no expression at all in stratigraphic practice—especially any notion of a numerical prior assigned before observation. Then render the distinction between learning a layer's properties and learning that the model predicts them using only geological concepts of cataloguing and prediction. Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue exposes a real limitation in how geology weights evidence, or whether it reveals that the Bayesian framing was smuggling in formal apparatus—precise probabilities, subjective priors—that never did substantive work in the scientific reasoning it was meant to capture.
