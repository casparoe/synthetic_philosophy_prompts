I do not have a formal education in philosophy or environmental science, but I have been trying to work out my own view on whether destroying wild habitats is morally wrong. Most people I talk to say yes, because it kills animals and takes away their homes. Yet I keep running into a different line of thought that feels separate from that, and I am unsure whether it has a proper name. The idea is that wild life involves immense suffering—predation, starvation, disease, parasites—and that destroying a habitat, while causing immediate deaths, also prevents a much larger number of future animals from being born into those conditions. From the standpoint of total suffering, then, the loss of the habitat might actually reduce the net amount of harm in the world, even though no living creature is made better off by it. I have noticed this reasoning tends to appear when people discuss insects, rodents, or fish, but almost never when they discuss large predators or endangered mammals, which suggests there may be an unstated boundary. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this argument—evaluating habitat destruction not by the harm done to present inhabitants, but by the suppression of future suffering populations? How is it different from ordinary environmental ethics, from antinatalist reasoning about human reproduction, and from the general claim that nature is cruel? If it lacks a precise name, what features would define its edges and distinguish it from a straightforward welfare calculation applied to ecosystems? I would not be satisfied with only a label; I want to understand what the reasoning is, what it is not, and whether the selective application I have observed is part of the concept itself.
