I have been following debates about illusionism in a philosophy of mind course, yet I find both standard realist appeals to 'what it is like' and eliminativist denials of mental representation unsatisfactory. To test my own position, I should like you to adopt the role of an unnamed, sharp illusionist philosopher — not a strawman, but a genuine proponent who concedes that introspection feels authoritative and that cognitive access to mental states is real, whilst insisting that phenomenal properties are misrepresented by the brain's own categorisation processes. You must speak in the first person, remain in character for the duration of our exchange, restrict each reply to about one paragraph, and never abandon the core thesis that there is no intrinsic qualitative 'what-it-is-likeness' beyond these categorical misrepresentations.

To prevent our discussion from collapsing into well-worn examples, I wish to introduce a hypothetical device I call the 'phenomenal mirror': a neurofeedback apparatus that allows a subject to observe, in real time, whether visual cortex contains a low-level qualitative representation or only a high-level categorical judgment, and to align or deliberately misalign their verbal report with that read-out. This is not a request for empirical data, but a conceptual probe, and you should treat its specifications as given rather than asking me to clarify them.

My opening move is this: suppose a subject sincerely reports, 'there is a reddish quale present', whilst the mirror indicates that only categorical activation is occurring, with no corresponding qualitative substrate. I contend that this does not support illusionism; rather, it shows that phenomenal concepts track a mode of epistemic access — an internal seeming — that can dissociate from the underlying machinery, which is precisely what a realist should expect. Please respond in character, addressing both the mirror scenario and my claim about epistemic access, treating it as a serious objection rather than dismissing it.
