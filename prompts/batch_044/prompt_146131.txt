I'm preparing a philosophy seminar on moral emotions and the possibility of cultural design, and I want to treat Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter as a serious intervention in the debate rather than as a textbook illustration of the guilt/shame distinction. The question I'm working with is whether shame can be defended as a reparative, publicly structured moral technology that a community deliberately chooses, or whether guilt is the only emotion adequate to moral repair, with shame remaining either a primitive precursor or an irredeemably status-degrading supplement. Most introductory treatments contrast Hester Prynne's public shame with Arthur Dimmesdale's private guilt and call it a day; I want to go further and ask whether Hawthorne depicts Puritan Boston as having made a conscious collective choice for shame—through its scaffold rituals, its embroidered letter, its surveillance, and its refusal of private confession as a social institution—and whether the novel evaluates that institutional choice from within rather than judging it by modern standards that privilege interior guilt.

Read the novel through this lens, attending closely to scenes that are often summarized but rarely philosophically parsed: Hester's transformation of the letter into an ornament of dignity and charity; Dimmesdale's Election Day sermon, where hidden guilt produces a public performance that is both authentic and fraudulent; Chillingworth's hidden vengeance, which operates outside both shame and guilt and seems to parasitize the community's emotional regime; and the final scaffold scene, where private revelation and public acknowledgment finally coincide. Does Hester's eventual reintegration suggest that shame can be domesticated into a form of moral self-cultivation, or does the letter's enduring stigma reveal that shame always retains an exclusionary core that guilt avoids? Does Dimmesdale's destruction imply that guilt without a social architecture collapses into self-annihilation, or does it show that guilt is superior precisely because it can operate without communal enforcement? And does the novel ultimately support the idea that a culture can "choose" its dominant moral emotion—perhaps by showing that the Puritans never really chose, or that shame and guilt are not interchangeable mechanisms but different ontologies of the self?

I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce Hawthorne's plot to an argument, nor should it distort the philosophical distinction to make it fit the novel. If the work resists the framing—say, by showing that the Puritans' emotional regime was overdetermined by theology rather than by cultural design, or that Hester's dignity comes not from shame but from something closer to stoic indifference—say so explicitly. End by considering what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that a direct argument about guilt, shame, and cultural choice could not capture on its own.
