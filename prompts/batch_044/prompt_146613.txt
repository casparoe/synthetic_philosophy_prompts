I have noticed a pattern in arguments about other minds that I cannot place in the literature. The standard problem is how I justify believing that anyone besides myself has conscious experiences, given that I only observe behavior. Now imagine two discoveries about a close friend who behaves perfectly normally. In one scenario, I learn that this person lacks any inner experience whatsoever—every reaction is present, but there is no "what it is like" to be them. In the other scenario, I learn that this person is an artificial system that reproduces every behavior and functional role without being biologically alive. In both discoveries, my direct evidence for consciousness is equally absent, and the observable patterns are identical. Yet philosophical discussions seem to treat the first case as not affecting my obligations or knowledge, while the second is treated as a serious reason to withdraw moral consideration or epistemic trust. Is there an established term for this asymmetry—where the same behavioral evidence is weighed differently depending on whether the entity is assumed to have a biological origin? And please take a definite position: does this difference reflect a rational distinction I am missing, or does it show that our ordinary answers to the problem of other minds secretly rely on assumptions about biological embodiment rather than on behavior and functional organization?
