I have recently found myself mediating a disagreement between two research colleagues who have both examined the same ambiguous dataset and arrived at starkly different posterior beliefs—one assigns roughly 85 percent confidence to mechanism A, the other roughly 80 percent to mechanism B. A mutual acquaintance invoked Aumann's agreement result and argued that, if rational agents share a common prior and have common knowledge of their posteriors, they cannot rationally agree to disagree; consequently, he claimed, at least one colleague must be reasoning irrationally or concealing private evidence. This made me realize I do not fully understand what the common prior assumption actually commits one to. Could you explain the core conceptual issue here: is the common prior meant as a normative constraint on rational belief formation, a descriptive claim about how actual agents reason, or merely a modeling convention in formal epistemology and game theory? I am especially interested in how the theorem's conditions interact with real disagreements where priors diverge not because of hidden data but because of different background theoretical commitments—what philosophers of science sometimes call theory-laden observation. In particular, can one maintain a non-circular distinction between disagreement that stems from genuinely different priors (which the framework seems to allow) and disagreement that stems from different processing of shared evidence (which it appears to rule out under common priors), once the interpretation of that shared evidence is itself shaped by the very priors in question? Please clarify whether applying the theorem to my colleagues' dispute is legitimate or whether it subtly presupposes that all rational divergence must trace back to information asymmetry rather than to rationally permissible differences in prior theoretical orientation.
