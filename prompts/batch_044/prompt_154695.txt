I've been toying with a pretty specific reading of Hegel's master–slave dialectic — not the usual intersubjective recognition story everyone rolls out, but that it's really a model for how instutional recognition works. Specifically, that any stable recognition structure needs one party to bear the risk of anihilation (the slave) and one to withhold it (the master). Apply that to modern citizenship: citizens get legal personhood only because the state monopolises violence they don't have to risk themselves, so citizenship is basically outsourced master-recognition with the police doing the risking. What would actually count against this? Not the standard "you misread Phenomenology" reply — I mean which historical cases of recognition that happen without violence, which features of modern legal status that don't fit the risk-structure, or which results in social ontology (say about group recognition or institutional facts) it can't accommodate. Which of these would defenders concede are real problems, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank them fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view is well-insulated or just stubborn. Also, does it risk collapsing into a rival theory of state legitimacy?
