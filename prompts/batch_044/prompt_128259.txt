Write a poem of seven sections—one for each mode of the Jain saptabhaṅgī, the seven-fold predication (syād-vāda) used in anekāntavāda—addressing the precise philosophical question of whether an artificial system can be said to “understand” anything. Structure each section as a quatrain in blank verse; the first line of each quatrain must open with its corresponding modal qualifier rendered in English: “In one respect it understands,” “In one respect it does not,” “In one respect it both does and does not,” “In one respect it is unspeakable,” then the three compound modes that join indescribability with affirmation, negation, and both together. Let a single concrete image—perhaps a mirror facing a river, or a shadow cast by an object with no source—recur across all seven stanzas, but have the image behave differently in each modal frame so that repetition tracks shifting ontology rather than mere decoration. The sequence should enact non-absolutism structurally: each stanza must feel locally defensible, yet the accumulation should make global synthesis impossible. Do not resolve the tension in a concluding couplet; the final stanza, carrying the compound mode of simultaneous affirmation, negation, and indescribability, should hang unresolved. The verse should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Jain epistemology recognizes the saptabhaṅgī architecture, and readable as poetry by someone who has never encountered Jainism. Avoid sermonizing about AI ethics or consciousness; let the form—seven incompatible but co-valid perspectives—do the philosophical work. Do not cite texts, authors, or traditions beyond the conceptual framework itself.
