The Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism—sparked by Devlin’s *The Enforcement of Morals* and answered in Hart’s *Law, Liberty, and Morality*—has been replayed in jurisprudence and political philosophy for decades without, to my eye, any genuine movement. The familiar choreography is well known: Devlin argues that a shared morality is the invisible bond holding society together and that tolerating private immorality risks collective disintegration; Hart distinguishes positive from critical morality, invokes a harm principle, and insists that legal coercion requires demonstrable injury rather than mere popular disgust. Every counter-argument has been filed, every distinction refined, and yet seminar rooms still reach the same stalemate.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock runs deeper than the standard framing suggests. Rather than a direct clash over whether law should enforce morality, the two sides may be operating with incompatible social ontologies: a constitutive view, on which shared moral sentiment is a pre-political condition of social existence, and an institutional view, on which social order is an achievement of cooperative rules that can survive deep pluralism. If this is correct, then much of the dispute about “morality” and “harm” is verbal packaging for a divergence about what a society is and whether cohesion is causally prior to individual rights or constructed through them.

Please diagnose the stall. Is the disagreement substantive—say, over hidden empirical claims about whether societies actually collapse when private vices are tolerated—or is it verbal, with “morality” and “harm” doing different work in each framework? Are Hart and Devlin answering different questions under a single heading: one about the normative legitimacy of state coercion, the other about the empirical sociology of social cohesion? Or does the impasse rest on bedrock intuitions about liberty and collective identity that neither side can argue for? Be precise about what each position actually commits to, and do not treat either as a straw figure.
