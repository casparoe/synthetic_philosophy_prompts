I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I have been thinking about normative architecture—specifically the idea that a principle might not directly command an action, but instead specify an idealized deliberation procedure that outputs the correct action. This is often discussed under the label of indirect normativity. I have built an argument that seems to show that if every norm functions this way, the framework collapses into circularity or emptiness, yet I cannot locate the exact misstep.

(1) If a principle is only indirectly normative, then its entire normative force consists in requiring an agent to run an idealized deliberation procedure P—complete information, full coherence, elimination of bias—and then perform whatever P outputs. The principle itself never directly mandates any particular action.

(2) Now consider the meta-principle M: use P whenever you face a practical decision. If M is also only indirectly normative, then applying M requires running P to determine what M demands. But P is defined by M, so running P to determine M's content already presupposes knowledge of what P is.

(3) To avoid this circular dependency, M must specify P directly, without itself being processed through P. In other words, M must be directly normative: it gives the content of P immediately, not via an output.

(4) Once we admit that M is direct, we have at least one principle whose normative force does not derive from an idealized procedure. But indirect normativity was presented as a universal account of how all practical norms function.

(5) One might reply that M is not a practical norm but a structural or architectural rule—like a bootstrap compiler that is not compiled by itself, or a lazy-evaluated thunk that is never forced. If so, then indirect normativity applies only to first-order norms, while meta-rules sit outside the system.

(6) If the domain is restricted in this way, then for any practical question that touches the boundary—such as whether to revise P itself—there must be a direct norm governing that revision. Otherwise we face the same circularity at a higher level, with no base case to force evaluation.

(7) Therefore, either indirect normativity is false as a universal claim, because M and its successors must be direct, or it applies only within a bounded subsystem anchored by direct norms. But the original motivation for the view was to explain all practical reasoning without appealing to direct, action-guiding principles. So the view either collapses into admitting direct norms everywhere or becomes an empty framework with no forcing condition—like a pure function with no argument to evaluate.

(8) I find (7) unacceptable: a normative theory that eliminates all direct guidance cannot actually guide an agent. So I want to reject something earlier. I would prefer to keep (1) and (3), since they seem like definitional truths about indirectness and circularity. But I suspect the error is hidden in (5) or (6), perhaps an illicit shift from this particular meta-principle being structural to every boundary case requiring directness.

Please tell me which step I should reject, explain why that step looked plausible despite being wrong, and describe what I have to pay conceptually if I give it up. In particular, does rejecting that step save universal indirect normativity, or does it merely relocate the direct norms to the architecture of the system?
