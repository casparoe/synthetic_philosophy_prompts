I am in the early stages of a novel that moves between the private notebooks of a young American prosecutor at the Palace of Justice in 1945 and the present-day trial of an aging militia commander before an international tribunal in The Hague, and I am trying to understand whether the moral architecture of punishment remains continuous across that half-century or whether it fractures under the weight of institutional spectacle. To that end, could you analyze the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg—held from 1945 to 1946—not as a historical event I need summarized, but as a live case study in the philosophy of punishment, tested against both retributivism and consequentialism?

Please map the actual agents onto the theoretical structure: the defendants facing capital sentences, the Allied judges who framed jurisdiction as a novel legal creation, the absent victims whose written testimony substituted for presence, and the executioners who carried out the sentences that followed the verdicts in 1946. For each framework, say precisely what it prescribes they should have done—how it weights desert against deterrence, individual culpability against collective warning, and whether it permits the tribunal’s hybrid identity as both court and pedagogical stage. Then identify which features of the episode each theory genuinely illuminates—perhaps retributivism’s discomfort with acquitting defendants whose guilt was manifest but whose jurisdictional status shielded them, or consequentialism’s ease in justifying the trial as a precedent for international law—and which features it misdescribes or renders silent, especially the strange temporal asymmetry by which punishment was meant to address a past that no longer existed while constructing a legal future that had not yet arrived. I am particularly interested in whether Nuremberg reveals that both theories depend on an unacknowledged third axis—something like the semiotics of institutional memory, where the sentence functions less as retribution or deterrence than as a durable inscription of state legitimacy. Does the episode ultimately confirm the standard dichotomy, fracture it, or quietly reformulate it in ways that a novelist trying to render two courtrooms across time ought to understand?
