Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find myself returning repeatedly to Daniel Dennett’s framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—particularly as it applies to biological and cognitive systems. At a recent colloquium on philosophy of science, two colleagues—an evolutionary psychologist and a philosopher of biology—became entangled in a dispute about whether patterns identified through the intentional stance ever possess a kind of predictive autonomy that neither the physical nor the design stance can replicate, or whether they are always, in principle, reducible to lower-level descriptions. I should emphasize that I am merely reporting their exchange as a bystander; I have no settled view, and I would value a careful adjudication.

The first interlocutor, Dr. Alina Reeves, opened by defending the claim that evolutionary convergence—say, the independent emergence of camera-type eyes in cephalopods and vertebrates—constitutes a real pattern only under the design stance. At the physical stance, she argued, one finds only disparate atomic and molecular configurations with no intrinsic unity; the functional equivalence that makes the pattern salient is introduced by an observer adopting the design stance, not discovered in the substrate itself. She therefore concluded that the design stance does not merely provide a convenient heuristic but actually constitutes the ontological conditions under which the pattern exists. The second interlocutor, Dr. Samuel Okonkwo, responded that this conflates epistemic accessibility with ontological dependence. If a pattern supports robust counterfactual predictions—if altering the design-level parameters systematically alters outcomes in ways that physical-level descriptions miss only because they are too unwieldy, not because they are incapable in principle—then the pattern is real and physically grounded, with the design stance serving as a compressed representation rather than a constitutive framework.

Reeves countered that Okonkwo’s appeal to counterfactual robustness smuggles in functional criteria that are themselves stance-dependent. To specify which physical alterations are relevant without invoking design-level functions, she insisted, requires an antecedent specification of the system’s purpose or goal—precisely the move that introduces the intentional stance. She then introduced what she called an error-management asymmetry observed in human threat-detection cognition: organisms systematically exhibit false-positive biases—treating ambiguous stimuli as threats—that appear irrational from a design-stance analysis of neural efficiency, yet become predictively coherent only when one attributes an intentional-level goal of survival optimization under uncertainty. Okonkwo replied that this asymmetry is fully explicable at the design stance as a cost-benefit calibration of signal-detection thresholds, and that invoking an intentional goal adds no predictive content that a properly parameterized design-level model could not, in principle, deliver. He challenged Reeves to identify a single predictive success of the intentional stance that could not, even in principle, be reconstructed by a sufficiently complex design-level or physical-level account.

Reeves offered one such case: in certain collective decision-making contexts, groups exhibit preference reversals under time pressure that violate the design-level predictions of rational-choice architectures, yet align precisely with intentional-level attributions of face-saving or social-commitment preservation—patterns she argued are not merely heuristic glosses but structurally necessary for prediction. Okonkwo maintained that these reversals are emergent properties of multi-agent design-level dynamics, and that labeling them intentional explains nothing that a dynamic systems model of interaction rules cannot. I found myself unable to determine whether Reeves had genuinely identified a predictive surplus belonging uniquely to the intentional stance, or whether Okonkwo’s reductionist commitment was defensible but empirically empty given our current computational limitations. Would you please adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating the strength of each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, identifying where both may be missing a crucial distinction, and indicating who, if anyone, carries the stronger overall position?
