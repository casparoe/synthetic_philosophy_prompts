I'm writing a feature on philosophical theories that fall apart when real organizations have to act fast, and I've been fixating on the Challanger disaster in January '86 — you know, the O-ring warnings, the Thiokol engineers saying no, the NASA managers saying go anyway, and the explosion. I want to analyze it through indirect normativity, specifically the view that what you ought to do isn't determined directly by the situation, but through some idealized deliberative procedue — like reasoning from behind a veil where you don't know whether you're the engineer with the data or the manager with the budget pressure.

But here's the part I'm making up to test the theory: I think indirect normativity might need what I'd call a 'threshold of institutional transparency' to function at all. If the organizational structure hides the relevant facts from the deliberator — or if the deliberator's incentives are structurally different from the people actually deciding — then the indirect norm collapses into whatever power dynamics are already in place. So in the Challenger case, who exactly is the 'idealized' agent? The individual engineer? The management chain? The protocol itself? And if we idealize away the schedule pressure and political incentives, do we still get a 'no launch' verdict, or does the theory stay silent?

Map it out for me: the players, the options they actually had, what indirect normativity predicts about the call, and where the mapping gets messy or the theory just doesn't speak. Also tell me whether this case helps indirect normativity — maybe it shows institutions need transparency for the norm to apply — or whether it reveals that the theory was never meant for high-stakes organizational failure in the first place. I'm not asking for a bibliography, just a clear-headed analysis I can use for my draft. Oh, and I keep mixing up whether this counts as 'indirect' or just 'procedural' normativity, so feel free to correct my framing if I'm off.
