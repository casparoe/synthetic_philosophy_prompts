I have been turning over two puzzles in my head that feel like the same knot seen from different angles. One is the preface paradox: an author can believe every single sentence in her book is true and still rationally believe the book contains errors somewhere. The other is the lottery paradox: I can believe of each ticket that it will lose, yet I know one must win, so I cannot consistently believe they all lose. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains, the standard tercets and closing quatrain—in which one refrain voices the preface admission (for instance, "somewhere in these pages a falsehood lives") and the other voices the lottery belief ("this ticket will not win"). Let the refrains take turns through the tercets, then stand together unreconciled in the final couplet, so the form's repetition enacts the accumulation of individually tolerable risks into a collective contradiction. Do not resolve the tension; let it hang open. The language should stay plain, without technical jargon, since I am not a specialist—just someone who keeps returning to this—and the logic should be accurate enough that someone who knows both paradoxes recognizes the structure immediately.
