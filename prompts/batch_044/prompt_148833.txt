In philosophical debates about downward causation and whether high-level patterns can genuinely influence their physical substrate, I keep running into references to Douglas Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment — a hypothetical billiard-ball universe in which substrate-level entities, called simmballs, are arranged so that their collisions trace out symbolic-level computations, called simms. A slogan that gets repeated in these discussions is: "The simms don't push the simmballs around; only the simmballs push each other." The people who invoke it seem to treat it as a knockdown argument that symbolic-level causation is an illusion — that any explanation appealing to symbols or meanings is causally redundant, and that the real causal work is always done by the substrate-level physics.

I'm not convinced I know what precise claim the slogan is making, and I want to avoid treating it as a black box. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might stand for — for instance, causal exclusion (that substrate-level causes leave no causal work for symbol-level ones), explanatory redundancy (that substrate descriptions are always sufficient), supervenience without downward causation, or ontological priority — and evaluate each one? For each reading, say whether it's plausible in general, whether the careenium setup actually demonstrates it or merely assumes it, and whether it actually supports the stronger conclusion that high-level descriptions are never causally necessary. I'm particularly interested in whether there's a trivial reading that just restates supervenience, versus a substantive metaphysical claim that needs independent defense, and whether the engineered nature of the careenium — where symbol-tracing dynamics are deliberately built into the collision rules — undermines the slogan's use as an argument against downward causation in naturally occurring systems.
