I have been thinking about guilt and shame without any formal training, and I keep returning to a distinction that feels important: guilt behaves like a private ledger, where each wrong creates a specific debt to a particular person that can be settled and cleared, while shame behaves like a public map, where a wrong redraws your visible position in a shared social space and no repayment can fully erase the mark because the mark lives in other people's eyes. From this I have developed a further thought: a culture that tries to deliberately choose guilt over shame must invent an unstable institution. It needs people to feel they have wronged specific others—so that private repayment is meaningful—while simultaneously preventing anyone else from seeing that they have been marked, because visibility is what activates shame. That middle ground seems hard to maintain, but I want a group to actually feel why it collapses.

Please design a 45-minute classroom activity for about sixteen adults—perhaps in a community workshop or continuing-education seminar—that makes participants run into this problem themselves before it is named. They should come away seeing why a culture cannot simply vote for guilt over shame without either smuggling shame back in through visibility or destroying the social acknowledgment that makes guilt meaningful.

Organize your response in these sections, roughly these lengths:

1. Conceptual Frame (around 150 words): Lay out the ledger-versus-map idea and the visibility paradox in plain language, without naming specific philosophers or texts.
2. Setup and Materials (around 150 words): Describe the physical layout, roles, cards or tokens used, and a payoff scheme that requires no real money but creates real small stakes.
3. Rules and Flow (around 250 words): Give step-by-step instructions for the 45 minutes. The rules should force participants into choices where they must either expose a wrong to settle a private debt—thereby triggering public map-redrawing—or conceal it to protect their social position, which breaks the acknowledgment guilt seems to require.
4. Debrief and Mapping (around 200 words): Map specific moments in the activity onto the ledger, the map, the visibility paradox, and the impossibility of pure cultural choice.
5. Predictable Failures and Limits (around 150 words): Say what will predictably go wrong the first time you run this, how to recover, and which feature of actual moral emotion—perhaps its involuntary or embodied quality—this staged simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.

Keep the tone formal and polished, but preserve the voice of someone who worked out these ideas independently.
