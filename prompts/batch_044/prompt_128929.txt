I'm a final-year undergraduate in political philosophy writing my honors thesis prospectus, and I'd like to develop a research proposal on a problem that emerged in my transitional justice seminar: when a democratic successor state issues a formal apology for wrongs committed by a prior authoritarian regime, is the apology normatively coherent only if the state treats itself as a continuous moral agent across constitutional rupture, or can it instead function as what I'll call a "prospective covenant renewal"—a performative act that deliberately refuses retrospective moral accounting yet still binds the present polity to the victims? The existing literature tends to classify state apologies either as instruments of retrospective justice that settle an inherited debt, or as purely symbolic gestures with no normative structure, but neither framework explains why victims and civil society often demand an apology precisely in cases where the successor state denies that it inherits the culpability of its predecessor. I want the proposal to frame this tension not as a political puzzle but as a conceptual one about institutional identity: whether diachronic collective responsibility requires ontological continuity of the state, and whether an apology can be authoritative without that continuity.

The proposal should contain a precise statement of the question, an explanation of why standard accounts of collective agency and transitional justice leave it open, and a clear method—likely conceptual analysis of institutional identity across constitutional breaks, paired with a philosophical (not empirical) examination of apology texts that illustrate the retrospective/prospective distinction. It should specify the available conclusions, the considerations that would decide between them, and an honest assessment of what could make the project too large for a single semester. I also want it to say what a successful result looks like—perhaps a taxonomy of apology-types mapped onto different theories of state continuity—versus what counts as failure, such as if the "covenant renewal" framing collapses back into standard retrospective justice without resolving the authority problem. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will compile sources separately, and I want the conceptual framing to stand independently. The proposal should treat well-known historical cases only as background illustrations rather than as data, keeping the central engine of the argument the conflict between diachronic responsibility and constitutional rupture.
