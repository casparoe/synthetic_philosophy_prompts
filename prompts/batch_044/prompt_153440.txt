I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind preparing a seminar contribution on transcendental arguments—specifically, the move from solipsistic conditions to the constitutive necessity of the other—rather than on any particular canonical text. For this session I want a poem in strict sestina form: six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi, using the standard rotation of six end-words. The end-words, in this order for stanza one, must be: I, other, world, condition, recognition, doubt.

The poem should perform the argument structurally. Stanzas one and two should present the "I" attempting to secure the "world" through empirical inference, only to find that "doubt" about the "other" undermines the coherence of that world. Stanzas three through five should shift the status of "recognition": it is not observed but is a "condition" of experience itself, so that the "other" is revealed as constitutive rather than derived. The envoi should bring "I" and "other" into direct adjacency—not fused, but necessarily co-present—while preserving all six end-words across its three lines.

Crucially, the philosophical accuracy should be such that a specialist recognizes the transcendental pattern (the other is not inferred but is a condition for the possibility of the world and of doubt) rather than a mere narrative about loneliness. At the same time, the poem should work as verse: the repetition and transformation of the end-words should carry the conceptual weight, with rhyme and rhythm serving the argument rather than decorating it. Please avoid named references to specific philosophers or texts, and do not frame the poem as exegesis; let the form itself do the transcendental work. I want the result precise enough for a philosophy audience but fully readable as poetry.
