I have just spent a long and somewhat frustrating evening arguing with a close friend about firearms regulation, and I am still trying to resolve an apparent inconsistency that neither of us could settle. In the United Kingdom, the licensing regime distinguishes sharply between shotguns and other firearms such as handguns and rifles: a shotgun certificate is obtainable with a lower threshold of justification, whereas possession of other firearms requires a stricter licence for which the applicant must demonstrate a specific, approved purpose, such as target shooting at a recognised club. In the United States, by contrast, constitutional jurisprudence since the early twenty-first century is frequently interpreted as protecting an individual right that exists independently of government grant, yet federal statute permanently disqualifies broad categories of people—including convicted felons, those adjudicated as mentally defective, and unlawful users of controlled substances—from possessing firearms, often without providing a clear procedural route for the restoration of that right comparable to the reinstatement of voting rights in certain states. At the same time, liberal societies that apply these stringent prior restraints to weapon possession generally impose no equivalent licensing requirement or demonstration of "good reason" upon activities that carry comparable or greater statistical risk to others, such as operating a motor vehicle or publishing inflammatory political material.

What philosophical theory of rights, harm, and state authority does this patchwork of practices actually presuppose? Does it treat the right to bear arms as a fundamental liberty that the state may only restrict ex post for wrongdoing, in which case the American constitutional rhetoric is in tension with categorical, status-based exclusions? Or does it presuppose that firearm possession creates a distinctive kind of standing risk that justifies ex ante filtering—a predictive, risk-management framework—in which case the strict UK licensing system is the more honest expression of the underlying principle, and the American framing is largely ornamental? I should like you to reconstruct the implicit theory that best explains why some jurisdictions apply a purpose-based filter, others apply categorical bans based on status, and others apply neither, and to say whether these practices are genuinely committed to one view or are philosophically underdetermined, compatible with rival accounts of liberty, responsibility, and public safety. Furthermore, if the underlying commitment is indeed that the state may prohibit possession in order to prevent speculative future harm rather than to punish past offences, what implications would that have for the consistency of a legal system that does not apply the same predictive logic to equally dangerous instruments?

Please provide your answer in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the conceptual reconstruction rather than scholarly apparatus.
