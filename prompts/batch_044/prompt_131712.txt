As a retired physicist now serving on a three-person technical review panel for a regional water-management authority, I have recently begun studying philosophy of logic—specifically dialetheism and the paraconsistent tradition—and I wish to turn those theoretical commitments into an operational tool for our practical work. We meet twice monthly, have forty-five minutes per report, and must sign off before any funding is released; none of us is a trained logician. Our consultant reports increasingly contain direct logical contradictions that are not mere typographical errors: for instance, one hydrological model in a report predicts aquifer depletion by 2030, while another model in the same document predicts sustained levels, with both presented as empirically supported conclusions. Under classical logic we reject the report, but I am convinced that some contradictions may be genuinely dialetheic—true contradictions in reality or in our best representations—while others are merely inconsistent but non-explosive and require paraconsistent management rather than dismissal.

Please design a reusable decision procedure that my panel can run in under forty-five minutes. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, indicate where it terminates in outright rejection, where it terminates in conditional approval with dual-track administrative management, and where it must hand the decision to the authority because the issue is one of values or statutory interpretation rather than logical form. It must distinguish contradictions arising from representational error, those arising from genuinely conflicting but equally valid measurements, and those indicating a dialetheic state of affairs that must be accommodated administratively rather than resolved. The procedure should also flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where it is likely to misfire.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a climate-adaptation report that asserts both that a coastal barrier must be raised immediately and that it must not be raised, with each assertion backed by peer-reviewed risk assessments; (2) an ecological survey that assigns a single population to two mutually exclusive taxonomic categories with equal evidentiary weight; and (3) a funding proposal that contradicts itself on whether a remediation technology is legally permitted, where the contradiction tracks a genuine ambiguity in the governing statute rather than an empirical disagreement. Include one case—likely case (1) or (3)—where the procedure yields an answer the panel will actively dislike, such as requiring simultaneous approval of contradictory actions, and explain how the panel should document that outcome. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do, especially with respect to contradictions that depend on normative interpretation, temporal indexing of predictions, or hidden premises about measurement error.
