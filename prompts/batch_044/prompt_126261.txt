I've been drafting a little essay for my reading group on whether Plutarch's Parallel Lives actually teaches morality or just gives us a fancy mirror. My argument is that he isn't writing hagiography exactly, but he isn't writing neutral history either — he's doing what I call 'diagnostic biography', where the small anecdote (Alcibiades cutting the tail off his expensive dog to distract gossip, Alexander refusing water in the Gedrosian desert) isn't there to prove the subject was virtuous, but to show the exact point where character fractures under pressure.

I contrast this with later hagiography — say Gregory of Tours or modern 'great man' biography — where the anecdote is always exemplary: it proves the virtue was present all along. In Plutarch, the same anecdote can be read against the grain. The water refusal shows Alexander's solidarity with his men, but also his reckless theatricality; Alcibiades' cunning with the dog is the same trait that lets him manipulate crowds and betray alliances. So the moral learning isn't 'copy this man' but 'watch how this trait turns toxic in this context'.

I'm not convinced I've nailed the distinction, and I think I might be overreading the Gedrosian episode — some scholars treat it as pure propaganda. I might also be unfair to hagiography; perhaps Gregory's miracle stories serve a diagnostic function too?

Could you comment on whether the essay's central distinction holds water, whether I've mischaracterised hagiographic anecdote, and whether there's a stronger example than the dog-tail one?
