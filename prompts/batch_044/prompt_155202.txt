My colleague Kessler works on indirect normativity — she thinks norms don't bite actions directly but only through some failure in an idealized deliberation setup. She's taken four stances. First, a factory hides pollution data from a poor adjacent neighborhood and dumps; she called it a clear indirect violation. Second, a legislature passes a carbon tax after full open debate, despite fossil-fuel workers hating it; she said no violation, the deliberative origin was sound. Third, a psych study uses mild deception on general adults, debriefs immediately, minimal risk; she said that's fine, no indirect norm broken. Fourth, a city takes homes via eminent domain for a private shopping center; she said violates, since homeowners never got real deliberative standing.

Now she's weighing a new thing: climate activists blockade a coal plant knowing it'll cause blackouts in a nearby low-income area. They ran open forums, invited those residents, got explicit "no" answers, and went ahead with non-violent direct action anyway.

What'd she say? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rules. I see at least three that cover her four calls: the "consent-under-parity" rule, the "mirror-rotation of practical identity" rule, and the "procedural-reflexivity" rule. They all fit the first four, but they split on the blockade. For each, what's the verdict? Which one matches her overall approach best and why? And what's the one further case that'd actually separate them?
