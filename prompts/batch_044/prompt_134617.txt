I am attempting to clarify my own reflective equilibrium on moral responsibility, particularly with respect to the worry—familiar from Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui in a manner no agent can satisfy. My verdicts on the following concrete cases are mostly consistent, but one pair strikes me as genuinely difficult to reconcile.

Case 1: An individual develops an aggressive, acquisitive character solely through genetic predisposition and an abusive upbringing over which they had no control; as an adult they rob a bank. My verdict: they are not morally responsible in the deep sense, though institutional responses may still be warranted.

Case 2: An individual with benign origins and full cognitive capacity deliberately, over ten years, cultivates an identical aggressive, acquisitive character as a philosophical project; they then rob a bank for precisely the reasons they have made their own. My verdict: they are fully morally responsible.

Case 3: A previously gentle individual develops a brain tumour that eliminates empathic inhibition; they rob a bank without grasping the wrongness of the act. My verdict: clearly not responsible.

Case 4: An individual employs a long-term "autopoietic value-restructuring protocol"—a hypothetical iterative procedure in which one's present evaluative weights are used, with no external input, to revise and eventually replace one's entire motivational architecture—until they settle into an aggressive, acquisitive disposition identical to that in Case 2; they then rob a bank. My verdict: they are not morally responsible, despite having authored every stage of the transformation.

Case 5: An individual's values gradually drift through years of unexamined exposure to peer groups and algorithmic recommendation, until they regard fraud as unproblematic and commit it. My verdict: their responsibility is significantly diminished relative to Case 2, though perhaps not wholly extinguished.

The tension I cannot shake lies between Case 2 and Case 4: both agents appear to have authored their characters through sustained, conscious effort, yet I judge responsibility present in the former and absent in the latter. Meanwhile, Case 4 and Case 1 share a negative verdict despite radically different origins—one exogenously determined, the other endogenously looped.

Is there a single principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what it predicts for the following unlisted scenario: an agent takes a single, irreversible neurocognitive intervention that permanently implants the aggressive values of Case 2, having chosen the intervention with full understanding but without any iterative self-revision; they then rob a bank.

Finally, please be honest about which of my verdicts—particularly the divergence between Case 2 and Case 4—must be abandoned if we prefer the simpler account, and whether that simpler account forces any surprising revision to Case 5. Please reply in British English.
