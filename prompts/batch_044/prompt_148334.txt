As a journalist covering moral philosophy, I have been tracking a long-running stall in care ethics. One camp treats care as an irreducibly relational practice grounded in specific attentional commitments to particular others; the opposing camp attempts to formalize care into distributive principles that could govern institutional design, such as health-care rationing or workplace flexibility mandates. Both sides invoke the same canonical cases—parental obligation, nursing practice, sustained friendship—yet neither has moved in years. Is this disagreement substantive, perhaps over whether moral attention can be scaled without losing its ethical character? Or is it verbal, with one side asking what responsive care phenomenologically requires and the other asking how a valued practice should be allocated? Alternatively, might the stall signal that care ethics is being asked to perform two incompatible jobs—articulating a phenomenology of attentive responsiveness and deriving action-guiding norms for policy—under a single heading? Offer a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, supported by what each side actually says, and indicate what, if anything—whether a sharper conceptual distinction, an empirical study of caregiver reasoning, or a reframing of the question—would count as genuine progress.
