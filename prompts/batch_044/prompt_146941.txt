I've been trying to work through an old Chinese philosophical puzzle I came across — the White Horse Dialogue, where the argument is made that a white horse is not actually a horse. I brought it up with two friends over coffee, and they fell into a sharp disagreement about what the argument is even doing, which I still haven't sorted out for myself.

Maya said she reads it as an ontological claim about how properties relate to things. She explained that "horse" names the pure form or shape, without any color, while "white horse" is a particular that carries both shape and whiteness. Because the universal "horse" lacks color altogether, combining it with "white" produces a different kind of entity, which is why asking for a horse won't deliver a white horse and asking for a white horse won't deliver just a horse. She said this shows properties are genuinely separable from their bearers in reality, not just in our descriptions, and that the School of Names was essentially probing this gap between "this particular" (zhi) and "the thing in general" (wu).

Jordan pushed back that she's importing modern metaphysics into what is really a linguistic point. He said Gongsun Long is only showing that compound names have stricter satisfaction conditions than simple ones: anything with the right shape counts as a horse, but only things with that shape plus whiteness count as white horses. The argument, on his view, is about how reference works — that our terms apply differently depending on specificity — and not about reality splitting into layers. He added that if Maya were right, then "brown horse" and "black horse" would also fail to be horses, leaving the category "horse" with no actual members, which is absurd and shows she's misreading language as ontology.

Maya replied that Jordan's "empty category" objection misses the point, because the dialogue isn't about taxonomy but about logical distinction: the term "horse" applies across colors, while "white horse" applies to a subset. She then returned to the zhi/wu distinction, saying Jordan collapses that gap by treating names as just labeling pre-existing buckets, when the School of Names was actually showing that pointing at a particular and grasping the universal are different operations.

Jordan countered that invoking zhi versus wu actually supports his reading, since those terms are about reference and indication, not about carving nature at the joints. He said Maya wants the dialogue to be deep metaphysics because it sounds more profound, but the simpler linguistic interpretation explains why the argument was controversial in its own context: it was challenging the Mohist assumption that names map neatly onto unified realities. He concluded that the separability is in our language, not in the world.

Can you walk through their exchange move by move and say who's making the stronger point at each turn — not just who wins overall? I'm particularly unsure whether Maya's appeal to the zhi/wu distinction strengthens her case or just shifts the debate onto different ground without resolving it. And is there a coherent third interpretation — one that takes the separability of form and color seriously without reducing it to either pure ontology or pure linguistics — that both of them are missing? If so, what would it say about the case?
