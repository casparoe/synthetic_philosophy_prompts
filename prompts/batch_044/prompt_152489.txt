I'm preparing a short module on the sociology and philosophy of science, and I've become convinced by a particular version of the inevitability thesis regarding simultaneous discovery. The view I want to examine is not the crude claim that calculus and evolutionary theory were simply "in the air," but a stronger structural claim: that the specific theoretical architectures developed by Newton and Leibniz, and by Darwin and Wallace, were overdetermined by the available mathematical and biological problem-spaces, such that any competent practitioner situated in those conceptual networks would have converged on functionally equivalent formalisms and mechanisms within a generation. On this view, priority disputes are essentially historiographic fictions; attributing calculus to Newton rather than Leibniz, or natural selection to Darwin rather than Wallace, matters only for national prestige or biography, not for the subsequent trajectory of science, because the notational and conceptual differences between their respective systems are superficial variations on a single underlying structure that was historically necessary.

But I'm uneasy about whether I've insulated this view too thoroughly. I want an honest map of its exposure: what would actually count against this stronger inevitability claim—not merely the standard observation that two people arrived at similar ideas, but evidence or arguments showing that the specific content of a discovery matters causally for what follows? For instance, if Leibniz's notation and differential framework genuinely enabled lines of analytical development that Newton's fluxional approach obstructed for decades, or if Darwin's theoretical package—including sexual selection and a provisional mechanism for heredity—constituted a research program that Wallace's more austere, biogeographically focused mechanism could not replicate, then the assumption of functional equivalence collapses. Similarly, if the contingent biographical events that shaped these discoveries—say, the particular intellectual path that led Darwin to Malthusian reasoning, or the specific circumstances of Wallace's insight during illness in the Malay Archipelago—turn out to be causally irreplaceable rather than merely triggering, then the structural claim is seriously weakened. I also worry that the view risks unfalsifiability: any divergence between the two discoveries can be dismissed as surface noise, while any convergence is cited as proof of inevitability. Is that insulation a theoretical virtue, indicating a robust core, or a vice that renders the claim empirically empty?

Please distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from those that are merely awkward. Which of these challenges would a committed structuralist concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they absorb by further qualifying the thesis? And if you conclude that the position is well protected, say explicitly whether that protection is a sign of strength or of emptiness—particularly given that the claim is meant to guide how we write the history of science, not merely to describe it.
