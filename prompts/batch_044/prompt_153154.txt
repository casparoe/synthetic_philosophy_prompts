I am applying for a one-semester independent research fellowship and need a two-page proposal on a conceptual tension in Keynes's vision of post-scarcity well-being. Keynes distinguishes between needs that are capable of full satisfaction—those tied to basic subsistence and comfort—and needs that are structurally insatiable because they depend on social comparison and status. From this distinction he derives a well-known prediction: once productivity eliminates scarcity in the first domain, rational societies will redirect labor toward leisure, culture, and living well rather than continuing to accumulate wealth for comparative advantage. The problem I want to investigate is not whether the prediction was empirically accurate, but whether the conceptual boundary Keynes relies on is stable. He never specifies the mechanism that prevents the second domain from colonizing the first: once basic needs are met, why wouldn't their satisfaction itself become a positional good—through conspicuous leisure, cultural capital, or experiential consumption—thereby dissolving the very separation his optimism requires?

My proposal is to reconstruct Keynes's implicit boundary-condition as a normative claim about human nature rather than a psychological forecast, then ask whether that claim can be sustained without metaphysical commitments he silently borrows from a strand of liberal humanism that treats certain goods as inherently non-comparative. I will examine whether the boundary can instead be maintained by purely institutional mechanisms—progressive taxation, working-time regulation, or norms of sufficiency—without any substantive theory of flourishing. The method is conceptual analysis paired with internal critique: I will formalize the two-layer model, identify its minimal normative premises, and test whether a deflationary, preference-satisfaction account of well-being can reproduce Keynes's optimism about post-scarcity leisure. The payoff for economists and policy designers would be a sharper diagnostic: they could see whether Keynes's vision depends on a view of human nature they might reject, or on institutional conditions they could actually build.

The deliverable will be a conceptual map with four parts: (1) the reconstructed boundary-condition between absolute and relative needs; (2) the minimal normative or institutional commitments required to keep the categories separate; (3) an assessment of whether preference-based well-being theories can support that separation; and (4) an explicit failure condition—namely, that the boundary collapses into a single continuum of positional goods, in which case Keynes's vision of a society that stops working once it is comfortable loses its normative grounding. This project avoids archival research, empirical economic forecasting, and bibliographic surveys; it requires only careful engagement with the primary essay and with contemporary debates in well-being theory. Given these limits it is feasible within one semester, though I will flag the risk that the metaphysical commitments turn out to be more deeply embedded in the text than a single term allows me to extract, in which case the project would need to be scaled back to a focused analysis of one institutional alternative.
