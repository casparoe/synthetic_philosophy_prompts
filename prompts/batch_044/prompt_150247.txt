I've been leaning pretty hard into illusionism about phenominal consciousness — basically the idea that there's no actual qualitative feel, just our brains running a kind of self-model that makes it seem like there is. But I want to know what would actually make me drop it. Not just the usual "but I know what red looks like to me" pushback, which illusionists just brush off as intrspective error. I mean, what specific results or cases would be genuinely damaging?

Like, imagine we found people with complete damage to the reporting networks who still show behavioral markers tracking qualitative differences in ways that can't be explained by access consciousness alone — maybe consistent avoidance patterns that don't line up with belief or desire. Or suppose someone proved that any self-model capable of generating the illusion must necessarily also instantiate veridical phenomenal properties, which collapses illusionism into some kind of realism. I'm not claiming these exist; I'm asking what the shape of a real defeater actually looks like.

Also, how does illusionism hang together with other stuff — transparency of experience, intrinsic qualitative character of mental representations, predictive processing accounts of introspection? Which of these links do illusionists admit are vulnerable, and which are just red herrings? And has the view been qualified so far — phenomenal consciousness is an illusion except in this technical corner case — that it's basically unfalsifiable? If so, is that a real problem for a metaphysical claim?

Rank the defeaters: fatal, serious but survivable, and merely awkward. I want an honest assessment of where the view is actually exposed, including what its defenders would concede versus dismiss.
