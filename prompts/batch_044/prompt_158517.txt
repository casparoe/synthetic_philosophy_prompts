I work as a software engineer with no formal background in political philosophy, though I have been reading about democratic reform out of personal interest. I keep returning to a structural pattern I have noticed in discussions of term limits and sortition, and I am unsure whether political science already possesses a precise concept for it, or whether I am merely conflating several familiar ideas under one impression.

The pattern is this: a reform is introduced specifically to break the hold of career politicians—strict term limits for legislators, or in the case of sortition, the replacement of elected representatives by citizens serving brief, randomised terms. In both cases, the individuals who hold formal office are deliberately made transient and inexpert. Yet in practice, the actual work of drafting legislation, negotiating with interest groups, and maintaining institutional memory does not disappear; it simply migrates to a layer that the reform does not touch—permanent civil servants, partisan staffers, and lobbyists who remain in place for decades. I have seen this described anecdotally in state legislatures with tight term limits, where senior staff effectively write bills for rotating lawmakers, and I have wondered whether random-selection assemblies would face an identical dependency, with professional administrators shaping the agenda for temporary citizens. What strikes me is that the reform succeeds in removing the visible careerist—the long-serving elected official—while leaving intact, and perhaps strengthening, a hidden careerist class beneath them. In my own field, we sometimes see an analogous migration when engineering leadership is forcibly rotated: the institutional knowledge does not vanish but accumulates in permanent middle-management roles that outlast every temporary lead.

Is there an established term for this specific displacement—where an anti-careerist mechanism eliminates experienced office-holders only to concentrate experience and influence in an unelected, permanent substrate? I am not asking about bureaucratic power in general, nor about regulatory capture, though I can see how both might be related. I want to know whether the phenomenon I have described—this downward migration of careerism when the top layer is forcibly rotated—has its own conceptual boundary, how it differs from ordinary staff influence, and whether sortition and term limits are genuinely parallel in this respect or only superficially so. If there is no dedicated concept, I would be interested in how one might delineate it and where its edges lie—whether it applies only to legislative bodies, or extends to any organisation where leadership tenure is artificially shortened, and whether the presence of random selection rather than electoral rotation changes the structure of the dependency in any meaningful way. A bare name will not satisfy me; I am after the carving itself—what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its limits are.
