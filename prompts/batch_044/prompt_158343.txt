There's this museum cataloging system I came across — they have a rule that if an object is one of thousands of factory-made copies, say a Meissen porcelain figurine, it gets filed under one universal entry: "Type 47-B" or whatever. But if it's a hand-thrown ceramic pot that happens to look exactly the same, it gets its own individual catalog number and a full biographical entry. Meanwhile their conservation team restores factory pieces back to the "ideal original" but for the handmade ones they preserve every chip and crack as part of its unique history. What view of universals is baked into that split? Are they treating the factory object as just an instance of a real universal form that exists independant of the copies, while the handmade one is a bare particular with no universal behind it? Or could a realist about tropes explain the same practice without committing to full-blown Platonism? And if you replaced their implicit ontology with a strict nominalist view — where there's no such thing as "the Meissen figurine" outside our labeling — could they still justify treating the two objects differently on purely pragmatic grounds, or would the whole cataloging and conservation practice have to be rebuilt from scratch?
