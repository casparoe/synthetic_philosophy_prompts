At a recent seminar on public ethics, two colleagues — Amara and Ben — debated a case I had raised, and I found myself unable to referee them. The case concerns a senior municipal engineer in a mid-sized Southeast Asian city. After each major infrastructure contract is awarded, the winning firm invites him to a private dinner where he receives a handcrafted ceramic and a sealed envelope containing cash equal to about five per cent of his monthly salary. The firm explains that this is not payment for the contract but traditional “gratitude for mentorship”: for years he has informally advised their young engineers, and in this context refusing the envelope would insult the family’s honour. There is no explicit quid pro quo, local authority is embedded in personal networks, and he is widely seen as technically impartial in bidding assessments.

Amara argued that this is corruption regardless of framing. Even without an explicit deal, the pattern creates structural dependency: he knows that future contracts sustain the very relationship producing these envelopes. Calling it “mentorship gratitude” is a rhetorical mask; the private service is inseparable from his public role because the mentorship occurs precisely through access granted by that role.

Ben replied that Amara imposes a Western transactional framework assuming sharp public–private separation. Here authority is relational. The envelope compensates a separate private service — genuine mentorship — not the contract itself. To label this corruption applies an alien normative architecture and risks criminalising ordinary social reciprocity.

Amara countered that “integrity within relationships” is too vague. Without an external procedural check, how do we distinguish legitimate reciprocity from disguised bribery? The envelope is fungible and concealed; the social cost of refusal shows coercion. That structural bias exists whether or not he subjectively feels partial.

Ben said Amara conflates relationship with distortion. All governance involves ties. The relevant question is whether bidding is actually unfair, not whether personal bonds exist. If he awards contracts on merit, the gift honours a parallel private bond. Western standards mistake “absence of personal ties” for integrity, but true integrity might mean maintaining fairness within relationships rather than abolishing them.

Amara responded that without a principled line between “fairness within ties” and favouritism, Ben’s position collapses into “anything is acceptable if no explicit deal exists.” The hidden cash and the inability to refuse without social punishment are precisely the coercive markers that define corruption, not culture.

Ben closed by saying that targeting hidden envelopes rather than demonstrable bidding bias turns anti-corruption into a tool for dismantling local solidarity networks under the guise of neutrality. Universal standards should prohibit actual distortion, not the form of social exchange itself. He added that Amara seems to rely on an “exit-cost” model (corruption is present when refusal is socially punitive), while he uses an “intent-to-distort” model, yet neither has defended why their model should prevail.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each contribution by Amara and Ben, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state your bottom-line verdict first — who is more right overall, and about what specifically — followed by your reasoning, and identify what both parties miss (for instance, whether a middle category such as “embedded clientelism” is needed, or whether an empirical check on bidding patterns would settle the dispute). Keep your response to no more than 400 words.
