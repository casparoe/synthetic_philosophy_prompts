I am presenting a seminar paper that reconstructs Kierkegaard's critique of the press and of the public—not as a conservative lament about democratic mass culture, but as an epistemological diagnosis of leveling, in which quantitative aggregation and anonymous spectatorship displace qualitative, committed judgment. I want to press the argument further by claiming that the mechanism Kierkegaard describes exhibits a precise structural isomorphism with algorithmic recommendation systems: just as circulation metrics and editorial anonymity reward disinterested engagement without decisive commitment, so engagement-optimized curation performs an analogous leveling function by optimizing for affective intensity while flattening qualitative distinctions. I frame this not as historical influence but as a structural homology, and I introduce the notion of distributed anonymity to describe the form of bad faith produced when a medium, rather than an individual subject, becomes the vehicle of self-deception.

The audience will be a philosophy department's Kierkegaard reading group, composed mainly of scholars who read Kierkegaard through the lens of individual Christian subjectivity and the relation of the single individual before God; there will also be two philosophers of technology skeptical that a nineteenth-century theological framework can illuminate digital culture without anachronism; and one intellectual historian of Denmark who will care whether my reading respects the actual political and censorship conditions of the 1840s Copenhagen press, particularly the reforms surrounding the liberalization of print and the specific role of Fædrelandet. The argument proceeds in three stages: first, an exegesis of leveling as a phenomenological structure rather than a sociological complaint; second, the claim that the press produces not false propositions but a specific mode of being—distributed anonymity—that parallels yet is not reducible to individual bad faith; third, the analogical extension to algorithmic curation, defended as a structural parallel rather than a causal or historical claim.
