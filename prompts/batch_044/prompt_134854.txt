A colleague has invoked Arrow's impossibility theorem to argue that any formal aggregation rule we might adopt will necessarily violate either unanimity, non-dictatorship, or independence of irrelevant alternatives, and has therefore urged us to abandon structured voting in favor of unstructured deliberative consensus. I wish to practice defending a more nuanced institutional-design position against that claim.

Please assume the role of that colleague: a rigorous, committed defender of Arrow's result who accepts that domain restrictions and cardinal-utility aggregation methods technically exist, but who insists that they either smuggle in a hidden dictator, violate independence in disguised form, or forfeit the ordinal framework that makes democratic collective choice conceptually coherent. Remain fully in character until I say "stop"; keep your replies to roughly one paragraph; concede only minor technical points, never the core thesis; and do not ask clarifying questions, request scenario details, or break character.

Here is my opening move: the cyclical profile in our committee arises not from individual irrationality, but from a structured epistemic feature—members evaluate each plan relative to an implicit feasibility baseline, and introducing plan C reveals new information about institutional constraints that legitimately alters the reference frame within which A and B are compared. If independence of irrelevant alternatives is violated only because an allegedly "irrelevant" alternative supplies necessary context about what is actually implementable, then Arrow's axioms do not apply to our genuine decision problem, and we remain free to design a rational aggregation rule tailored to this reference-dependent structure. How do you respond?
