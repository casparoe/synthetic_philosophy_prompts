I've been going back and forth with my superviosr about whether hypocrisy actually undermines standing to blame, and I'm starting to think the whole debate is stuck in a loop that has nothing to do with the actual cases. The standard literature setup is familiar enough: person A blames B for doing X, but A also does X, or did it worse, and then we ask whether A has forfeited their standing. One camp says yes, it's basically a tu quoque problem applied to moral reponsibility—if you're dirty you can't call others dirty without being a hypocrite. The other camp says standing is relational or victim-based, so your own moral record is irrelevant unless you're the one B wronged.

But every time I try to push past the interpersonal version—like asking about an ethics board that sanctions students for plagiarism while ignoring labor violations in the university's own supply chain, or climate activists who fly to conferences and then blame governments—my supervisor just says those are 'instutional' or 'collective' cases and not the core issue. Meanwhile I read papers that treat standing as if it's about moral authority or sincerity, but never explain why those matter for blame specifically. So I'm wondering: is this actually a verbal disagreement about what 'standing' even denotes? Are we smuggling in different concepts—relational standing, epistemic authority, social license—under one word? Or is there a hidden substantive split about whether blame has to be authoritative or sincere to be legitimate at all? And if the real divide is just that one side is talking about interpersonal blame and the other about institutional accountability, what kind of move—a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the cases, or just accepting bedrock intuition—could actually break the impasse?
