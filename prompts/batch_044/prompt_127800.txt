I serve on a municipal advisory committee that has developed an implicit framework for evaluating behavioral interventions without adopting any formal charter. Our track record is as follows. We endorsed a city program that defaulted households into a green-energy tariff that was slightly more expensive, provided every bill included a prominent, one-click opt-out and a clear explanation of the default's purpose. We rejected a supermarket partnership that rearranged shelf layouts to make processed snacks less visible and nutritious items more prominent, without posting any notice that the layout was engineered rather than organically organized. We conditionally approved a public-library recommendation algorithm that used borrowing history to suggest books, on the condition that the selection criteria were published in full and patrons could disable suggestions with a single toggle. We rejected a workplace wellness initiative that sent employees messages framing exercise as a moral duty to their families, even though the messages clearly identified themselves as wellness communications, participation was entirely voluntary, and all data were anonymized. We also endorsed a retirement-plan design that automatically escalated contribution rates each year unless the worker actively paused the increase, with explicit disclosure in every pay stub and an immediate, no-penalty pause mechanism.

We are now reviewing a proposal in which a widely used navigation application, contracted by the municipality, subtly adjusts displayed drive-time estimates through school zones during pickup and drop-off hours—adding approximately thirty seconds to the estimated route—without informing users that the adjustment serves child-safety goals. Drivers retain full legal right to travel through the zones, the burden is minimal, the affected population is the general adult user base rather than a specifically vulnerable group, and the only way to avoid the altered estimates is to discontinue use of the application altogether.

If our committee wants to remain consistent with itself, should we approve this navigation intervention? I am less interested in a bare verdict than in identifying the two or three general rules that best account for our prior judgments—perhaps one centered on transparency and targeted reversibility for self-regarding choices, another on the preservation of rational deliberation and avoidance of deceptive information, and a third that distinguishes other-regarding harm prevention from self-regarding choice architecture—stating what each rule predicts about this case, giving your best judgment of which rule we are actually running with supporting reasons, and specifying one further hypothetical case that would most sharply distinguish between them.
