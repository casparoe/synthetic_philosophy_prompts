I spent much of last night in an exhausting but productive argument with a friend over whether emergency price controls are a philosophically coherent response to scarcity or merely an emotional refusal to accept market reality. She insisted that any ceiling on prices during a crisis denies the informational function of cost, while I kept returning to the intuition that there is a structural difference between charging more for a luxury good and extracting surplus from someone’s immediate, non-substitutable need for shelter, medicine, or safe passage. We never resolved the dispute, but it made me realize that my role on the city’s Emergency Resource Allocation Board lacks any rigorous, reusable framework for distinguishing permissible scarcity pricing from genuinely repugnant commodification. I need an operational procedure—something my team can execute in under thirty minutes without an economist on staff—that turns a specific theoretical distinction into ordered, auditable steps.

The principle I want translated is a hybrid I have been sketching: a transaction crosses into repugnant commodification not simply when the price is high, but when the pricing mechanism converts a vulnerability-specific dependency into a revenue opportunity, while simultaneously generating what I am calling a “status asymmetry”—a visible, durable social ranking that persists after the crisis has ended and that affects the buyer’s future civic standing. To make this usable, the procedure must ask three ordered questions. First, substitutability: can the buyer delay, substitute, or forgo the good without catastrophic harm to basic functioning? Second, informational asymmetry in crisis context: does the seller possess crisis-specific knowledge or control that the buyer cannot reasonably access or evaluate? Third, persistence of status markers: does the transaction record, display, or institutionalize access in a way that creates a lasting marker of who was able to pay? At each stage, the procedure must specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where it terminates with a clear “permissible scarcity pricing” or “prohibited commodification,” and—crucially—where it must halt and hand the decision to a board member because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than assessment.

Please construct this as an explicit checklist with tests for each stage, including the hard intermediate case where substitutability is partial—for instance, a generator rental that prevents business failure but not bodily harm. Then apply the procedure to three proposals we might actually receive: (1) dynamic ride-share pricing that triples fares during a mandatory evacuation order; (2) a private water-broker contract that sells reserved tanker access to households by sealed bid, with the broker retaining ownership of the access records for future verification; and (3) a hospital pharmacy that raises prices on post-exposure prophylaxis by forty percent during a contamination event, directing the surplus into an expanded charity-care fund. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer that a committed free-market advocate would reject, and flag precisely where the procedure misfires or cannot settle the matter and therefore requires a value judgment signed off by the board chair within one hour. Remember that every step must be documentable for public audit, that no economic modeling expertise can be assumed, and that the deliverable is a reusable procedure, not a one-time recommendation.
