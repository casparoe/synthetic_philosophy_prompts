Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning recently to philosophy of science and aesthetics, I have become preoccupied with W. E. B. Du Bois’s insistence that all art is propaganda—not in the pejorative sense of deception, but as the unavoidable, group-serving shaping of perception and representation. I wish to apply this framework to a domain closer to my former discipline: algorithmically reconstructed scientific imagery—false-color astronomical composites, generative-model-augmented microscopy, or diffusion-enhanced medical scans—where aesthetic, epistemic, and institutional choices are embedded in the reconstruction pipeline long before any human observer interprets the result.

Please describe the most important implications of treating such scientific visualization as propaganda in Du Bois’s sense. In particular, address: (1) whether this dissolves the traditional philosophical distinction between “objective” scientific representation and politically motivated art, or merely complicates it; (2) how it ought to reshape the ethical evaluation of visualization choices made by research teams, funding bodies, or automated systems; and (3) whether recognizing the propagandistic nature of these images demands a new form of transparency—perhaps analogous to Du Bois’s call for artists to openly own their collective purpose—within scientific publishing and public communication. Keep your analysis conceptual rather than empirical; I am not asking for bibliographies, case studies, or citations, but for the philosophical structure that emerges when a scientist’s own representational tools are seen through Du Bois’s lens.
