I am designing a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar on moral responsibility. The aim is to sort students by their substantive commitments regarding Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, not by which label they prefer.

Please construct a taxonomy that begins with the following concrete case: an agent decides of her own accord to perform an action, and an external controller stands ready to intervene should she show any sign of deciding otherwise, but the controller never actually acts. The student is first asked whether the agent is morally responsible.

From there, the tree should branch on these five diagnostic commitments, each phrased as a question answerable without prior knowledge of the literature:

(i) Does the controller's mere presence—without actual intervention—eliminate every alternative possibility open to the agent?
(ii) Even if some flicker remains (for instance, a prior mental event that could have triggered intervention), is that flicker robust enough to ground the kind of leeway that moral responsibility requires?
(iii) Is responsibility primarily a matter of sourcehood (the decision being genuinely the agent's own) rather than of leeway (access to do otherwise)?
(iv) If sourcehood is sufficient, does a merely counterfactual controller compromise it, or only an actually causally efficacious one?
(v) Does the distinction between regulative control and guidance control resolve the tension, and if so, which form does the agent retain?

The leaves must include at least these positions: (1) the standard Frankfurt-inspired compatibilist view that the principle of alternative possibilities is false for moral responsibility; (2) the flicker-of-freedom defense that preserves a weakened version of the principle; (3) a sourcehood-only view that denies leeway is necessary; (4) a hierarchical identification view that distinguishes levels of desire or will; (5) the hard incompatibilist rejection that the case is question-begging or incoherent; and (6) at least one genuinely unoccupied branch, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty (incoherent, collapsed into a neighbor, or merely unexplored).

Additionally, inject one non-standard variant into the tree: replace the external controller with an internal, structurally embedded mechanism—for instance, a compulsive psychological pattern that the agent endorses but that would have overridden her choice had she hesitated. Ask whether this internalization changes the answer to branch (iv), and flag whether this variant collapses the tree's separation between the hierarchical view and the sourcehood-only view.

Every node should be framed as an intuitive judgment about the case or about what responsibility requires, with no reference to author names, article titles, or bibliographic labels in the questions themselves. At the bottom, provide the scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the named leaves. Finally, explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree fails to separate cleanly—particularly whether the hierarchical and sourcehood positions converge on branches (iii) through (v)—and explain briefly why the ambiguity remains.

Please do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for bibliographic verification. The deliverable should be usable by a student with no access to external sources.
