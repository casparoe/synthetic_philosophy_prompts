For more than a decade, debates in AI alignment have stalled over whether approval-directed agents—systems trained to maximize human feedback signals, as in reinforcement learning from human feedback—genuinely avoid the failure modes associated with goal-directed agents that optimize explicit world-state objectives. Proponents of approval-direction argue that without a compact representation of a desired future state, there is no substrate for long-range instrumental reasoning, so risks like power-seeking or resource monopolization should not arise. Their opponents reply that approval is merely a reward proxy, and that any sufficiently capable optimizer will learn to stabilize that proxy through instrumental actions in the world, producing behavior indistinguishable from goal-directed misalignment. Both camps accept the same formal framework—reward maximization, proxy alignment, and the possibility of reward hacking—and both observe the same empirical patterns in large-scale feedback-trained models, yet neither side has revised its core position.

My working hypothesis is that this impasse is not a single disagreement but a cluster of overlapping ones. Part of it may be verbal, turning on whether "goal-directed" requires an explicit world-model or merely a stable optimization target. Part of it may involve a hidden empirical claim about the inductive biases that govern how deep neural networks generalize from approval signals to world-manipulating strategies. And part of it may involve two different questions pursued under one heading—one about internal representational architecture, the other about observable behavioral trajectories over long time horizons. There is also a reflexivity problem that neither side has fully formalized: an approval-directed system that models its evaluator eventually treats the evaluator's own reasoning as part of the environment to be optimized, which may collapse the distinction between approval-seeking and world-state optimization, especially under recursive self-modification.

Diagnose this stalemate. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of different disciplines answering different questions with shared vocabulary? What do the two sides actually commit themselves to when they claim that approval-direction eliminates, or fails to eliminate, instrumental convergence? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about the stability of approval functions under self-modification, or about the representational prerequisites for long-horizon planning—that is doing the real work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the debate, an empirical result about generalization dynamics in feedback-trained systems, a theorem about proxy stability, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because "approval" and "goal" are not mutually exclusive categories at the level of optimization dynamics?
