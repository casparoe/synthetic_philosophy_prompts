I am designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of action and rational agency. I need one 90-minute essay question on the absent-minded driver problem, structured so that a student cannot earn a high mark merely by summarizing the standard pre-commitment solution. To prevent rote answers, the prompt should embed the classic problem in a modified scenario—call it the divided-driver case—in which the driver anticipates not simple forgetfulness but active resistance from a future cognitive state. Specifically: the driver knows he is absent-minded, but he also expects that at the decision point a distinct hot reasoning state will want to override any plan endorsed by his cool planning state; he therefore installs an automated exit-lock system that takes exit B unless he manually overrides it, though the lock has a known 2% mechanical failure rate and the manual override remains physically available.

The exam question should have three parts. Part A should ask students to reconstruct the original absent-minded driver problem and to identify the precise logical tension that makes a naive stick-to-your-plan recommendation collapse at the moment of action, distinguishing diachronic planning authority from synchronic belief. Part B should present the divided-driver case and ask students to analyze whether the automated lock resolves the original difficulty or transforms it into a problem about strategic self-design: in particular, they should discuss whether rationality requires disabling the manual override entirely, and what this implies about treating one's future self as an unreliable instrument rather than as a reasoning partner. Part C should ask them to evaluate two competing interpretations of what the case reveals—one holding that rationality is essentially diachronic strategic control over one's own mechanisms, the other holding that it requires executive integration of cognitive states—and to argue which interpretation better explains why disabling the override might still be rational even when the hot state protests.

Please write the full exam prompt, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points. The marking scheme should separate an A-grade response— which must contain a precise logical reconstruction of the original tension, correct identification of how the divided-driver case shifts the problem from forgetfulness to anticipated resistance, evaluation of the two interpretations with explicit reference to the override decision, and recognition that the 2% failure rate introduces a separate risk-calculus layer—from a B-grade response, which summarizes the original problem accurately but treats the modified case superficially or conflates motivational resistance with mere memory failure. List five must-hit moves with point values totaling 20, and for each include a one-line note on the typical failure mode that costs marks. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should be self-contained.
