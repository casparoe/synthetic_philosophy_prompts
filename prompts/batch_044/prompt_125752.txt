My advisor keeps pushing Lakatos on me and I think I've built an argument that shows MSRP makes every research programme unfalsifiable in practice—call it the reconstruction-latency problem—but I know I'm cheating somewhere. Here goes.

(1) Lakatos says we should judge a research programme not by its current anomolies but by its progessive problemshift—whether its theoretical adjustments predict genuinely novel facts.
(2) Any anomaly can, in principle, be absorbed by expanding the protective belt with an auxilliary that saves the hard core.
(3) For any such auxilliary, we can always append a further conjecture that generates a currently untested, genuinely novel prediction, even when the saving move itself is totally post-hoc.
(4) So for every apparently degenerating programme, there exists a possible progressive reconstruction that hasn't been tried yet.
(5) Rational abandonment requires knowing the programme is degenerating, not just that it currently looks degenerating.
(6) But since (4) shows there's always an untried progressive path, we can never actually know a programme is genuinely degenerating.
(7) Therefore, Lakatos's normative rule—abandon degenerating programmes—can never be applied in real time; every programme deserves indefinite patience.
(8) That makes his framework useless for demarcation, which can't be right.

I feel like (3) to (4) sneaks in a modal slide—possible reconstruction vs actual trajectory—but I'm not sure if that's the real damage, or if I'm misreading what "progressive" means. I want to keep (1) and (5) if I can, since they match the texts I have. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Does it break the hard-core/protective-belt distinction, or just show that Lakatos needs a stricter time-bound on patience?
