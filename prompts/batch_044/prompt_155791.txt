Having recently relocated to a country where parliamentary debates over reinstating selective civic service are unfolding explicitly within a framework of declared armed neutrality, I find the usual historical surveys unsatisfying. I do not want another comparison of Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland as case studies; I want the conceptual architecture first. Before I evaluate any particular model, I need to know what an adequate theory of defense organization for a permanently neutral state is actually required to explain.

Write the full list of desiderata that such a theory must satisfy. The theory must account for the institutional distinction between a professional standing army and a conscript or militia-based system, but not reduce that distinction to combat efficiency or budgetary cost. Instead, it must address what I will call neutrality credibility—the visible dependence of defense capacity on broad societal participation rather than on a specialized military class, and the signal this sends that the state possesses no credible expeditionary or offensive posture. Specify the cases behind this requirement: why neutrality demands a different form of deterrence (deterrence by denial anchored in territorial depth and societal will) than alliance-based collective defense, and why credibility depends on the distribution of military competence across civilians rather than on its concentration in professionals. The theory must also explain the automation-paradox of neutrality—namely, why several technologically advanced neutral states have retained or revived conscription precisely as precision weapons, cyber capabilities, and autonomous systems reduce the traditional need for mass manpower. It must distinguish the democratic-participation argument (that conscription fosters civic equality and social cohesion) from the strategic-signaling argument, treating them as separate desiderata that may or may not align.

For each requirement, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp—for instance, whether fiscal sustainability is a binding constraint or merely one factor among others, and whether the theory must preserve a strict distinction between defensive and offensive organizational structures or may treat that distinction as politically constructed. Apply the list to three boundary conditions: the Swiss militia model with its high reserve integration and minimal active-duty footprint; the Swedish shift from suspended universal service to selective gender-neutral conscription; and the Finnish doctrine of universal male conscription coupled with territorial defense despite rapid modernization. Note which cases support, complicate, or undermine each desideratum. Crucially, flag any subset of requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable—such as the tension between demanding that conscription be strategically indispensable for credible neutrality and acknowledging that professional forces in comparable geopolitical positions achieve equivalent or superior deterrence. A brief scoring of the pure professional-army, universal-conscription, and selective-service models against the criteria is welcome, but keep the weight on the specification itself and on identifying which single requirement eliminates the largest number of competing theories.
