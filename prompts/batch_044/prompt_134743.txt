I'm writing this after a tense discussion in my cognitive science seminar, where my advisor urged me to adopt a rigorously heterophenomenological stance for my thesis on visual awareness. I have been treating my subjects' first-person reports not as direct windows onto phenomenology but as interpretive texts expressing beliefs about experience, and I now find myself caught in a puzzle of my own construction that threatens to swallow the method entirely.

Here is the argument I sketched for the seminar. (1) Heterophenomenology requires that every first-person report about consciousness be treated as an interpretive text rather than as direct evidence for the existence or character of subjective experience. (2) This requirement applies universally: there is no principled reason to exempt the scientist's own first-person reports, since the method is defined by its stance toward reports, not by who utters them. (3) Therefore, when I report that the subject's response latency decreased, or even that I am now aware of a green stimulus on the monitor, these utterances must also be treated as texts requiring interpretation rather than as direct observational data. (4) But scientific practice depends on scientists treating at least some of their own perceptual and instrumental reports as direct evidence; if all such reports are merely texts for interpretation, then there is no observational bedrock, only an infinite regress of interpretations with no uninterpreted ground. (5) One might reply that the scientist can split herself into an interpreter and an interpreted subject, but this only pushes the problem back, since the interpreter's own reports are then subject to the same treatment. (6) Hence heterophenomenology either applies to all reports and collapses into skepticism about observation, or it applies selectively and violates its own universalist commitment. (7) Therefore the method proves too much: it undermines the very scientific practice it was designed to serve. I feel the slip is somewhere between (2) and (4)—perhaps an equivocation between treating phenomenological reports as texts and treating all scientific observation-reports as texts—but I cannot locate it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the coherence of the method? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (2) if possible, but I want to know exactly what I am paying.
