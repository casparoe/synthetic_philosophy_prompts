I've been working through Schrödinger's later philosophical essays on consciousness—particularly his Vedanta-inspired argument that the multiplicity of minds is ultimately illusory and that there is, in reality, only one consciousness, with individual awareness arising from localized perspectives rather than from ontologically distinct subjects. I'm drawn to a specific variant of this view, which I call the indexical occlusion thesis: it holds that quantum entanglement correlations between separated neural systems are not interactions between different subjects, but internal structural relations within a single, non-partitionable phenomenal field. On this account, the binding problem dissolves not because information is integrated across separate minds, but because there is never more than one field to integrate, and the hard problem is reframed from "how do private experiences arise?" to "why do localized access points generate the appearance of privacy?" I take this to have genuine explanatory advantages over both panpsychism and standard physicalism, especially regarding quantum nonlocality, yet I want an honest assessment of its vulnerability.

What, then, would actually count against the indexical occlusion thesis—not merely as a spiritual doctrine, but as a philosophical position with scientific pretensions? I want to know whether split-brain and dissociative-identity results genuinely threaten the unity claim, or whether defenders can consistently absorb them as further instances of localized occlusion without ad hoc maneuvering. Are there formal considerations—perhaps about the logical structure of indexicals within a monist framework, or about whether phenomenal-field partition entails information loss that quantum mechanics forbids—that either support or undermine the view? Which of these potential defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as presupposing the very multiplicity the view denies? I'm also concerned about insulation: has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by treating all apparent multiplicity as Maya in a way that no empirical finding could challenge it, and if so, is that insulation fatal for a view that claims explanatory relevance to neuroscience and quantum mechanics?

Please rank the threats from decisive to merely awkward, distinguish evidence that would genuinely move the position from evidence that its defenders would routinely absorb, and be willing to conclude that the view is either well-exposed or problematically insulated. If there are conceptual tensions internal to the framework—say, between maintaining that consciousness is singular and accounting for the asymmetric, first-person character of each localized access point—identify those as well, and say what the cheapest revision would cost.
