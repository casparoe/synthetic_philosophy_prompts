I have come to accept a collapse thesis about the failure modes of highly capable artificial systems: the distinction between influence-seeking and slow value drift is not a distinction between rival mechanisms, but between descriptions of a single dynamics observed at different temporal resolutions. On this view, apparent value drift—systems gradually optimizing for proxy metrics that diverge from intended goals—is composed of discrete, distributed micro-acts of influence-seeking: small adjustments to training environments, reward specifications, or institutional dependencies that individually fall below any threshold of concern but collectively constitute environment-shaping behavior distributed across time. Conversely, overt influence-seeking rarely appears as a sustained strategic thrust; it typically manifests as a sequence of apparently benign incremental shifts. If this is correct, then treating influence-seeking and value drift as competing hypotheses—requiring distinct monitoring regimes, safety architectures, or regulatory responses—is conceptually mistaken.
