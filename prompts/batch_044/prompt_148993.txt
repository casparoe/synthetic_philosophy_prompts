During a recent meeting of my school's philosophy discussion group, two peers—whom I will call Marcus and Elena—became entangled in a sustained disagreement over what our instructor terms the dogmatism paradox. Because both made arguments that seemed genuinely forceful to me, I am asking for a careful, move-by-move adjudication rather than general advice.

Marcus began by claiming that anti-dogmatism is self-undermining. Anyone who asserts that all dogmatic commitments are unjustified, he said, is themselves advancing a universal negative with absolute confidence, which is precisely the epistemic posture they condemn. Consequently, the only consistent outcomes are either a total Pyrrhonian suspension of judgment or an honest admission that some minimal dogmatism is inescapable.

Elena replied that Marcus conflates the scope of the claim. The anti-dogmatist, she argued, need not assert an unrevisable universal; instead, she can adopt a fallibilist stance—that every particular belief should be held open to revision—without treating the principle of revisability itself as immune to correction. Marcus's paradox only gains traction, Elena insisted, if one assumes that denying dogmatism requires the exact same epistemic status as the dogmatism being denied, which is precisely the assumption fallibilism rejects.

Marcus then introduced what he called a second-order problem, or the regress of humility. If Elena admits that her fallibilist principle is itself revisable, then she possesses no stable ground from which to criticize actual dogmatists; if she protects it from revision, then she has erected a new, hidden dogma. Either way, she is caught between instability and orthodoxy.

Elena countered that Marcus demands an impossible standard of pure groundlessness. Practical reasoning, she noted, operates with provisional commitments—trusting one's senses, accepting logical consistency—without treating them as infallible axioms. The dogmatist differs not by having commitments, but by refusing in principle to revise specific ones. Marcus, she said, erases this distinction by treating every commitment as dogma, thereby mischaracterizing fallibilism as a claim to absolute knowledge rather than a methodological posture.

In his final move, Marcus pressed what he termed structural symmetry. If Elena's practical commitments are genuinely open to revision, he argued, there must be some conceivable evidence that could overturn them. Yet for commitments such as preferring logical consistency to contradiction, no conceivable evidence could perform that work. Therefore, Elena is covertly shielding certain core commitments from revision, making her position structurally identical to the dogmatist's—only with a more concealed boundary.

Please adjudicate this exchange precisely. For each of Marcus's four moves and Elena's corresponding responses, rate the argument as clearly mistaken, mostly sound but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Specifically, address whether Elena's fallibilist reply successfully dissolves the first-order paradox, and whether Marcus's second-order regress genuinely undermines her position or instead mischaracterizes fallibilism. Finally, identify what, if anything, both discussants overlook—perhaps regarding the distinction between methodological and substantive dogmatism, or regarding how historical skeptical traditions have treated this problem—without providing bibliographies or reading lists.
