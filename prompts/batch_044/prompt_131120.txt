To check whether I understand how the framework applies consistently, my instructor presented four hypothetical cases and asked us to classify them; I have recorded her verdicts and am now trying to work out the underlying rule. She held that Chamberlain’s wealth from voluntary basketball-ticket payments is just; that inherited wealth is just provided every prior link in the chain was just; that a firm profiting from a state-enforced patent is unjust, even though every customer buys voluntarily, because the monopoly itself violates just initial acquisition; and that a musician who becomes rich through streaming royalties distributed by a private algorithm is just, even though the algorithm was designed without musicians’ consent, because the listeners’ payments remain fully voluntary. I want to test whether these four answers hang together.

Consider now a new case that did not come up in class. A digital creator legally acquires a large corpus of publicly available texts—none of which she authored—trains an artificial intelligence on them, and then sells access to the model’s outputs through purely voluntary subscriptions. The revenue is split automatically according to an influence-tracking formula that sends small payments back to the original authors, but the creator retains the majority share and becomes extraordinarily wealthy. The original authors never consented to being included in the training set, and the computing infrastructure was initially funded by a government subsidy that required open publication of the model’s architecture; she then built a proprietary service layer on top of that open foundation. Given my instructor’s four earlier verdicts, should she regard this creator’s accumulated wealth as just? I would like you to identify at least two competing principles that could explain her pattern—perhaps one that looks only at the voluntariness of the final exchange and another that requires justice at every prior stage, including background conditions and consent—say which principle best fits her answers, predict her verdict on this AI-training case, and specify what further hypothetical case would most clearly distinguish the two principles from one another.

Please present your reasoning in a formal manner appropriate for a philosophical discussion, and be explicit about how confident you are in the reconstruction of my instructor’s implicit rule; in particular, single out the specific step in your analysis—whether the classification of one of her earlier verdicts, the mapping to a principle, or the prediction about the new case—about which you are least sure.
