I'm working through an argument that emerged in an interdisciplinary seminar on expert judgment, and I keep circling back to the same suspicious inference that seems to prove far more than it should. The argument runs as follows. (1) Cognitive penetration of perception is real only if a cognitive state—say, an expectation or theoretical belief—alters the phenomenal character of the perceptual experience itself, rather than merely influencing the judgment that follows it. (2) Trained forensic document examiners, after years of exposure to specific forgery patterns, report that ambiguous ink-trace configurations literally look fraudulent to them in a direct, non-inferential way; novices confronted with the identical stimuli experience them as visually neutral. (3) If we take these reports at face value, the examiners' prior commitments about the prevalence of fraud in this document class are causally shaping their visual phenomenology, not just their downstream inferences. (4) But then the same mechanism should operate across every domain of expert observation—cosmologists interpreting telescope arrays, pathologists scanning tissue slides, geologists reading seismic traces—where strong theoretical expectations systematically precede observation. (5) If expert perception is penetrated in this way throughout the sciences, then empirical observation cannot function as an independent tribunal for theory, since the theories being tested are already embedded in the perceptual data themselves. (6) Scientific realism and the predictive success of experimentation depend on observation being at least partly independent of the theories it evaluates. (7) Therefore, either cognitive penetration never occurs anywhere, including in the forensic examiners, or the epistemic authority of expert scientific observation collapses into vicious circularity. (8) Because the second disjunct is intolerable, we are forced to deny that penetration occurs in the examiners, and by extension everywhere.

The conclusion strikes me as far too strong—surely there is a middle path—but I cannot locate the precise misstep. Is the error in (1)'s demand for phenomenal alteration, in (2)'s interpretation of the examiners' reports, in the universalization in (4), or in the slide from widespread penetration to vicious circularity in (5)? If you reject one of these steps, what does that cost? In particular, does saving expert perception require embracing a sharp distinction between "phenomenological penetration" and "access penetration" that current small-scale models of perceptual learning already undermine? And what should the repaired argument say about a case I haven't listed—say, an experienced sommelier who genuinely tastes "minerality" in a wine only after being taught that the concept applies to that region?
