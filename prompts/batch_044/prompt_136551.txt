I am drafting a historical novel set in a provincial asylum around 1895, and I have been working through psychiatric manuals from that period to render my characters' diagnostic debates with some philosophical precision rather than mere period color. I keep returning to a passage—rendered here from my own reading of the original—that describes what the author terms a “reflexive class of moral insanity.” In it, the physician observes that once a patient is assigned to this category, the patient begins to exhibit precisely the emotional flatness and impulsive conduct that define the class, almost as though studying the diagnosis itself. The text then distinguishes, briefly, between “organic” cases, in which these traits precede the label, and “induced” cases, in which the label seems to generate the traits; yet it concludes that the distinction is therapeutically useless, since both groups respond identically to institutional discipline and neither can be separated from the other in clinical practice.

What I need help understanding is the conceptual architecture of this passage, not its historical provenance. Specifically, does it anticipate what Ian Hacking later described as looping effects—the feedback by which human classifications alter the people they classify, with those alterations then feeding back into the classification itself? Or is something structurally different occurring here? The passage seems to recognize a loop, yet it collapses the distinction between discovered and manufactured pathology for pragmatic reasons. I want to know whether that collapse represents a conceptual failure—an inability, within the logic of the text, to maintain the boundary between ontology and epistemology once looping is admitted—or whether it reveals that, in psychiatric taxonomy, the loop is so tight that the distinction between “organic” and “induced” was never stable to begin with.

More precisely, I am trying to map the passage onto a distinction I have not seen clearly articulated in the literature: between looping that merely modifies behavior within an existing category, and looping that operates as a kind of performative script, in which the diagnosis supplies the very vocabulary through which the patient constructs a new self. Does the author’s indifference to origin—treating both manifestations identically—suggest that he implicitly accepts this stronger, script-like form of looping? Or does it suggest that he has not recognized looping at all and is merely being administratively pragmatic? I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to modern philosophy of psychiatry; I want to understand the internal logic of the passage well enough to render my characters’ arguments without anachronism, and to know whether the conceptual knot they face is genuinely Hacking-like, or whether it belongs to a different, perhaps still unnamed, failure mode of classification.
