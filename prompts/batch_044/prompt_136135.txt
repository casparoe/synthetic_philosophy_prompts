In epistemology, mental states are often described as luminous: if you are in pain, you are in a position to know you are in pain without any further observation. The guiding image is a self-illuminating object, like a phosphorescent substance that emits its own visible light or a lamp that needs no external source to shine. Defenders of the KK principle for mental states invoke this image to argue that knowing a mental state automatically includes knowing that you know it, because the illumination of awareness is already present in the state itself. Audit this analogy carefully. Lay out the correspondence table—what corresponds to the phosphor or lamp, the emitted light, the observer or eye, the conditions of darkness or blockage, and the mechanism that detects the illumination. Then identify where the mapping breaks: can mental states be present but remain undetected, as with subliminal or non-conscious states that are like phosphorescent material kept in a sealed dark box? Does the analogy smuggle in an external observer that mental self-knowledge does not have? Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the KK principle from those that are harmless. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that mental knowledge is automatically second-order survives once these hidden imports are exposed, and suggest whether a different comparison—perhaps to an internal feedback loop rather than to visible light—would serve better.
