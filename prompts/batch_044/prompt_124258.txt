I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level metaphysics and philosophy of science seminar, 90 minutes, one essay question worth 30 points, with a model answer of about 800 words and a detailed marking scheme. The topic is counterfactual versus interventionist theories of causation, but I want the question to sit well away from the standard textbook summaries students can recite. So inject the following crux: present a self-contained scenario in which an institutional failure is brought about both by a particular administrator's discretionary order and by the constitutive rule that defined that administrator's authority in the first place, with the two factors definitionally linked so that neither can be varied independently. Ask students to determine, for each theory, whether it can select the administrator's order as the cause without double-counting the structural rule, whether it can treat the rule itself as a cause without collapsing into circularity, and where the two theories come apart on whether "intervention" is even coherent here. The model answer should reconstruct the counterfactual account's reliance on possible-world comparison under holding-fixed conditions, the interventionist account's reliance on modular structural equations with exogenous manipulation, and then apply both to the scenario. The marking scheme must separate an A answer—which must (i) identify why constitutive dependence blocks standard intervention, (ii) show that counterfactual holding-fixed fails for definitional links, (iii) propose a coherent resolution or stalemate rather than declaring one theory "wins," (iv) distinguish causal selection from causal production, and (v) avoid conflating statistical dependence with causal dependence—from a B answer, which typically summarizes both theories correctly but applies them mechanically to the scenario without seeing the structural-blockage problem, and should list the specific mistakes that cost marks (e.g., treating the rule as a background condition without argument, assuming interventionism requires physical manipulability, ignoring the difference between selection and production). No reading lists, citations, or bibliographic questions; the scenario must be fully self-contained so a student without web access can work through it entirely from the prompt.
