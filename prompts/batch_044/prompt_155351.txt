I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the legitimacy of self-amending legal rules, going by my gut on concrete cases.

A town charter requires a three-fourths council vote to amend the charter. The council achieves exactly three-fourths and amends the charter to require only a simple majority. That feels legitimate: the old rule authorized its own replacement.

The same council, now acting by simple majority, immediately amends the charter to sell the town's central park to a private developer. Here my verdict splits: the procedural change was valid, but using it in the very same session to do something that could never have cleared the old threshold feels like pulling up the ladder after climbing it—procedurally authorized, yet somehow illegitimate.

A state statute reads: "This statute repeals itself upon enactment." That strikes me as perfectly coherent—the rule exists just long enough to execute its own disappearance.

A court's internal rules state: "The Chief Justice may revise these rules unilaterally." The Chief Justice revises them to require unanimous court consent for any further revision. A subsequent 5-4 majority attempts to revert to majority rule. The Chief Justice's revision feels valid—the old rule authorized it—but the 5-4 attempt feels invalid, since the new safeguard is now in force.

A constitutional article declares: "This article may never be amended, and any purported amendment is void." Despite this, a referendum passes with 75% support, formally amending the article. My gut says the amendment is invalid: explicit entrenchment should bite, even when the procedural vote is overwhelming.

Yet this conflicts with my acceptance of the charter case. In the charter case I allowed a three-fourths vote to lower the threshold; here I reject a 75% vote to remove an explicit protection. What distinguishes them?

A legal code contains a meta-rule: "Any rule that refers to its own validity or invalidity is void." Applied to itself, this seems self-undermining—if it voids itself, it is not there to void anything; if it doesn't, it violates its own terms. My instinct is that it applies to itself and collapses, leaving the system without its meta-rule, which feels wrong but unavoidable.

I keep waffling on this case: a university faculty senate's bylaws state, "These bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote. No amendment may diminish the voting rights of tenured faculty." The senate votes 20-10—exactly two-thirds—to replace that clause with "These bylaws may be amended by simple majority." They then vote 15-10 to eliminate tenure-line voting rights. Is the first vote legitimate? They met the procedural threshold, but they used it to destroy the safeguard that was meant to constrain exactly that kind of move.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps with more than one clause—that captures all of these verdicts? Or must I drop one? If there is a principle, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it says about the university case and about a case I haven't considered: a constitutional convention adopts a founding rule stating that any norm adopted by the convention itself is valid law, then uses that rule to declare that the convention's own adoption procedures are henceforth non-binding. Which of my verdicts, if any, must be sacrificed for simplicity?
