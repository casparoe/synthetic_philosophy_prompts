After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about G. E. Moore's account of intrinsic value—particularly his discussion of friendship and aesthetic appreciation in Principia Ethica—I have tried to systematize the pattern of judgments she offered across a series of hypothetical cases. She maintained that: (1) a relationship involving mutual awareness of mental states and appropriate emotional warmth counts as friendship with full intrinsic value even in the absence of physical co-presence; (2) the intrinsic value of appreciating a beautiful object survives the observer's false belief about the object's origin, so long as the experience itself is genuinely aesthetic; (3) a sustained bond with an entity that entirely lacks mental states, however behaviorally responsive, does not possess the specific value of friendship; (4) a universe containing beautiful objects but no conscious beings to experience them retains a non-zero but diminished intrinsic value; and (5) affection that is purely instrumental, even when accompanied by the correct emotional form, fails to constitute friendship because the mental state lacks direct orientation toward the other person's consciousness.

Consider now a further case: two people develop a deep, reciprocal emotional attachment in which each correctly believes the other to be conscious, yet all interaction occurs through an opaque mediation system that replaces every direct expression of mental states with reconstructed approximations. Neither party ever accesses the other's mental states directly, though each experiences what seems like appropriate emotional warmth and mutual recognition.

If my friend wishes to remain consistent with her prior rulings, what should she say about this mediated bond? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the competing rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well—perhaps rules emphasizing direct mental-state access, correct belief coupled with emotional form, or the intrinsic quality of the experienced relation independent of causal pathway. For each such rule, state its prediction for the mediated case. Then offer your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors it over the alternatives. Finally, identify the single further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the two leading rules.
