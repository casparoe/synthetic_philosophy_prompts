I'm giving the keynote remarks at the retirement dinner for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty-two years as chief allocation officer and later dean of clinical ethics at a major public hospital network. The audience is deliberately mixed: her family and longtime nursing colleagues sit alongside health-policy scholars, clinical ethicists, and former students who will catch every philosophical reference. Dr. Vasquez is widely respected for refusing to treat patients as fungible units in a welfare-maximization calculus, yet her career was defined by making precisely the kind of hard distributive decisions that tempt pure aggregation. I want a six-minute speech that takes the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism as its structural spine—not decorative jargon, but the actual reason we can honor her without claiming she merely saved the most lives through summed welfare. The address should reject the framing that moral success in her role meant maximizing an aggregate total, and should instead articulate how she operated within what she privately called parallel sovereignty: the principle that in crisis allocation no single patient's claim to care can be canceled by multiplying the needs of others, even when needs must still be weighed within a shared framework of public justification. The speech must comfort a non-philosophical family, avoid false hagiography, never imply she watches over anyone from beyond, and should acknowledge that her work was genuinely difficult and sometimes left her with lasting regret. Please write it in polished American English, with no citations, no references to specific philosophical texts by title or author, and no bibliographic recommendations.
