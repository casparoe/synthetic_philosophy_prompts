Write an essay on the interpretive controversy surrounding Kierkegaard's treatment of Abraham—specifically, whether the so-called teleological suspension of the ethical represents a genuine metaphysical rupture in moral universality or merely an internal reordering of ethical commitment under a higher, but still rationally structured, telos. To test the coherence of the latter reading, apply it to a reconstructed variant: imagine a deliberative agent who receives what they experience as an authoritative, first-person command to perform an act that flagrantly violates universal ethical duties—say, breaking a promise that sustains a vulnerable dependent—while being unable to articulate, verify, or share with any interlocutor the specific telos that allegedly justifies the exception. The agent has no observable divine sign, no communal ritual validation, and no predictive success that might ground an epistemic defense of the command's authority; they have only an irreducible subjective conviction. Assess whether Kierkegaard's framework can accommodate this radically private suspension without collapsing into either arbitrary decisionism or a hidden form of consequentialist calculation. Your essay should weigh the strongest objections from both the rupture and reordering camps, and it should reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that the suspension necessarily requires some form of intersubjective legibility to avoid moral nihilism, or that it can remain genuinely private and still be ethically meaningful. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the conceptual structure of the problem itself.
