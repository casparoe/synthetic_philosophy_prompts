In a recent rationalist forum thread on moral risk, two participants argued over whether the scale argument for insect sentience survives correlation across phylogeny. (1) The first said that with ~10^19 insects alive and even a 0.1% prior on sentience per individual, expected sentient beings dwarf humans by orders of magnitude, so dismissing insect welfare requires near-certainty they lack consciousness. (2) The second replied that sentience probabilities are not independent: consciousness likely requires specific neural architectures, so updates should happen over evolutionary lineages, not individuals. Multiplying 10^19 by 0.001 is a category error; you should weight by the number of independent evolutionary experiments. (3) The first conceded correlation but argued this mainly changes the value of information—learning about one beetle updates beliefs about millions—without lowering prior expected harm. For present action, expectation should still dominate. (4) The second countered that with heavy-tailed, correlated outcomes under deep uncertainty, linear expectation is decision-theoretically inappropriate; one should use a maximin-style rule over plausible worlds rather than naive expected value. (5) The first responded that this smuggles in an unjustified decision framework, noting that by the same logic we should reject expected-value reasoning for climate risk.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain precisely how phylogenetic correlation affects both prior expectation and the value of information, and say what both parties miss about the interaction between correlation structures and decision rules. In particular, separate what is settled (e.g., that individual-level independence fails) from what is genuinely contested among people who understand the topic (e.g., whether deep uncertainty undermines expectation-based moral reasoning here), and do not present a contested decision-theoretic view as the consensus.
