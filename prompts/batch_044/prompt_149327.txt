I hold the view that epistemic akrasia—maintaining belief in P while simultaneously judging that one's belief-forming processes regarding P are unreliable—need not be irrational when the first-order belief and the second-order doubt are produced by functionally isolated cognitive subsystems. Rather than treating such cases as failures of doxastic integration, I take them to reflect a rational response to modular architecture: the agent correctly assigns low weight to outputs from an impaired module without thereby disabling the module's own continued operation, somewhat as a pilot might distrust a particular instrument reading while still responding to the aircraft's overall flight dynamics because the navigation and control systems are decoupled. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this modular-insulation account. Which empirical findings about belief revision, which formal results in epistemic logic or decision theory, and which conceptual arguments about the unity of rational agency would be decisive in undermining it, and which would be merely awkward to accommodate? In particular, please address whether defenders of this view could concede certain defeaters—such as evidence that meta-cognitive judgments necessarily recruit the same cognitive resources they evaluate—without abandoning the core claim, whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability by appealing to hidden modular boundaries, and whether it predicts anything that rival accounts of higher-order evidence do not. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and say clearly whether you think the view is well insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory of this kind. Throughout your response, separate what is well settled in the philosophy of higher-order evidence from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present a disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus.
