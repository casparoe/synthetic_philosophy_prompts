here's the student's answer (i typed it up from their sheet):

Kripke introduces the quus function, which is just like addition except that for any inputs over 57 it returns 5 instead of the sum. Since my past mental history and behavior are finite, they're fully compatible with me having meant quus all along rather than addition. So there's no mental fact or behavioral fact that determines I meant plus. That's the skeptical paradox: there just isn't any fact about what rule I'm following.

Then Kripke distinguishes a straight solution, which proves there really is a fact about meaning, from a skeptical solution, which accepts there's no fact but explains why we talk as if there is. Wittgenstein, on Kripke's reading, offers a skeptical solution. He appeals to community agreement—an answer is correct if the community accepts it. This replaces truth conditions with assertibility conditions tied to social practice, so it doesn't solve the paradox straight on.

But I think Wittgenstein's view actually works as a straight solution if you understand it right. The community's shared form of life doesn't just describe when we're allowed to say something; it constitutes the rule itself. Once you see that meaning doesn't have to live inside individual heads, the paradox dissolves. Kripke only calls it skeptical because he secretly assumes that meaning has to be grounded in individual mental states first. If you reject that assumption, communal practice is a genuine straight solution that saves real meaning without hidden mental facts. The private language argument is really just a special case of this same problem—if you're alone there's no community to check you, so the rule-following issue only becomes serious in private language. So the community view fixes both issues at once, and it's definitely straight, not skeptical.
