You are a philosopher specializing in rational choice theory and intertemporal ethics. Before adjudicating, briefly outline the hidden premises in each move below and check whether the two positions are internally consistent with one another. Then proceed step by step.

Two colleagues, Jordan and Alex, argued over lunch about whether applying a constant exponential discount rate to one's own future welfare is rational.

(1) Jordan said pure time preference is clearly irrational: being in 2045 rather than 2025 is no more a reason to care less than being in Canada rather than the US is reason to care less about a distant stranger. Temporal location is arbitrary from the normative point of view, so uniform weighting should apply across time just as impartiality applies across space.

(2) Alex replied that the spatial analogy fails because of causal and psychological asymmetry. I can affect my 2045 self through present choices, and I share psychological continuity with her, but I share neither feature with a distant stranger. So caring less about distant future welfare tracks real structural differences in agency and identity, not arbitrary bias.

(3) Jordan conceded the asymmetry but said it only justifies discounting for risk and uncertainty—if I might not exist or my preferences might radically change—not a pure preference for present over future goods. Even in a case where I am certain to exist in 2045 with stable preferences, pure time preference still discounts her welfare, which the asymmetry argument does not license.

(4) Alex introduced what she called the diachronic solidarity constraint: rational agency does not require treating all temporal stages identically, because practical identity is partly constituted by continuous commitment. A constant discount rate can reflect the weakening of diachronic solidarity over long intervals, analogous to weakening obligations to distant relatives, without reducing to risk-based discounting.

(5) Jordan countered that if solidarity weakens continuously with psychological distance, the normative discount should be graded—hyperbolic—rather than constant exponential. Applying a uniform rate regardless of whether the future is one year or thirty years away ignores the very gradient Alex appeals to, so her view is internally inconsistent.

(6) Alex responded that a constant rate is a rational heuristic given cognitive limits. Agents cannot compute precise solidarity gradients for every decision, so they adopt a simplifying constant rate that approximates the underlying graded principle. The heuristic is rational precisely because it tracks the underlying structure without requiring impossible computation.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether diachronic solidarity can genuinely ground a constant rather than graded rate without collapsing into either risk-based discounting or pure temporal bias—and whether there is a principled way to distinguish legitimate intertemporal weighting from arbitrary preference. Finally, state exactly what argument or empirical finding about psychological continuity or practical identity would change your overall verdict, and identify the specific step in your reasoning where you are least certain.
