I'm presenting next month to the municipal heritage and public-ethics board of a mid-sized post-industrial city (population ~420,000) that is currently deadlocked over a 1932 bronze of a local industrialist—he funded worker-cottage housing in the 1910s but also directed private security against union organizers in 1923. The board's composition matters: two urban historians who treat the commemorative landscape as an archival primary source, a legal positivist who insists that renaming requires explicit legislative authorization, a public-choice economist who suspects moralized politics of rent-seeking, and a community organizer who demands participatory veto rights over symbolic space. Rather than defending removal or retention, I'm advancing what I call "symbolic depreciation scheduling": the thesis that statues, street names, and building dedications should be governed by a rolling evaluation protocol rather than by crisis-driven removal or permanent entrenchment. Under this framework, each commemorative asset carries a "moral half-life" computed across three dimensions—the continuing salience of the values it promotes, the density of available counter-narrative infrastructure within a 2-mile radius, and the distributive burden its presence imposes on marginalized users of that public space. When depreciation crosses a threshold, retirement becomes obligatory, not discretionary; but retirement takes the form of contextual relocation to a curated historical site with full interpretive framing, not destruction or silent erasure. The framework is meant to apply uniformly, including to monuments that currently enjoy protected status.

For the Q&A prep, I need objections phrased in the board's actual vocabularies—not generic culture-war talking points, but the specific moves each member will make. Order them by likely damage: start with the legal-positivist's authorization problem, then the historian's archival-integrity objection, then the economist's rent-seeking concern, then the organizer's participatory-democracy challenge, and include at least two that cross disciplinary lines (e.g., the economist using the historian's argument). For each objection, give me: the precise formulation as they would phrase it; whether it targets the depreciation metric, the public-good framing, or the relocation mandate; a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes; and a frank note on where that reply is weakest—particularly if it relies on an analogy to physical-capital depreciation that may not carry over to symbolic goods, or if it concedes that the "moral half-life" coefficient involves normative judgment that cannot be fully proceduralized. I also want the reply I'm hoping nobody asks: specifically, whether this framework would have required retiring civil-rights monuments during periods of dominant backlash (say, a 1956 Birmingham), and how to answer without either collapsing into presentism or abandoning the framework's universalist ambition. Finally, flag which objections I should concede partially up front—the board values intellectual honesty over defensive rhetoric—and which I should hold firm on, since conceding the wrong point would undermine the relocation mandate.
