I hold that the paradox of legal self-amendment—where a rule appears to authorize its own alteration—can be dissolved by indexing authority to distinct temporal stages. Consider a constitutional reflexive suspension protocol: it empowers a legislature to suspend the very rules governing amendment, but only with binding effect upon the succeeding session, never upon the session that enacted it. By decoupling the authorizing instance from the affected instance, there is no vicious circularity, no infinite regress of meta-rules, and no need for an external ground of authority. The paradox, on this view, arises only from an illegitimate collapse of temporal indices into a single present authority. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked by decisiveness: formal results showing that temporal decoupling still conceals a hidden regress or an undefined base case; jurisprudential or institutional evidence that legal systems treat such temporal indexing as a formalist evasion rather than a substantive resolution; conceptual arguments that legislative authority is essentially atemporal, making session-relative splitting incoherent; and the possibility that defenders can retreat into ever-finer modal or temporal distinctions to insulate the view from every counterexample. For each, specify whether a committed defender should concede damage, whether they can consistently dismiss it, and whether such retreat renders the position unfalsifiable in a way that weakens it.
