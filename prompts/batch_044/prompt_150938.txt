I am writing a position paper for an advanced philosophy of mind course, and I find myself returning to a problem that sits at the intersection of historical metaphysics and contemporary cognitive architecture. The standard contrast runs between Descartes's Cartesian theater—the notion that conscious experience requires a single, unified mental space where representations are displayed for an internal observer—and Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model, according to which the brain produces numerous parallel, competing narratives that are never projected onto one central screen. Yet I am not convinced that the standard framing captures the deepest issue. To push the debate further, I propose distinguishing what I will call editorial convergence from distributed coherence: editorial convergence holds that even without a literal theater, conscious unity still requires a selective mechanism that weights, suppresses, and hierarchically binds disparate drafts into a temporarily dominant narrative, whereas distributed coherence claims that such integration can emerge purely from local interactions among drafts without any central selector.

Write an essay that evaluates whether Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater can be sustained if one accepts distributed coherence alone, or whether it secretly depends on some form of editorial convergence to avoid collapsing into phenomenal fragmentation. You should weigh the main objections—especially the argument that temporal binding and the sense of a continuous self cannot be accounted for without some integrating function, and the reply that introducing any such function reintroduces the very homunculus Dennett sought to eliminate. Do not remain neutral: defend a clear verdict about whether the multiple drafts model genuinely eliminates every center of consciousness, and justify your position by analyzing the internal logic of the model rather than appealing to empirical brain science or to specific neuroscientific findings.
