Analyze the 1971 break-in at the FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, and the subsequent exposure of COINTELPRO records as a case study in archival power and silence. Rather than treating the episode as a straightforward uncovering of concealed truth, examine how the surviving archive is itself a constructed object: identify the well-known categories of documents that were preserved intact, those that were destroyed or heavily redacted, and how that selective preservation shapes what can historically be said about the scope and targets of state surveillance. Apply the framework of archival silence—treating absence as an active structural feature rather than a neutral gap—to interpret the pattern of preservation and destruction, and contrast this with a conventional secrecy model that treats missing records merely as hidden information awaiting disclosure. Then evaluate what the case demonstrates about the limits of transparency alone as a remedy for archival distortion, and specify one feature of the episode—such as the public and institutional reaction to partial disclosure—that the archival-power framework explains poorly or fails to anticipate.
