In debates about rational design, one frequently hears that giving an agent an extra information channel is like installing an additional window in a room: the occupant can always pull the shade, so more visibility can never hurt and may only help. The analogy is often invoked to support the stronger claim—grounded in Good's result on the nonnegative expected value of free information—that a rational decision-maker should never refuse a costless observation opportunity, since any update can be ignored but never lowers prior expected utility.

Audit this analogy in detail. Provide an explicit correspondence table: identify what plays the role of the window, the shade, the occupant, the glare, and the architectural load of the frame within the decision-theoretic framework. Then examine where the mapping fractures: is information acquisition ever genuinely costless, or does it always carry attentional, computational, or structural overhead? Does "pulling the shade" correspond to a rational suppression of a belief update, or does observing an event irreversibly alter the agent's epistemic state? Most importantly, consider cases where the information source is not exogenous—for example, when the agent's choice to observe correlates evidentially with the outcome (as in predictor-dependent decision problems), or when the content observed includes information about the agent's own reasoning, generating self-referential instability. Does Good's theorem still apply when the act of observation is not independent of the state space? Finally, evaluate whether the normative conclusion—that rational agents ought to seek every available free observation—survives these disanalogies, or whether the domestic imagery of passive windows and voluntary shades was doing the argumentative work all along.
