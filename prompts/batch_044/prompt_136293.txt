I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and next Thursday I'm presenting to a philosophy seminar at the Institute—mostly not philosophers, actually, just sharp people who think about risk and allocation. My thesis is a twist on the Taurek line: numbers shouldn't count in rescue cases, but not because persons are "separate" in some metaphysical way. I think the right explanation is what I'm calling causal proximity decay—moral weight fades as the causal chain between agent and victim gets too diffuse, which is why saving five versus six feels like a direct duty but saving five versus five hundred feels like a statistical problem, not a moral one. It's a bit made-up, I know, but I think it saves the intuition without the weird ontology.

The room: three effective-altruism philosophers who live in QALYs, two Kant scholars who care about the formula of humanity, one medical-triage ethicist who actually writes disaster protocols, and a few grad students who've read the Taurek literature closely. They're tired of trolleys and skeptical of anything that lets aggregation slide.

I need a prep sheet, not a polished defense. Give it to me in exactly these sections, in this order, with rough lengths noted:

1. "The thesis in my own sloppy words" — about 120 words, casual, you can include the typo "releational" if it fits, just capture the causal-proximity-decay idea and why I think it beats the standard separateness story.

2. "Who's in the room and what they care about" — brief, ordered by who'll speak first.

3. "The objections, ordered by damage" — about six, phrased the way each camp would actually say them (the EA folks will talk about aggregation failure, the Kantians about treating people as replaceable, the triage person about real-world protocols). For each, note who'd ask it.

4. "Two-minute reply for each" — tight, conversational, not an essay.

5. "Where that reply is soft" — one honest line per objection.

6. "What I should concede up front" — before anyone asks.

7. "The question I hope nobody asks" — and specifically why it kills my position.

8. "Bottom line" — one paragraph on what I'm actually walking in with.

Don't ask me for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list—I'm not writing a journal article, I'm trying not to get laughed out of the room. Don't lean on some obscure 1983 triage manual or a niche decision-theoretic derivation I wouldn't have memorized; stick to ideas either in the standard debate or straight out of my setup above. Keep it casual—this is for my notebook, not publication.
