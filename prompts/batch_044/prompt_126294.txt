For a conceptual-clarity session on behavioural genetics and social policy, I have tried to state my position with precision. I want an audit of whether these commitments hang together before I present them. Here is my profile:

(a) Heritability (h²) is strictly a population-level, environment-relative variance-partitioning statistic; it is neither an individual-level causal coefficient nor an index of immutability.

(b) The high heritability of cognitive ability observed in affluent Western populations—commonly near 0.70 in adulthood—reflects compressed environmental variance rather than heightened genetic determinism.

(c) Therefore, equalising environments further through universal provision (standardised education, nutrition, healthcare) should shrink the environmental denominator and drive heritability upward, possibly above 0.85.

(d) Despite this expectation, I hold that such universal equalisation is both morally required and practically effective, because heritability concerns the distribution of differences, not the population mean, which can still be lifted en masse.

(e) At the same time, I believe that once heritability exceeds roughly 0.90 in a given population, the remaining variance is so dominated by genetic differences that further broad environmental investment produces diminishing returns; policy should then pivot toward differentiated, targeted support—without treating genetics as destiny.

(f) I reject the ontological interpretation of the additive variance model (V_P decomposed into V_G, V_E, and interaction terms), since gene-environment correlation and interaction make the separation a statistical artefact of the linear model, not a division of separable causal powers.

(g) Nevertheless, I rely on the h² coefficient—derived from that very model—to justify the policy pivot in (e). I also hold that comparing heritability coefficients across traits—e.g., personality traits near 0.40–0.50 versus cognitive ability near 0.70—licenses the inference that personality is more environmentally malleable.

Please examine this set for internal coherence. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear so, and what is the least damaging revision in each case? Rank the conflicts by severity. I am particularly concerned whether (e) can be maintained alongside (f) without special pleading, and whether (c) combined with (e) creates a self-defeating structure for universal policy—since equalisation seems to generate the very condition that, on my view, justifies abandoning it. I do not want a general primer on quantitative genetics; I want to know what my own position commits me to, where I am smuggling in an unstated premise, and which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies.
