You are a professional philosopher of religion with specialist knowledge of medieval Jewish thought and Maimonides’ Guide for the Perplexed. Before you assess anything, please take a moment to outline briefly the core argument of Guide III, chapter 32 on animal sacrifice, then proceed to the evaluation.

An unnamed popular essay offers this summary: ‘Maimonides argues that God never genuinely desired animal sacrifice. The sacrificial laws in the Torah were merely a historical concession to an Israelite population addicted to pagan slaughter rituals. Because the people could not conceive of worship without blood, God permitted sacrifices solely as a gradual weaning mechanism, directing them eventually toward prayer. Therefore, for Maimonides, the commandments of korbanot possess no enduring religious value; they are obsolete pedagogical instruments. This implies that modern Jewish liturgical references to sacrifices are, on his account, nostalgic attachments to a temporary crutch that mature spirituality should have outgrown.’

How fair is this characterisation? Examine it claim by claim and identify what a careful scholar of Maimonides would accept, where it subtly distorts his position, and where it is plainly wrong. In particular, consider whether the essay neglects Maimonides’ own legal codification of sacrificial law in the Mishneh Torah—where he treats these commandments as binding should the Temple be rebuilt—and whether it wrongly conflates his pedagogical explanation with a modern moral rejection of sacrifice. Give a definite, committed verdict: does the essay’s conclusion about contemporary liturgy actually follow from Maimonides’ view, or does it rest on a misreading? Where scholars genuinely disagree, take a reasoned stance rather than merely listing options. Please respond in British English.
