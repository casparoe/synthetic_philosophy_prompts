You chair the acquisitions and display ethics committee for a national museum of antiquities. The committee operates under an unwritten but consistently applied three-tier framework. Tier One: for individual artifacts or events with contested interpretation—say, the dating of a specific volcanic eruption or the attribution of a manuscript—the museum presents the mainstream view alongside any peer-reviewed alternative, labeling the dispute as open and supplying the evidentiary basis for both. Tier Two: for claims that invoke lost high-technology civilizations such as Atlantis construed as an engineering society, non-human architects of megaliths often called ancient aliens, or globally rewritten chronologies of the kind advanced by Anatoly Fomenko in his New Chronology, the museum applies an absolute exclusion; these may not appear in permanent or temporary exhibits, not even in a rejected-hypotheses case, regardless of publication status. Tier Three: if a Tier-One revision is later retracted by its authors or undermined by a reproducible counter-study, the museum archives the dual-label and reverts to a single account, treating the withdrawal as ordinary disciplinary self-correction; however, no analogous retraction pathway is defined for Tier-Two claims, because they are never admitted into the evidential space in the first place.

The board justifies this framework as curatorial common sense rather than philosophy. Your task is to excavate the implicit philosophy of historical knowledge and demarcation that makes the three tiers hang together. What view of the boundary between legitimate revision and pseudohistory does the system presuppose—evidentiary insufficiency, methodological violation of source criticism, metaphysical constraints on what entities can exist in the past, sociological credentialing, or something about the scale of auxiliary assumptions required? Is the distinction between Tier One and Tier Two coherent, or does it smuggle in an unstated principle, perhaps that local claims are falsifiable while global narratives are not, or that peer review can legitimize small adjustments but never paradigm-scale reconstructions? Examine whether the Tier-Three treatment of retraction presupposes a Popperian image of science that sits uneasily with the refusal to let Tier-Two claims enter the arena of falsification at all. Finally, reconstruct what the museum’s practice would have to become if it replaced this implicit demarcation theory with two alternatives: first, a strict Bayesian view in which every claim receives a prior weighted by its coherence with established knowledge and is displayed proportionally; second, a radical social-constructivist view in which all chronological frameworks are recognized as culturally situated narratives with equal standing. In each case, specify which tier rules would collapse, which might survive in modified form, and what the visitor experience would actually look like. Identify the points where the procedure forces a value judgment that cannot be eliminated by further philosophical clarification.
