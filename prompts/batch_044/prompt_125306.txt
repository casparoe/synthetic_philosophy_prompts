I'm an auditor at a small formal-verification shop and every week clients want us to sign a confidence sheet for logical claims about their code — things like "this loop invariant holds" or "this spec implies termination" — but running the full proof checker takes three days and we only have an hour. I need a decision procedue my team can actually run in sixty minutes, grounded in whatever logical induction and logical uncertainty actually say about assigning credences to sentences you can't verify yet, but stripped down so a junior auditor without a logic PhD can use it.

The deliverable should be a checklist in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where we stop with "defer / can't sign," and where we have to hand it to a senior person because it's a values call not a fact. It needs to survive hard cases: a claim that looks syntactically similar to past verified ones but hides a contradiction, a claim about a huge but finite search space where polynomial-time "trader" moves wouldn't have found a counterexample yet, and a claim that mixes empirical runtime data with pure logical consequence. Don't drop the distinction between "the market hasn't priced this low yet" and "we actually have reason to think it's true" — that has to survive in the procedure even if simplified. Include a synthetic-trader-budget step (quick syntactic contradiction searches, short counterexample patterns) and a referance-class step (compare symbol counts and quantifier structure to past verified/inverified claims). Also build in an automatic punt-to-human gate for claims that reference their own verification status or involve unbounded quantification over strings longer than anything in our referance class.

Then run it on three proposals:
1. "The loop invariant I(n) holds for all n < 2^64 in this binary search routine."
2. "The formal spec S logically implies termination for every input, even though we haven't run the checker."
3. "There exists a counterexample to the invariant with exactly 17 nested recursive calls."

One of these should spit out an answer the client won't like — probably telling us to defer or assign low confidence when they want a green check. Flag where the procedure will misfire, like if we over-rely on syntactic similarity or confuse "no trader disproved this" with "this is probably true." Finally, be honest about what it can't do — maybe it can't resolve conflicts between two equally priced logical markets, or it can't handle self-referential verification claims. Don't ask for a bibliography or reading list; I just need the tool.
