I serve as chief methodological auditor for a federal forecasting unit that issues ten-year policy projections—energy-transition scenarios, public-health trajectories, economic-impact models. Our charter requires that when evidence undermines a previously endorsed projection, we issue a revised confidence report, but over the past year we have developed a troubling bifurcation: some analysts discard the entire projection the moment its source model is criticized, while others absorb the meta-criticism yet leave the recommendation untouched, appealing to structural robustness. In discussions with my colleagues last quarter we kept circling the same philosophical problem—what epistemologists call the dogmatism paradox—where rational belief revision seems to demand either stubborn retention of prior beliefs despite higher-order evidence about one's own reasoning, or a self-undermining skepticism that dissolves belief whenever methodological error is detected. I need a reusable decision procedure my audit team can run whenever a challenge targets not the world itself, but the reliability of the reasoning process that produced a prior endorsement: it should specify, in order, how to classify the challenge as first-order or higher-order, what conditions must be met for partial retention versus full revision, the exact point at which the question stops being factual and becomes one of institutional risk-tolerance, and explicit checks that prevent both dogmatic retention and excessive dissolution. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a climate-economy model whose key elasticity parameter is shown to derive from a systematically biased sample; (2) a public-health transmission model later found conceptually misspecified in its core dynamics; and (3) a contested meta-evidence scenario in which one review alleges fundamental bias while another defends the original estimation, so that the procedure must yield an answer the audit team will dislike and must flag precisely where judgment is irreducible. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether it can settle disagreements about the prior plausibility of the original claim, or whether it requires access to information that is typically unavailable to auditors.
