After a long argument with a friend about whether the trolley problem reveals anything about morality or merely manipulates our cognitive biases, I want a sharp comparison between two accounts of how intuition pumps work. On one side is the familiar clarificatory model, often associated with Dennett: a thought experiment strips away noise to focus judgment on the essential conceptual or moral feature, functioning like a microscope for intuition. On the other side is what I will call a "friction pump" — a scenario engineered not to isolate a single variable but to force two or more of our core commitments into direct conflict, so that answering requires us to abandon one; its purpose is not to reveal a hidden answer but to map the fault lines in our conceptual scheme. Compare these structurally: how does each relate to the charge of being persuasive rather than revealing? Which is more vulnerable to framing effects and to modal contamination — the tendency to mistake the ease with which we imagine a scenario for the moral weight of the feature it highlights? Then apply the comparison: if Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist case were redesigned as a friction pump — for instance, by stipulating that disconnecting saves five strangers but kills the violinist, while staying connected saves no one — would it still serve as an argument about bodily autonomy, or would it collapse into a different kind of conceptual exercise? I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want a focused conceptual analysis.
