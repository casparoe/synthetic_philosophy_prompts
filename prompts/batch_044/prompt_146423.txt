My seminar tracks how meta-level considerations affect the urgency of far-future moral concern, which we call “fanaticism” in the technical sense of extreme willingness to trade present welfare for highly uncertain distant benefits. Our log so far: we recorded that the doomsday argument (near-term extinction risk) dampens it, because it collapses the timeline; that moral uncertainty about the value of digital minds does not dampen it, since it cuts both ways; that the value-lock-in argument (an unaligned AI fixing values permanently) amplifies rather than dampens it; that an infinite universe with causal isolation between regions does not dampen it, because our local causal chain remains intact and morally weighty; and that the hypothesis we inhabit a simulation terminating within a century does dampen it, because the causal pathway to any genuine long-term future is severed.

The new consideration is a formulation of the simulation argument holding that simulated civilizations vastly outnumber base-reality ones, so with high probability we are in a simulation; consequently, the “far future” that actually contains value is mostly composed of simulations causally independent of our choices, and resources we expend to shape the distant future are almost certainly being spent inside a temporary substrate. Should our log record this as dampening fanaticism? Identify the closest analog among our precedents, state whether our track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, and if so articulate the principle we would be adopting with each choice. Then set out the two most plausible rules that jointly fit all five prior entries, predict the verdict each gives for this case, and specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish them.
