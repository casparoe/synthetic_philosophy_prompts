Write a philosophical dialogue between a disjunctivist about perceptual experience and a common-factor representationalist, centered on whether the argument from illusion establishes that veridical perception and hallucination share an intrinsic mental kind. Rather than using standard examples, have them examine a constructed case: a subject in a high-fidelity virtual-reality environment knowingly interacts with a rendered object—a holographic apple projected onto a physical table—that lacks an ordinary material substrate. The disjunctivist should argue that even here the good case and the bad case share no fundamental mental property, since the virtual case involves a genuine perceptual relation to a display, whereas true hallucination involves no relational object at all; the representationalist should counter that shared phenomenology and representational content (there is an apple-shaped entity present) are sufficient for a common factor, and that the disjunctivist illicitly redefines "object" to avoid the conclusion.

Require genuine back-and-forth: the representationalist must directly answer the disjunctivist's claim that relational form, not representational content, determines mental kind, and the disjunctivist must answer the charge that this view proliferates mental kinds implausibly. Have them agree on what specific empirical result—say, identical predictive-error-minimization patterns in visual cortex during virtual-object viewing and during genuine hallucination—would force one of them to revise their position. Keep both voices equally rigorous; neither should be a straw figure, and neither should appeal to authority or citation.
