(1) physics is causally closed—every physical event has a complete physical sufficient cause. (2) mental events supervene on neural ones, so there's no mental change without a physical change. (3) so for any mental event M that seems to cause movement B, there's a neural event N that's physically sufficient for B. (4) if N is already sufficient, M is reduntant—this is the exclusion principle. (5) but apply the same logic to omissions: my not voting supervenes on the absence of a specific motor signal, and that absence is physically sufficient for my staying home. (6) same for social facts—the contract being signed supervenes on ink marks, which are sufficient for the legal obligation. (7) so exclusion wipes out mental causation, negative causation, and social causation all at once, leaving only bare physics. (8) worse, our belief in closure (1) becomes just a neural twitch with no rational authority—if reasoning has no causal power, we can't be rationally led to it. (9) but we clearly treat omissions and contracts as causal, and we take reasoning seriously. (10) so either (1), (3), or (4) is wrong, or we accept that everything non-physical is causally inert. where's the actual mistake? i really want to keep (4) if i can—what does giving it up cost me? please end with \answer{step, cost} naming the step you reject and the price in one phrase.
