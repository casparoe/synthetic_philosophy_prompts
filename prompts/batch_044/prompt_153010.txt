In rationalist forum discussions of infinite ethics, particularly arguments about comparing worlds that contain infinitely many well-off agents, Hilbert's Hotel is frequently used as more than a cardinality illustration. The analogy maps each hotel room to a discrete welfare-unit, full infinite occupancy to a world with infinitely many happy people, and the manager's shift operation — moving every occupant from room n to room 2n — to the structural difference between, say, a world with welfare distributed across all integers and one distributed only across even integers. Proponents invoke it to support two specific conclusions: that we can rank some infinite worlds above others by comparing their floor-plan density rather than by cardinal number alone, and that introducing one additional welfare-unit into an already infinite world is like accommodating one more guest in a fully booked infinite hotel — technically feasible but morally negligible compared to altering the hotel's architecture itself. I want a committed audit rather than an option menu. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table — room, occupant, shift, vacancy, floor — and identify precisely where the mapping from physical accommodation to moral value fractures: welfare is not discrete, not relocatable by a central authority, and not arranged in a linear ordered space. Second, isolate which properties of actual hotels — exclusivity of occupancy, linear ordering, intentional managerial agency — are being quietly imported into the ethical reasoning and whether they do any work that survives once they are made explicit. Third, take a definite position: does the analogy genuinely license ranking infinite worlds by density, or does it collapse under the disanalogies? Finally, if it collapses, outline a less misleading structural comparison — not a literature review, but the core of a better model — that could do the same comparative work without smuggling in spatial or managerial metaphors. Give your reasons and do not leave the verdict open.
