I'm running a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of language with twelve students who have already worked through the standard sorites examples and tend to regard supervaluationism as the sober, technically adequate response, while treating epistemicism about vagueness as an implausible metaphysical excess. I want an in-class simulation—not a lecture or guided discussion—that makes them feel why epistemicism might be less strange than it appears, but specifically in a technical rather than everyday domain. To that end, I want the activity centered on a constructed scientific taxonomy: synthetic thermoresponsive polymers graded by chain length, where there is an unpublished but precise functional cutoff (around 200 monomers) that determines membership in the category "eligible for rapid-release coating," yet working chemists in the scenario treat the boundary as genuinely fuzzy. The students need no actual chemistry; the case should be fully self-contained in the materials you design.

Please design a structured game with assigned roles, explicit rules, physical or card-based materials, and a non-monetary payoff scheme that forces participants to coordinate around a hidden sharp boundary they must discover through classification votes. Arrange the incentives so that one group of students acts as classifiers earning points for matching the hidden cutoff, while another group acts as observers earning points for correctly diagnosing whether the boundary they see is sharp-but-unknown or genuinely indeterminate, with a third group (the "engineers") earning points only if coordination collapses at the borderline cases. The rules should ensure that participants experience the coordination failure at the boundary region themselves—receiving inconsistent feedback, splitting votes, or failing to converge—before any debrief names epistemicism or supervaluationism. Include a precise timeline for the 90 minutes, the scoring mechanism, and the debrief that maps each game phase onto the formal distinction between epistemic ignorance of a unique correct precisification and semantic indeterminacy resolved by admissible precisifications.

Also include an honest note on which feature of epistemicism—such as the claim that there exists exactly one correct precisification that we are merely ignorant of—the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally; and predict what will likely go wrong the first time the activity is run, for instance whether students conflate the hidden-rule condition with noisy feedback or whether one role dominates discussion. Do not ask participants to read specific texts in advance, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation of particular authors in the design.
