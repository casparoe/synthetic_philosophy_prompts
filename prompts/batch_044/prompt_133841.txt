Here is my philosophical profile on truth, justification, and vocabulary choice, largely shaped by neopragmatist and antirepresentationalist commitments, but with a specific extension I want to test. I hold that (1) beliefs are not mental representations that mirror an intrinsic structure of reality; truth is at best a term of commendation for beliefs that help us cope, not a relation of correspondence. (2) Justification is entirely internal to social practices of giving and asking for reasons; there is no extra-linguistic tribunal that can adjudicate belief. (3) Despite this, scientific vocabularies are genuinely superior to, say, astrological ones—not merely different—because they deliver greater predictive control and reduce suffering, and I take this superiority to be an objective comparative fact, not just a preference of my community. (4) Nevertheless, I deny that there exists any neutral, vocabulary-independent standpoint from which to demonstrate that superiority; every evaluation of problem-solving efficacy presupposes values and descriptive resources drawn from some contingent final vocabulary. (5) I further maintain that cross-vocabulary persuasion remains possible: by redescribing the costs of another vocabulary in terms that its own practitioners can recognize, we can induce change without appealing to a shared perspective-independent standpoint. (6) Finally, I extend antirepresentationalism to modality: there are no mind-independent possible worlds, and counterfactual reasoning is merely a heuristic for reorganizing our belief-set, not a guide to metaphysical possibility.

Do these six commitments hang together? In particular, I am worried that (3) and (4) pull in opposite directions—claiming objective comparative superiority while denying any standpoint from which to establish it. I also wonder whether (2) undermines (5): if justification is wholly internal to a linguistic community, on what grounds can one community legitimately critique another's internal norms? At the same time, (1) and (3) may only seem incompatible: antirepresentationalism denies representational mirroring, but it need not deny that causal interaction with the environment explains why some vocabularies work better. And (6) might create hidden tension with (5) if the redescriptive strategy implicitly relies on counterfactual reasoning that I have just deflated.

Please audit the package: rank the genuine tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict and why, specify for each real tension what commitment has to give and what the least costly revision would be, and tell me whether a single revision—say, weakening (3) or modifying (5)—would clean up the most problems. Be precise about which motivations for each view are incompatible, not just that they sound different.
