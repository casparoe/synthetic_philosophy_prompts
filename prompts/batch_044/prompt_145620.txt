In philosophy of biology, a common analogy treats a biological species as being like a single organism: the constituent organisms are its parts (like cells), speciation events are its births, extinction is its death, and gene flow within the species plays the role of physiological integration binding the parts together. Proponents use this mapping to argue that species are not abstract kinds or taxonomic conveniences but concrete, spatiotemporally bounded individuals with evolutionary agency. Audit the analogy in detail. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the genome, of homeostatic regulation, of the reproductive boundary, of the environment, and of death? Second, identify the points of breakdown—consider horizontal gene transfer across putative species boundaries, the absence of a unified metabolic or nervous system, the fact that organisms within a species can compete rather than cooperate, and the problem of ring species where reproductive continuity forms a loop without a discrete boundary. Third, examine what features of organismality the analogy quietly imports—functional integration, a shared fate of parts, perhaps teleological organization—and whether those imports are available in the target without begging the question. Fourth, assess whether the metaphysical conclusion—that species are real individuals rather than kinds—actually follows from the analogical mapping, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. Finally, if the organism analogy fails, suggest whether a better analogy (say, to a river, a language community, or a historical lineage) preserves what was correct about it without the misleading baggage.
