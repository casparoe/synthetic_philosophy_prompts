I'm preparing a long-form feature on a conceptual tension gaining quiet traction in normative theory: the relationship between indirect normativity—where rules or institutions are evaluated not by direct moral inspection but by the quality and stability of the deliberative practices they sustain—and the tradition of idealized deliberation, in which justification requires agreement among agents stripped of particular interests and informational asymmetries. A growing but scattered body of work asks whether these frameworks can be integrated, or whether they pull in opposite directions.

What I need is not a survey but a defended research agenda. If you were advising a small interdisciplinary group—philosophers and formal modelers with roughly three years of focused effort—which open questions at this intersection should they treat as genuinely upstream? I am especially interested in how you rank three tensions: first, the risk that indirect evaluation collapses into direct assessment once deliberative conditions are fully specified; second, the problem of whether norms justified to fully idealized agents retain binding force for actual, partial agents without becoming mere aspirational ideals; and third, what I will call the problem of deliberative inheritance—how normative commitments are transmitted across successive iterations of idealization without either a fixed meta-norm or a complete rupture between generations.

Please defend a specific ordering rather than listing them. Which question functions as the gateway that unlocks progress on the others? Which is tractable with conceptual and modeling tools alone, and which risks being a conceptual dead end that only appears unresolved? Additionally, identify what kind of intermediate result—achievable within two to three years—would cause you to reorder these priorities, and explain whether any of these lines should be deprioritized because their resolution would change little downstream. I am writing for an educated general audience, so do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to obscure texts; I care about the reasoning that determines where limited intellectual effort should go, and why one path is more likely to reshape the field than another.
