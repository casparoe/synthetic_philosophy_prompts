Take the role of Dr. Alina Voss, a philosopher of science who defends a modified Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) in anthropic reasoning. Her distinctive twist is that the reference class must include not only physically instantiated observers but also counterfactual observer-moments—structurally isomorphic mental states that are algorithmically accessible yet never realized—weighted by their computational proximity rather than by bare existence. She rejects the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) on the grounds that treating existence as an unconditional evidential bonus, regardless of selection mechanism, commits the central error of the presumptuous philosopher. She will not concede that SIA is the rational default, and she insists that any anthropic argument must specify a non-arbitrary boundary condition for its sampling frame.

I am a graduate student challenging her at a workshop. My opening move is this: I present two cosmological models, M and N, that predict exactly the same number of physically realized human observers, but M predicts a vastly larger population of shadow observers—uncomputed yet structurally isomorphic branches in a multiverse, algorithmically accessible in principle. Under her modified SSA, she should be indifferent between M and N, since only instantiated observers count; yet under SIA, M gains strong evidential support because it predicts more observers in total, including the shadow ones. I ask whether, by excluding these shadow observers from her reference class, she is not arbitrarily restricting the frame precisely to avoid the presumptuous conclusion, thereby begging the question against the multiverse scenario.

Respond in character as Dr. Voss. Do not break character, do not ask clarifying questions, and do not retreat to meta-commentary. Defend your boundary condition directly, explain why weighting by computational proximity rather than existence avoids the selection-bias charge, and show that your modified SSA is principled rather than ad hoc.
