As a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind, I have become preoccupied with Douglas Hofstadter’s argument that the first-person “I” is not anchored to a particular brain but is rather a distributed pattern—something like a strange loop that could, in principle, be instantiated across multiple minds or survive partial migration into others. I am convening an interdisciplinary reading group of twelve participants, mostly scientists with little formal philosophy training, for a single ninety-minute session; I want them to discover for themselves that maintaining a coherent first-person narrative becomes unstable once fragments of autobiographical memory, preference-structure, and self-reference are distributed, duplicated, or merged across different people, with no original “master copy” remaining privileged.

Please design a structured, participatory exercise—not a lecture—that forces this instability to arise from the rules themselves. Assign each participant fragments of a synthetic, shared autobiography that they must defend, extend, and vote upon; build in an incentive structure that rewards narrative coherence but penalizes exclusive ownership claims, so that by the midpoint of the session the group is actively arguing over whether “I” still refers to anyone in particular when two participants share seventy percent of the same pattern. Include explicit roles (e.g., a “substrate guardian” who insists on bodily continuity versus a “pattern advocate” who tracks functional similarity), a timeline with material requirements, and a debrief that maps specific moments of conflict onto the distinction between token substrate and type continuity. Be frank about which feature of distributed selfhood the activity cannot reproduce—most likely the phenomenological first-person quality of being that specific loop—and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as participants refusing to abandon intuitive ownership of “their” fragments despite the rules. The philosophical problem should emerge from what the participants do, not from anything I say afterward.

Conclude with a brief note on how to handle the anticipated failure mode, and present the full design with rules, payoff scheme, debrief questions, timeline, and the honest limitation. End your response with a one-sentence summary of the core pedagogical mechanism, placed alone on the final line.
