I have been following arguments, common among defenders of restricted or weighted suffrage, that compare democratic elections to a hospital ward in which every patient is given an equal vote on the collective treatment plan regardless of medical training. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that universal suffrage is structurally irrational: just as we defer to physicians because physiological knowledge is asymmetrically distributed, political authority ought to be weighted toward those with demonstrated competence in governance. Before I accept that conclusion, I want you to audit the comparison itself. Lay out the correspondence in continuous prose—what in democratic politics plays the role of the patients, the physicians, the disease, the treatment protocol, the ward meeting, and the outcome of recovery or harm—then show precisely where the mapping breaks: whether politics has an analog to an objective cure, whether disagreement about values can be treated like disagreement about physiological facts, what happens when citizens are not passive recipients of treatment but co-authors of the rules they live under, and whether the fiduciary obligations inherent in medicine have any parallel in political representation. Examine also what the picture quietly imports: does it treat political knowledge as a single technical domain comparable to medicine, does it assume voters suffer from a kind of collective anosognosia about their own ignorance, and does it smuggle in a contested conception of the common good under the guise of clinical objectivity? Some versions extend the image further by comparing democratic campaigns to marketing campaigns that sell placebo policies and comparing voting booths to suggestion boxes in a ward designed for comfort rather than cure; assess whether this extension strengthens the analogy or merely compounds its errors. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that democratic equality is a category error analogous to patient self-medication—survives once these disanalogies are fully visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. Write everything in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a careful letter. In doing so, state explicitly how confident you are in what you claim and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
