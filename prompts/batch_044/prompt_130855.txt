I serve on the review board of a journal that publishes empirical work at the intersection of cognitive neuroscience and philosophy of mind, and we repeatedly receive manuscripts asserting mental-state causation—claims that belief, attention, or anxiety cause neural or behavioral outcomes—without addressing whether those mental properties are already causally excluded by the physical properties with which they covary. I need a reusable evaluation procedure I can run in about thirty minutes per submission, designed for a reviewer with solid scientific literacy but no specialist training in metaphysics. The procedure should operationalize Kim’s causal exclusion argument together with the principal non-reductive responses—supervenience without reduction, the possibility of non-strict mental laws, and the Woodward-style interventionist test for causal relevance—while being explicit about where mechanical rules end and a judgment call begins. Structure it as an ordered flow of diagnostic questions: for each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where a negative answer stops the evaluation with a verdict that the mental causal claim is redundant, where it stops with a verdict that exclusion is avoided, and where the issue is conceptual rather than empirical and must be flagged for a specialist. Include two worked applications to hypothetical submissions: first, a study arguing that conscious intention introduces a novel causal pathway in motor-cortex dynamics that cannot be reconstructed from prior neural states alone; second, a study that attributes anxiety-driven avoidance behavior solely to amygdala activation, using “anxiety” only as a descriptive label for the neural pattern. For each case, run the full procedure, identify the feature doing the work, note how a defender of mental causation could attack the evaluation, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle without further metaphysical commitment.
