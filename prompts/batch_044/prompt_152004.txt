Take the Solvay conferences of 1927 and 1930, together with Einstein's 1935 EPR paper and the subsequent Bell-type experiments, and analyze this episode through the lens of the claim—advanced by Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman—that philosophy is largely useless to, or obstructive of, working science. Map the dispute with discipline: identify Einstein's philosophical commitments to realism, separability, and a criterion of physical reality in the EPR argument, and Bohr's commitment to complementarity as a regulatory framework rather than an empirical claim. Ask whether these commitments functioned as heuristics that generated testable predictions, as post-hoc rationalizations of already-secure formalisms, or as a hybrid of the two. Specifically, examine whether the EPR argument was scientifically fruitful—whether it motivated the experimental program that eventually tested local hidden-variable theories—or whether it was a philosophical dead end that only acquired scientific relevance retroactively. Conversely, ask whether Bohr's philosophical framing guided the actual construction of quantum mechanics or merely decorated results secured independently. Then apply the anti-philosophy critique precisely: does the case show that Einstein's philosophical intuition misled him, that Bohr's vocabulary obstructed conceptual clarity, or that the distinction between philosophical reasoning and scientific reasoning collapses under pressure? Conclude by stating what the episode reveals about the thesis—whether it must be restricted to ornamental rather than heuristic uses of philosophy, whether it fails to explain how philosophical framing can identify anomalies worthy of testing, and whether the eventual experimental resolution counts as science triumphing over philosophy or as proof that philosophical framing was necessary to formulate the question at all.
