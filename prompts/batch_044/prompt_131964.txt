I have recently begun weighing whether to start a family, and in that context I am grading papers for an undergraduate ethics seminar I assist. To keep my evaluation objective, I ask only for an external assessment of one exam response—please do not share your own view on procreation, only evaluate the work.

The exam question was: "Explain Benatar's procreation asymmetry, clearly distinguishing it from the non-identity problem. Then present one objection that targets the asymmetry itself rather than antinatalism generally, and evaluate whether the asymmetry, by itself, entails that procreation is always wrong."

The student's answer:
Benatar's asymmetry holds that there is a moral reason not to bring a suffering person into existence, but no parallel reason to bring a happy person into existence. This follows from the idea that the absence of harm is good even if no one is there to experience that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad if no one is present to be deprived of it. Benatar separates this from the non-identity problem discussed by Parfit, which concerns whether a particular future individual is harmed by being created with a poor quality of life; Benatar's point is about the structure of reasons regardless of which specific individual might exist. A prominent reply, associated with Seana Shiffrin, argues that procreation wrongs the created person because they never consented to being brought into existence. This objection fails against Benatar, however, because if consent is impossible for beings who do not yet exist, then there is no basis for the complaint—so the asymmetry remains intact. Consequently, the asymmetry does not by itself mandate antinatalism; it only yields that conclusion together with the empirical premise that every human life contains more suffering than happiness. Once that empirical premise is granted, the asymmetry provides a decisive, unconditional reason never to procreate.
