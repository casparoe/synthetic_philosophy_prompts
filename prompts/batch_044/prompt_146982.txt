I have recently begun advising a clinical forecasting team that selects predictive models using information-theoretic criteria, and a worry about our formal justification for penalizing complexity has become personally urgent. Consider the following argument. (1) Solomonoff induction assigns prior probabilities proportional to 2^{-K(x|U)}, where K denotes Kolmogorov complexity relative to a universal Turing machine U. (2) The invariance theorem states that switching to another universal machine U' changes K(x) by at most c(U,U'), a constant that depends only on the two machines and not on x. (3) In our clinical setting, sample sizes are typically below two hundred, so the log-likelihood term in Bayes' rule is bounded by O(n). (4) Because c(U,U') can be arbitrarily large—there is no universal upper bound across all pairs of universal machines—one can always select an interpreter U' that encodes any favored model M with lower complexity than its rivals, irrespective of empirical fit. (5) Whenever c exceeds the log-likelihood bound, the prior dominates the posterior, reversing the model ranking according to the interpreter chosen rather than the data. (6) Therefore any formalization of Occam's razor via Kolmogorov complexity imposes no genuine constraint on model selection beyond our arbitrary choice of programming language, and our team lacks a rational basis for preferring simpler hypotheses. (7) So the information-theoretic defense of simplicity collapses. I am reluctant to deny (4) or (5), since they seem mathematically sound, and I wish to preserve a principled simplicity bias if possible. Which step should I reject, why did it appear plausible, and what am I paying to keep my commitments—whether accepting that simplicity is language-relative, that it holds only asymptotically, or some other concession? Where does this argument go wrong, if it does?
