I am serving as a grading assistant for an advanced seminar on distributive ethics, and I have been asked to evaluate take-home responses to a prompt that deliberately moves beyond the standard welfare framing. After discussing the assignment with colleagues, I decided to ask students to apply the leveling-down objection to prioritarianism when it is reformulated over basic capability sets rather than utility or welfare, and to examine whether a threshold-sensitive variant avoids the objection or instead collapses into sufficientarianism. I want to be rigorous about subtle errors—conflations between objections, misattributed defenses, or inferential steps that do not actually follow—rather than rewarding fluent but imprecise writing. Below is the exam question I assigned, followed by one student’s answer. I should not reveal my own assessment in advance; please provide a granular, point-by-point evaluation. Assign a specific grade, then justify it by identifying exactly where the answer is correct on the visible points, and where it makes one or two subtle mistakes that a hasty reader might overlook. Your comments should refer to particular sentences or moves in the text, not offer a general impression.

Exam question: Prioritarianism holds that benefiting people matters more the worse off they are, without treating equality itself as intrinsically valuable. First, explain the leveling-down objection in its canonical form, taking care to distinguish it from objections that depend on equality being valuable for its own sake. Second, consider a reformulation in which prioritarian weights apply not to welfare levels but to basic capability sets—the range of substantive freedoms and functionings available to a person. Would the leveling-down objection still apply if reducing everyone to a uniformly minimal capability set counted as benefiting the worst-off? Third, evaluate whether adopting a threshold-sensitive prioritarianism—where weights diminish sharply above a sufficiency line but remain steep below it—successfully blocks the objection, or whether this move collapses the view into sufficientarianism. In your answer, be precise about which premises the objection requires and whether the capability-based framing changes any of them.

Student answer: Prioritarianism was developed to capture the intuition that we should help the worst off first, with benefits weighted by how badly off the recipient is. The leveling-down objection claims that because prioritarianism cares only about weighted benefits and not equality itself, it must prefer a world where everyone is at a very low welfare level over a world where some people are well off and others are only moderately badly off, as long as the total weighted sum is larger in the equal case. This seems unacceptable because dragging everyone down to equality is not a genuine improvement.

If we shift the framework to capability sets, the picture changes. Capabilities concern what people are actually able to do, and they are structurally non-aggregative: you cannot combine one person’s capability for education with another’s in a single sum. Therefore, applying prioritarian weights to capability deprivation means that reducing everyone’s capabilities to a bare minimum does not produce any weighted gain, because there is no lower capability level that would increase priority. The objection therefore dissolves, since leveling down no longer registers as a benefit.

To defend prioritarianism further, one can adopt a threshold-sensitive version, where benefits above a sufficiency threshold receive only minimal weight. This ensures that we never have reason to pull people below the threshold, which addresses the leveling-down worry directly. In fact, the philosopher who originally formulated prioritarianism endorsed this threshold-sensitive approach as the correct reply, showing that the view naturally accommodates our resistance to downward leveling. However, this defense comes at a cost: once we say that only getting people above the line matters, and that further differences above it are almost irrelevant, we are no longer doing prioritarianism but sufficientarianism. The two views become indistinguishable in practice.

Thus, when applied to capability sets with a threshold-sensitive structure, prioritarianism avoids the leveling-down objection, but only by transforming into a different theory altogether. The objection therefore succeeds in showing that pure prioritarianism is unstable, though the capability-based variant escapes it by changing the subject.
