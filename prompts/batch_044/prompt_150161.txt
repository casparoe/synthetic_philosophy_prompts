I have been thinking, without any formal training in philosophy or science, about a parallel between Darwin and Turing that I cannot quite resolve. Darwin constructed the theory of natural selection with no knowledge of genetics—he observed the outputs of inheritance and variation, but the internal mechanism that transmitted traits was entirely opaque to him. Turing, analogously, designed computational systems and reflected on machine intelligence primarily through external behavior and formal rules, not through any internal transparency into how a machine might understand its own operations. In both cases, it seems, reasoning proceeded from observable effects back to hidden causes, or from functional success back to internal structure, without the middle being visible to the reasoner.

I want to understand whether this represents a genuine but unconventional kind of comprehension—knowing a system well enough to manipulate or explain it without being able to open it up—or whether it is exactly what philosophers call competence without comprehension: a method that produces correct results without corresponding understanding.

Please write a dialogue between two interlocutors who take opposing sides on this question. One should maintain that Darwin and Turing achieved a real, if inverted, comprehension: they grasped the structural logic of their respective domains precisely because they worked from the outside in, and that this form of understanding is no less valid than mechanistic transparency. The other should maintain that both cases illustrate pure competence without comprehension—that Darwin's model was an accurate black-box description of inheritance, and Turing's machines were black-box processors, and that the success of both depended on not requiring, and perhaps not achieving, any real grasp of internal processes.

Place the dialogue in a concrete setting—for instance, a long train journey where two strangers, each carrying books on evolutionary biology and early computing, fall into conversation. Both characters must be equally competent; neither should be a straw figure. They should respond directly to one another's points, allowing the comparison between evolutionary reasoning and computational reasoning to do real structural work in the exchange rather than serving as mere illustration.

Within or alongside the dialogue, please distinguish clearly between what is settled and what remains genuinely contested among those who study these questions, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus.
