Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains and two rhymes—staging a skeptical reversal of Moore’s proof of an external world without quoting the lecture text. Invent a parallel case: a speaker holds up a mirror and argues that because here is the frame, and here is a hand resting upon it, therefore there exists a world beyond the reflecting surface. Use the refrain “Here is the frame, and here is my hand upon it” for the premise-line, and “Therefore, a world beyond the glass exists” for the conclusion-line. In each repetition, shift the grammatical subject or spatial relation—the hand becomes its shadow, the frame becomes an edge of consciousness, the mirror becomes the mind itself—so that the refrains gradually collapse into circularity: the “outside” is defined only relative to the “inside” that was never independently established. The villanelle’s required turn, where the two refrains finally meet side by side in the closing quatrain, should enact the exact moment the proof reveals itself to presuppose the distinction it claims to demonstrate. Render the logical architecture precisely—premises, modus ponens, the claim that the proof is perfect if the premises are true—through imagery alone, so that a reader familiar with Moore recognizes the argument being undermined, while a reader unfamiliar with it still receives a coherent, serious lyric about circular justification and the boundary of the self. Keep the tone formal and unsentimental; do not resolve the tension in the final lines, but let the two refrains stand adjacent and unreconciled.
