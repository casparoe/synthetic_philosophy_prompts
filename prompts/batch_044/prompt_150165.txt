Draft the joint statement that would emerge from a rigorous adversarial collaboration between a defender of British imperial rule in the empirical-developmentalist tradition associated with Niall Ferguson and a critic in the moral-reparative tradition associated with Shashi Tharoor. Structure the document in two parts. First, present a numbered section of agreed claims that both parties can sign: these must cover precise agreement about what the formal historical record establishes regarding institutional export, economic extraction, and demographic outcomes; about what conditions make a counterfactual comparison to a non-colonial South Asian trajectory methodologically legitimate rather than speculative; and about which normative premises any adequate moral evaluation must make explicit rather than smuggle in. Second, present a disagreements table in which every residual dispute is phrased in wording that its own side would accept, with explicit marking of which disagreements are substantive and which are merely verbal or terminological.

In drafting both parts, treat the following three methodological constraints as shared ground rules, not as points of contention. Both sides must analyze outcomes through a counterfactual cohort framework rather than through aggregate national statistics: benefits and harms are to be assigned to specific generational cohorts, not averaged across the full span of colonial and post-colonial history. They must distinguish between institutional stock—lasting administrative, legal, and infrastructural structures that persist after independence—and extraction flow—resource transfers, labor coercion, and market distortions occurring during direct rule. They must also address whether moral amortization applies: specifically, whether demonstrable benefits to later cohorts can diminish the moral weight of harms inflicted on earlier ones, and if so, according to what rate of moral discounting and over what temporal horizon.

Next, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling one in either direction would move at least one party. For each crux, specify (a) the empirical case or normative argument both sides agree is genuinely relevant and not a straw man; (b) the evidentiary or reasoning standard both accept as appropriate for resolution; and (c) each side’s explicit prediction, recorded in advance, of what the answer will turn out to be. Suggested domains for these cruxes include: whether railway construction and legal codification represent net institutional gifts or instruments of control whose value is fully offset by concurrent extraction; whether the catastrophic famine of the mid-1940s in Bengal should be weighted as a systemic failure of colonial governance or as an exogenous shock amplified but not caused by imperial policy; and whether post-independence institutional continuity demonstrates a beneficial legacy or a path-dependent lock-in that constrained autonomous development.

The final statement should avoid bibliographic appeals, citation lists, or references to specific texts that a reader without access to a library could not independently verify. It should be precise enough that a specialist in political philosophy or colonial historiography recognizes the exact stakes, yet structured so clearly that the boundary between agreement and disagreement is visible without external commentary. Do not adjudicate the dispute; the deliverable is the constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement rendered exact and the verbal disagreements separated out.
