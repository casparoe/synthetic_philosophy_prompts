I've been in this philosophy reading group for like two years now and we keep circling back to same-sex marriage but it's like we're running on a treadmill. One side — mostly people with a traditionalist bent — insists marriage is essentially oriented toward reproduction and child-rearing in a way that requires sexual complementarity, not just as a statistical fact but as a kind of institutional telos that constrains what law can redesign. The other side says it's basically a state-recognized commitment between adults that happens to often involve kids but doesn't depend on reproductive capacity at all; they think talking about the purpose of marriage is just projecting a contingent history onto a legal contract. Both sides cite the same sociological studies and legal precedents and neither moves an inch. My working theory is that this isn't really about marriage per se but about whether social institutions have natural ends that limit legislative redesign — so we're actually arguing about metaphysics of institutions disguised as family policy? Or maybe it's verbal: marriage just means different things to each camp, one side using it for a natural-kind category and the other for a status-contract? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of well-known court cases. Is there a hidden empirical disagreement about child welfare outcomes doing the work, a normative equality claim masked in definitional language, or just bedrock intuitions about gender complementarity that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — separating the institutional-purpose question from the access question, some conceptual distinction between function and definition, or should we just accept that no argument will bridge it?
