I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about the moral demands of memory and forgetting, testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than just repeating arguments I've encountered from Avishai Margalit on shared memory and David Rieff on the politics of forgetting. Here are my verdicts.

When my grandmother died, my parents kept her handwritten recipe cards in the kitchen drawer and continued to cook from them. That feels like a proper, even beautiful, form of remembrance—keeping her present through practice rather than through monument.

In a town where a factory collapse killed thirty workers, residents voted to demolish the ruins and replace them with an ordinary park, with no plaque or marker. I think that choice is permissible, perhaps even wise, allowing the living to reclaim space without being permanently anchored to trauma.

Years ago a close friend betrayed my trust in a serious way. The anger and hurt have naturally faded; I can no longer recall the precise details without effort, and I feel no obligation to preserve them. This organic forgetting strikes me as healthy, not blameworthy.

But in that same situation, I once considered systematically deleting all our messages, avoiding anyone who might mention her, and actively suppressing any mental image of the event. That feels wrong—not like healing, but like an evasion of my own moral biography, as though I were trying to edit myself into innocence.

A government emerging from civil conflict maintains open historical archives for researchers but abolishes mandatory state remembrance days, leaving commemoration to families and private groups. I think this is an acceptable balance: the record survives, but the state no longer forces collective grief.

Yet when that same government publishes school textbooks that replace the actual names of perpetrators with generic terms like "the authorities," deliberately obscuring who ordered the violence, I judge that wrong. It seems less like letting the past rest and more like falsifying it.

These judgments feel coherent to me, but I see a tension: destroying physical traces of trauma can be right in the town's case, yet actively suppressing personal traces feels wrong in my own, and institutional omission of names feels wrong even though ending public ceremonies feels right. Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing remembrance that honors victims, forgetting that serves the living, and suppression that serves the guilty or the comfortable—that captures most of these verdicts? If so, what does it predict for a case I haven't settled: should an algorithm that curates public digital archives be permitted to automatically degrade and eventually erase posts about a tragedy after a fixed period, on the grounds that perpetual visibility retraumatizes survivors?

Please also tell me which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and whether I should overturn the judgment about the factory site, the personal suppression, or the textbook redaction.
