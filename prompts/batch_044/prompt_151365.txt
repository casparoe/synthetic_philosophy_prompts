So me and my friend have been arguing about this history/philosophy thing and I cant tell who's being more reasonable. We were talking about those police battalions in Poland during WWII, like the one Browning wrote about where regular guys were shooting civilians. One of us thinks Browning basically won the debate - that these were ordinary men pushed by peer pressure and authority and gradual escalation, not some special breed of monsters. The other thinks Goldhagen was closer to the truth, that you can't explain the scale and the voluntary parts without this deep cultural antisemitism that made killing Jews thinkable as a job.

Here's how it went, a few rounds:

Jordan: Look, Browning's study shows only a handful asked to be excused. That proves refusal was possible. If they were truly willing executioners in Goldhagen's sense, why would they need alcohol and peer pressure to do it? The fact that they needed those crutches shows they weren't ideologically eager - they were ordinary people in an extreme situation.

Sam: Your misreading what Goldhagen means by willing. He doesn't claim they skipped into the forest humming. He means they operated within an eliminationist antisemitism that made Jews into a problem to be solved. The alcohol was for the physical disgust, not for overcoming moral horror. If they were just situational victims, why did they keep going for months, and why did some hunt Jews in the woods voluntarily? That's not peer pressure - that's initiative. Plus there's this idea of institutional moral outsourcing - the unit structure let them feel the decision wasn't really theirs personally.

Jordan: But that initiative came after months of desensitization! Browning's whole point is that violence escalates incrementally. First you round them up, then you guard them, then you shoot them. By the time your hunting them, your moral categories are already scrambled by repetition. You dont need deep ideology for that - you need routine and distance from the victims.

Sam: Your collapsing culture back into situation. Yes, they were socialized, but Goldhagen's argument is specifically that German culture had already done the socializing before they put on uniforms. They didn't become antisemites in the battalion; they arrived with a worldview where Jewish life wasn't fully human life. The peer pressure wasn't do it or we'll hurt you - it was do it or your a weakling who doesn't understand the racial reality. That's ideology working through social pressure, not instead of it.

Jordan: Okay but then your view cant explain the variation. Some guys consistently refused or asked for other duties without being severely punished. If the culture was total, refusal should have been impossible or fatal. The fact that refusal was socially awkward but not lethal suggests the pressure was situational conformity, not deep worldview enforcement. And Browning allows for antisemitism as background - he just denies it was the engine.

Sam: Goldhagen actually addresses those refusers - they were often people with personal relationships that disrupted the abstraction, or who hadn't fully bought in. But the important thing is that refusal was rare not because authority punished it, but because peer contempt made it unthinkable. That's culture, not just situation. Plus, your ignoring that they brought their wives to watch, or celebrated weddings right after killings. That doesnt fit ordinary men reluctantly doing a job. That fits people who saw this as legitimate work.

Jordan: I think your overreading the wedding thing. People compartmentalize. The same surgeon can do a brutal operation and then have dinner with family. Compartmentalization is a human universal, not proof of special ideology. And bringing wives - that could be showing off the authority of the job, like look at my important work, not look at us exterminating subhumans. You need a prior commitment to Goldhagen's specific theory to read it his way.

Sam: But that's circular! Your saying they must have had ideology because they acted like people with ideology, and then using that to prove the ideology existed. Browning gives a cleaner account: authority structures, incremental commitment, and the desire not to let comrades down. Those are universal mechanisms. To prefer Goldhagen, you need to show the culture was uniquely eliminatory - but every army in history has used peer pressure and compartmentalization. The specificity has to come from the ideology, and you've just admitted the behavior is generic.

Jordan: Not quite. I'm saying Browning's mechanisms are universal, which is exactly why they're sufficient to explain this case without adding a special German cultural toxin. Goldhagen needs that toxin to be special and pervasive. But the refusal rate, the need for alcohol, the psychological breakdowns - those look like people who were being pushed past their limits, not people expressing a shared cultural mission.

Anyway, can you adjudicate this? Go through each round and say who's making the stronger point, and what both are missing. Also, if I'm framing this wrong - like if the whole ordinary men vs willing executioners framing is just a false dichotomy that historians have moved past - tell me that straight up instead of playing along. I don't want you to fake confidence if the premise is confused.
