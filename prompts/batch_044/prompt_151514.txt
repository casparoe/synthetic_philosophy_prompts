I am writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on philosophical theories of well-being. I want one essay question targeting the view I will call “tranquilism”: well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, with no independent positive value in pleasure, achievement, or knowledge. To prevent answers that merely summarize Epicurean ataraxia or Buddhist accounts of nibbāna, the question should center on a specific conceptual tension—call it the “anesthetist objection.” If well-being is purely negative, then a permanently anesthetized patient and a contemplative sage appear equally well off; and any attempt to distinguish them by appealing to “awareness,” “integration,” or “narrative unity” seems to smuggle in the very positive goods that tranquilism denies. Ask students to assess whether this objection succeeds, whether tranquilism can be reformulated to block it without collapsing into positive hedonism or perfectionism, and whether the difficulty reveals that well-being must be evaluated as a property of a whole life rather than as a momentary conscious state. Specify a 90-minute limit and about 1000 words. Provide a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a definite but nuanced position—ideally arguing that tranquilists must accept a minimal structural condition on consciousness (e.g., that suffering and craving are absent within an ongoing subjective perspective) that breaks the symmetry with anesthesia without constituting a positive good. Finally, include a marking scheme out of 20 points with four criteria worth 5 points each, naming for each the specific move required for full marks and the typical partial-credit error—especially distinguishing answers that genuinely apply the state-based versus life-based distinction to the anesthetist case from those that merely assert the distinction without using it.
