I sit on the final review panel of the Voss Foundation, which awards exactly one annual grant for research in theoretical ecology. This cycle we have two proposals—Project Meridian and Project Drift—that received identical peer-review scores, identical budget justifications, and address precisely the same gap in the literature. Our charter requires that “every funding decision be supported by a substantive reason distinguishing the selected proposal from its alternatives,” and it explicitly forbids splitting a single grant.

One panelist argues that we must therefore postpone the award until some hidden asymmetry emerges, invoking the classical problem of choice without preference: if there is genuinely no reason to prefer one to the other, then selecting either is arbitrary and violates the charter’s rationality requirement, just as Buridan’s ass starves between identical bales rather than choose irrationally. Another panelist counters that postponement imposes real costs—both teams have tied up personnel and lab space for a decision window that closes in ten days—and that insisting on a “substantive reason” in a genuinely tied case forces the panel to manufacture distinctions, which is itself a form of irrationality. She proposes we invoke a pre-registered random lottery, arguing that an institutional body need not have psychological preferences to act rationally, and that procedural fairness can substitute for substantive differentiation.

A third panelist raises a less common concern: even if we adopt the lottery, our charter asks for a “reason,” and “the coin came up heads” is a causal explanation, not a normative justification; she worries that collective bodies are held to stricter standards of reason-giving than individual agents, because no single member can claim personal preference to cover the decision.

Draft the panel’s formal decision memo to the board of trustees: recommend either Project Meridian, Project Drift, or the lottery mechanism, with a concrete implementation timeline and conditions, and defend that recommendation. The memo should take seriously the strongest version of the “no choice without reason” objection—do not dismiss it by asserting that perfect ties are impossible in practice—and should address whether an institutional body can be said to “prefer” one option over another in any meaningful sense, or whether our charter’s demand for substantive justification applies differently to collective decision-making. Give a definite recommendation with explicit reasons, rather than laying out the options for the board to resolve. Note also where your recommendation depends on a specific view about collective agency or procedural rationality that a dissenting trustee might reject.
