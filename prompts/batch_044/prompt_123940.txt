I have been discussing life priorities with a close friend who is at a crossroads between two very different professional paths. One path is in a competitive, high-stakes field—something like venture-backed entrepreneurship or elite performance art—where success brings intense exhilaration and public recognition, but also constant anxiety, status comparison, and periodic collapse from exhaustion. The other path is far more modest: steady work with clear boundaries, little ambition for distinction, and a lifestyle deliberately designed to minimize desire itself—no chasing promotions, no craving for acclaim, just enough comfort to avoid hardship. My friend asks me which life is genuinely better, not merely more pleasant, and I find myself unable to answer with the usual language of balance or following one’s passion.

What makes me hesitate is a view I encountered recently in some philosophical writing about well-being—though I cannot recall the exact source—which suggested that the only thing that genuinely improves a life is the reduction of craving and suffering, and that the so-called positive states of joy, excitement, or triumph are not intrinsic goods at all, but merely temporary disturbances of an otherwise neutral baseline. On that account, the high-stakes career is not a mixture of goods and bads; it is essentially a machine for generating suffering disguised by occasional spikes of intensity that leave no lasting credit. The modest life, by contrast, is closer to the ideal not because it is happier in any conventional sense, but because it eliminates the root conditions for discontent. I am not fully convinced, but I want to know whether this reasoning holds up, and whether it should actually guide my friend’s decision, or whether there is a hidden flaw in treating all positive affect as illusory compensation.

If I take this view seriously, it seems to imply that we should not merely prefer tranquility when it is available, but actively avoid building lives around goals that stimulate intense wanting, even when those goals are culturally celebrated. Before I advise my friend to reject the exciting path entirely, I want to understand whether this is a coherent philosophical position or just a rationalization of risk aversion, and whether there are concrete objections—perhaps about the value of agency, the meaning of achievement, or the asymmetry between creating and removing suffering—that show why the absence of craving, however peaceful, might still fail to constitute a complete account of human flourishing.
