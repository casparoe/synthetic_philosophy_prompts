I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine that revisits the philosophical question of whether dreams are experiences in any robust sense, using the disagreement between Norman Malcolm's skeptical line and Daniel Dennett's reconstructive challenge as its backbone. Rather than a standard prose comparison, I want a taxonomy presented as a decision tree: each node should be a concrete claim or case judgment—phrased so that an educated reader can answer it without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to—and each branch should split the logical space cleanly. The root should ask whether phenomenal consciousness occurs during REM sleep. From there the tree should move through whether access-consciousness is present but blocked by sleep-state amnesia, whether awakening reports are direct retrievals or reconstructions, and—this is the angle I want pressed—whether any reconstruction is best understood as episodic contamination (waking memories bleeding into and reshaping the report) or as narrative imposition (the mind generating coherent story structure with minimal specific input from sleep-state traces).

Please build the tree so that it contains no fewer than six and no more than eight leaves, with Malcolm's position included (there is no criterion for ascribing experience during sleep, so dream reports are not reports of prior experience), Dennett's family included (experience may be present but is either formed during sleep and only experienced upon awakening, or constructed de novo at the moment of waking rather than recalled), and the ordinary common-sense direct-recall view included. Each leaf should carry the view's established name if it has one, or be marked "unnamed" if it does not, plus one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—say, what it must say about false awakening reports or about the phenomenology of lucid dreaming. Where two leaves turn out to be genuinely indistinguishable on every branch, flag them rather than forcing an artificial split. Identify any combination of answers that produces an apparently unoccupied leaf—especially a view that accepts phenomenal consciousness during sleep yet insists that awakening reports involve no reconstructive element whatsoever—and briefly diagnose whether that gap is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Write for an educated general audience; do not request bibliographies, citations, or specific publication dates, since the taxonomy should rest entirely on reasoning about cases and commitments.
