I am writing a term paper for my metaphysics and philosophy of science seminar, where we have been examining Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and the classic objections raised against it. We spent considerable time on Max Black's thought experiment of a symmetric universe containing nothing but two spheres that share every qualitative property and stand in identical spatial relations to each other, and I understand why this is usually taken to show that two objects can be numerically distinct despite being perfectly indiscernible.

Recently, however, I encountered a line of argument—discussed briefly in our graduate reading group—that extends Black's symmetric-world model into quantum mechanics. The idea is to treat identical particles in an entangled symmetric state as a micro-scale analog to Black's spheres: because they lack distinct positions within a shared wavefunction and have no relational properties that could differentiate one from the other, they appear to be indiscernibles without spatial distinction in precisely the way Black imagined. From this, I have developed a view that I want to defend in my paper: that quantum exchange symmetry does not violate Leibniz's principle but actually confirms it, since fermions that share all properties—including the absence of definite spatial coordinates—are not genuinely distinct individuals. On this account, the Pauli exclusion principle should be interpreted not as evidence that two particles occupy different states because they are numerically different, but rather as a statistical constraint on state occupancy that applies to a single, non-individuated quantum field.

Could you assess whether this reasoning is sound? In particular, I want to know whether the absence of spatial differentiation in Black's macro-spheres genuinely entails numerical identity in the quantum case, where the analogy between symmetric universes and quantum statistics might break down. I am especially interested in whether my interpretation of the exclusion principle as a purely statistical artifact misdescribes its physical role, and whether there is a principled reason—within either metaphysics or physics—to resist collapsing quantum particles into Leibnizian indiscernibles despite their lack of distinguishable relational properties.
