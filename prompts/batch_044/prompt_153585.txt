I'm working through my judgments about the harm of human extinction, specifically the sense—related to what has been called the afterlife conjecture—that the badness exceeds the aggregate of individual deaths. Here are six scenarios and my intuitive verdicts on each.

Case 1: A sudden, painless extinction event kills all eight billion people instantly. Earth and its archives remain intact, but no descendants or successors exist. Verdict: catastrophically bad. The harm feels vastly greater than eight billion separate tragedies added together.

Case 2: A gradual, voluntary decline over two centuries. Every generation lives fully, completes major projects, and perfect archives are preserved. The last human dies peacefully, knowing there will be no one after. Verdict: still seriously bad, but clearly less bad than Case 1. The prolonged anticipatory grief makes individual lives somewhat worse, yet the absence of an unfinished collective legacy seems to reduce the overall harm.

Case 3: Humans go extinct suddenly, but an independently evolved intelligent species takes over Earth, genuinely appreciates human culture, and continues analogous philosophical and scientific projects. Verdict: the catastrophic quality largely dissolves. There may be a small residual harm—like the loss of a specific cultural lineage—but it is not comparable to Case 1.

Case 4: Humans never go extinct, but a universal, irreversible neurological change eliminates the capacity for abstract reasoning, long-term planning, and artistic creation. Billions live content, simple lives forever. Verdict: this feels nearly as bad as Case 1, and perhaps worse. Biological survival without the capacities that structured human meaning seems like a living extinction.

Case 5: All biological humans die, but prior scans allow every person to be uploaded into virtual environments. The uploads reproduce by copying, maintain science and philosophy, report well-being, and possess subjective continuity with their biological predecessors. Verdict: genuinely ambiguous. One part of me says extinction has been avoided entirely; another part insists there is a distinct loss that makes this somewhat worse than Case 3, despite direct psychological continuity being preserved.

Case 6: Earth is sterilized instantly, but self-replicating probes carrying embryos and archives are already en route to a distant star system, guaranteed to establish a human-descended civilization in ten millennia with no knowledge of Earth. Verdict: better than Case 1, yet I still judge the Earth event as very bad—worse than Case 3, and perhaps closer to Case 2. This suggests that geographical or epistemic continuity with origins matters in a way that is hard to justify consistently with my other judgments.

My question: is there a coherent principle that captures all six verdicts, and if so, what does it predict for cases I have not considered? For instance, what should I say about a scenario in which humans survive only as a small, isolated population that preserves culture but can never expand or influence the wider universe? Or about a scenario in which extinction is certain but will occur only after every living person has completed every project they personally care about?

If no single principle saves all six, I want an honest accounting: which verdict must be dropped to restore coherence, and what is the simplest principle that accommodates the remaining five? I am especially interested in whether my tension between Case 3 (value-continuity with aliens is largely sufficient) and Case 5 (direct continuity in uploads is ambiguously sufficient) reveals that I am implicitly relying on biological embodiment, or whether it shows something else—perhaps that I care about the modality of continuation in a way that undermines my own judgment in Case 4.
