I am an undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on vagueness, semantic indeterminacy, and the sorites paradox, and I also serve as a student adjudicator in our university’s informal resolution office. We must decide whether a petition demonstrates that a peer’s conduct has moved from merely awkward interaction into substantial interpersonal disruption—a predicate that is intentionally undefined in our charter. The decisions recur weekly, each must be rendered in roughly twenty minutes from a written case file alone, the adjudicator needs no legal training, and the determination can affect a student’s housing and registration status. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what the philosophy of vagueness actually establishes—specifically the sorites structure, epistemicist claims about sharp but unknown boundaries, supervaluationist gap zones, and the margin-for-error principle—translated into an ordered sequence of tests that a non-philosopher can apply consistently.

Please construct the procedure as a flowchart in words: list the questions in the exact order they must be asked, specify for each what counts as a sufficient answer, indicate whether a yes or no advances the case, stops it with a negative determination, stops it with a positive determination, or triggers a hand-off to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than determinable fact. The procedure must explicitly flag where judgment cannot be eliminated, where it is likely to misfire, and which theoretical distinctions—such as higher-order vagueness about the boundary of the boundary, or the precise location of an epistemicist cutoff—have been simplified or dropped for usability, with a brief defense of whether that simplification is acceptable.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical petitions. First, a complainant alleges that a lab partner altered shared workspace arrangements seven times over one semester, each time offering a different preference-based reason, and now asserts that the cumulative pattern is disruptive; address how the procedure handles aggregation across a sorites-like chain of individually permissible acts. Second, a respondent admits to exactly three instances of the contested behavior, and the charter’s only quantitative guidance is an illustrative list that uses the word repeated without specifying a number; show how the procedure resolves or suspends judgment here. Third, include one case in which the procedure delivers a determination that the complainant will find unacceptable—specify which of the three this should be, explain why the procedure mandates that outcome despite the complainant’s objection, and identify the precise point at which a human adjudicator could invoke a values-based override that the procedure itself is not authorized to grant.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot settle, under what conditions it should be suspended entirely, and whether there are nested vaguenesses—such as the vagueness of unwelcome itself embedded inside the vagueness of substantial disruption—that render any step in the chain permanently unstable. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need something I can run from memory in the office, not something that sends me to a library.
