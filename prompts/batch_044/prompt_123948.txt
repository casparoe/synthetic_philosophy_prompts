Draft the joint statement that a committed Nordic-model advocate and a committed full-decriminalization advocate—specifically one who takes New Zealand’s removal of sex-work-specific criminal provisions and application of general labor law as the benchmark—could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on policy and principle. I want the document structured as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

In the agreed-claims section, include only claims both sides would genuinely endorse: about the actual legal architectures in the Nordic-style regimes, in the German and Dutch legalization frameworks with their licensing and registration provisions, and in the New Zealand-style framework; about which empirical patterns are genuinely disputed versus established; about the normative aims both sides accept as legitimate (worker safety, reduction of coercion, autonomy, and state accountability) even if weighted differently; and about the conceptual distinction between decriminalizing sellers, criminalizing buyers, and applying general commercial labor regulation.

In the disagreements table, present each residual disagreement in two parallel formulations—one phrased so the Nordic-model side would accept it as accurate to its view, one phrased so the decriminalization side would accept it—without either formulation being a straw man. Cover at least: whether asymmetrical criminalization reduces net harm or displaces risk; whether state-funded exit services expand meaningful choice or impose a conditional welfare framework that undermines the voluntariness it claims to protect; whether sexual services can be coherently governed by standard labor-contract logic or whether intimacy and stigma require sui generis regulation; and whether legalization regimes that license brothels create a dual market that systematically disadvantages undocumented or unregistered workers.

Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. I want at least three cruxes specified precisely. One should be conceptual: whether consent to exchange sexual services under severely constrained economic alternatives should be indexed to the same moral framework as consent in other labor markets, and whether the responsibility for expanding those alternatives lies with the state or with the individual worker. Another should be predictive: what both sides agree is the relevant evidence about displacement effects and worker-reported safety under each regime, with each side recording in advance what pattern it predicts and what it would count as undermining its view. A third should concern institutional design: whether third-party management in this sector is more analogous to standard labor brokerage or to a uniquely coercive risk vector, and what concrete regulatory feature—say, worker-led cooperative governance versus licensed-employer oversight—both sides agree would be the decisive test case.

For each crux, state the concrete case, argument, or empirical pattern both sides accept bears on it, then record each side’s explicit prediction. Where the disagreement turns out to be partly verbal—say, about whether “decriminalization” means removing all sex-work-specific law or merely removing penalties for sellers—say exactly which part and why.

Keep the prompt self-contained: do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific studies by author and year. Do not invoke obscure theoretical frameworks that depend on highly specialized literature; build the conceptual distinctions—such as the labor-contract versus service-contract framing or the indexing problem for consent—directly into the prompt’s own wording so that a model can engage them without external lookup. Write in formal, polished prose.
