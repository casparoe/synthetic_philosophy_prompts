Design a 60-minute seminar activity for 12 graduate students in epistemology, with no technology except printed cards and a whiteboard, that makes them inhabit the tension of epistemic akrasia driven by higher-order evidence—specifically before anyone names the concept. The idea they should feel is not merely "I should lower my confidence" but a sharper, less-discussed variant: asymmetrical reflective access, where an agent has transparent access to the content of her own reasoning but only opaque, statistical access to its reliability, and this gap produces a form of rational self-undermining that standard peer-disagreement models do not capture. The rules, roles, and incentives—not the facilitator's explanation—should generate the experience.

Deliverables: (1) the full rules and materials, including a payoff or scoring scheme that uses only small, non-monetary stakes (e.g., points toward a collective reward or individual ranking); (2) the staged sequence of rounds, with exactly what information each participant receives when, and what decision they must record; (3) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal structure of first-order judgment, higher-order evidence, and the asymmetry of reflective access, and that states frankly which feature of the idea the activity can reproduce and which it cannot (the latter must be supplied by the facilitator); (4) a prediction of what will go wrong the first time this is run and how to recover. The activity must work without any participant having read about asymmetrical reflective access beforehand, and the facilitator should not lecture about it until after the debrief mapping is complete. Keep all framing in everyday language—no bibliographies, citations, or assigned readings.
