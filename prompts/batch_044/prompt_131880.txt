I've been arguing with my roomate about whether the French Revolution actually has this structural tendency to devour its own children, or if that's just a catchy phrase people slap on 1793 because Robespierre got guillotined. I built this argument for a study-group and now it feels like it collapses everything into nonsense. Here are the steps:

(1) If class-struggle explains the Terror—meaning the bourgeois leadership eventually gets purged because it can't stabilize state power without being replaced by more radical popular forces—then any revolution led by a class that doesn't already control the bureacracy should eventually turn on its own founders.

(2) That applies not just to 1789 but to 1848 Germany, 1917 Russia, and 1949 China, since in each case a rising group seized state machinery it hadn't previously run.

(3) But 1848's leaders were crushed mostly by Prussian armies from outside, not by internal purges; and 1949's founding generation wasn't consumed by an internal bloodletting comparable to 1793 (the Cultural Revolution was later and structurally different).

(4) So the class-struggle view either makes false predictions or has to save itself with ad-hoc excuses—like saying 1848 failed before it could devour, or that China's delay just proves the rule.

(5) To avoid those saves, reject class dynamics and accept pure contingency: the Terror was caused by the specific convergence of foreign war, Vendée rebellion, and economic crisis in 1793.

(6) But if it's pure contingency, then the idea that revolutions devour their children isn't a special feature of revolution at all—it's just extreme crises sometimes kill leaders, which applies equally to non-revolutionary regimes like the Roman Republic during its civil wars.

(7) So either we keep the idea and accept it applies to every class-led revolution (which history contradicts), or we keep accuracy and admit it describes nothing unique to revolutions.

(8) Either way, the interpretive framework for 1789 collapses.

I feel like there's a hidden slide between (5) and (6), or maybe (1) is smuggling in an equivocation about class, but I can't pin it down. Which step should I reject, and what's the cost—do I have to give up the idea that contingency and structure can both play a role, or is there a cleaner exit?
