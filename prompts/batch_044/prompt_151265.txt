I sit on the Allocation and Ethics Committee of the Meridian Foundation's Frontier AI Program, and we face a decision that has split the board sharply. A research consortium, the Interpretability-First Lab, has submitted a proposal requesting a three-year protected funding envelope—approximately forty percent above standard rates—to develop a frontier-scale language model under what they term an explicit alignment-tax protocol. The protocol mandates mechanistic interpretability milestones that must be satisfied before each capability scaling step; they estimate this will reduce final benchmark performance by eighteen to twenty-two percent relative to an unconstrained baseline, and they agree to a binding deployment freeze until those milestones are verified externally. Crucially, they ask not only for funding but for a public institutional endorsement stating that voluntary acceptance of such performance penalties is the ethically required standard for frontier research. Half the committee argues that this is precisely the kind of structural intervention needed to neutralize the competitiveness constraint—the dynamic by which competitive pressure forces every actor to minimize safety investments—because it demonstrates that high-alignment-tax pathways are institutionally viable. The other half contends that endorsing this framework creates a dangerous normative crowding-out effect: by segregating responsible development into a subsidized niche, we implicitly license the broader field to treat alignment costs as optional externalities that only niche actors bear, thereby accelerating the very race dynamics we intend to slow, while our protected branch remains too small to influence the frontier.

Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the funding request outright, approve with specific conditions, or reject it—together with an actionable timeline and criteria for implementation or denial. It should treat the competitiveness-constraint argument with full seriousness, including the claim that institutional shelter can alter the strategic calculus for other actors, and it should give an equally honest account of the strongest counter-argument: that institutionalizing the alignment tax as a special-case virtue risks normalizing its absence everywhere else. The reasoning should be accessible to board members without specialized philosophy backgrounds, but it should not flatten the conceptual tension into a generic risk-benefit analysis; in particular, it should engage the specific question of whether treating the alignment tax as a permanent, constitutive design feature rather than a temporary inefficiency changes what obligations we have as funders. Include a clear bottom line that the committee could vote on, and identify one specific condition or follow-up action that the losing side could plausibly demand as a concession.
