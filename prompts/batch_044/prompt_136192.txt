I've been working through Augustine's argument that lying is always impermissible, even to save a life. He claims that language was instituted to express one's thoughts, and that lying corrupts this purpose by making the speaker's words diverge from their mind. Recently I encountered a thought experiment meant to test this framework: consider a language model that generates a false statement without holding any beliefs or intending to deceive anyone. By Augustine's definition, this is not a lie, since there is no mental content being misrepresented. But now compare it to a human who sincerely believes a falsehood and repeats it in good faith: here too there is falsehood without deceptive intent. Both cases violate the communicative purpose of language — they transmit what is not the case — yet Augustine allows honest error while condemning lying. This suggests that the corruption Augustine cares about is not the failure of language to match reality, but specifically the willful divergence of word from mind. If that's right, then his appeal to the purpose of language is actually doing less work than his moral psychology: he objects to lying because it damages the liar's soul through an act of will, not because false speech is inherently dysfunctional. But then why does he need the linguistic argument at all? Couldn't he just say that willfully misrepresenting one's thoughts is sinful, regardless of language's purpose? And if so, doesn't this reduce his famous prohibition to a claim about internal integrity rather than about speech — which would mean that silent deception, or deliberately leaving out the truth, should be just as sinful, yet he treats them differently?
