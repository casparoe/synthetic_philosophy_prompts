I am preparing a chapter for my metaphysics seminar on the problem of material constitution—specifically the long-running dispute about whether a statue and the clay that constitutes it are one object or two. The literature since the 1970s has produced an impressive array of technical distinctions—constitution versus identity, temporary intrinsics, dominance conditions, relative identity, and various mereological solutions—yet the central disagreement seems impervious to refinement. Constitution theorists maintain that the statue and the lump of clay are numerically distinct entities that occupy exactly the same region of space at the same time, differing in their modal and historical properties; their opponents, whether identity theorists or eliminativists about artifacts, insist that this multiplies entities without explanatory gain and that 'statue' and 'lump of clay' are just different descriptions, or different temporal phases, of a single material object. Despite the sophistication of both sides, I find that no argument advanced in either direction is ever accepted by the opposition as anything other than a restatement of prior commitments.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily empirical or formal—everyone accepts that there is clay arranged in a particular shape, and everyone accepts that destroying the shape destroys the statue—but rather a disagreement about what metaphysical questions are worth asking and what counts as an acceptable answer. Is this a substantive dispute about ontology and coincidence, a verbal dispute about the semantics of artifact terms, or a case of two research programs—one concerned with objecthood, the other with our conceptual taxonomy—talking past each other under the label 'material constitution'? I want a diagnosis that engages the actual moves each side makes: do they disagree about facts, about standards of explanatory parsimony, or about bedrock intuitions concerning essential properties like artistic origin and shape? Furthermore, I want an honest assessment of whether anything could break the deadlock—a formal result in mereology, a distinction that dissolves the puzzle, a demonstration that the question is ill-posed because artifacts lack metaphysically serious kind-conditions, or whether the recent framing in terms of temporary intrinsics is genuinely progressive rather than a relabeling of the same impasse. What, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress?
