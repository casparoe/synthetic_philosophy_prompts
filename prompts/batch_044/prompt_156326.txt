Having spent my career in structural engineering and only recently turned to philosophy of biology, I find myself returning to an analogy that seems to do heavy argumentative work: evolutionary features are said to be like the spandrels of San Marco—those triangular interstitial spaces that arise inevitably between arches and domes, carrying decorative mosaics not because the architect intended ornament there, but because the geometry of load-bearing structure makes them unavoidable. Critics of what is often called the adaptationist programme invoke this image to suggest that biologists routinely mistake such inevitable byproducts of developmental or structural constraints for purpose-built adaptations shaped by natural selection.

I would like you to audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the arch, the spandrel, the builder's design intent, the decorative mosaic, and the structural necessity? Second, map the disanalogies precisely. Biological structures are self-assembling, capable of phenotypic plasticity, and subject to co-option—exaptation—whereas stone spandrels are static and cannot be repurposed as load-bearing elements; evolution has no conscious architect, only differential reproductive success; and developmental constraints, while real, operate through pleiotropic gene networks rather than through the rigid geometry of masonry. Third, distinguish the breakdowns that genuinely undermine the anti-adaptationist conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the analogy illegitimately import the idea that evolutionary "constraints" are as non-functional and inert as architectural ones? Finally, if the spandrel comparison fails in significant respects, what more accurate analogy—perhaps drawn from engineering redundancy, thermodynamic waste heat, or modular system design—would better capture the notion of a non-adaptive byproduct that may nonetheless become functionally integrated?
