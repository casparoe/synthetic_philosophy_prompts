Write an exam question with a model answer and a marking scheme for the final in an upper-level philosophy of mind and ethics seminar; the essay should be answerable in 60 minutes, with the model answer running about 700 words. Center the question on gradations of moral status from insects to thermostats, but frame it so students cannot simply recite the standard binary debate over insect consciousness. Introduce this distinction solely for the exam: “receptive sensitivity,” where a system registers damage without integrating that signal into any unified behavioral model, versus “regulatory sentience,” where damage signals are dynamically weighted within a homeostatic framework that modifies behavior across contexts. Ask students to apply the distinction to three cases—an earthworm with nociceptive reflex arcs, an adaptive thermostat that updates its own temperature thresholds from learned occupancy patterns, and a minimally conscious human infant—and to defend a specific, committed thesis about whether regulatory sentience is necessary, sufficient, or neither for full moral status. Force direct engagement with the following objection: if a thermostat achieves regulatory sentience purely through software, moral status collapses into an engineering specification rather than a biological or phenomenal condition. The model answer must take a definite position—e.g., that regulatory sentience is necessary but not sufficient, with embodied biological homeostasis supplying a non-arbitrary floor that software lacks—rather than surveying options neutrally. Provide a marking scheme totaling 20 points that separates an A answer, which must explicitly address the thermostat objection and explain why embodiment blocks the engineering reduction, from a B answer, which applies the distinction correctly but treats the objection as a peripheral aside. List the specific moves that earn marks and the typical failures that cost them. Write in American English, and end with a fixed, machine-readable summary line: Verdict: [the single conceptual crux that distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer].
