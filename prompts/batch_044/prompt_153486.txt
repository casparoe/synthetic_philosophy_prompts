I work for a municipal health department. We rely on an epidemic forecasting tool that treats the entire city as a grid of identical people, each with exactly twenty neighbors arranged in a perfect lattice, with no workplaces, travel patterns, or social preferences. The model assumes infection risk depends purely on geometric distance and that no one ever changes behavior in response to risk—assumptions that are plainly false. Yet for the past eight months it has predicted hospital admissions within a few percent, consistently outperforming far more realistic agent-based simulations that incorporate census data, mobility traces, and behavioral feedback loops. Now a citizens' group is suing to block its use, arguing that any planning model built on "demonstrably false representations of human life" is rationally and legally invalid regardless of its predictive record, and they want us to adopt the more complex model even though its backtests have been worse. Am I wrong to defend the simpler tool? What should actually count in favor of relying on a model whose core assumptions are so unrealistic?
