Right, so I was at a rationalist meetup in London — well, more of a pub corner after the talk — and two people I know got stuck on the St Petersburg paradox. I thought they both made decent points but also talked past each other a bit. Here's roughly what went down, with me just listening.

Jordan said the usual diminishing-utility fix feels like cheating. You're just bending the curve until the expectation converges, but if the payout schedule is genuinely linear in value then a rational agent should still pay infinite, so the paradox hasn't been solved — it's been hidden behind an arbitary assumption. Riley said that's unfair, risk behavour isn't pulled out of nowhere, it tracks the fact that an extra quid matters less when you're loaded. But Jordan wasn't convinced, and said okay, let's forget utility curves for a second: any real bank has finite capital, so there's always a physical payout cap. Once you model the truncated game with max payout 2^N, the fair price is about N pounds, which is totally sane. The infinite version is just a maths fiction that ignores implementation.

Riley pushed back: you can always scale the stakes. If round one pays £1, round two £4, round three £16, eventually you blow past any fixed cap, however large. The physical limit only tells us the game stops at round N; it doesn't explain why you'd reject round one at a fair price. Jordan said that's like saying Zeno's paradox is solved because people can't take infinitely many steps in finite time — you're mixing up the map and the territory. The paradox asks about the idealised object, and for that object you still need a decision rule that handles infinite expectations. Riley admitted that point but said risk aversion is the only principled one we have.

Then Jordan dropped this thing he calls "cognitive coherence" — basically, human minds can only integrate outcomes up to a certain narrative scale. If a payout exceeds your lifespan, your social network, or your capacity to form intentions about it, it doesn't register in your utility function properly. So the curve flattens not because of money, but because of representational limits in the brain. That's a physical-cognitive boundry, not an ad hoc curve.

Riley said that sounds like diminishing utility with neuroscience branding. The result is still "utility flattens," so nothing new is solved. Plus a superintelligence could play with linear utility up to astronomical values, so Jordan's resolution only applies to humans, not rational agents generally. Jordan said maybe that's correct — the paradox was always about human rationality anyway — but he also argued that the deeper lesson is that rational choice needs bounded expectations, not just bounded payouts. If expected value breaks down at extremes, maybe it's the tool that's broken, not the agent.
