As a novelist examining how societies justify inertia, I keep returning to the stalled dispute over Burke's argument from prescription joined with Chesterton's fence. Defenders present the fence as an epistemic humility heuristic: institutions survive because they solve problems we no longer understand, so reform requires knowing the purpose of the barrier. Critics reply that this is either an argument from ignorance or a status quo bias that preserves exclusionary structures, and they catalogue both successful revolutionary dismantlings and oppressive fences kept intact by conservative rhetoric. The exchange has not moved in decades. I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Is the disagreement verbal—turning on whether "knowing why" requires explicit, articulable justification or can be satisfied by institutional longevity—or is there a substantive, hidden claim about whether social institutions encode tacit knowledge that resists individual retrieval? Are the sides actually answering different questions, one about rational risk-management under collective uncertainty and another about the moral weight of inherited injustice, while using the same metaphor? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would genuinely count as progress: a formal distinction between epistemic and moral reasons for preservation, an empirical study of institutional knowledge transmission, or an argument that no resolution should be expected?
