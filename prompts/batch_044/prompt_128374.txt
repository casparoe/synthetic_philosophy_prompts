I am trying to settle my own view on a precise claim: in domains where the option space is combinatorially explosive and the cost of further computation is part of the outcome being evaluated, selecting the first alternative that exceeds an aspiration level—set without reference to a global optimum—is not merely a second-best heuristic but the rationally correct procedure, and attempts to approximate full optimization are actually irrational because they violate a principle of non-self-defeating deliberation.

Please give me your actual credence in this claim, decomposed into four sub-questions with a specific number and a one-line justification for each, then combined into an overall figure. Specifically: (a) the probability that bounded rationality is best conceptualized not as an external resource constraint but as a constitutive feature of agency that shapes what counts as a reason; (b) the probability that a coherent normative framework exists prescribing stopping rules for deliberation that do not covertly depend on the optimal benchmark they claim to reject; (c) the probability that existing institutional practices—such as fixed judicial sentencing grids that halt once statutory ranges are met, or triage protocols that cease diagnostic testing once a treatable cause is identified—implicitly treat satisficing as legitimate rather than as a failure mode of rationality; and (d) the probability that the aspiration level in a genuinely normative satisficing procedure can be defined in a non-circular way without smuggling in distributional knowledge available only to an optimizer. Then tell me which of these four terms you are least confident in, what specific argument or empirical finding would most shift it, and whether your overall number is driven more by skepticism about the normative framework or by doubts about the institutional interpretation.
