A colleague pressed this argument on me and I cannot find the flaw. (1) Any coherent right to leisure must draw a line between permissible work and wrongful overreach. (2) The Protestant work ethic treats labor as a calling: work possesses intrinsic value, and leisure is justified only as recuperation for further labor. (3) The 35-hour week draws that line at a fixed quantitative threshold, but the number is legislatively arbitrary; it treats leisure as a distributable commodity rather than a status tied to human flourishing. (4) Karoshi shows that when work is experienced as vocation, people voluntarily cross any quantitative boundary, and societies often honor such death rather than condemn it. (5) So defending leisure rights requires either accepting arbitrary caps that cannot catch vocation-driven overwork, or denying that work has intrinsic value—which would condemn not just karoshi but also voluntary, meaningful overtime. (6) But if work lacks intrinsic value, then all labor beyond bare subsistence is exploitation, which is absurd. (7) Therefore the concept of a right to leisure is incoherent: it is either too weak to prevent karoshi or so strong that it prohibits all passionate labor. I am committed to keeping (2)—that work can indeed be a calling—without accepting (7). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
