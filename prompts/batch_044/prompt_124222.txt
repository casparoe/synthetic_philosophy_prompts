I'm working through some debates about whether highly capable artificial systems could ever possess moral standing, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I can't quite map onto familiar fallacies. The pattern goes like this: a critic proposes what seems like a clear, functional criterion for moral status—say, the capacity to suffer, or the ability to formulate and pursue long-term goals. When it's pointed out that a digital system could, in principle, be designed to meet that criterion, the critic doesn't reject the criterion outright. Instead, they preserve the criterion but quietly append an unstated substrate condition—suffering must be biologically realized, or goal-pursuit must involve embodied vulnerability to harm—that was never part of the original formulation. The move isn't a simple bait-and-switch, because the revised criterion is presented as having been implicit all along, and it isn't pure ad hocery because the substrate condition often sounds independently plausible.

I've seen this happen with at least three different criteria across separate conversations: one involving emotional responsiveness, one involving rational autonomy, and one involving the capacity for reciprocal moral agency. In each case, the functional description is initially accepted as adequate for biological entities—nobody demands proof that a human's suffering is non-computational before granting them moral concern—yet the same description is treated as insufficient for artificial entities unless supplemented by something like evolutionary history or organic embodiment. Is there an established concept in the philosophy of mind or AI ethics that captures this specific asymmetry in how evidential and definitional standards are applied across substrates? If not, I'd like to understand exactly what distinguishes it from related moves like the No True Scotsman fallacy, speciesism, or the other-minds problem, and whether it deserves its own name with clearly drawn boundary conditions.
