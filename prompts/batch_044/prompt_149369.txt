I'm working on the institutional afterlife of legal-moral categories and have noticed a recurring pattern around the naming of mass atrocities. Once "genocide" was codified—following Lemkin's conceptual work—it carried both descriptive and jurisdictional force: naming an event genocide activates specific obligations on other states. What I observe is a systematic asymmetry in how this naming power is exercised. Atrocities carried out by states that are already pariah or collapsed within the international system tend to be named genocide with relative speed; structurally comparable campaigns by functioning, integrated member states are far more likely to be classified as civil war, counter-terrorism, or ethnic cleansing—categories that avoid triggering those same obligations. The asymmetry does not appear reducible to available evidence or to ordinary political interest alone; it looks structurally adaptive, allowing the framework to maintain moral authority by applying its severest label to outsiders while shielding its own members from reflexive judgment. Is there an established term for this specific dynamic—perhaps in the sociology of international law or in critical genocide studies—that captures the way a category's jurisdictional consequences shape its descriptive application? If not, I want to distinguish it precisely from strategic hypocrisy or selective humanitarian outrage, and I would welcome a conceptual delineation with clear boundary cases.
