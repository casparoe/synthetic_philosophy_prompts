I'm preparing a presentation for an advanced course on philosophy of mind and cross-cultural epistemology, and I've become stuck on a problem that seems to sit between two usually separate debates. The first concerns what philosophers sometimes call ascription universality: the claim that the conditions for attributing mental states—beliefs, intentions, and other propositional attitudes—are governed by norms that apply uniformly across all possible thinkers, rather than being indexed to particular communities or cognitive architectures. The second is the critique of epistemic dominance, which charges that presenting one tradition's norms of reasoning as universally binding is not a neutral description but an exercise of power that delegitimizes divergent forms of cognition, whether in neurodivergent populations or in non-Western epistemic traditions.

The tension arises because if ascription universality is false and mental-state attribution is genuinely local, then cross-cultural interpretation collapses into incommensurability; yet if it is true and enforced as a criterion for mindedness, it appears to license exactly the kind of epistemic dominance that critics warn against. Write an essay that examines whether this tension is real or merely apparent. Consider whether one can defend a form of universality—perhaps by distinguishing descriptive conditions for interpretation from normative prescriptions about rationality—that avoids the charge of dominance, or whether the two commitments are necessarily intertwined. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it: either show that universality can be preserved in a non-dominating form and explain how, or argue that the cost of avoiding dominance is too high and universality must be abandoned. I want a committed response that takes a clear stand, makes its underlying conceptual distinctions explicit—especially any distinction between descriptive and normative universality—and does not retreat into a survey of possibilities without a verdict.
