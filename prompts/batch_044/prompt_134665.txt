You are a philosopher of mind with experience designing seminar exercises. Before drafting the activity, briefly brainstorm two distinct mechanics that could force students into an interpretive stance rather than an introspective one. Then design a single 90-minute classroom activity for 12 advanced undergraduates or graduate students. The goal is to make them feel the methodological force of heterophenomenology: treating first-person reports of conscious experience as texts requiring interpretation rather than direct windows onto inner states, and recognizing that this interpretive stance applies symmetrically regardless of source. To avoid a familiar recap, inject this specific twist: students work in triads (interpreter, skeptic, recorder) with a sealed envelope of ten ambiguous experience reports—five drawn from human diarists and five generated by a large language model describing subjective states—without knowing which is which. Their task is not to identify the source but to build a coherent heterophenomenological world for each report using only consistency, predictive coherence with other reports, and explanatory utility; they must assign each report to one of three categories (stable world-entry, unstable or confabulated entry, indeterminate) and justify the assignment without appealing to source ontology. Structure three 20-minute rounds with shifting roles, include a constrained crisis round in which two reports directly contradict and the group must decide which to preserve in the world-model and which to bracket, and provide a debrief that maps round 1 onto the rejection of introspective privilege, round 2 onto ontological neutrality, and the crisis round onto the distinction between interpreting a report and denying its reality. Be explicit about the feature this activity cannot reproduce—namely, the long-term empirical integration of heterophenomenological data into a full science of consciousness—and predict the predictable failure mode: students will try to reverse-engineer source identity through stylistic tells rather than applying interpretive criteria. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
