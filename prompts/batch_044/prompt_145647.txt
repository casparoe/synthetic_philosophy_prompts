I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned to philosophy and the conceptual foundations of economic theory. Having worked through the canonical texts of the socialist calculation debate—Mises's argument that rational economic calculation requires actual market prices for capital goods, Hayek's emphasis on dispersed tacit knowledge and the entrepreneurial discovery of genuinely unknown opportunities, and Lange's reply that iterative trial-and-error adjustment by administrative boards could simulate competitive market outcomes—I wish to push the dispute into a less familiar reconstruction. Please compose a dialogue between two interlocutors of equal philosophical competence: one defending Hayek's epistemic and entrepreneurial critique, the other advancing a modernized Langean market socialism. Their exchange should center specifically on whether a fully decentralized algorithmic pricing architecture—one in which autonomous local nodes adjust prices through real-time feedback loops driven by local excess-demand signals, without any central computation of general equilibrium and without private ownership of the means of production—can genuinely replicate the discovery function of entrepreneurship, or whether it necessarily collapses into an engineering simulation that misses the speculative, risk-bearing element Hayek considered inseparable from true market discovery.

The Hayekian should argue that residual claimancy and exposure to genuine financial loss are not incidental institutional decorations but constitutive conditions for the kind of error-correcting exploration that reveals previously unknown uses for resources; a distributed algorithm, however responsive, merely optimizes against given signals rather than creating new knowledge through proprietary judgment. The Langean should respond that if the feedback architecture aggregates dispersed local information through iterative adjustment—precisely the informational logic Lange intended—then the entrepreneurial function is reduced to a problem of signal processing, and private ownership becomes redundant rather than essential. Ensure genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must address the specific claim just made by the other, not merely restate a familiar position. I ask for a committed response—take a definite position on whether this algorithmic reconstruction escapes Hayek's critique or ultimately falls back into it, defend that verdict with explicit reasoning, and identify the conceptual point on which the disagreement genuinely turns.
