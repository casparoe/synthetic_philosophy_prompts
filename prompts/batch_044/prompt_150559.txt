I'm writing up my commitments on institutional reform and epistemic humility for a forum thread, and I'd like a consistency audit before I post. Here is the set:

1. I hold a Burkean view: long-standing social institutions embody distributed tacit knowledge that exceeds what any individual can explicitly reconstruct, so sweeping rationalist redesign is usually epistemically reckless.

2. I take Chesterton's fence as an epistemic precaution, not just a metaphor: one should not dismantle a norm, practice, or rule until one understands the specific failure mode it was erected to prevent.

3. I think the fence binds with different strength across domains. For evolved cultural practices—common-law traditions, kinship structures, ritual norms—the burden for removal should be extremely high, because the design rationale is tacit and distributed. For recently engineered systems with explicit documentation—corporate governance rules, technical standards—the burden is lower, since the rationale is recoverable from records.

4. I add a strong preservationist clause: if the original rationale for a fence is not merely unknown but conceptually opaque—because the problem was framed in categories our current conceptual framework no longer possesses—then we should preserve the fence even when it appears harmful, unless we have direct empirical proof that the underlying problem is obsolete.

5. At the same time, I believe institutional and moral progress is real and sometimes requires tearing down fences. I think removal is justified when we have robust empirical evidence that the fence is net harmful and that the original problem is obsolete—even if we still do not understand exactly how the fence solved it.

6. I restrict Burkean deference to institutions that have survived multi-generational selection pressure. Recent social experiments or top-down engineered policies do not earn the same epistemic deference, because they have not been filtered by long-term survival.

My worry is that 4 and 5 pull in opposite directions: if the rationale is conceptually opaque, how can we ever claim good reason that the problem is obsolete without grasping the problem? There also seems to be friction between 2 and 5, though I suspect that dissolves once you distinguish understanding the mechanism from knowing the problem has expired. Meanwhile, 3 and 6 look redundant but might diverge on engineered systems that have survived long periods—say, a century-old corporate charter.

Please audit this set. For each pair or cluster that is genuinely in tension, rank the severity and identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency—specifying exactly which commitment gives and what it costs. Also flag any pairs that only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if revised, would clean up the most tension overall. Treat the tensions as uneven: not every pair is equally bad, and I am not signaling which one I am prepared to drop.
