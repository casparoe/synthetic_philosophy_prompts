Write a sestina on the contest between cyclical and linear visions of history, using the rotating end-words "spring," "decline," "line," "return," "empire," and "ruin." Let the formal cycle of the sestina—each stanza reshuffling the same six terms—stand for the cyclical view (Polybius’s constitutional rotation, Ibn Khaldun’s decay and renewal of group solidarity, the Spenglerian and Toynbean life-cycles of civilizations), while the poem’s irreversible march through six stanzas toward an envoi stands for the linear-progressive view. The envoi should bring all six words together not to declare a winner but to suggest that the pattern is neither pure circle nor straight line—perhaps a spiral that claims to be returning. Do not merely name-check the thinkers; let their mechanisms appear in the imagery (solidarity fraying, regimes degenerating into their opposites, the illusion that each new empire is unprecedented). Keep it accurate enough that a historian of ideas recognizes the theories being enacted, and precise enough that the form itself is the argument.
