I have been thinking lately about a well-known philosophical complaint called the separateness of persons — the idea that treating people as interchangeable units in a moral calculation misses something important about how each life stands alone. I am not a student of philosophy, but I have been trying to work out exactly what that complaint amounts to, and I discussed it with two friends who are also non-specialists. They ended up in a disagreement that I could not settle. Here is their exchange.

(1) Mara said the heart of the objection is that every individual possesses a distinct moral ledger. A harm inflicted on one person cannot be canceled out by a benefit conferred on someone else, just as one person's debt cannot be settled with another person's bank account.

(2) Leo responded that this interpretation makes the objection self-defeating. If ledgers can never be merged in any way, then it should be impermissible to save five people rather than one, since that decision requires weighing five separate ledgers against a single one. The objection must therefore be narrower: it targets only those cases in which one person is actively used as a means to produce benefit for others, not every comparison across persons.

(3) Mara then proposed a more precise case. Suppose a fixed quantity of some good — for instance, years of healthy life — can be distributed in either of two ways: one person receives eighty years and another receives twenty, or both receive fifty. The aggregate is identical. Mara argued that the separateness objection should lead us to prefer the equal division, not for the usual reasons of fairness or envy, but because allocating the first person's potential thirty years to the second person involves a form of cross-personal borrowing. Even though no one is actively injured, treating one individual's possible well-being as fungible with another's violates the principle that each person's goods are structurally non-transferable.

(4) Leo countered that this notion of cross-personal borrowing is merely egalitarian redistribution disguised in metaphysical language. If we truly forbade any movement of potential well-being between persons, he said, we would have to reject every pool of shared resources, every progressive contribution, and every organ-sharing system. Mara, he claimed, is not defending separateness but smuggling in equality under a different name.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four points above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are overlooking, and specifically address whether Mara's concept of cross-personal borrowing represents a legitimate extension of the separateness objection or merely restates an independent egalitarian principle.
