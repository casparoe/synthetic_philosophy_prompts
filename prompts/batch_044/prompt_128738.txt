I'm developing a framework that treats the self not as a metaphysical substance or as a stream of psychological continuity, but as a center of narrative gravity—an organizational abstraction around which experiences, intentions, and memories cluster in roughly the way physical bodies cluster around a center of mass. I want to push this further than the standard individual-level account: I think narrative gravity is scalable. A collective agent—say, a tightly integrated research group, a long-running institution, or even a hybrid human-machine system—can generate a distributed center of narrative gravity if its members produce interlocking first-person reports that achieve high predictive compression. On this view, mass is the density of these interlocking reports, and gravity is the causal scaffolding they provide—the way they constrain future action, consolidate memory, and make certain explanations more available than others. The self, then, is not merely a fiction told about an underlying reality; it is a real pattern of causal organization, but one that exists only at the level of narrative dynamics and can in principle be instantiated in non-biological substrates.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for the standard objections—say, that narrative is too loose to do metaphysical work, or that distributed selves violate our intuitions about moral responsibility—unless they strike at the heart of the scalable, causal-scaffolding claim. I want the defeaters ranked: which observations, conceptual results, or empirical findings would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward? For instance, if predictive processing in collective cognition turns out not to depend on first-person narrative integration at all, does that collapse the view or only force a retreat to individual-level gravity? Would a demonstration that artificial systems can achieve equivalent causal scaffolding without any interlocking first-person reports undermine the claim that narrative is doing the work, or could the defender absorb it by redefining report? I also want an honest assessment of whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—whether any failure of narrative integration can be explained away as a shift in center of mass rather than the absence of one—and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory that aspires to explain real organizational patterns. Please include what a committed defender of this scalable narrative-gravity model should themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics say, and conclude with whether the position is well exposed or largely protected.
