I'm attempting to determine whether everyday social conventions—tipping, queuing, jaywalking—generate genuine moral obligations for temporary visitors, or whether they remain merely local etiquette that outsiders may disregard without fault. To frame the question, I distinguish between what I shall call "normative assimilation" (adopting local rules because they sustain welfare-sensitive coordination practices) and "moral tourism" (treating local norms as optional cultural spectacles that impose no duty on the observer). Here are my intuitive verdicts on particular cases, which I hope will reveal whether there is a coherent principle behind them, or whether some verdict must be abandoned.

In a Manhattan diner where staff wages are legally sub-minimum and depend upon gratuities, I judge that a British tourist who refuses to tip acts wrongly—this feels closer to wage deprivation than to mere rudeness. In Tokyo, where tipping can embarrass staff and violate hospitality norms, I judge that the same tourist ought to refrain, and that pressing cash upon a server would be the moral error. Queuing at a London bus stop strikes me as obligatory for anyone present, visitor or resident, because the line is a fragile coordination device that collapses if even one person defects; but jaywalking in central Berlin when no vehicles are present seems permissible for a visitor, unless they are accompanied by local children, in which case it becomes wrong—not because of traffic risk, but because it models contempt for a shared rule that locals treat with near-sacred seriousness. I also think there is a meaningful difference between violating a convention that harms identifiable individuals (skipping the queue means someone else waits longer) and violating one that merely expresses cultural difference: ignoring a "no shoes" rule in a private home feels rude but not immoral, whereas ignoring it in a mosque feels genuinely wrong.

Yet I encounter tension. I believe a visitor ought to follow local tipping norms even when they find them morally arbitrary—say, the expectation that one tips hotel porters in New York but not in Oslo—yet I also believe a visitor need not participate in conventions they regard as unjust, such as gender-segregated queuing in certain public contexts, even if refusal causes local offence. Is there a unifying principle—perhaps involving direct harm, coordination failure, complicity in injustice, or respect for autonomous communal practice—that accommodates all these verdicts? Or must I drop either the assimilation obligation or the conscientious-objection exemption? Furthermore, what does the principle predict for a case I have not resolved: should a visitor to a culture with mandatory "gift-giving" to public officials (a practice they regard as bribery) participate to avoid social ostracism, or does the moral-tourism exemption apply?
