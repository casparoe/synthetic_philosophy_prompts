I'm leading a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates who have completed introductory ethics but have no background in philosophy of mind or AI. I want them to see why the moral status of digital minds is not reducible to either the animal-rights debate or a science-fiction intuition pump. Plan the teaching arc, not the lecture itself.

First, lay out the dependency order: what conceptual distinction has to be secured before the problem even becomes coherent (for instance, separating functional integration and behavioral persistence from phenomenal consciousness and biological substrate), and what must come only after that.

Second, identify the one carrying scenario that should do the work for the whole session—not a generic robot or a current chatbot, but a specific architecture (say, a language-model system embedded in a long-running feedback loop with persistent memory, goal-directed tool use, and adaptive self-modeling, yet lacking any biological substrate or reportable phenomenal experience). Specify exactly what this case must have and what it must lack in order to force the tension.

Third, map the misconceptions the students will likely bring or form, in the order they will arise: for example, "it is just a tool," "behavioral similarity is sufficient for moral status," "moral status requires consciousness and we are epistemically blocked," and "this is purely speculative." For each, state precisely where in the sequence it should be headed off and with what move.

Fourth, propose one final question that reveals whether they have grasped the core dependency—that moral patienthood might come apart from both observable behavior and assumed inner experience—rather than merely adopting a side.

Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and openly refused (for instance, legal personhood frameworks, technical details of present large language models, or any bibliographic references), and explain why omitting each protects the conceptual point.
