I'm currently enrolled in a philosophy of mind course that examines how mental states come to be about things. Outside of class, I volunteer with a small community archive that relies on an old automated sorting apparatus—essentially a mechanical decision tree with only two binary input sensors—to categorize donated artifacts. Over the years, regular users have begun speaking of the machine as if it genuinely prefers certain historical periods or resists misfiled items, attributing to it a kind of autonomous judgment. Last week, a fellow volunteer argued that because the apparatus consistently tracks specific features of the artifacts and adjusts its outputs without direct human intervention at each step, it possesses the same fundamental kind of directedness that our own beliefs have, only in a more minimal form. I found myself resisting this, but my resistance seemed to rest on the idea that whatever the machine is "about" comes entirely from the intentions of the archivists who designed its rules and the users who interpret its outputs; if everyone stopped caring about its classifications, its outputs would cease to be about anything at all. I am unsure whether this distinction is philosophically defensible or whether I am simply importing an unjustified human exceptionalism.

Could you help me think through whether the machine's directedness is genuinely intrinsic to its operation or conferred by the interpretive practices around it? In particular, I am interested in whether there is a principled way to draw the line between a system whose aboutness belongs to it in its own right and one whose aboutness is borrowed from its designers and users, without merely appealing to biological complexity. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
