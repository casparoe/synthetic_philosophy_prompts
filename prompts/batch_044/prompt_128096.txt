I am presenting a thesis at a research seminar to defend the claim that microhistorical reconstruction of bureaucratic mispractice—specifically the misfiling of tax records, the misrouting of military requisitions, and the contradictory annotations left by local clerks in early modern state archives—reveals a structural fragility in state formation that macro-level histories of elite consolidation and institutional design systematically suppress. I argue that this fragility is not incidental noise but constitutive: the state’s capacity to govern depended upon continuous, error-ridden negotiation with local intermediaries whose actions left material traces precisely where official narratives claim coherence.

The audience will be six people: three social historians working in the Annales tradition, accustomed to quantitative serial data and longue durée structural analysis; two Marxist historians of state formation, who treat bureaucratic apparatuses as instruments of class consolidation directed from above; and one archival theorist concerned with the materiality, silence, and noise of record-keeping. None are trained microhistorians, and all are sceptical of methodological individualism and of treating administrative error as historically significant in itself.
