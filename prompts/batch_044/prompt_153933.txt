I've been chewing on simultaneous discovery and now I've got this argument that seems to eat itself, but I can't tell if it's valid and just ugly or if there's a sneaky mistake hiding in the middle. Here's the setup.

(1) The Newton-Leibniz calculus case and the Darwin-Wallace 1858 essay show that when two people hit the same theory within months or years, the idea was already "in the air" given the conceptual tools of the era.

(2) If an idea is in the air, then in nearby possible worlds where the actual discoverer never lived, someone else produces an equivalent result within a short window.

(3) So the actual discoverer's causal contribution is replaceable: they didn't create the idea, they just happened to be the first mouthpiece.

(4) Replaceability undermines desert for credit, priority, or fame — if you're just the first mouthpiece for an inevitable result, the achievement belongs to the logical structure, not to you.

(5) Now generalize: every result that can eventually be derived from current mathematics or science is already implicit in the existing toolkit, so anyone who proves a hard theorem — say Wiles with Fermat's Last Theorem — is just performing an unavoidable extraction that a sufficiently persistent researcher would eventually do.

(6) But we clearly think Wiles did something extraordinary and that his specific proof path matters, not just the fact that Fermat's statement was eventually resolved. So (5) is false, and the chain from (1) to (5) must break somewhere.

I'm stuck between rejecting (3) and rejecting (1). If I reject (3), I'm saying that being the actual causal pathway matters for credit even when the result was counterfactually inevitable. That saves Wiles, but then I have to explain why Darwin should have cared so much about Wallace sending that essay in 1858 if Wallace was just another inevitable mouthpiece. If I reject (1) and deny inevitability altogether, I'm ignoring what the paired discoveries actually suggest.

There's also this weird edge case I've been calling a "conceptual bottleneck" — suppose only one specific formulation of a theory unlocks an entire field, and two people hit that exact formulation within months. Does that make the formulation less theirs? I feel like there's a hidden slide between "someone else would have done it eventually" and "it dosen't matter who actually did it," maybe an equivocation on "replaceable," but I can't quite name the step.

Which premise do I actually have to give up, and what's the least painful option? If I keep (1) and (4), am I forced into some weird view about scientific authorship?
