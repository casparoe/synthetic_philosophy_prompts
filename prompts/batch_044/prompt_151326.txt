Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been attending a philosophy of mind seminar that applies a rigorous eliminativist framework to belief and desire, treating them as dispensable in favor of dynamical descriptions rather than reducible to them. The instructor's verdicts are these: an adult human's belief that it will rain should be eliminated and replaced by hierarchical predictive-coding dynamics; a rat's apparent desire for food should be replaced by reward-prediction-error signaling; a thermostat should not be ascribed beliefs, not for lack of complexity but because it possesses no globally revisable representational structure; yet a large language model maintaining coherent, context-sensitive representations across extended reasoning should be described with belief-like terms, since its states are genuinely revisable and semantically integrated in ways that resist replacement by weight-level dynamics alone. I am trying to determine whether her principle tracks explanatory replaceability—eliminating folk talk whenever lower-level dynamics fully substitute—or tracks substrate and architecture together, so that biological organisms are always eliminated, artificial systems are retained when functionally complex, and simple mechanisms are excluded outright. The new case is a brain-computer interface that directly modulates a human subject's predictive-coding hierarchy, such that the hybrid system, rather than the biological person, updates and maintains globally accessible representations about external conditions. Predict her verdict: would she eliminate belief-talk for the human component, extend it to the hybrid system, apply it only to the artificial module, or invoke a different distinction? Identify the two rules that best fit her prior four judgments, state what each predicts for this interface, and name one further case—say, a distributed swarm of robots with shared predictive models but no central controller—that would best distinguish them.
