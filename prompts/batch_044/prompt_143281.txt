I'm defending a thesis I'm calling "diachronic asymmetry" at next Thursday's interdisciplinary workshop on Intergenerational Equity and Climate Policy: pure time preference is irrational and should be eliminated within a single life, yet it is rational and even obligatory across generations, because the metaphysical and moral conditions that bind temporal stages of one agent—narrative continuity, anticipatory self-concern, and special obligations of diachronic care—do not obtain between non-overlapping generations, leaving intergenerational justice governed solely by distributive fairness under scarcity. From this I derive that climate-cost models should apply a zero pure discount rate to welfare within current lifespans but may legitimately apply a positive pure rate to benefits falling beyond a generational boundary, a position that inverts the usual structure of the Stern-Nordhaus debate rather than splitting the difference. The audience will be climate economists steeped in Ramsey discounting and present-value accounting, moral philosophers for whom Parfit's "mere location in time" objection is second nature, and Treasury policy analysts who care less about metaphysics than about whether my view can survive insertion into a cost-benefit framework without collapsing into either present-biased parochialism or intergenerational paralysis.

Please prepare me for the Q&A as a speaker's prep sheet, not as a defense of the view in the abstract.
