I spent thirty years in behavioral ecology and insect neuroethology before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind and action. From that vantage I have developed a view about sphexishness and free will that I want rigorously stress-tested. The standard discussion—evoked by the well-known presentation of the digger wasp Sphex—treats mechanical rigidity and cognitive flexibility as opposing categories. I reject that binary. On my account, sphexishness is not merely the absence of flexibility but a specific architectural property: open-loop execution of fixed motor programs that lack counterfactual branching at the point of action. Human cognition, I argue, is constructed from the same kind of micro-rigid nodes, but arranged in nested closed-loop architectures with sufficiently high-dimensional parameter spaces that the global system exhibits context-sensitive behavior indistinguishable from genuine choice. Free will is therefore not an illusion in the eliminativist sense, but a macroscopic functional description of deterministic, sphex-like processes operating across complex feedback loops. Moral responsibility survives, but only as a pragmatic stance we take toward high-dimensional agents, not as an ontological category that distinguishes human beings from wasps.

What would count against this? I want the specific arguments, empirical findings, and conceptual distinctions that should actually move me—not the routine compatibilist reply, but the observations from comparative cognition, neuroscience, or philosophy of action that undermine either the continuity thesis (that human cognition is micro-rigid at its base) or the pragmatic reconstruction (that responsibility can coherently be rebuilt on this architecture). Rank these defeaters from decisive to merely awkward. Say which ones defenders of a high-dimensional sphexishness account would themselves concede as damaging, and identify whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—particularly if any apparent counterexample can always be absorbed as "just another feedback loop." Finally, tell me whether any single result could force a choice between abandoning the micro-rigidity thesis and abandoning the pragmatic account of responsibility, or whether these two commitments are independently protected in ways that make the view immune to refutation.
