I serve on the allocation committee for our university's newly launched Interdisciplinary Consciousness Initiative, which must award a single $180,000 pilot grant by the end of this quarter and report preliminary outcomes to the provost within six months. We have received two competing internal applications that both claim to address the meta-problem of consciousness—the challenge of explaining why we have such persistent intuitions of an explanatory gap between physical processes and subjective experience, regardless of whether physicalism is ultimately correct—but they diverge radically on what resolving it requires. Application A, led by the cognitive neuroscience unit, proposes a twelve-month "introspective-access disruption" study: subjects perform concurrent working-memory loads while making fine-grained qualitative reports, with the working hypothesis that degrading introspective access weakens the gap intuition, thereby treating the meta-problem as an artifact of representational architecture rather than as a feature of consciousness itself. Application B, a collaboration between philosophy and clinical phenomenology, proposes a "phenomenal-concept mapping" protocol that treats the gap intuition as structurally significant: they aim to formalize the precise representational format of phenomenal concepts—specifically, whether they operate indexically, analogically, or through some hybrid mode—arguing that dissolving the intuition prematurely would miss something essential about how consciousness presents itself to itself.

The committee must recommend funding A, funding B, splitting the award with enforceable conditions, or rejecting both and reopening the call; whatever we choose must include concrete implementation milestones tied to our institutional mandate that any funded consciousness research demonstrate a credible pathway to either clinical diagnostics or AI-alignment applications within eighteen months. This mandate has become the central axis of disagreement: Application A claims that dissolving the meta-problem removes conceptual obstacles to neurofeedback interventions, while Application B claims that only an accurate map of phenomenal concepts can ground safe artificial systems that do not systematically misrepresent human values. I need a formal decision memo—no more than three pages—to circulate before Thursday's vote. It should state a clear recommendation with a specific timeline and review checkpoints, engage seriously with the strongest philosophical argument on the losing side (in particular, whether the meta-problem is best understood as a problem about conceptual isolation between phenomenal and physical-functional vocabularies or as a problem about the non-propositional format of first-person representation), and address honestly whether the eighteen-month relevance constraint forces a false choice between empirical tractability and conceptual rigor. The memo should treat neither proposal as conceptually confused, should avoid requesting further bibliographies or citation lists, and should not rely on references to specific named theories that we have not already discussed in committee; our task is to decide on the basis of the conceptual and methodological merits already presented.
