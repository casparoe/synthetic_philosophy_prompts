I'm giving the remarks at my old advisors retirement dinner next friday — she taught the course where I first read Hobsbawm and Anderson, and about half the room are historians who will wince if I pretend universities are ancient guilds. The other half are her family, who just want to hear shes loved and dont need a lecture on imagined communities. I want the speech to do the actual work of her scholarship: treat this dinner itself as an invented tradition, since the idea that a career ends with a ritual banquet is basically modern bureaucratic theater pretending to be timeless. Write it so I cant fall back on words like legacy or lineage that she spent forty years debunking, but it still lands warm and not like an exam. Structure it as exactly five parts, each just a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; maybe note that were an imagined community right now — students and cousins — held together only because we all showed up and agreed to act like we share something older than last tuesday.
