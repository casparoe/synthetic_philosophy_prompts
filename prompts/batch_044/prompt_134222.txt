Design a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with concrete case-nodes—for positions on whether human death is defined by the irreversible loss of organismal integration, the irreversible loss of consciousness/personhood capacities, or a pragmatic disjunction of circulatory and neurological markers. Build it for a philosophy-of-medicine workshop audience, with the explicit goal of sorting participants by what they say about edge cases rather than by which label they prefer. The tree must open with a root node asking whether artificial maintenance of circulation and respiration counts as preserving the life of the organism when the brain is absent, then proceed through four diagnostic scenarios presented in neutral language without naming theories: (A) a brain-dead body sustained on ECMO with spontaneous cardiac contraction and gas exchange; (B) an anencephalic newborn with intact brainstem reflexes and spontaneous breathing but no cerebral cortex; (C) a decapitated cadaver maintained on mechanical perfusion for isolated organ preservation; and (D) a patient in long-term persistent vegetative state with intact hypothalamic and brainstem homeostasis but no cortical responsiveness. Each internal node should pose a concrete yes/no/three-way question—e.g., whether brainstem function alone sustains organismal integration, whether the absence of consciousness alters biological status, or whether decapitation and brain death should be treated identically—so that a respondent can navigate without knowing where the branches lead. The leaves must include at minimum a strict circulatory criterion, a whole-brain criterion, a brainstem-only/higher-brain split criterion, an unnamed organismal-integration criterion (if it occupies a distinct region of the logical space), and a disjunctive legal-pragmatic criterion; for each leaf, provide one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment, such as being forced to classify the ECMO-sustained brain-dead body as alive or the anencephalic infant as dead. After locating the named positions in the tree, map the full logical space of answer-combinations across scenarios A–D, mark which combinations are occupied, and for each unoccupied combination briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, practically unattractive, or merely unexplored—paying special attention to any combination that accepts brain death as biological death while denying it applies to the decapitated perfused body. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your diagnostic questions genuinely fail to separate—specifically, positions that diverge only on metaphysical grounds not captured by the case answers—and explain exactly why the tree collapses them together. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named philosophers, or requests for source verification; construct the taxonomy entirely from the logical structure of the cases and their combinations.
