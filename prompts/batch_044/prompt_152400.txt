I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral taxonomy and historical memory, and we recently reached an impasse when a classmate invoked a line that circulates constantly in memorial discourse and human-rights rhetoric: "To rank suffering is to diminish it." She used it to block my attempt to distinguish, for analytic purposes, between an atrocity structured as centralized state policy and one that emerges as systemic collateral damage—not to excuse either, but to ask whether the category "atrocity" itself conceals important internal distinctions. She insisted that any such ordering, even purely conceptual, violates the dignity of victims by treating incomparable horrors as entries on a scale. I sense that her objection bundles together several different arguments under one sentence, and I want to pull them apart.

Could you disambiguate that slogan into its precise logical forms? For each possible reading—epistemic (we lack standing or data to compare), ontological (suffering is not the kind of thing that admits degrees or ordering), axiological (comparison corrupts moral response by substituting calculus for reverence), and performative (the public act of ranking causes specific harm to survivors or collective memory)—state whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, false, or conceptually confused. Then indicate which versions actually support shutting down comparison entirely, and which merely demand that comparison be restricted to specific dimensions or carried out with extreme care. I am especially interested in whether the phrase smuggles in a semantic ambiguity—treating "ranking" as necessarily cardinal quantification rather than ordinal or qualitative differentiation—and whether exposing that ambiguity dissolves her objection or simply relocates it to a deeper dispute about what kinds of moral distinctions are permissible.
