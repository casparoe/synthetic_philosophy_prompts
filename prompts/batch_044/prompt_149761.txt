Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and epistemology, I have developed the habit of treating conceptual disagreements as if they possessed measurable parameters, and I find the literature on peer disagreement especially puzzling in cases that are not straightforwardly empirical. Peer A treats this adaptability as a genuine epistemic virtue that warrants maintaining belief despite the failures; Peer B treats it as a post-hoc rationalization that should lower confidence. The dispute is therefore not about a particular observable fact but about whether a second-order methodological feature should carry first-order epistemic weight.

What is your actual credence—expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1—that conciliationism, rather than steadfastness, is the correct normative response in precisely this kind of case?
