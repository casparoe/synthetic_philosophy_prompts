I am writing a literary novel in which a journalist conducts an extended, intellectually demanding interview with an unnamed contemporary philosopher who defends a reconstructed, rigorously Leibnizian account of morality—one that derives the moral law not from sentiment or social convention but from the metaphysics of perfection and the principle of sufficient reason, treating moral truths as necessary features of the eternal region of ideas that God contemplates in selecting this world. Because the scene must feel philosophically authentic rather than merely decorative, I need the interview to avoid biography and summary entirely and instead press on the live tensions in the view. Before drafting anything, please take a moment to think carefully about the specific vulnerabilities in this position: the apparent circularity between defining the good via perfection and perfection via the good; the difficulty of explaining why a finite, self-interested agent should care about global metaphysical harmony; and the problem of evil as it interacts with the claim that this world maximizes compatible perfection.

Please act as both an expert in early modern philosophy and an experienced producer for a serious podcast or literary magazine interview series. Begin with brief brainstorming on three clusters that are genuinely hard for this reconstructed view: first, whether Leibnizian moral rationalism can accommodate deep moral disagreement without reducing every opponent to mere intellectual blindness; second, how the doctrine of pre-established harmony affects the possibility of genuine moral agency, intersubjective obligation, and the moral significance of interpersonal encounter; and third, whether there is any conceptual room for supererogation—acts that are good but not strictly required—given that every actual state is already part of an optimally arranged whole. Then draft ten to twelve questions in total, arranged in these clusters. For each cluster, provide an opening question that assumes familiarity with the doctrine, a brief sketch of the reply the philosopher is most likely to give, and a sharp follow-up keyed precisely to that reply, pressing where the literature actually presses. The tone should be formal, precise, and accessible to an educated general reader, suitable for publication in a high-end literary magazine's interview column, and the questions should never ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic recommendations.
