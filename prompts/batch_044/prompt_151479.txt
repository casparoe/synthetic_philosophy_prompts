I am preparing the interview guide for an upcoming episode of my philosophy-of-mind podcast, and I want the questions to be genuinely probing rather than introductory. The guest is an unnamed philosopher—let us call her Dr. Voss—who defends a specific variant of the extended mind thesis. She accepts the parity principle in roughly the Clark-Chalmers formulation, but she rejects the standard accessibility conditions—constant availability, automatic endorsement, and prior endorsement—as necessary for cognitive extension. In their place, she advances what she calls functional transparency without accessibility: an external resource counts as part of cognition when its absence produces a distinctive epistemic discontinuity in reasoning, an abrupt failure of inferential coherence that the subject experiences as a gap in her own thought rather than as the loss of a mere instrument. She applies this especially to long-term digital archives that a researcher consults only intermittently, arguing that if the archive's organizational structure mirrors her inferential patterns closely enough, the discontinuity upon removal is constitutive of extended cognition even in the absence of conscious awareness or continuous coupling.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual conceptual architecture of her position, with a follow-up for each that anticipates her most likely reply and then presses it further. I want one question on the coupling-constitution fallacy: ask how her discontinuity criterion distinguishes genuine cognitive extension from mere causal coupling when the agent lacks conscious access to the resource, and anticipate her appeal to the phenomenology of reasoning gaps; the follow-up should ask whether that phenomenology can be reliably distinguished from the ordinary frustration of losing a useful external tool. Include a second pair on cognitive bloat as applied specifically to institutional memory—if a court's case-law database meets her criteria, does the court itself possess an extended mind, or does her view require an individual subject? Anticipate her reply that institutions are mere aggregates, and then press whether her discontinuity criterion can coherently apply at the collective level without collapsing into a group-mind thesis she likely rejects. Add a third pair on artificial intelligence: when a researcher relies on a large language model as an inferential partner, does the model become part of her extended cognition, or does the absence of shared biological history and evolutionary coupling block the application? Anticipate her distinction between embedded and genuinely extended AI use, and follow up by asking what observable feature of the interaction would reveal which category applies rather than being interpreted post hoc. Finally, include at least one question on non-derived content—whether representations in her intermittently accessed archive must carry meaning that is not derived from her own mental states, and whether her rejection of accessibility undermines the usual arguments for such content—again with a follow-up keyed to her probable defense. Throughout, avoid generic biographical questions; keep the focus on the conceptual structure, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
