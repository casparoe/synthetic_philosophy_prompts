At a recent philosophy-of-mind discussion group I attend outside of work, two members—Jordan and Sam—got into a sustained argument about luminosity and the KK principle. I work in software engineering and have no formal philosophy background, so I followed the logic without recognizing every reference. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not tell who was right at each turn. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander; I do not favor either side, and I want a point-by-point adjudication rather than a simple overall winner.

Jordan opened by defending a restricted KK principle: if a subject genuinely knows that p, and that knowledge is based on direct introspection rather than inference, then the subject is in a position to know that she knows that p. Jordan said this holds at least for clear, attended mental states—feeling acute pain, being certain of a simple arithmetic result, or recognizing a familiar face—because in those cases there is no hidden intermediary that could fail without the subject noticing.

Sam countered with the standard anti-luminosity argument, but applied it to a modified case. Rather than “feeling cold,” Sam proposed “feeling professionally certain about a design decision.” Sam argued that certainty, like temperature, comes in continuous gradations, but our introspective access works with discrete thresholds—like a digital sensor that bins analog input into “confident” or “uncertain.” Under this “binned introspection” model, there will be boundary cases where a subject is in state S (say, barely over the threshold into certainty) but the introspective mechanism registers “uncertain” because of quantization error. Sam said this means you can be in a mental state without being in a position to know you are in it, which violates luminosity, and if luminosity fails, the unrestricted KK principle collapses.

Jordan replied that Sam had smuggled in an engineering metaphor and mistaken measurement error for metaphysical vagueness. Jordan said mental states themselves are either present or absent; the binning is a feature of the reporting mechanism, not of the state. So if you genuinely are in the state “certain,” you can know it—the fact that a poor measuring tool might misclassify near-boundary cases does not show that knowledge of one’s own state is impossible in principle.

Sam came back with a sharper point: even if the state is discrete, its boundary conditions are unknown to the subject. There will be cases near the boundary where the subject is genuinely certain but does not know exactly where the boundary lies, so she cannot rule out being just barely over the line into uncertainty. Sam said this is structurally identical to the warming-room case: at each step the change is imperceptible, but eventually the state is gone. Jordan said this only shows that knowledge requires being safely away from boundaries, not that KK fails altogether.

Jordan then proposed a “safety-margin” version of KK: if you know p and your knowledge is robust—well away from the boundary—then you know you know p. Jordan claimed this saves the principle for all practical purposes and explains why boundary cases feel puzzling without undermining the core idea.

Sam said this saves KK only by retreating to a trivial version that applies exactly when introspection is already unproblematic. Sam argued that if the principle is restricted to “safe” cases, it loses its philosophical bite, because the interesting claim was that knowledge is transparent to itself even in marginal conditions.

Finally, Sam extended the point to my own field: when a senior engineer says “I fully understand this legacy module,” there is usually a gradual accumulation of hidden complexity. The engineer can be wrong about her own level of understanding in exactly the boundary-zone way Sam described. Jordan objected that understanding a codebase is a skill-based, object-directed judgment, not a bare mental state like feeling pain, so the analogy to luminosity is a category mistake.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of Jordan’s initial defense, Sam’s binned-introspection counter, Jordan’s measurement-error reply, Jordan’s safety-margin proposal, Sam’s trivialization objection, and Sam’s software-engineering analogy, rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Also say whether their disagreement is genuinely about the KK principle and luminosity, or whether part of it concerns something else—such as whether mental states have sharp boundaries, whether engineering analogies are legitimate in philosophy of mind, or whether the principle needs to cover marginal cases to be interesting. Finally, identify anything important that both parties are missing.
