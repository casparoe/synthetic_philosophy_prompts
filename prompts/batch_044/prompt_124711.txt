Give an example of a conceptual issue arising within functionalism’s commitment to multiple realizability that does not turn on the familiar human-versus-alien or carbon-versus-silicon contrast, but rather on an actual composite architecture: suppose a subject’s working-memory system is partially implemented by neural circuitry and partially by an externally coupled digital module that replicates the same functional transitions, input-output relations, and error-correction dynamics, with causal traffic flowing continuously across the biological-digital boundary so that neither subsystem can complete the relevant cognitive task in isolation. The functionalist must decide whether there is one mental state with two simultaneous realizers, two synchronized mental states, or a single extended state whose substrate is genuinely heterogeneous. Construct the case in sufficient detail—specify the mental state, the functional profile shared by both substrates, and the point at which causal coupling becomes identity—to show exactly where the standard criteria of organizational invariance and absence of substrate-specific qualitative differences fail to yield a determinate answer about the unity of the state. Then explain what this ambiguity reveals about whether multiple realizability is best understood as a modal claim about possible worlds or as a claim about the actual constitution of hybrid cognitive systems, and identify which revision to functionalism—if any—the case demands.
