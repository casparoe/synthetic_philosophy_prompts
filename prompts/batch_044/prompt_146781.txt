After a long argument with a friend about Mary's room, I have been struck by an asymmetry in how my university's neuroscience and design divisions actually operate. In the clinical research wing, investigators treat first-person phenomenological reports—detailed descriptions of synesthetic colors, migraine auras, or phantom-limb sensations—as indispensable primary data, and regard brain scans without accompanying subjective testimony as methodologically incomplete. Yet in the vision-science curriculum and in digital-accessibility certification, the same institution behaves as if every fact about color is fully capturable by physical and algorithmic descriptions: students can complete the entire color-vision sequence without any check on whether they personally experience the phenomena they study, and accessibility offices approve color-coding schemes using spectral-simulation filters that have never been validated against the actual experience of color-blind users. Meanwhile, our philosophy-of-mind seminars present Mary's case as asking whether complete physical knowledge leaves something out, with the standard replies—that she only acquires abilities, or acquaintance, not new propositional knowledge—taught as if they resolve the tension. So what implicit theory of phenomenal knowledge does this institutional pattern actually presuppose? Must it hold that there is an irreducibly first-personal kind of knowledge, or can the clinical and educational practices be reconciled under a consistent physicalism that simply assigns different pragmatic weights to subjective reports in different contexts? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology that makes both sides cohere, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, indicate where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in either the research protocols or the certification standards if we replaced the underlying view with one that denies any special epistemic status to first-person color experience.
