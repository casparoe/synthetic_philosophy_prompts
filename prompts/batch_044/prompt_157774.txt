Consider the institutional practice in famine studies and international humanitarian taxonomy of treating Amartya Sen’s three canonical cases—the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Chinese famine during the Great Leap Forward—as belonging to different moral and causal categories, despite Sen’s uniform entitlement-collapse analysis applying to all three. In standard development economics and humanitarian law, the Irish case is typically framed as a colonial political failure compounded by crop blight; the Bengal case as a wartime market and administrative entitlement failure; and the Chinese case is often placed in a separate category of development-policy error or, in older taxonomies, partly treated as a natural disaster, with corresponding differences in whether the event is classified as a political crime, a governance failure, or a complex emergency. What philosophical commitments about causation, collective moral responsibility, and the boundary between natural and political harm does this tripartite institutional sorting presuppose? Does the practice require the view that democratic sovereignty transforms the moral status of starvation—that famine under an accountable government is a categorically different wrong from famine under colonial or authoritarian rule—or can it be reconstructed on purely epistemic grounds about evidence and attribution? And is the practice actually consistent with the implicit theory that underwrites Sen’s own argument, or does it quietly rely on a competing conception of what makes mass death a moral failure rather than a misfortune? Reconstruct the implicit theory the sorting depends on, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what a classification system built on Sen’s unified view would have to change about how these three events are recorded, funded, and held to account.
