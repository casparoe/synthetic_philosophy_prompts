I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends the self-locating-uncertainty solution to the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics — the view that, even though every measurement outcome occurs in some branch, an observer who does not yet know which branch they occupy should assign credences proportional to the squared amplitudes of those branches. I have no formal training in physics or philosophy, but I have been working through the idea on my own, and I want questions that go well beyond asking him to restate the position.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one that presses what happens when branches that have split later recombine or interfere: if an observer can eventually exist in a superposition of previously distinct branches, does the original act of self-location dissolve, and can probabilities still be assigned? At least one should ask whether treating squared-amplitude weights as rational credences requires treating them as physically causal rather than purely epistemic — and if they are only epistemic, what prevents a rational agent from placing a large bet on a branch with near-vanishing weight? At least one should ask how the view distinguishes a branch with amplitude approaching zero from one with amplitude exactly zero without implicitly smuggling in a collapse postulate or a discontinuity in reality.

For each of these three, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give — for instance, if he answers that macroscopic interference is practically irreversible, ask whether practical irreversibility is strong enough to ground objective probabilities rather than merely subjective confidence; if he says the weights are rational constraints anchored in symmetry, ask what those symmetries are anchored in if not in observed frequencies; and if he appeals to decoherence, ask whether decoherence explains the appearance of collapse or actually replaces it with a deterministic branching that still needs probabilistic interpretation. Keep the tone conversational but precise, avoid generic biographical questions, and do not ask for citations or reading lists.
