In AI alignment and formal decision theory, there is a widespread engineering practice of building explicit "world models"—tree structures in which each node represents a counterfactual trajectory, complete with its own internal dynamics, agent populations, and welfare states. Practitioners then aggregate expected value across these branches using measures that do not privilege the branch that actually occurs; from the framework's internal point of view, every branch is evaluated on equal footing and can bear its own moral weight. This looks like more than a convenient heuristic. What ontology of possibility does the practice presuppose? Does treating these branches as first-class, welfare-bearing entities commit us to something like David Lewis's modal realism, on which they are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated realities? Or can an ersatzist or strict actualist account—where only this world exists and the others are abstract representations or fictional descriptions—fully license the same design choices, including the equal aggregation of counterfactual welfare? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, and say what would have to change—conceptually and computationally—if we replaced its underlying ontology with one on which only the actual present world is real.
