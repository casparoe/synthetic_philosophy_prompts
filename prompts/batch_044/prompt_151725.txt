I'm working on a novel about an investigative journalist who works at this magazine that has a weird internal policy — they literally keep a spreadsheet they call 'epistemic risk tiers' that sorts every factual claim in a story by how much 'actionable harm' it could cause if wrong. High-tier stuff needs two primary sources and a legal review; low-tier things — like descriptions of a character's emotional state or historical ambiance — get a single fact-checker's nod, and sometimes nothing at all if the author frames it as subjecive experience. I was talking with my editor friend and she said it just makes practical sense, but I keep thinking there's some hidden philosophy of knowledge baked in. Like, does this practice presuppose that knowing something depends on what's at stake for the reader? Or is it just liabilty management dressed up? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory — maybe pragmatic encroachment, maybe contextualism, maybe something messier — and tell me where the policy is philosophically underdetermined, so it could fit more than one view. There's also this one consistance that bugs me: they apply the high-tier standard to medical claims in an opinion column but not to the same claims dropped into a fictional dialogue inside a reported story. What does that discrepancy actually say about whether they think knowledge is context-sensitive in the philosophical sense, or just legally sensitive? And if you replaced their implicit view with a pure, stakes-independent theory of justification, what parts of their workflow would have to change?
