Write a proposal for a four-month postdoctoral fellowship. The core idea: recent historiography has largely dissolved the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution as unified periods, treating them instead as retrospective clusters of local innovations with no shared essence. Yet philosophy of science still routinely invokes a sharp pre-modern/modern divide, using "the Middle Ages" as a stable negative foil—say, when contrasting modern experimental reproducibility, mathematized nature, or institutionalized peer review with an allegedly authority-bound, qualitative, and institutionally fragmented past. I want to ask whether that philosophical distinction can survive if the ruptures that supposedly created "modernity" are themselves redrawn as gradual, uneven gradients stretching from roughly 1400 to 1750. The precise question: is there a philosophically coherent account of "pre-modern scientific rationality" that does not secretly depend on treating the Renaissance or the Scientific Revolution as real breaks?

Why the existing answers leave it open: historians have shown that practices now labeled "modern" emerged at different rates across Europe and the Islamic world, and that "medieval" natural philosophy contained sophisticated quantitative and experimental work. Philosophers, however, continue to speak of "the modern scientific method" as a discrete achievement. No one has systematically tested whether the philosophical distinctions that depend on that achievement collapse once the historical boundary is replaced by local, practice-based descriptions.

Approach and method: I will take three canonical philosophical distinctions—between observation and experiment, between natural philosophy and empirical science, and between authority-based and evidence-based knowledge—and reconstruct each using only local, non-periodized language (e.g., describing specific institutional settings, instruments, and norms rather than invoking "the modern period"). I will then examine whether importing a non-European historiographical framework—specifically, how medieval Islamic scholars periodized their own intellectual history—exposes hidden Eurocentric assumptions in these reconstructions. Month one: conceptual reconstruction of the three distinctions. Month two: translation into local practice-language. Month three: comparative framework analysis. Month four: synthesis and failure assessment.

What a result looks like and what counts as failure: a successful outcome is a 12,000-word paper arguing either that philosophy of science must abandon period-based vocabulary for describing epistemic change, or that "the Middle Ages" remains philosophically usable as a negative category even if it is historiographically artificial. The project fails if all three distinctions translate cleanly into local descriptions without philosophical residue—meaning periodization is indeed inert for philosophy of science. I will report that negative result rather than invent an artificial problem.

Realistic scope: four months is sufficient for deep conceptual reconstruction and one comparative framework; I will not attempt a global historiography, only enough cross-cultural material to test whether the European tripartite scheme is parochial. No bibliography or citation list is requested; I will supply references after the conceptual framework is fixed.
