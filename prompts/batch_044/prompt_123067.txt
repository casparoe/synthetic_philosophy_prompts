Hey, I'm drafting a short essay for a phil of mind seminar and wanted to check my reasoning before I write it up proper. The basic idea is that Darwin and Turing both pull off a kind of inversion where you get competence without comprehension, but they do it in opposite directions, and I think people usually miss this.

Darwin's point, as I understand it, is that nature can produce incredibly well-adapted structures without any mind understanding the problem it's solving. The evaluation is done by the environment over generations — the mechanism is blind, but the loop is smart. Turing's imitation game, by contrast, keeps the evaluation inside the room with a human judge. The machine manipulates symbols with no understanding, but the competence is only recognised because the interpreter assigns meaning to the outputs. So Darwin outsources comprehension to evolutionary time; Turing outsources it to the human interlocutor.

I'm calling this the 'feedback-loop asymmetry' — probably a clunky name, but I think it sticks. My claim is that this matters for how we think about LLMs. They don't have an environmental selection loop like Darwin's, and they don't have a live interpreter in the loop like Turing's test. Instead they learn patterns from texts that were originally produced by comprehending minds. So they borrow competence without ever having the loop that justified it in the first place. It's like getting the outputs of both inversions without the machinery that made them legitimate.

Is this just a muddle? I feel like there's a real distinction here but I might be forcing it. I'd especially like you to take a committed view — not just lay out options and leave me to pick — on whether the asymmetry actually holds up and whether LLMs represent a genuinely different case or just Turing's scenario run at scale. Also, check whether I'm mischaracterising Turing's position on machine intelligence — I want to be sure I'm not attributing a view he wouldn't recognise.
