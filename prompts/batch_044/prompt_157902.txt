Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Mara and Jo—hanging around outside a community center where a participatory-budgeting meeting is about to start. Mara is a Deweyan experimentalist: she thinks democratic deliberation about public goods is literally social inquiry, not just preference aggregation, and that the clash between engineers, residents, and policymakers works like competing hypotheses being tested inside an institutional laboratory. Jo is skeptical. She doesn't deny democracy has value, but she thinks calling it "experimental inquiry" is a category mistake unless democratic bodies have falsifiability conditions and error-correction mechanisms comparable to scientific peer review. Jo thinks what actually happens is adversarial bargaining masked as reason-giving, and that Deweyans confuse rhetorical pluralism with epistemic progress.

Have them argue over the specific case on tonight's agenda: whether to fund a flood-barrier system designed by climate engineers versus direct cash transfers to residents in the flood zone, where the predictive models are contested, the engineers' risk assessments conflict with local knowledge about old drainage patterns, and there's no obvious "control group." Mara should defend the idea that the disagreement between experts and locals is part of the inquiry—like hypotheses held in tension—rather then noise to filter out before acting. Jo should push back that without a shared success metric like predictive accuracy or replicability, there's no genuine learning from outcomes, so "experiment" is just a metaphor for "we tried something and now we're stuck with the consequences." They need to actually respond to each other: Mara should address the metric problem by proposing that democratic experiments correct errors through institutional reflexivity—sunset clauses, participatory audit, iterative redesign—rather then replication. Jo should then ask whether those mechanisms are just political checks, not epistemic ones, and whether Mara is smuggling normative commitments in under the guise of method. Keep them both sharp, no strawmen, like a real argument you'd overhear at a conference bar. You can let one of them misspell "institutional" or drop a comma somewhere.
