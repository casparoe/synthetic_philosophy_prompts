Draft the full specification—desiderata, not a defense—of what a theory of moral sainthood, conceived as a limit case of moral development rather than a discrete category, must deliver to count as adequate. The specification should cover: (a) how to preserve the distinction between being maximally responsive to moral reasons and being maximally good for a life, without reducing either to subjective preference; (b) the boundary conditions under which supererogatory acts become obligatory, and whether a ceiling on moral demand is conceptually required or merely psychologically necessary; (c) the remainder problem—whether moral considerations retain normative force even after maximal fulfillment, and if so, how they are weighted against completed obligations; (d) the psychological boundary conditions, specifically whether sustained maximal other-regarding behavior can be compatible with stable autonomous agency or necessarily tends toward erosion, and what mediating structures the theory permits; and (e) how the theory distinguishes genuine moral saints from pathological self-sacrifice and from merely efficient altruists. For each item, provide the motivating conceptual distinction or case, label it negotiable or non-negotiable, and identify which single desideratum does the most damage to standard virtue-ethical, consequentialist, and deontological reconstructions. Keep the weight on the criteria and their interrelations; brief scoring against the three traditions is acceptable but should remain secondary.
