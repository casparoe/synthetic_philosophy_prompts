I've been working through what makes every instance of the letter 'a' count as the same letter, even though printed, handwritten, and wildly stylized versions can look almost nothing alike, and I want a consistency check before I settle on a view.

Here are my commitments:

(1) There is no single visible feature—no loop, no tail, no specific angle—that every 'a' possesses, so recognition cannot be running on a checklist of parts.

(2) Instead, recognition works by something like family resemblance: we absorb thousands of examples and respond to an overall pattern without being able to state an explicit rule that covers every case.

(3) But I also think there is a deeper structural backbone—an invariant connectivity graph in which one enclosed region is linked to a stem in a specific topological way. This backbone is not itself one visible feature; it is a relational structure among strokes. I think it explains why the family-resemblance learning succeeds at all.

(4) Artistic distortions that preserve this backbone—say, an 'a' formed by scattered dots, or bent into a spiral—are still clearly 'a's, because the backbone remains intact.

(5) If you break the backbone—detaching the enclosed region from the stem so they float free—the result stops being an 'a', even when it looks visually almost identical to a normal one.

(6) At the same time, rapid handwriting constantly breaks the backbone: loops stay open, stems merge into loops, connections disappear—yet readers identify them as 'a's instantly and without confusion.

(7) Style—whether Times New Roman, cursive, or decorative—is pure noise layered over the backbone; it never changes the letter's identity, only the effort required to read it.

(8) I think an AI should learn letters the same flexible way humans do: by absorbing examples, not by being programmed with the backbone rule. In fact, I suspect explicit backbone programming would make recognition brittle rather than robust.

(9) There is also this specific case: in a child's drawing, three separate marks are arranged like an 'a' but have no actual stroke connections at all; adults still call it an 'a'. By principle (5), since the backbone is broken, it should not count.
