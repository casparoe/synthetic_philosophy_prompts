I've been teahing Aumann's agreement result and I keep running into students who treat the common prior assumption like a normative law: if two rational experts persistantly disagree about climate sensitivity or drug efficacy, at least one is being irrational or hiding privite information. I want to defend this, but I also want to know where it's actually exposed. Play it straight: what would have to be true for that normative claim to be wrong? Don't just give me 'priors differ'—I want the serious formal escapes, like epsilon-approximate common priors, specific bounded-rationality error structures, or disagreements over whether Bayesian updating applies at all. For each: does it kill the claim, bruise it, or can a defender absorb it by qualifing? What does this view predict that its rivals don't, and is there any observation or result that would be genuinely fatal? Also, be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a normative constraint of this kind?
