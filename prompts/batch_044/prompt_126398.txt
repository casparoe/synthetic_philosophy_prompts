I am a doctoral student in the history and philosophy of science, and next month I am facilitating a seminar session for advanced sociology graduate students on the conceptual architecture of disciplinary power. The participants have encountered Foucault’s analysis of the prison and the panopticon in prior coursework, but they consistently reduce the argument to a generic critique of surveillance, missing the specifically productive relation between power and knowledge—that disciplinary mechanisms do not merely suppress behavior but generate categories of normality, deviance, and the self. I want to design the session’s explanatory arc rather than deliver a lecture, and I need guidance on the dependency structure: what prior distinction—perhaps between juridical and disciplinary power, or between repression and production—must be firmly in place before the productive claim can be presented without sounding like a contradiction in terms?

For the carrying example, I am torn between the clinical examination and the prison timetable; I need to know which one actually sustains the dependency chain and why the panopticon, despite its familiarity, might mislead at this stage. I also anticipate specific misconceptions: that productive power means all knowledge is merely ideological, that Foucault is advocating the abolition of norms, and a conflation with Althusser’s interpellation—I need to know exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Finally, I want a concluding diagnostic question that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the power-knowledge nexus from mere affective agreement that surveillance feels bad, and I should explicitly state which elements—perhaps the genealogical method or the specific archival history of the prison—I will bracket so that the session remains focused.
