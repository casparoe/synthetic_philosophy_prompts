Since retiring from the biology lab I've been reading philosophy, mostly Nozick's stuff on animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia. I'm trying to work out his actual rule from the cases he discusses. From what I can tell he says factory-farming pigs for cheap bacon is wrong (too much suffering for trivial gain); he allows rat experiments that save human lives if suffering is minimized; he rejects keeping a smart octopus in a bare tank for entertainment; and he thinks pasture-raised beef is okay if the cow lived decently and died quick. So far so good.

But now I'm stuck on a synthetic case that didn't exist when he wrote: a "painless pig" engineered to have no nociceptors but normal social cognition, raised in dense indoor pens, harvested young for organs that save children. No physical pain, but also no autonomy or natural life.

If I want to stay consistent with Nozick's four rulings above, should this be permitted? Which of his earlier cases is the closest analog—the medical rats or the pasture beef? And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what's the principle I'd be adopting by allowing it versus banning it?

Also, I wonder if their's a different rule that fits all four verdicts just as well—maybe it's not about suffering versus benefit but about whether an animal can have a long-term life-plan that gets frustrated. If that's the real rule, then the painless pig might still be wrong because its social plans are blocked. Which of the four original cases best distinguishes that interpretation from the utilitarian one?
