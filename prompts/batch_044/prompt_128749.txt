Here's an argument I cooked up for a study session and now it feels like it proves way too much about cooperation. I think there's a hidden slide somewhere between evolutionary explanations and what an individual should do, maybe a quantifier shift, but I can't name it.

(1) In evolutionary game theory, cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma only survives if there's repetition, punishment, or reputation tracking—some shadow of the future that eventually makes defection costly.

(2) So if we observe cooperation in a population, we can always trace it back to an underlying interaction structure that isn't genuinely one-shot.

(3) Now, what's rational for a specific agent in a specific case ought to line up with what those evolutionary dynamics stabilize; otherwise people are systematically acting in ways that should have been selected out.

(4) Therefore, in any particular interaction, cooperating is rational only if there's actually a hidden repetition or reputation mechanism operating behind the scenes.

(5) But in genuinely anonymous, one-off exchanges—like leaving a tip in a diner you'll never recieve another chance at, or returning lost cash with no witnesses—there's no such mechanism present.

(6) So in those cases defection is rational and cooperation is irrational, unless we deny that such interactions ever really occur.

(7) Most economists and philosophers think large-scale human cooperation depends precisely on tons of these one-off anonymous exchanges being common.

(8) So to save the rationality of ordinary cooperation, we have to claim there's never truly a one-off interaction—there's always indirect reciprocity, an internalized norm, or some other shadow createing a hidden cost.

(9) But then defection is never rational, not even in clear one-time cases where it should clearly be okay—like refusing to cooperate with a captor in a single hidden interogation with perfect secrecy and no future contact.

(10) So either everyday anonymous cooperation is systematically irrational, or defection is always wrong. Both conclusions suck.

I'd like to keep (1) and (6) if possible, and I have a hunch the damage is in (3) or (4). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up actually cost? Does it break the link between evolutionary theory and individual rationality, or is there a scope problem I'm missing?

Please separate what's pretty settled in evolutionary game theory from what's genuinely contested about how it connects to individual rational choice—don't present one side as the consensus.
