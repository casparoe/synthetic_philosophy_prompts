hey so i've been thinking about world-history syllabi that call themselves 'polycentric' or 'de-centered' but still run every region through the same evaluative grid -- state capacity, market integration, bureacratic rationalization, all metrics that come straight out of european state-formation experience. the syllabus says multiple perspectives but the assignment asks students to compare how china, the inca, and benin all responded to modernity in one linear argumentative essay with chicago footnots.

what philosophical commitments about history, comparability, and time does that institutional form actually presuppoze? does it need a universal linear chronology where every society is on the same timeline just at different positions, or is it compatible with something like layered or cyclical temporal ontolgies where the past isn't a separate dimension but co-present? reconstruct the implicit theory of what makes an event historically comparable across cultures. is the practice strictly tied to that view, or could you run the same syllabus with incommensuarble frameworks and just change the grading rubric?

and here's the weird injection: suppose we took seriously that for some traditions ancestors are not past figures but active contemporaneous agents in ritual space. then asking students to write about how the inca responded to modernity already frames the inca past as dead and distant in a way that might be ontologically false to the tradition itself. does the syllabus presuppose a specific metaphysics of presence and absence, or can it accommodate that without collapsing into pure relativism?

say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- maybe several theories of history license it -- and tell me what an actual de-centered course would have to change: the sources, the essay format, the very idea of a survey, maybe the footnote system? don't just say add more voices; be concrete about the institutional machinery.
