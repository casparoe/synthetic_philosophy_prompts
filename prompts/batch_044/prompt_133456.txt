Imagine you are advising a philosopher-led working group that has three years and no empirical laboratory to construct a defensible, non-individualist framework of moral and legal standing, drawing on Ubuntu's relational ontology but intended for institutional contexts in which communal recognition is fragmented, technologically mediated, or altogether absent — for instance, in adjudicating the status of synthetic agents sustained by distributed maintenance networks, or of ecological collectives that persist across generations without unified consciousness. I am not asking for a survey of Ubuntu scholarship, nor for citations or a reading list; I want a defended research agenda.

Identify the four open conceptual questions or lines of philosophical work that matter most for making such a framework actually operational rather than merely evocative. Rank them according to what should be tackled first, and for each explain:

- what makes it conceptually upstream — which other questions become tractable or moot once it is resolved;
- whether it can be advanced by conceptual analysis and argument alone, or whether it stalls without empirical or ethnographic input that philosophers cannot supply;
- what specific conceptual development — not a named theory or citation — would cause you to elevate or demote it in the ranking;
- and, crucially, which questions bear directly on whether an institution could justifiably extend or withhold standing from an entity that lacks psychological continuity but participates in sustained relational structures, and which are largely matters of metaphysical tidiness with little institutional consequence.

Your answer should treat Ubuntu not as a cultural slogan but as a specific ontological claim about the constitution of persons through interdependent recognition, and it should address the tension between that claim and liberal-legal frameworks that presuppose bounded, pre-social rights-bearers. Avoid named scholars, bibliographies, and references to specific texts.
