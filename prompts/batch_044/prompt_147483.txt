I have been thinking about the story of Gyges and his ring from Plato's Republic, and I keep returning to what seems like an unstated premise in Glaucon's challenge. Most people treat it as a psychological question: if you could become invisible and escape punishment, would you still act justly? That framing makes justice sound like a habit maintained by fear. But I think Glaucon is driving at something more structural — what I have started calling a visibility condition on moral obligation. The idea is not merely that justice is rewarded when observed, but that a moral norm actually binds an agent only within a framework where the action is, in principle, observable and sanctionable. Complete invisibility — not just physical concealment, but an epistemic opacity such that no observer could ever reconstruct the choice — would not merely tempt injustice; it would place the act outside the domain where moral predicates apply at all.

If that is correct, then modern conditions of anonymity — encrypted transactions, unrecorded choices, spaces with no audit trail — do not just make immoral behavior easier; they dissolve the moral framework in those spaces and replace it with pure strategic calculation. This leads me to ask whether moral realism can survive without collapsing into what I think of as observational ethics: the view that an obligation exists only where an observation relation is possible. Can we defend the claim that moral duties are genuinely real even when no possible observer exists, without relying on the circular-sounding assertion that justice is valuable independently of its rewards? Or does Glaucon's ring actually show that justice was never a real property of actions, but only a social technology that requires visibility to function?
