There's a weird parallel between mental-health law and constitutional design that I think hides a disagreement about what makes someone "the same person" over time, but I can't tell if it's a real philosophical conflict or just two different practical fixes.

Take psychiatric advance directives — basically Ulysses contracts for psych care. In a lot of places, if you're competent now you can sign a paper saying "when I go manic and refuse meds, treat me anyway," and clinicians will honor that even if you're screaming no in the moment. The legal reasoning usually appeals to something like that's not really you, that's the illness speaking. So the practice treats your future refusal as lacking authority because it doesn't come from your authetic self.

Now compare that to constitutional precommitment — entrenchment clauses, supermajority rules, things that make it hard for a future democratic majority to just tear up the old rules. Here the law never says that's not really the people, that's democracy-sickness. Instead it treats the future majority as fully real and authoritative, just procedurally constrained. Their refusal to be bound isn't dismissed as inauthentic; it's just that they haven't followed the amendment channel.

What view of diachronic personal identity and autonomy does this pairing actually presupposses? Does the psychiatric practice strictly need a psychological-continuity theory — that the psychotic future self literally isn't the same person — or could it work with a thinner account, maybe just an epistemic claim about who knows your values best? And does constitutional law need a corporate-identity theory where "the people" persists regardless of mental states, or can it get by without any metaphysics of collective identity?

Also, are the two practices even consistent with each other? If we really think a future refusal can be inauthentic in the clinic, shouldn't we be able to say a future generation's urge to repeal the constitution is also inauthentic — maybe collective delusion? Or going the other way: if we take future democratic will seriously in politics, why don't we take the future patient's refusal seriously?

I'm wondering what would have to change instutional if we replaced whatever view is doing the work with a rival one. If we dropped the identity-based story and went with pure present-autonomy — every refusal is authoritative — do Ulysses contracts collapse? And if we imported the psychiatric model into constitutional law, treating some future democratic movements as "not the real people," what would that do to amendment rules?

Also, is this pairing philosophically underdetermined? Could both practices be explained by tractability or risk-management concerns rather than by any theory of the self, so that the identity talk is just post-hoc rationalization?
