My friend and I have been arguing for months about G. E. Moore’s pairing of friendship and aesthetic appreciation as the two highest intrinsic goods. We both accept his non-naturalist claim that goodness is a simple, unanalyzable property of mental states, and we both grant that in Principia Ethica he treats personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects as parallel cases of the ideal. Where we stall is over whether friendship, for Moore, is genuinely relational: my friend insists that if friendship’s value depends on the other person’s mental states being appropriately directed back at me, then Moore has smuggled in a dyadic, relational good that cannot be reduced to two monadic states of consciousness, undermining his atomistic ontology of value. I counter that Moore can treat friendship as an organic whole in which each party’s mental state is modified by the relation without that relation being itself a bearer of value—similar to how he treats organic unities elsewhere—so the non-natural property still attaches only to individual mental states, now structurally complex. We have not moved because we agree on every textual point about Moore’s examples and his rejection of naturalism, yet we disagree about whether his framework can consistently accommodate what I have started calling value-constitutive reciprocity without collapsing into the kind of pluralism he rejects. Is our disagreement verbal—are we using relational in different senses—or substantive, about whether non-natural properties can have relational conditions of instantiation? Or is there a hidden formal question about how organic unities apply to intersubjective states? Given your diagnosis, what distinction or conceptual test would actually move us, and should we expect progress?
