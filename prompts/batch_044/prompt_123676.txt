I've been reading Plato's Republic on my own and got stuck on Glaucon's story of the ring of Gyges—the idea that anyone made perfectly invisible would act unjustly because morality is only reputation management. I've turned this into a personal thesis: that modern "algorithmic invisibility" (acting inside systems that log everything but show nothing back to a human witness—anonymous forums, opaque recommendation feeds, automated moderation) produces exactly the moral collapse Glaucon predicted, which suggests moral motivation was never internal but always a response to being seen.

Next Thursday I'm presenting this at a local philosophy salon of about fifteen people: half are psychology grad students who study moral cognition and assume some innate fairness or empathic drive; half are religious ethicists who treat conscience as real and not reducible to social pressure; and two software engineers who build recommendation systems and have seen anonymous online behavior up close. They don't do professional metaethics, but they care about whether morality can survive modern anonymity.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. For each: rate how damaging it is, give me a two-minute reply I can deliver without citing papers, note exactly where that reply is soft, and flag whether I should concede something up front. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks—probably whether I'm just describing corruption rather than explaining morality. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" that states the single weakest point in my position I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
