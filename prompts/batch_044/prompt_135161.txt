Grade the following exam response as if you were the instructor for an upper-level philosophy of logic seminar. The exam prompt was: "In Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, the Tortoise refuses to accept the conclusion of a syllogism unless an explicit premise licensing that inference is added, generating an apparent infinite regress. Explain the logical structure of this regress, evaluate whether it genuinely undermines the justification of deduction, and discuss whether Carroll's puzzle should be read primarily as a problem about inference rules themselves or about the psychological processes by which agents apply them."

Student answer (approximately 400 words):

The dialogue works like this: Achilles presents premises (1) if A and B then Z, and (2) A and B, expecting the Tortoise to accept Z. The Tortoise accepts both premises but demands a third premise stating that from (1) and (2) together Z follows. Once that is added, he can demand a fourth premise licensing the application of that rule, and so on forever. So the regress is structural: any attempt to justify an inference by adding a rule as a premise just creates a new inference that also needs justification. This means deduction can never be fully justified without circularity, which is why Carroll anticipates the modern conventionalist view that logical rules are arbitrary conventions rather than necessary truths.

However, the regress is benign in practice because we eventually stop asking for further justification once we reach psychological satisfaction, not logical completion. This shows that Carroll's real target is the psychologistic fallacy—the mistake of treating logical laws as descriptions of mental processes. Once we see that logic belongs to a meta-level and inference rules are not premises in the object-language, the problem dissolves. In fact, the distinction between object-language and meta-language solves Carroll's puzzle exactly: rules operate at a different level, so they never need to be added as premises.

Still, Carroll's point remains philosophically important because it parallels the epistemological problem of basic beliefs: just as foundationalists say we must eventually accept some beliefs without further proof, logicians must eventually accept some inference rules without derivation. Therefore, the Tortoise's challenge is best understood as showing that all reasoning eventually rests on ungrounded psychological acceptance, which confirms that Carroll was arguing against psychologism rather than for it.
