I'm drafting an essay for a political philosophy course on whether national governments should treat international happiness rankings as direct policy targets. In a debate with friends about whether a state ought to aim for the top of reports like the World Happiness Report, one person dismissed the entire framing by saying, "Well, money can't buy happiness," leaving me unsure which precise thesis was being invoked.

Could you take that proverb and dissect the distinct claims it could be expressing—separating the trivial observation that wealth does not guarantee contentment from the substantive empirical claim about diminishing marginal returns to subjective well-being, from the normative claim that GDP growth should not be pursued as a proxy for national flourishing, and from the false universal claim that material conditions never influence happiness? I want you to evaluate which of these readings actually undermines the legitimacy of cross-national happiness metrics, given that such reports combine economic indicators with measures of social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, and perceptions of corruption, and whether attempting to top the rankings commits a category error by treating multidimensional well-being as a competitively ordered public good. Please also distinguish what is relatively settled in the well-being literature—such as the existence of the Easterlin paradox and its later qualifications—from what remains genuinely contested, including debates about cultural bias in subjective well-being surveys and the normative status of comparative national happiness scores, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
