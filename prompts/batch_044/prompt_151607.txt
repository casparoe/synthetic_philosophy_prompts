You are grading a final examination in philosophy of law. The prompt asks students to apply the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism to a novel statutory proposal.

Exam question:
Lord Devlin defends legal moralism on the ground that a shared morality forms part of the seams holding society together; without it, society risks disintegration, just as political subversion threatens the state. H.L.A. Hart replies that this disintegration thesis lacks empirical support and that criminal law should be confined to preventing harm to others, not to enforcing the positive morality of the majority. Consider a proposed statute—the Civic Integrity Act—which criminalizes the deliberate public desecration of national memorials not because of property damage, but because the act is said to symbolically reject shared democratic values and thereby erode institutional trust. Would Hart's framework necessarily block this statute? In your answer, distinguish (i) direct harm to individuals, (ii) institutional harm supported by demonstrable causal mechanisms, and (iii) offense to majority sentiment, and explain whether Devlin's argument tends to conflate these categories.

Student answer:
Hart would necessarily reject the Civic Integrity Act because his harm principle, adopted directly from Mill, allows state interference only to prevent direct physical or psychological injury to specific others. Devlin treats the erosion of shared democratic values as an institutional harm analogous to treason, but Hart denies that any form of institutional harm can justify criminalization unless it can be translated into direct individual injury. Since desecrating a memorial causes no physical harm to anyone, Hart must permit it. Furthermore, Hart's distinction between critical morality and positive morality shows that he believes law should never embody popular moral sentiment, even when that sentiment protects democratic institutions. Devlin, by contrast, collapses the three categories by treating offense to shared values as itself a form of disintegration. Therefore the statute enforces positive morality without preventing demonstrable harm, and is impermissible on Hart's view.

Do not indicate whether you agree with the answer's conclusion; assess only its fidelity to the two thinkers, its logical structure, and its handling of the three categories.
