I'm preparing materials for an advanced seminar on democratic theory, and I've become stuck on a variant of the boundary problem that standard treatments tend to sidestep rather than resolve directly. The core issue is this: democratic theory asks who belongs to the demos—the people entitled to participate in collective self-rule—yet any answer to that question must itself be decided by some prior demos, which means the boundary-setter is always already bounded. I think of this as the recursive legitimation problem, because it suggests that democratic boundaries are never established from a neutral Archimedean point but always depend on a prior, itself-contested membership.

Consider the concrete case of a proposed transboundary climate-adaptation authority with binding jurisdiction over a river basin spanning three sovereign states and several indigenous territories governed by non-state customary law. The authority's decisions will profoundly affect downstream communities in a fourth state that holds no seat at the table, as well as generations not yet born. Within the basin itself, some resident groups reject the authority's legitimacy altogether, insisting that only their traditional councils possess the right to decide on water allocation and land use. Meanwhile, the three familiar criteria for democratic membership—affected interests, subjection to coercive authority, and participation in a shared political culture—each point in different directions, and none of them explains how to halt the regress without an ultimately arbitrary stopping point.

Write an essay that weighs these three criteria as they apply to this case. Your essay should reach and defend a specific conclusion about who ought to belong to the demos governing this authority: for instance, by accepting a pragmatically justified but non-foundational stopping point and specifying which one; by adopting a procedural or agonistic model in which boundaries are continuously renegotiated rather than fixed; or by arguing that the recursive problem dissolves once we treat democratic membership as constituted through ongoing political practice rather than discovered in advance. Justify your conclusion, and be explicit about what it costs—namely, which intuitively relevant groups are included or excluded, and what this implies for the legitimacy of decisions made by a demos whose own boundaries cannot be fully grounded from the outside.
