Draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after an adversarial collaboration on George Berkeley’s master argument for idealism. Name the camps: the Transparency Defenders, who hold that the argument succeeds because every conception necessarily includes the conceiving act in its representational content, and therefore no genuinely mind-independent object can be conceived; and the Modal-Separation Critics, who hold that the argument fails because it collapses the conditions of conceiving (the necessary presence of a conceiver) with the conditions of the object conceived, and who grant that all conception requires a mind but deny that the object is thereby constituted as mental.

Assume both camps accept the standard structure of the argument—that to conceive of a sensible object existing unperceived is to conceive of it as being conceived, hence as perceived—and that Berkeley intends this to show that sensible objects exist only in being perceived. They should also agree on the formal distinction between conceiving an act of conception and conceiving the absence of that act, and they should agree that if the argument is sound then idealism follows. Require them to frame the central dispute in terms of representational closure: the thesis that the representational content of a conception cannot include properties that are inaccessible to the representational act itself, with both sides accepting that this framing captures what is genuinely at stake even though they interpret closure differently.

The deliverable must contain, first, a numbered section of agreed claims—including (a) the formal inference structure as both sides understand it; (b) the shared act/object distinction; (c) the conditional that soundness entails idealism; and (d) the representational-closure framing together with each side’s agreed interpretation of it. Second, include a disagreements table with at least three rows, each giving the precise formulation of divergence in wording that the respective camp accepts for its own view. The rows must cover: (i) whether representational closure is a metaphysical constraint on objects or only an epistemic constraint on conceivers; (ii) whether Berkeley’s move from “I cannot conceive X without conceiving my conception of X” to “X cannot exist without being conceived” commits a modal fallacy; and (iii) whether the analogy between conceiving an unperceived tree and conceiving a color that is never visually experienced supports or undermines the argument.

Then identify two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. The first crux concerns whether there is a coherent thin conception of an object that specifies its existence conditions independently of any thick representational context—for instance, whether one can conceive of a geometric solid persisting in absolute darkness without embedding that conception in a perceptual narrative. The second crux concerns whether phenomenal residue—the non-intentional qualitative character present in every act of conception—functions as a vehicle of information about the object or merely as an index of the act itself; specifically, whether the presence of this residue in every conception of an object proves that the object is mental, or only proves that the conceiving is mental.

For each crux, provide: (a) a concrete case or thought experiment that both camps agree is relevant and well-described, with no disputed empirical premises; (b) each camp’s explicit prediction about what the case shows; and (c) the precise reasoning each camp offers, phrased in terms both would recognize.

Conclude with a brief joint assessment of whether any part of the dispute is verbal—specifically, whether disagreement over the phrase mind-independent existence reflects a substantive metaphysical split or a divergence in how each camp deploys the concept of independence in representational contexts.
