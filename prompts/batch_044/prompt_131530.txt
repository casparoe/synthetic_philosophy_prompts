The dispute over whether Moral Foundations Theory reveals anything normative about left-right differences has persisted for well over a decade without apparent convergence. Proponents observe that liberals and conservatives exhibit reliably different weightings across the five foundations—care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity—and they invoke cross-cultural replication and moral-dumbfounding experiments to argue that these patterns reflect deep, evolved cognitive structures rather than products of rational deliberation. Critics accept the descriptive data but insist that identifying an evolutionary origin or a dumbfounding effect tells us nothing about justification: a widespread intuition can be systematically distorted, and descriptive clustering across populations does not entail normative pluralism. Both camps treat substantially the same empirical literature as decisive for opposite conclusions, and neither has modified its core stance in response to more recent meta-analyses or cross-national extensions. Is this deadlock primarily a verbal disagreement about whether "foundation" denotes a causal mechanism or a normative reason? Or do the sides differ about the standards of evidence required to move from psychological description to ethical conclusion—specifically, about whether evolutionary ubiquity or cultural stability can ever serve as a proxy for moral authority? Alternatively, are they answering two different questions—one about the architecture of moral cognition, the other about the epistemology of moral belief—while using the phrase "what the psychology tells us" to cover both? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates causal explanation from normative justification, a new empirical result about the malleability or context-dependence of foundation weights, or an argument that the framing question itself is ill-posed?
