I have spent the evening arguing with a close friend about the philosophical problem of verisimilitude, and I have built an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems absurd. Here it is, set out in steps. (1) A representational artefact succeeds to the degree that it exhibits verisimilitude — structural and qualitative resemblance to its target, including what we might call counterfactual fidelity: the preservation of how the target would behave under altered conditions. (2) If an artefact R and its target T share every property that is representationally relevant — including modal and counterfactual properties — then R does not stand in an asymmetrical relation of depiction or reference to T; rather, R either is T or is a perfect duplicate of T, and perfect duplication eliminates the distinction required for representation. (3) Consequently, as R approaches perfect verisimilitude, it approaches the point at which it ceases to represent T at all. (4) A representation that has achieved perfect verisimilitude is therefore no longer a representation of T; it is merely another instance of T. (5) From this it follows that the optimal representation is not the perfect one but the one that comes infinitesimally close to perfection without reaching it — in other words, the representation that is maximally capable of deceiving an observer about its ontological status while technically remaining truthful about its own representational nature. (6) But this makes representational excellence depend on a kind of calculated near-deception, which conflicts with our practice of praising scientific models, portraits, and testimony precisely for being transparent about their own representational status and recognisably distinct from reality. (7) Therefore, either verisimilitude — including counterfactual fidelity — is not the correct measure of representational success, or the ideal representation is an idealised fraud. I am inclined to keep (1) and (6) if I can, but I need to know exactly which step collapses. Is the error an equivocation between property-sharing and ontological identity? Does representation require an intentional or causal link that survives perfect resemblance? Or is the slide in step (5) the real problem? Please respond in American English, and explain clearly what rejecting the faulty step costs for any theory that takes resemblance seriously.
