At a philosophy workshop, two participants—Maya and Leo—debated a hypothetical they jointly designed: an infinite linear sequence of isolated rooms, each holding one person, arranged so that the occupant of room n can press a button that saves the occupant of room n+1 from serious harm at the cost of minor discomfort to themselves. They agree that in a ten-room version every occupant should press. In the infinite case, Maya argues that since the pattern is uniform—each local act produces one serious rescue and one minor harm—there is no deontological asymmetry that blocks aggregation, and the infinite sum of rescues clearly outweighs the infinite sum of discomforts, so every occupant is obligated to press. Leo responds that deontology refuses to sum across persons, and that because the chain has no final room there is never a "completed" rescue that grounds the first act; he claims this creates an infinite regress of reasons, dissolving rather than strengthening the obligation. Maya counters that Leo illicitly assumes obligations need a terminus: the successor relation is well-defined at every finite index, so each local duty stands on its own without requiring a last link. Leo then challenges her consistency: if local duties are sufficient, why does it feel wrong for a single remote agent to press every button simultaneously, saving all at the cost of only one minor discomfort? They turned to the group for adjudication. Please go through their exchange step by step: who is right about whether the lack of a terminus undermines obligation, whether uniform local duties survive infinite extension, and whether the remote-trigger variant genuinely breaks Maya's view? Also say what principle both are neglecting, and who is more right overall. End with Verdict: [your assessment].
