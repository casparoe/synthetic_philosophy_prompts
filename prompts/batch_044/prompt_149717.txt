I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on philosophy of art and race with about 14 students. Design a structured in-person role-play or deliberation game that makes participants feel the force of W. E. B. Du Bois's claim that all art functions as propaganda, that aesthetic neutrality is an impossible luxury, and that artists from marginalized communities carry a specific moral duty to direct their work toward justice rather than autonomy. Do not reveal Du Bois's name or label the concept until the debrief. Create assigned roles—artists with hidden obligations to a marginalized audience, patrons with hidden incentives to reward either commercial appeal or political commitment, and critics who score works on aesthetic purity versus social impact—together with a token economy representing cultural legitimacy and communal survival. The rules should make attempts at neutrality systematically costly: for example, artists must allocate limited production tokens across works, patrons secretly fund only works that serve their hidden agendas, and critics award legitimacy points that determine survival. Include a clear timeline, interaction rules that require negotiation, and a payoff scheme using only paper tokens. The debrief must map each phase onto Du Bois's reasoning—why neutrality collapses, why the duty is binding rather than optional, and what makes the burden particular to marginalized creators. Predict what will go wrong: participants will likely conflate propaganda with deception rather than committed advocacy, and some will view the token system as unfairly rigged; say exactly how to correct these reactions. Be frank about which element of Du Bois's view—his insistence that beauty and truth are subordinate to the propaganda of justice—the activity's mechanics cannot reproduce, and specify the exact statement or framing you will supply in the debrief to cover that gap. The design must be fully runnable without web access for participants.
