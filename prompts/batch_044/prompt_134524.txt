I run an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar (about 12 students, 75 minutes, no prerequisites beyond an introductory course). Design an in-class activity—using only printed colored dots, a stopwatch or basic slide projection, and recording sheets—that makes students feel the conceptual tension between Orwellian and Stalinesque models of revision as applied to the color phi phenomenon and to debates about the temporal structure of consciousness. The activity should proceed in three phases: first, a brief perceptual demonstration in which students observe rapid sequential flashes of differently colored dots and record what they see; second, a structured role-play in which half the group acts as Narrative Editors (who must construct a continuous, coherent experience-report that accounts for motion and intermediate color) and the other half acts as Frame Archivists (who may only report discrete, verifiable inputs and must flag any interpolation); third, a forced reconciliation in which pairs with opposing roles must agree on a single testimony about whether an intermediate color was experienced, inserted, or deleted. Include explicit rules for each role, a scoring or payoff system that rewards internal consistency rather than correspondence to an objective record, a timeline broken into 10- or 15-minute blocks, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the philosophical distinction: where does the brain rewrite the past versus airbrush the record, and what does the failure of reconciliation in the color-phi case suggest about whether conscious content is determinate at the moment of occurrence? Be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce—specifically, whether it can capture the possibility of retroactive determination of experience without explicit facilitator framing. The idea should emerge from the rules and roles, not from a lecture; students should not hear the terms Orwellian or Stalinesque until the debrief, and the prompt should not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific authors.
