The legacy of Article 231 treats a state as the bearer of "war guilt" for 1914, and post-war reparative frameworks have addressed obligations to later generations of citizens and institutions; yet the same legal tradition rejects inherited criminal liability for individuals and, following the Fischer controversy, historians have largely relocated blame from "the nation" to specific decision-makers and structural conditions. What conception of collective agency and intergenerational responsibility does this pairing presuppose? Does it require viewing the state as a corporate moral person whose intentions survive regime change, or can a purely distributive, synchronic account of shared responsibility explain the assignment of reparative duties? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it coheres with the legal refusal to punish descendants for ancestral crimes, and identify what a strictly individualist, non-transmissive framework would have to alter about commemorative and reparative practice.
