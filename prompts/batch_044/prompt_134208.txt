I've been working through Darwin's The Descent of Man (1871), and I'm stuck on a particular argumentative move in the chapters on moral and religious development. The passage I'm wrestling with—paraphrased from the 1871 edition—goes roughly like this: Darwin observes that a dog will often bark at a parasol moved by the wind, apparently unable to determine whether the object is alive or inanimate, and he uses this as an analogy for the origin of religious belief in humans, comparing it to the fetishism he attributes to so-called savages. From this he derives that belief in spiritual agencies is not a rational inference but an evolutionary byproduct of the same social-instinct machinery that produces moral feelings. He then asserts that moral progress in civilized societies consists in the widening of sympathy—from family to tribe to nation and eventually to all sentient beings.

What I need help understanding is the internal consistency of the transition from this descriptive evolutionary story to the normative language of progress. If both religion and morality arise from the same root in evolved social instincts, and if religious belief is essentially a misapplication of causal reasoning analogous to the dog's confusion, then on what grounds can Darwin treat the widening of sympathy as genuinely progressive rather than just another contingent evolutionary trajectory—perhaps no more justified than the dog's false belief? Does he need a hidden teleological premise—say, that greater scope of sympathy represents a more developed form of the instinct—to avoid collapsing the normative claim into pure description? And how does his private agnosticism about divine design interact with this: does the absence of a transcendent standard make the evaluation of moral change as improvement impossible, or does Darwin think that evolutionary dynamics themselves supply an immanent standard?

I'm not asking for a reading list or for citations to the secondary literature—this is a conceptual crux. I want to understand whether Darwin's account contains an implicit evolutionary debunking argument that applies to morality as well as to religion, and if so, whether there's a rationalist reconstruction of his view that can preserve normative force without smuggling in purpose.
