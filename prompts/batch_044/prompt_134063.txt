I'm working on a philosophy project about political reason and religious speech, and I'd like you to write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally sit together in the last two lines—on the tension in Rawlsian public reason between secular demands for translation and religious citizens who refuse to split their identity. Rather than the standard classroom debate, I want the poem to take up the more specific claim that the translation requirement is not politically neutral: it asks religious people to bifurcate their comprehensive self from their public voice, and that this split itself rests on a contested view of personhood rather than on plain common sense. Let one refrain carry the secular demand (for example, render your faith in terms we all share) and the other carry the refusal to divide the self (for example, I will not cut my soul in two to speak). The form should do the philosophical work: through the tercets the refrains should circle past each other without resolving, and in the final quatrain they should appear side by side without synthesis, showing that the two positions keep passing each other because they begin from incompatible pictures of what a person is. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Rawls's proviso and the critique that public reason smuggles in a metaphysics of the split subject would recognize the idea, but it should work as poetry first. After the poem, please include a brief note explaining where it takes liberties with Rawls's actual view or relies on a contested interpretation of that split-subject critique, and tell me what argument or consideration would lead you to change that interpretation.
