I defend a strict internalist thesis about practical reasons: for any consideration R to genuinely be a reason for agent A to φ, R must be recoverable—without primitive normative injection—from some element of A's actual subjective motivational set S via a chain of sound practical inferences that A could in principle come to endorse. Moral demands that lack this derivational path are not reasons that A possesses, but at most agent-neutral facts that carry no direct practical weight for A; I take common-sense morality to survive because commitments to coherence, non-arbitrariness, and minimal cooperation are already widely distributed in S, letting most moral requirements be reconstructed as anchored hypothetical imperatives. What would genuinely count against this position—not merely seem awkward, but be decisive? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings or conceptual cases undermine the recovery claim; whether there are intuitions about agents having reasons they could never derive from their S that defenders must concede rather than redescribe; and whether the view risks unfalsifiability if every counterexample is dissolved by attributing a hidden element to S. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, specify which ones a committed internalist should accept as damaging and which they should reject as misdescriptions, and take a definite stance on whether this insulating capacity is a defensible feature or an explanatory vice that robs the theory of substance. Do not survey options neutrally; argue where the view lives and dies.
