I have recently come into a position of modest financial security—enough that I no longer need to maximize income at the expense of everything else. This has made Keynes's essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren personally urgent, not as a macroeconomic forecast but as an ethical question about what surplus is actually for. I am drafting notes for a short essay arguing the following.

Keynes distinguishes absolute needs, which are satiable, from relative needs, which are insatiable because they depend on feeling superior to others. He predicts that within about a hundred years the economic problem of scarcity will be solved, leaving humanity with the real problem: how to use freedom from pressing economic cares to live wisely and agreeably. Most readers treat this as a failed prediction about a fifteen-hour work week. I think the more interesting and more neglected part is Keynes's diagnosis of the love of money as a somewhat disgusting morbidity—one of the semi-criminal, semi-pathological propensities we hand over to mental specialists. He seems to think capitalism is not merely inefficient at distributing leisure but actively cultivates a comparative, endless accumulation that will persist even after material scarcity ends.

My essay pushes this further by connecting it to Keynes's biography: his departure from the Treasury for Bloomsbury, his conviction that pursuing money for its own sake is a moral problem, and his suggestion that once needs are met we should value ends over means. I want to argue that Keynes offers not a timetable but a moral psychology of abundance—the danger is not that we work too long, but that we become unable to stop competing when competition serves no purpose.

Please comment on these notes, especially on accuracy: is Keynes framing the love of money as a kind of pathology rather than a rational preference, and do other writings complicate that reading? I also welcome thoughts on whether this interpretation is genuinely distinctive or collapses into a familiar critique of consumerism. In your response, please separate what is well-established in Keynes scholarship from what is genuinely contested among knowledgeable readers, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus.
