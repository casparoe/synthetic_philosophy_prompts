The long-running dispute about whether determinism undermines moral agency — and about which varieties of free will are genuinely worth wanting — has become curiously static. Compatibilists maintain that freedom is not a matter of escaping causal laws but of possessing a particular internal architecture: actions arise from an agent’s own deliberative mechanisms, remain responsive to reasons, and are not the product of external coercion or manipulation. Libertarians respond that this captures at best a diminished form of control; for full moral responsibility, the agent must have been able to do otherwise in a robust, non-conditional sense, and determinism rules that out. A third, more sceptical voice adds that empirical research on the neural precursors of conscious choice undermines the causal role that both camps assign to conscious deliberation, leaving neither with a secure psychological foundation.

Despite decades of refinement — with compatibilists appealing to hierarchical models of desire and libertarians developing agent-causal or event-causal accounts — the exchange feels circular. Each side has its canonical thought experiments, its standard replies, and its criteria for what counts as an adequate model of agency, yet the literature shows little sign of convergence.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not purely substantive. It may be that the disputants are using the term ‘control’ in incompatible ways: one group treats it as a normative status that justifies practices of praise and blame, the other as a metaphysical property requiring causal openness in the agent’s history. There may also be a hidden pluralism: some participants are asking which concept of agency best supports our social institutions, others are asking whether human cognition includes a specific kind of causal indeterminacy, and a smaller number are asking which psychological capacities make a life genuinely one’s own. These are different questions, yet they are debated under a single heading.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement. To what extent is it verbal, substantive, or a case of distinct questions being pursued under one name? Do the sides share any common evidential standards — for instance, about which conceptual cases should serve as data, or about what empirical findings in cognitive science would be relevant — or do they differ even about what would count as progress? And if your diagnosis is correct, what should happen next: should we introduce a sharper distinction between sourcehood and present alternatives, should we separate the normative institutional question from the metaphysical one, should we await specific empirical results, or should we conclude that the framing is ill-posed? I am especially interested in whether introducing an under-discussed variety of free will — defined not by causal history or by available alternatives, but by an agent’s diachronic capacity to revise the evaluative framework through which they choose — would clarify the disagreement or simply add another layer to the loop.
