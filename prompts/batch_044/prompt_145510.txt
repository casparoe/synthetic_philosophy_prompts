hey — I'm writing a feature on whether countries can be guilty long after the fact, and I need to sort out two ideas that keep getting blurred together. One is Jaspers' framework from his post-war analysis: he slices guilt into criminal, political, moral and metaphysical kinds, and he's adament that metaphysical guilt isn't something a nation can be tried for — it's more like an individual spiritual burden. The other is this newer notion I pulled from an essay I read last month — let's call it institutional inheritence guilt. The idea is that a state can carry a functional debt not because anyone alive is blameworthy, but because its institutions still run on power structures that were built by past wrongs, so restructuring is owed regardless of personal feelings.

My question is whether these are actually different, or if Jaspers' metaphysical guilt just becomes institutional inheritence guilt once you strip away the emotional part. And if they do split, which one gives you a real argument that a nation — not just its government — is guilty fifty years on? I feel like Jaspers gives you accountability without collective blame, while the other gives you collective obligations without anyone being guilty. That tension seems important but I can't quite name it. Can you map the comparison and say where each one breaks down?
