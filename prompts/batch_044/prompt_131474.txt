I'm trying to nail down what heritability actually means by testing my gut on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts, some of wich feel shaky.

1. Twin studies in Sweden put BMI heritability around 0.75, so my first instinct is that genes cause about three-quarters of obesity differences there — but that feels wrong, I just can't say why.

2. Now imagine two populations with the exact same gene pool. One has totally uniform nutrition, the other huge diet varience. Heritability is near 1 in the first, near 0.3 in the second. I think the genetic importance hasn't actually changed, even though the number flipped completely.

3. There's this niche result that Finnish sauna attendance is heritable around 0.6, but I don't think that means there's a sauna gene — it's probably correlating with personality or culture. Still, should I treat that 0.6 differently than BMI's?

4. If we could perfectly equalize every environment, heritability of IQ would hit 1.0. But I don't think that means environment suddenly stopped mattering; it feels like the number tracks variance left over, not causal power.

5. Someone tells me schizophrenia is 80% heritable so therapy is useless. I think that's a total misreading, but I'm not sure exaclty why.

6. And I keep waffling on this: if height heritability is 0.8 in one country, and then I move to a place where everyone is malnourished, does the heritability drop because environment now explains more, or stay the same because genes are still doing the same work?

Is there one principle that makes sense of 2, 4, and 6 together, and explains why 1 and 5 are misunderstandings? What does it predict for case 3 — should heritability of culturally-mediated behaviors be interpreted differently? And apply it to something I haven't mentioned: what if heritability of wearing glasses is high where vision care is free, but low where it's expensive?
