I was discussing with a friend late into the evening whether Keynes’s distinction between risk and uncertainty, especially as developed in his Treatise on Probability and carried into The General Theory, marks a genuine metaphysical claim about the openness of the future or merely an epistemic observation about the limits of human reasoning under partial information. We kept circling the same texts—his discussion of the weight of argument, the example of the prospect of a European war being unquantifiable, and his insistence that long-term expectations are not reducible to mathematical expectation—yet neither of us could locate the precise point of fracture. Is this disagreement about what Keynes meant, about whether the category of uncertainty applies to the world or only to our models of it, or are we actually disputing whether rational economic agents should behave as if the future were probabilizable even when they cannot assign precise probabilities?

The literature seems stalled in the same loop: philosophers of economics tend to treat Keynes as a proto-subjectivist about belief, while macroeconomists tend to treat his uncertainty as a practical constraint on forecasting. But my working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not empirical—everyone cites the same passages—nor purely verbal. Rather, one camp is asking a normative question about the standards of rational belief under ignorance, and the other is asking an ontological question about whether economic time is structurally different from physical time. If that diagnosis is right, then the debate might be two distinct inquiries running under the single heading “Keynesian uncertainty.” But I am unsure whether there is a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about whether imprecise probabilities or second-order beliefs can capture what Keynes called weight—or whether the impasse is bedrock, rooted in incompatible intuitions about whether a decision made without a calculable distribution is still rational in a meaningful sense.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? I want you to engage with the specific positions—those who read Keynes as saying the future is genuinely indeterminate versus those who read him as describing a severe but surmountable epistemic limitation—without giving a survey of interpretations. Tell me whether the conflict is substantive, verbal, or a case of different questions being asked under one name; whether there is a concealed empirical or formal issue (for instance, about the coherence of non-probabilistic decision rules); and, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a change of framing—rather than another round of textual exegesis. Please answer in American English.
