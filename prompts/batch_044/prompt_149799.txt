I’m a retired polymer chemist — thirty years in industrial labs measuring reaction yields — and since I hung up my goggles I’ve been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the local college. Last tuesday our study group was arguing in the library basement (someone brought those cheap cookies that taste like cardboard). Two members, Maya and Jonas, sat in a seperate corner and got stuck on Scanlon’s contractualism and the aggregation problem. Here’s the back-and-forth.

(1) Maya said contractualism is paralyzed in the classic rescue case: we can save five only by actively killing one. The principel allowing that can be rejected by the one, and the principel forbidding it can be rejected by each of the five. Since Scanlon refuses to just sum complaints, there’s no principled tie-breaker, so the view either collapses into paralysis or sneaks in intuition. (2) Jonas replied that Maya misses Scanlon’s distinction between being actively killed and being allowed to die; the five are only suffering a misfortune if we don’t act, whereas the one is actively harmed, so the one’s rejection carries a different kind of weight and the tie breaks naturally without counting heads. (3) Maya came back that this directness distinction falls apart in institutional triage — say, pulling one patient off a respirator to free five machines — where the harm is structurally direct. She also said that if Jonas lets “generic reasons” be weighted by severity, he’s already smuggling in aggregation with a non-linear curve, just not calling it that. (4) Jonas then said Maya treats contractualism like a one-shot calculus, but it’s really about ex ante institutional principels. From behind partial ignorance about whether you’ll be the one or one of the five, everyone might reject a rule permitting lethal triage, not because numbers don’t matter but because the catastrophic risk to being the victim dominates the probabilistic benefit. He added that this doesn’t aggregate; it weights the kind of reason each person has from a generic standpoint.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn’t. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say who’s more right overall, and what — if anything — both are missing about how contractualism handles collective harm in institutional settings. Don’t just hand me a bibliography; I want to know whether Jonas’s ex ante move actually saves Scanlon or whether Maya’s institutional counter is fatal.
