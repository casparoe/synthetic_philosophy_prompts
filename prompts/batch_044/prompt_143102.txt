I have been trying to understand Arrow's impossibility result without relying on formal proofs, and I keep returning to the same conceptual sticking point. Most popular explanations present independence of irrelevant alternatives as an obvious fairness condition—if I prefer A over B, the appearance of C should not reverse that preference. But I am unsure whether this condition is meant to apply to fixed, pre-existing mental states or to judgments that are genuinely constructed in response to the choice set itself.

To make this concrete, imagine a committee ranking three environmental regulations that vary along two dimensions: the speed of carbon reduction and the burden on small businesses. When only regulations A and B are available, a voter might rank A above B because A offers a moderate pace with modest relief for businesses. If a third option C is introduced—one that demands immediate, total decarbonization with zero relief—the voter might now rank B above A, not because she has become irrational, but because C has shifted her internal reference point: A now looks like an inadequate half-measure rather than a balanced compromise. In other words, the relative evaluation of A and B depends on whether C is present to frame what "extreme" means.

So my core question is whether Arrow's theorem treats such context-sensitive shifts as violations of rationality, or whether it assumes from the outset that individual preference orderings must be invariant across menus. Is the impossibility driven mainly by the difficulty of aggregating fixed preferences, or does it also depend on suppressing the kind of framing effects that seem ordinary in multidimensional decision-making? I would like a gut-level explanation of where the real tension lies—between IIA and non-dictatorship—especially if it turns out that allowing constructed preferences changes whether the theorem applies at all.
