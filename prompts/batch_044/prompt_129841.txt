Exam question (Metaphysics 301, final assessment): The special composition question asks under what conditions a plurality of objects composes a further object. In your answer, (a) clearly distinguish unrestricted mereological composition, restricted composition, and nihilism; (b) explain why unrestricted composition might be thought to violate ontological parsimony; and (c) evaluate whether causal interaction between parts is a sufficient condition for composition, with particular attention to the hypothetical case of a diachronic fusion — a single object composed of parts that exist at non-overlapping temporal intervals with no causal link between them.

Student answer:

The special composition question is about when parts make a whole. There are three main answers. Unrestricted composition, or universalism, says that any plurality of parts always composes a further object, so there exists an object made of my mobile phone and the moon. This violates ontological parsimony because it multiplies entities far beyond what is necessary. Restricted composition says that parts only compose a whole when they are arranged in a particular way, such as being causally connected or functioning together as a unit. Nihilism says that there are never any composite objects, only simple parts arranged in various ways; this is essentially restricted composition taken to its extreme limit. Peter van Inwagen argues for restricted composition by appealing to the idea of life — living organisms are special because they possess a unified life-process. However, in the case of diachronic fusion, where parts exist at different times with no causal interaction whatsoever, there is clearly no unified life or causal link, so restricted composition correctly denies that such a fusion exists. Therefore, causal interaction is sufficient for composition, and unrestricted composition ought to be rejected because it is far too extravagant.
