I am preparing the closing section of a long-form magazine feature on how Gödel's incompleteness theorems travel outside mathematics—into recent debates about artificial intelligence alignment and constitutional design—where they are often invoked to claim that any sufficiently complex system must harbor an undecidable proposition, without regard for the premises that actually carry the result. For this, I want to commission a poem in strict sestina form, using the six end-words system, truth, axiom, outside, proof, and shadow in rotation, so that the compulsory returns of the form enact the looping self-reference of a Gödel sentence. Arrange the envoi so that outside is paired with axiom and proof with shadow: the structural point is that the theorems depend on a system being both consistent and recursively axiomatizable, conditions present in formal arithmetic but usually absent in the domains where the analogy is applied, and the poem should make that absence visible rather than conceal it. Do not resolve the tension into either a triumphalist claim that all reasoning is incomplete or a dismissive shrug that Gödel means nothing for other fields; let the form show how the result keeps being pulled back into contexts where its premises fail. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with logic recognizes the distinction between the first and second theorems—particularly that consistency must be assumed, not proved within the system—and should specifically gesture toward the requirement of recursive axiomatizability, a condition almost always dropped in popular translations, without becoming didactic. Write for an educated general reader who has heard the theorems named but does not yet see why they do not license broad conclusions about the limits of human reason, legal deliberation, or machine cognition.
