I am a retired experimental physicist—my career was in thermal spectroscopy—who has recently begun auditing philosophy courses and discussing them with a small group of friends over dinner. In those conversations, the Quinean line that qualia are an incoherent theoretical posit, as developed by thinkers such as Chase and Sanborn, has come up repeatedly: the claim that subjective qualitative states fail to meet basic criteria we require of any respectable scientific or theoretical entity, including structural determinacy, explanatory non-redundancy, and intersubjective calibration. I have come to hold that this dismissal is largely correct, but I want to know precisely what would count against it. Not merely what feels intuitively compelling—since I have learned to distrust raw intuition in this domain—but which observations, formal arguments, or conceptual shifts would genuinely weaken the thesis that qualia are incoherent. For instance, suppose neuroscientists were to identify what one might call a phenomenal calibration curve: a robust, cross-subject deterministic mapping between specific cortical dynamics and fine-grained qualitative distinctions, functioning analogously to how a spectrometer correlates emission lines with molecular structure. Would such a result force the Quinean to concede that qualia meet calibration conditions after all, or could the thesis absorb it by treating the curve as tracking only functional dispositions? I want the potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest assessment of which ones defenders of the incoherence view would themselves concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as question-begging. I also want to know whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every possible discovery can be reinterpreted as not addressing real qualia—and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue of conceptual rigor or a vice that empties the claim of content.
