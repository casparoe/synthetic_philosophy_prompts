After three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have spent my retirement reading decision theory and social philosophy with the patience of someone used to checking derivations twice. I have become convinced of a position that strikes me as formally tight yet almost entirely absent from the literature on collective action. I.J. Good's theorem on the value of information shows, under standard von Neumann-Morgenstern conditions, that an agent able to acquire costless evidence bearing on the outcomes of available actions ought always to acquire it before deciding. I wish to extend this result directly to the tragedy of the commons. The standard narrative, following Hardin, treats overuse of shared resources as the inevitable consequence of individually rational maximization in the absence of coercive institutions. I contend that this framing quietly assumes a static informational environment: agents act on private, unrevised beliefs about resource abundance and about others' extraction behavior. Yet in actual commons scenarios—fisheries, groundwater basins, atmospheric carbon budgets—there is typically abundant, near-costless evidence available regarding stock levels, peer behavior, and the payoffs of cooperation. By Good's result, rational agents should gather this shared information and update collectively before committing to extraction strategies. On my view, the tragedy is therefore misdiagnosed: it is not primarily a failure of institutional constraint but a failure of collective epistemic rationality, in which agents defect informationally as well as behaviorally. Many standard policy prescriptions—taxes, quotas, privatization—thus address the wrong failure mode, treating the symptom of overuse rather than the prior failure to satisfy the conditions of rational belief revision.

That said, I recognize that moving from an individual decision-theoretic result to interdependent collective action involves leaps that may not be licensed. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this position—not superficial reminders about bounded cognition or strategic deception, but the deep defeaters. Which of Good's core assumptions—the independence of outcomes from the information-gathering act, the existence of a well-defined prior over collective states, or the coherence of defining acquisition as genuinely costless—fail in collective settings in ways that are conceptually fatal rather than merely practical? Could there be structures of interdependence in which acquiring information is collectively destructive, for instance by revealing payoff asymmetries that destabilize cooperative equilibria, so that the value of information turns negative once social interaction is fully modeled? Does the view risk being insulated by defining costlessness so broadly that no empirical counterexample can reach it, and if so, is that insulation a philosophical vice rather than a formal virtue? I am also interested in whether results in social epistemology or cooperative game theory—perhaps about the functional value of ambiguity or about the instability of common-knowledge conditions—directly undermine the inference from individual rational updating to collective information-gathering obligations. Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones I should concede even as someone convinced by the formal result, and tell me whether there exists an analog to the familiar aggregation objection: does treating collective belief-revision as a simple sum of individual Good-style updates illegitimately suppress the distinctness of agents' epistemic positions? I want to know exactly where this view stands exposed, and whether its exposure is a strength or a hidden weakness.
