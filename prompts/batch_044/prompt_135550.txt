I've been working through an argument for a seminar essay on historical path dependence, specifically whether institutional history is genuinely "locked in" or whether functional pressures eventually wash out initial conditions. I'm skeptical of the strong lock-in thesis associated with the institutionalist tradition, but I want to make sure I'm not simply strawmanning the position that contingency at critical junctures permanently constrains later development.

My core claim is that path dependence operates reliably at what I'm calling the "design layer"—the specific legislative form, organizational architecture, or procedural rule an institution takes—yet rarely survives intact at the "functional layer," where societies tend to converge on equivalent problem-solving arrangements despite divergent origins. To test this, I'm comparing the development of bankruptcy and insolvency regimes in the German Empire after unification and in the United States following the Panic of 1873. Both jurisdictions eventually arrived at creditor-protection frameworks that prioritized orderly liquidation and market stabilization over debtor imprisonment, yet they reached this point through radically different legislative sequences: Germany through centralized commercial code revision shaped by Prussian administrative tradition, the United States through a patchwork of state-level experiments eventually consolidated into federal bankruptcy legislation in the late 1890s. By the turn of the century, the functional outcomes—stabilized credit markets and predictable insolvency procedures—look remarkably similar, even though the institutional pathways share almost no common intermediate nodes.

Against this convergence story, I want to introduce a concept I'm tentatively calling "contingency layering": the idea that historical events do not lock outcomes in a linear causal chain, but rather accumulate as stacked constraints, such that a later functional crisis can override, reroute, or dissolve an earlier contingent choice if the systemic pressure is severe enough. This would explain why some technologies or standards—railway gauge being the classic case—remain genuinely path-dependent, because no functional crisis demands their alteration, whereas bankruptcy law was repeatedly reopened by financial panics and thus never achieved true lock-in. I'm also wondering whether there's an epistemic analog here: historians construct narratives by selecting successful trajectories and treating failed alternatives as retroactively impossible, which creates an illusion that convergence was inevitable rather than the result of functional replacement wearing down an earlier design choice. I'd like your comments on whether this framework holds together, particularly on whether my bankruptcy comparison is fair or whether I'm flattening important differences in enforcement culture and creditor power between the two cases. I'm also unsure whether "contingency layering" adds conceptual value beyond standard talk of "critical junctures"—it might just be redundant jargon. Finally, does my argument risk the opposite error, assuming functional equivalence where there is actually persistent divergence masked only by superficial institutional similarity?
