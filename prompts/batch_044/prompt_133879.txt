I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist, and since retirement I have been working through a philosophy of science reading group with colleagues from the humanities. I have formed a specific package of views about how scientific thought experiments can yield knowledge, and before I present them I want a rigorous consistency audit rather than a general lecture. Here is exactly where I stand.

(1) Thought experiments in science—Galileo’s falling bodies, Einstein’s elevator, Schrödinger’s cat—can produce substantive knowledge about nature, not merely pedagogical clarity or conceptual tidying. (2) I remain committed to a broadly empiricist epistemology: any claim about matters of physical fact must ultimately be anchored in observation or experiment; pure armchair reasoning cannot by itself establish how the world is. (3) Galileo’s argument that bodies of different weights fall at the same rate in a vacuum yielded genuine knowledge, even though no vacuum chamber existed in his era and he performed no new measurement. (4) Einstein’s equivalence principle, derived entirely from the elevator scenario without fresh observational input, is best classified as empirical knowledge that was later corroborated by torsion-balance experiments, though the reasoning that originally produced it was not itself empirical. (5) I defend a sharp boundary between a priori conceptual analysis and empirical science; allowing the two to blur undermines the objectivity that distinguishes scientific reasoning from metaphysical speculation. (6) I accept that thought experiments may legitimately invoke physically unrealizable conditions—perfect vacua, frictionless planes, point masses—provided they serve to isolate a single variable within an otherwise well-confirmed theoretical framework. (7) By contrast, I reject thought experiments that turn on macroscopic intuitive scenarios involving conscious observers, such as Wigner’s friend, on the grounds that consciousness is not yet physically integrated and such scenarios therefore smuggle in undefined ontological commitments.

Please examine whether this set hangs together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? I am especially uncertain whether my acceptance of (3) and (4) can survive the sharp empiricism in (2) and the sharp boundary in (5), and whether the criterion in (6) for legitimate idealization consistently explains my rejection in (7) or whether I am applying a double standard. Order the tensions by severity, specify for each what I would have to surrender—particularly whether I must abandon the sharp a priori/empirical distinction, weaken my empiricism, or concede that consciousness-based scenarios are not specially problematic—and tell me which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies. Be specific about the logical commitments: for instance, does (3) implicitly rely on premises that (2) forbids, and does (4) require me to treat retroactive experimental confirmation as altering the epistemic status of the original reasoning?
