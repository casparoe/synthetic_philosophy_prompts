I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on metaphysics and philosophy of science, and after a recent session on determinism I found that two of my colleagues—representing opposing camps—have been talking past each other in ways that a disciplined adversarial collaboration should be able to clarify. I want you to draft the joint statement that such a collaboration ought to produce, written in a formal register suitable for circulation among the participants.

The two parties are, on one side, the Epistemic Chaos Deflationists: they maintain that deterministic chaos—specifically in Hamiltonian systems exhibiting positive Lyapunov exponents, strange attractors, and exponential divergence of trajectories—is strictly an epistemic and computational limitation, leaving ontological determinism fully intact; unpredictability, on their view, arises solely from the finite resolution and embedded perspective of observers, not from any genuine openness in nature. On the other side are the Ontological Openness Theorists: they contend that chaos reveals a substantive metaphysical deficiency in determinism, arguing that when microscopic differences are amplified beyond any physically achievable resolution, the future state of a system is not merely unknown but is, in a meaningful modal sense, not fixed by present conditions plus laws—what they term “procedural openness” within an otherwise deterministic formalism.

Please structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include shared commitments about the mathematics of chaos (sensitivity to initial conditions, the topology of strange attractors, the practical failure of pointwise long-term prediction), about what determinism requires metaphysically (a modal relation between complete state descriptions and laws), and about the in-principle limits of embedded observers. Second, a disagreements table that states each side’s precise position in wording its own proponents would accept, distinguishing substantive metaphysical divergence from merely verbal disagreement—particularly on whether “fixing” a future state requires in-principle computability by an embedded agent or only an abstract modal dependency. Third, identify the cruxes: sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, including specifically how to interpret the shadowing lemma in numerical analysis, and whether the algorithmic incompressibility of certain chaotic trajectories undermines determinism or merely marks our representational limits. Fourth, for each crux, present a concrete case, theoretical result, or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, together with each camp’s explicit prediction or interpretation recorded in advance.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Avoid verbatim quotations from real texts unless you can fetch and verify the exact wording; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not resolve the dispute in favor of either camp—leave the residual disagreement exactly formulated, and ensure that any niche conceptual machinery you introduce (such as the distinction between pointwise and structural predictability, or the role of shadowing in philosophical interpretation) is presented clearly enough that a reader without web access could follow the argument. Write from the perspective of a professor drafting this for actual colleagues, with appropriate formality and precision.
