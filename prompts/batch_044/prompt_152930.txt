Consider the following line of argument. Functionalism defines mental states by causal-functional roles rather than by physical substrate, and a canonical defense appeals to multiple realizability: pain, for instance, might be instantiated in carbon neurons, silicon circuits, or alien biochemistry so long as each implements the same profile—receiving damage inputs, producing avoidance outputs, and interacting with belief and desire states. This essay contends that the defense fails once we recognize what can be called asymmetric normative realization. If pain is genuinely multiply realizable, then two systems may share an identical functional architecture yet diverge radically in normative status. A biological human and a precisely isomorphic silicon android could both instantiate the pain-role, yet only the biological system plausibly generates direct moral obligations and possesses phenomenological authority. Functionalism, identifying mental states solely by functional profile, must then attribute pain equally to both or deny it to both. The first option erases substrate-sensitive differences that seem morally and phenomenologically significant; the second abandons the intuition that functional identity alone suffices for mentality. The difficulty is not merely epistemic—how we determine which system feels pain—but metaphysical. Multiple realizability was meant to establish the autonomy of mental taxonomy from physical taxonomy, yet if that autonomy permits normatively divergent realizers, functionalism severs mental ascription from the very properties—moral considerability, phenomenological presence—that motivate attributing mental states in the first place. Rather than supporting functionalism, then, multiple realizability reveals a realization gap: functional identity does not guarantee mental uniformity when normative properties are realization-sensitive.

Please comment on this reasoning. Specifically, I am interested in feedback on: (1) whether the notion of asymmetric normative realization is conceptually coherent, or whether it illicitly imports non-functional properties that functionalism can consistently exclude; (2) whether the argument improperly generalizes from functionalism about intentional states to functionalism about phenomenal consciousness; and (3) whether standard replies—such as appealing to realization-sensitive functional roles or to a two-factor theory—can close the gap without surrendering the core functionalist commitment.
