I'm not a student or anything, just someone who reads alot about how people figure stuff out. I got really into inference to the best explanation — like when your car makes a weird noise and you guess it's the belt, not because you checked everything but because that story just hangs together best. But lately I've been wondering if philosophers are asking the wrong questions about it, or at least missing something that matters for regular people.

There's this thing I started calling "explanatory overhang" — when one explanation covers like 90% of the weird data but needs one extra assumption that feels like a patch, versus a messier explaination that needs no patches but explains less overall. I don't know if anyone's named that, but it feels like it should change which explanations we actually pick. So I'm trying to figure out: in the IBE world, which open questions or lines of work actually matter most if the goal is real decision-making — for scientists, jurys, or just people trying not to be wrong? Should we prioritize fixing the "loveliness" problem where the best explanation is also the most complicated one, or should we focus on how IBE works when different people start with different background beliefs? And is there meaningful work on whether IBE should have stricter rules for high-stakes choices versus everyday guesses?

If you had to defend a ranking of like three directions worth spending actual effort on, which ones and why? I'm less interested in a survey than in the reasoning — what's upstream of what, what would actually change how people reason, and whether my "explanatory overhang" idea connects to any of this or is just a dead end I should drop.
