A colleague recently summarized Karl Jaspers's account of collective guilt—drawn mainly from his postwar writings on the subject, with some influence from recent discussions of intergenerational moral debt—as follows. Jaspers distinguishes four kinds of guilt: criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. Criminal guilt belongs solely to individuals who committed specific offenses and can be adjudicated in court; because a nation lacks legal personhood, collective criminal guilt is impossible. Political guilt is genuinely collective: citizens share responsibility for state actions they authorized or tolerated, which means a nation can be held politically liable, for instance through reparations or institutional reform. Moral guilt is also collective, according to this summary, because those who benefit from an unjust regime inherit a moral stain through cultural membership; it operates like a structural debt transmitted across generations, so descendants bear it even if they personally did nothing wrong. Metaphysical guilt arises from human solidarity—survivors feel guilt for living while others died—and Jaspers applies it specifically to the German nation as a permanent, non-transferable burden that institutional change cannot dissolve. The summary concludes that since both political and moral guilt are collective, Jaspers ultimately accepts that nations can be guilty in a full personal sense, and that this legitimates permanent collective punishment.

I am not sure which parts of this are accurate to Jaspers and which are distortions introduced by framing guilt as an inherited debt. Could you audit the summary for me? Please go through its main claims and tell me what a careful reader of Jaspers would accept, what they would correct or qualify, and whether the conclusion that he endorses full collective personal guilt and permanent punishment actually follows from his view. In particular, please separate what is relatively settled in Jaspers scholarship—such as the fourfold distinction and his rejection of collective criminal guilt—from what remains genuinely contested, such as the precise scope of metaphysical guilt and whether it can apply to later generations; and do not present any contested reading as if it were the consensus view.
