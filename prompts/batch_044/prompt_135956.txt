As a novelist, I am constructing a character who lives with pain asymbolia — she registers severe tissue damage with clinical precision yet reports no suffering whatsoever — and I want the philosophy underpinning her condition to be exact rather than decorative. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of why pain is bad must satisfy, with the motivating case or distinction behind each: it must explain the normative weight of ordinary suffering; accommodate asymbolia without reducing it to a mere absence of affect; distinguish pain’s badness from that of nausea, grief, or dread; remain consistent with the neuroscientific separation of nociception from affective processing; and address what I call the “mirror-case” — intense negative affect occurring in the complete absence of nociceptive input — specifying whether that state shares pain’s normative weight or belongs to an altogether different category. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which reflect the preferences of particular camps, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, especially whether accommodating asymbolia forces us to abandon the claim that badness is intrinsic to phenomenal character. Briefly score hedonist, representationalist, and desire-based accounts against the list. State how confident you are in each claim, and identify the single point about which you are least certain. Please write in British English.
