I am a high school senior preparing to defend my independent research thesis at the regional philosophy and science symposium next Thursday. My argument holds that Darwin's evolutionary mechanism and Turing's computational framework constitute structurally parallel inversions of reasoning: both produce outcomes that appear to require comprehension—adaptive biological design on one hand, intelligent linguistic behavior on the other—through processes that possess no comprehension whatsoever. I label this shared structure a "selectionist architecture," and I extend it to contemporary machine-learning optimization, arguing that gradient descent over high-dimensional parameter spaces achieves predictive competence without representational understanding of the domain being predicted. The audience will include Dr. Reed, our biology faculty advisor, whose research is in evolutionary developmental biology and who is skeptical of reductionist evolutionary narratives that ignore developmental plasticity and niche construction; Mr. Chen, our computer science instructor, who insists on a sharp distinction between symbolic computation and statistical pattern matching; and two peers from the philosophy club who have recently studied Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality and are prepared to invoke the Chinese Room objection. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging each is likely to be, phrased in the vocabulary each interlocutor would actually use; for each, provide the reply I should deliver in roughly two minutes, together with an explicit note on where that reply is softest; and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—namely, whether applying my selectionist architecture to human neural development implies that human comprehension is itself merely derived from non-comprehending processes, thereby collapsing the very distinction between competence and comprehension that my thesis depends on.
