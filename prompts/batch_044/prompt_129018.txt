I sit on a municipal task force redesigning housing assistance. The framework we adopted is progressive conditionality: benefits begin unconditionally, but maintaining them at full value requires quarterly documentation of active reciprocity—verified job applications, training enrollment, or assigned community labor. The stated rationale is that this distinguishes the genuinely needy from those who could support themselves, since anyone with a legitimate barrier can request an exemption. In practice, the paperwork burden is severe enough that a large fraction of recipients who intend to comply lose benefits for missed deadlines, unreturned employer signatures, or clerical errors. This produces a specific population: individuals who, by any account of need and intent, should qualify, but who become ineligible because the verification procedure itself creates the non-compliance it is meant to catch. The standard economic framing—people responding rationally to incentives—feels inadequate, because the failure is not strategic gaming but procedural breakdown. Is there a recognized philosophical distinction between being undeserving in substance and being rendered undeserving by the architecture of the screening process? If the literature does not have a precise term for this dynamic, what conceptual tools—drawn from theories of procedural justice, desert, or reciprocity—best capture the difference, and where exactly does this case sit relative to familiar welfare-state debates about fraud, moral hazard, and conditionality?
