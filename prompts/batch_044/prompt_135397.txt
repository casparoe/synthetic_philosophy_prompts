I'm drafting an essay that attempts a third way between the standard situationist critique of virtue ethics and the usual defensive responses. Rather than denying that moral behavior varies sharply with situational factors—presence of bystanders, time pressure, ambient mood, institutional framing—I want to argue that virtue should be reconceived not as a cross-situationally consistent trait but as a threshold-gated narrative capacity. The core idea is this: virtues are not continuously distributed dispositions that should manifest in every context, but discrete capacities of practical reasoning that activate only when a situation crosses a certain moral salience threshold. Below that threshold, behavior is driven by low-cost heuristics and environmental affordances; above it, a stable narrative identity takes over and guides action. This borrows loosely from ecological psychology—affordances are only perceived when the environment is structured to reveal them—but applies it to moral character. The essay then uses this model to explain why laboratory experiments (low stakes, artificial framing, absence of narrative significance) consistently show weak trait-behavior correlations, while high-stakes, identity-constituting contexts show stronger coherence. I also sketch a corollary: cultures do not choose whether to be virtue-based or situation-based, but they do calibrate the salience threshold differently—some lower it through ritual and public accountability, others raise it through bureaucratic depersonalization. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list. I would like comments on three specific points: first, whether the threshold model genuinely avoids collapsing into tautology—if I define the threshold post hoc by the presence of virtuous behavior, have I said anything substantive? Second, whether this framing addresses the deepest version of the situationist challenge or merely relocates it from traits don't exist to traits exist but are usually dormant. Third, whether the ecological-psychology analogy is conceptually productive or just decorative metaphor. Beyond these, please point to the weakest structural move in the argument and suggest how to either defend it or concede it gracefully. If you see an implicit commitment I'm not aware of—particularly regarding the ontology of character—please flag it.
