At our philosophy of physics reading group, two members — Leo and Mara — became locked in a prolonged disagreement about whether the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics is genuinely resolved by decision-theoretic arguments. I am writing as a bystander who could not referee them adequately and would like a point-by-point adjudication.

Leo opened by observing that in the many-worlds interpretation the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically and never collapses; every outcome with non-zero amplitude is realised in some decoherent branch. He argued that this renders talk of probabilities apparently incoherent unless one can justify the Born weights. He claimed that the decision-theoretic derivation developed by Deutsch and Wallace solves the problem: a rational agent who knows the quantum state and must place bets on measurement outcomes should assign credences proportional to the squared amplitudes, rather than distributing them uniformly across branches. Leo insisted this is not circular, because the rationality axioms employed — such as diachronic consistency, measurement neutrality, and a symmetry condition across physically equivalent acts — make no explicit reference to probability. They constrain preferences over actions, and the unique weighting that satisfies all of them is |ψ|². Thus, Leo said, the derivation is epistemic rather than ontological: it shows why an agent ought to care about branches in proportion to their amplitude, without claiming that low-amplitude branches are less real.

Mara replied that this merely relocates the mystery rather than dissolving it. She pointed out that in Everettian fission, every post-measurement copy of the agent experiences its particular outcome with complete certainty. There is therefore no single “I” who is genuinely uncertain about which branch she will inhabit; rather, there will be copies in every branch. Consequently, she argued, framing the issue as “what should I expect?” misapplies the apparatus of decision theory, which presupposes that one and only one outcome will be realised for the deliberating agent. She then introduced a further consideration: after decoherence, the branches are causally and epistemically separated, so no observer can ever gather statistical data across them. This means frequentist justifications of the weights are impossible in principle, not merely impractical. For Mara, this showed that the decision-theoretic argument does not explain why an individual should care about low-amplitude branches; it formalises a weighting that was already tacitly assumed in the statement of the problem.

Leo countered that Mara’s fission objection confuses personal identity with rational concern. Even if all copies exist, each is a temporal continuation of the pre-measurement agent, and rationality requires distributing concern among all one’s future selves. The correct question, he said, is not “which copy will I be?” — since that presupposes uniqueness — but “how ought I to weigh the welfare of my various successors?” The squared-amplitude distribution, he maintained, is the only one that respects the symmetries of the quantum state and avoids arbitrarily privileging one physically identical branch over another.

Mara responded that “distributing concern” is exactly where the circularity enters. Without an independent normative reason to treat squared amplitude as the correct metric of concern, one could just as well distribute concern equally per branch (branch-counting) or per unit of physical volume in configuration space. She added that Leo’s appeal to symmetry overlooks that, in the ontology of the theory, all branches exist with equal reality; the amplitude is a coefficient in the wavefunction, not an ontological weight. To treat it as a measure of rational concern, she argued, is to smuggle the Born rule back in through the normative back door, disguising an ontological assumption as a rationality constraint.

They continued without convergence. Leo believes Mara misses that rational decision theory can generate probabilities without any ontological commitment to weighted existence; Mara believes Leo misses that the derivation assumes precisely what needs explaining by treating amplitude as a normative measure of concern.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess Leo’s claim about the non-circularity of the rationality axioms, Mara’s objection concerning fission and epistemic separation, Leo’s reply about distributing concern among successors, and Mara’s charge that the argument smuggles in the Born rule. Say who is right about which specific point, identify anything important that both are neglecting — for example, whether the debate conflates rational expectation with predictive success, or whether there is a principled distinction between epistemic and ontological readings of the Born rule in this context — and indicate who is more right overall. Please write your answer in British English.
