In algorithmic auditing, regulators and engineers apply two distinct anti-discrimination tests that are usually treated as rival measurements of the same underlying unfairness. The first is an indicative statistical check: it asks whether membership in a protected class correlates with adverse outcomes in the observed dataset. The second is a counterfactual fairness criterion: for a given individual, it asks whether the decision would have flipped had the protected attribute been different, holding constant every feature that is not causally downstream of that attribute. Many institutional frameworks present these as interchangeable operationalizations, yet they rely on different conditional logics—one on observed-world correlations, the other on cross-world causal dependencies. What philosophical commitments about indicative versus counterfactual conditionals does this practice presuppose? Does treating them as measuring one thing require assuming that counterfactuals in causal models reduce to indicative statistical patterns, or that indicative correlations can be interpreted counterfactually without loss? Reconstruct the implicit theory of conditionals embedded in the auditing practice, say whether its parts are consistent, and tell me what would have to change in regulatory design if we refused to collapse the indicative/counterfactual distinction.
