Proponents of direct democracy frequently liken a referendum to a jury trial: citizens are presented with competing arguments and render a binding verdict, an image used to defend the Swiss optional referendum and to explain the democratic appeal of Brexit or California ballot propositions. Critics reply that voters lack deliberative isolation, face campaign advertising rather than vetted evidence, and operate without analogues to unanimous verdicts or judicial instruction. Audit the analogy in detail: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping judge, jury, evidence, verdict, and appeal onto the referendum process; identify which jury-trial norms—such as the presumption of innocence or the exclusion of prejudicial material—are silently imported and whether referendum design can accommodate them; distinguish the Swiss mechanism, where repeated votes allow revision, from one-off events like Brexit; and determine whether the analogy genuinely supports the legitimacy claim, the incompetence objection, or neither once the disanalogies are fully on the table.
