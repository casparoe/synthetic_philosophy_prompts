I run a grad seminar on philosophy of mind — about 8 people, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes participants feel the force of behavioral criteria for mind, then feel them collapse. Don't do the classic imitation game; instead, inject a niche twist: four students are 'sources' who write private answers to philosophical prompts, but a hidden 'mediation layer' (a set of rules that acts like a cheap predictive filter) rewrites every message before the other four 'judges' recieve it. The judges must vote, with small point stakes they can loose, on whether the chat stream comes from one unified mind or from several. Arrange the rules so that judges start confident — they should be able to ask follow-ups and feel they are tracking a single interlocutor — and only crash into ambiguity after they learn the mediation layer exists and that no source understands the rewritten output. Include roles, a timeline, payoffs in points, and a debrief that maps the crash onto behavioral criteria specifically: what was working, what broke, and why the filter makes the criterion unreliable. Be honest about which feature of distributed or mediated cognition the game can't reproduce (probably the actual phenomenology of being spread across agents). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I think someone will try to reverse-engineer the filter rules. Keep it casual; a typo or two is fine, and don't ask for bibliographies or citations since I want this runnable without anyone looking stuff up.
