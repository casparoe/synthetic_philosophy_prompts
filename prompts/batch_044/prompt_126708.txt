Having retired from experimental physics after several decades in the laboratory, I have lately been studying philosophical decision theory, and I have constructed an argument about the Pasadena game that I cannot dismantle without losing either mathematical rigor or practical common sense. The game itself is straightforward: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; on the nth toss one receives ±2ⁿ/n, with the sign positive for odd n and negative for even n. The probabilities are perfectly well defined, and each outcome carries a definite utility, yet the expectation is only conditionally convergent: by reordering the terms of the series one can make the expected value equal any real number whatsoever, including zero, positive infinity, or negative infinity. Standard expected-utility theory therefore assigns no definite preference to playing or refusing. Here is the argument that now troubles me.

(1) Any complete normative theory of rational choice must deliver a preference ordering whenever probabilities and utilities are fully specified for every outcome.
(2) In the Pasadena game, probabilities and utilities are fully specified.
(3) Expected-utility theory delivers no preference ordering, because the expectation is undefined and reorderable.
(4) Therefore expected-utility theory is incomplete: it fails to satisfy its own completeness condition.
(5) Many actual scientific and institutional decisions—allocating funds to high-risk, long-tail research programs, or assessing climate damages with power-law distributions—exhibit payoff structures whose moments are undefined or conditionally convergent in precisely the same formal manner.
(6) By direct analogy, expected-utility reasoning is silent for these real-world scientific allocations.
(7) Yet funding agencies and policy bodies do reach reasoned, non-arbitrary decisions in such domains, which implies they operate with a decision rule other than pure expected-utility maximization.
(8) Any alternative rule capable of handling undefined moments must either (a) impose a canonical ordering of outcomes, or (b) reject the additivity of utility across outcomes.
(9) Option (a) requires a metaphysically privileged ordering that physics itself appears to deny—quantum field theory employs different regularization schemes that reorder formally divergent series without producing contradictory physical predictions—while option (b) abandons the very axiom that distinguishes expected-utility theory from mere descriptive psychology.
(10) Hence either scientific rationality rests on an arbitrary regularization choice lacking philosophical justification, or the additivity axiom is false and expected-utility theory collapses as a universal framework for rational choice.

This conclusion seems far too destructive: it threatens not only decision theory but the coherence of scientific risk assessment itself. I suspect that one of these steps contains a hidden slide—perhaps the analogy between an idealized infinite game and truncated real-world distributions, or the inference from descriptive practice to normative failure, or the claim that physics denies canonical ordering. But I cannot locate the precise error without either dismissing the mathematical fact of conditional convergence or accepting that rational choice in science is merely conventional. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Must I concede that rationality is locally defined by institutional convention, or is there a principled way to preserve the normative force of expectation even when the formal sum diverges?
