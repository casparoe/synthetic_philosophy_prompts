I'm preparing an argument for my philosophy of action course about whether sphexishness—the kind of mechanical rigidity exhibited by the digger wasp Sphex, which will complete an invariant burrow-inspection sequence even when the burrow has been experimentally emptied—makes free will impossible. Here is the chain I have ended up with:

(1) Sphexishness is total behavioral rigidity: an agent is sphexish just in case it cannot modify its action-pattern in response to any novel obstacle.

(2) Free will requires flexibility; an agent whose every move is locked into an invariant sequence is merely mechanical, not self-directing.

(3) Yet an agent that lacks all rigidity—whose responses are entirely flexible, with no stable patterns—lacks determinacy. There is no unified source of action, only an unconnected series of reactions.

(4) So free will requires both the absence of sphexishness (to allow flexibility) and its presence (to provide the determinacy that makes someone a single agent).

(5) Nothing can both be and not be sphexish.

(6) Therefore free will is impossible.

The conclusion is clearly too strong. I want to preserve (2) and (3), since both seem true: complete rigidity precludes agency, and complete randomness undermines it. But I am unsure whether (4) is valid, or whether (1) sets up a false dichotomy by treating "some stable patterns" as equivalent to "sphexishness." Where does the argument fail—at the inference to (4), in the definition at (1), or elsewhere? And what do I have to give up if I reject that step?
