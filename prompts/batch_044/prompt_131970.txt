I’m putting together a grad seminar on institutional design and obligation — about 10 people, 75 minutes, mostly philosophy students with a little politics backround. Weve been talking about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, but the conversation keeps sliding back to the Odyssey and debt-ceiling debates, and I want them to actually feel the structural tension rather than just name it.

Could you design an in-class simulation that forces them into that tension? Here’s the framing I’ve been playing with: split the group into three “generational cohorts” (each stands in for a governing body with a 30-year mandate). Cohort 1 drafts a binding “mirror clause” — a rule that Cohort 2 can only invoke past commitments if they can demonstrate, through a structured deliberation ritual, that their present values map onto the original intent without interpretive stretching. Cohort 2 then recieves a crisis scenario where honoring the clause blocks a policy they genuinely need, but breaking it collapses the legitimacy of the whole precommitment architecture. I want roles with real stakes: maybe present-session voting weight is tied to how strictly each cohort adheres to prior commitments, so betrayal is individually rational but collectively destructive. Include concrete rules, materials (just paper and maybe tokens), a payoff scheme using points rather than money, a 75-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the Ulysses/constitutional problem — especially where honoring the past becomes authoritarian and where breaking it becomes self-undermining. Be honest about what feature of real constitutional precommitment this can’t reproduce (probably the actual temporal distance and cultural drift between generations). And tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run — I’m guessing people will immediately try to game the mirror clause by writing ambiguous original language.
