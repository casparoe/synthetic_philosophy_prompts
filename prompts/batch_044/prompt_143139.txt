I’ve been thinking about meritocracy since a recent discussion with friends—mostly people who’ve read the old LessWrong sequences on rationalist virtue—about whether the luck-egalitarian critique can be answered by shifting from outcome-desert to something thinner, like epistemic or procedural desert. The standard debate pits Rawlsian fairness against Nozickian entitlement, but both sides usually measure merit by results: income, status, or social contribution. That framing feels stale, especially in noisy domains where success is heavily shaped by factors outside anyone’s control, and I want to push past the usual stalemate.

Write an essay addressing this controversy: can a meritocracy be justified solely on the basis of procedural rationality—sustained calibration, willingness to update on counter-evidence, and resistance to motivated reasoning—without collapsing back into outcome-desert? To make the issue concrete, consider a specific institutional design: a national science-policy advisory board in which members’ voting weights are assigned proportionally to their long-run Brier scores in structured, domain-relevant prediction markets. The board does not directly reward wealth or social origin; it rewards predictive accuracy and epistemic hygiene. Even so, critics will observe that access to high-quality data, the cognitive bandwidth to process it, and the early educational environment that fosters good calibration are themselves distributed by a genetic and social lottery. Defenders might reply that procedural virtue is genuinely agential in a way raw outcomes are not—that updating well is something one does, not merely something that happens to one.

Weigh both sides with care, but do not end in agnosticism. Reach a clear verdict: is this form of procedural meritocracy philosophically defensible, or does the luck of one’s epistemic environment and cognitive endowment undermine even this thinner claim to desert? Justify your conclusion with explicit reasoning about the nature of agency, the moral relevance of process versus result, and the institutional implications of accepting or rejecting this model.
