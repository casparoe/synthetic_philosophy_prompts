In my seminar on market ethics and price theory, our working group has developed a track record on proposals involving wage and price controls. Our precedents are as follows. We endorsed a municipal rent-stabilization ordinance that caps annual rent increases at regional wage growth plus two percent, arguing that urban housing supply is inelastic and tenant-specific investments generate bilateral monopoly conditions. We rejected a five-year statewide rent freeze with vacancy control, predicting capital flight, maintenance neglect, and black-market allocation. We supported a sectoral minimum wage for nursing-home aides set eighteen percent above the local competitive benchmark, citing regional employer concentration and monopsony power. We opposed a universal twenty-two-dollar hourly floor for all retail and food-service workers in our county, on the grounds that labor markets in those sectors are competitive and lack employer concentration. Finally, we endorsed temporary emergency price caps on insulin and generic antibiotics, but only when paired with government bulk-purchasing contracts that guarantee manufacturer volumes.

We now face a new proposal: a permanent nationwide cap on tuition for all public graduate professional programs—law, medicine, and business—set at four thousand dollars per year, funded entirely by a progressive payroll tax on graduates in those fields earning above the median income, with direct state subsidies to universities to maintain enrollment capacity and faculty salaries. The national market for these programs is competitive, with abundant private and public alternatives, and there is no empirical evidence of monopsony in the hiring markets for these graduates. The stated aim is to eliminate debt burdens and reduce credential inflation.

If we wish to be consistent with our own precedents, what should our verdict be? Identify the closest analog among our five positions, say whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, and—if they do—state clearly what principle we would be adopting with an approval and what principle with a rejection. I want a committed answer: take a definite position, defend it with reasons rather than presenting the options as equally valid, and tell me which further case—one that shares the structural features of this proposal but varies in one critical respect—would best reveal whether we are running a market-failure-correction principle or a distributive-access principle.
