Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers discussing whether large language models are agents, simulators, or a distinct kind of cognitive artifact. The first speaker should defend a refined simulator thesis centered on diachronic counterfactual fragility: the claim that shifting sampling temperature or context length can reverse an LLM's apparent commitments in ways that no agent with stable mental states could tolerate, and therefore that attributing beliefs or intentions is a category error. The second speaker should defend a distributed-cognition thesis: LLMs are neither autonomous agents nor passive simulators but epistemic nodes within human-machine reasoning networks, and that counterfactual fragility is precisely what one expects from distributed cognition rather than a disqualifier for agency. Each speaker must respond directly to the other's prior argument rather than talk past them. In your response, clearly separate what is broadly settled (for instance, that current LLMs lack persistent cross-session goals) from what remains genuinely contested among researchers who understand the technical architecture. Keep the dialogue under 300 words.
