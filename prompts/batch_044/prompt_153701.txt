As a novelist who has written extensively on historical fiction and on the ethics of anachronism, I have arrived, over several published essays and reviews, at a working stance on what I call Whig narration—the temptation to write the past as an unwitting prelude to the present. I summarize four of my judgments below.

First, I condemned a recent popular history that describes the sealing of Magna Carta as “the first tremor of modern constitutional democracy,” on the grounds that it dissolves the barons’ feudal particularism into a teleology they would not have recognized.

Second, I defended a biography of a nineteenth-century labor organizer that frames her campaigns as “seeds of the modern welfare state,” but only because the biography demonstrates, through her own speeches and correspondence, that she conceived of her work in precisely those progressive, state-oriented terms.

Third, I criticized a well-received novel about medieval surgeons that portrays them as “practicing an early form of evidence-based medicine.” Even though the novel never claims that these surgeons intended modern clinical methods, I argued that imposing that conceptual framework robs them of their own medical cosmology, regardless of how carefully their intentions are left unstated.

Fourth, I endorsed an account of a collapsed 1920s cooperative farm that calls the failure “a necessary lesson from which modern agronomy eventually learned.” My endorsement rested on the fact that the farm’s own internal archives—letters and meeting minutes—reveal that the members themselves adopted exactly this pedagogical framing, assessing their collapse in terms of future collective success.

Now I am preparing an essay on a recent scholarly biography of an eleventh-century astronomer working in Cairo. The biography is scrupulous about vocabulary: it describes his corrections to Ptolemaic models using only his own theological and mathematical language, never attributing to him a “scientific method” or any desire to overturn tradition. Yet in its conclusion, the biography asserts that “the true historical weight of his tables lies not in his own intentions, but in the empirical substrate they unknowingly provided for Newton’s mechanistic cosmology.” The author explicitly denies any projection onto the astronomer’s mind, while deriving the entirety of his significance from his unwitting causal contribution to modern physics.

If I wish to remain consistent with my four prior judgments, how should I assess this biography? Identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly account for my earlier verdicts—one that emphasizes whether the narrative’s conceptual framing remains faithful to the actors’ own categories, and one that emphasizes whether the narrative derives the value of past actions primarily from their service to present conditions, regardless of how carefully intentions are rendered. For each principle, state whether it condemns or accepts the biography. Then offer your best judgment of what my consistent position should be: identify the closest analog among the four precedents, explain which principle I would actually be privileging, and state honestly what further commitment I would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—clearly distinct from the astronomer—that would decisively distinguish the two principles, describing both the case and the opposite verdicts the two rules would yield. Write in a formal tone appropriate for a novelist addressing fellow writers on the craft of historical narrative.
