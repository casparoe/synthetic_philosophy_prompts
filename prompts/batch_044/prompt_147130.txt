Essay notes for comment:

The standard critique of Nuremberg and Tokyo collapses two distinct phenomena: jurisdictional retroactivity, which concerns the creation of a tribunal empowered to judge past conduct, and normative retroactivity, which concerns the ex post facto creation of a criminal standard. At Nuremberg, the London Charter did both, yet the defense arguments and the tribunal's own reasoning largely acknowledged—explicitly—that crimes against peace and aggressive war were being defined for the first time, justifying the innovation through appeals to moral necessity and the prior wrongfulness of the acts under general international law. By contrast, the Charter of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East introduced conspiracy charges that lacked a clear, pre-existing analogue in international criminal law. Rather than openly admitting normative innovation, however, Tokyo's reasoning tended to treat instruments such as the Kellogg-Briand Pact and patterns of state practice as containing an already-latent criminal norm, one that the trial merely rendered explicit. I propose calling this interpretive retroactivity: a legislative act disguised as judicial discovery, in which the tribunal denies its own novelty by claiming to uncover norms implicitly embedded in treaty interstices and customary conduct.

The philosophical concern is that interpretive retroactivity may be more troubling than explicit retroactivity precisely because it obscures the source of legal authority. Explicit retroactivity, as at Nuremberg, at least makes transparent that a new standard is being applied and invites direct scrutiny of its justification. Interpretive retroactivity, by presenting itself as interpretation rather than legislation, evades this scrutiny and undermines the predictability that the prohibition on ex post facto law is meant to protect. I want to examine whether this distinction holds up analytically, whether Tokyo's treatment of conspiracy actually exemplifies interpretive retroactivity rather than straightforward normative innovation, and whether transparency about legal change is genuinely a defensible moral requirement in transitional justice contexts—or whether it is merely an aesthetic preference for candor over strategic ambiguity.
