Design a diagnostic decision-tree taxonomy for debates about the paradox of deontology, tailored specifically to contexts of distributed or institutional agency—cases in which harmful outcomes emerge from the coordinated actions of many individuals, none of whom individually intends or directly causes the harm (e.g., structural supply chains, algorithmic decision architectures, or democratic voting protocols that produce rights-violating policies). The tree must be constructed from logical commitments rather than named schools: begin with a root question that separates those who think deontological constraints apply identically to collective entities from those who deny this, then proceed through branches defined by concrete diagnostic scenarios—such as whether a programmer who contributes one module to a harmful automated system bears direct moral culpability comparable to an individual perpetrator, or whether a citizen who votes for a rights-violating policy is complicit in the same way as its enforcer. Each leaf should identify a position (named if it has an established label in the literature, otherwise marked 'unnamed'), state its resolution of the paradox in distributed contexts, and articulate its most awkward commitment—particularly regarding either the attribution of individual blame or the feasibility of institutional redesign. Map all logically possible combinations of answers; for any unoccupied branch, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, theoretically unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, explicitly flag any pairs of named positions that the tree's questions fail to separate, explaining which shared commitment causes the overlap.
