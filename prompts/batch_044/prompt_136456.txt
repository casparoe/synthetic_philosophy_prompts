I was talking with a friend about Adam Smith's view on colonies, especially how he argued Britain was actually harming America through mercantile restrictions even while claiming to protect it. That got me thinking about a pattern I keep seeing in different eras: a dominant power builds up institutions in a weaker region that genuinely improve some concrete metrics—trade volume, roads, literacy rates—but those same institutions are arranged so the region can never actually govern itself. The local economy gets locked into exporting raw materials, legal authority stays with the metropole, and local leaders are appointed rather than accountable. Defenders always point to the improvement data and say "they're recieving more benefit than harm," but the dependency is structurally baked in, not accidental. I know Smith noticed something like this, but I'm wondering if there's a specific established concept for this exact configuration—not just generic colonialism, not ordinary hypocrisy, and not just failed development aid, but the particular case where benevolent improvement and enforced dependency are two sides of the same institutional design. Does it have a precise name in political economy or postcolonial thought? And if there isn't one, how should I draw its boundaries—say, to distinguish it from paternalism where the authority genuinely intends to eventually let go?
