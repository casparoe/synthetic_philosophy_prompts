In a recent graduate seminar on political ethics, my colleagues keep returning to Arendt's diagnosis of the banality of evil, but they use the phrase to do very different work: one invokes it to argue that genocide requires no monsters, only compliant functionaries; another uses it to diminish moral culpability, claiming that Eichmann lacked the inner grandeur of true wickedness; a third treats it as a sociological thesis about bureaucratic compartmentalization. I want to bring a more precise disambiguation to our next meeting. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that this slogan might be expressing—separating, for example, a psychological thesis about Eichmann's personal motives and emotional constitution; a sociological thesis about role morality within administrative hierarchies; an epistemic thesis about a specific failure of judgment, sometimes characterized as a kind of thoughtlessness rather than stupidity; and perhaps an aesthetic or normative thesis about the absence of depth, intentionality, or demonic grandeur in modern wrongdoing? For each reading, indicate whether it is a plausible interpretation of Arendt's analysis of the Eichmann case, whether it is trivial or genuinely substantive, and whether it actually supports the particular excuse, accusation, or structural diagnosis being made in the seminar. I am particularly interested in whether there is a defensible reading that connects this banality not merely to ordinariness or conformity, but to a failure of what Arendt elsewhere called representative thinking—the inability to imagine the perspective of others in one's judgment—rather than to a generic lack of intelligence or to purely bureaucratic conditioning. Please present the readings as a menu with verdicts, so that I can identify which interpretation each colleague is implicitly relying on and whether their conclusion survives the precisification.
