I'm working on a novel where one character is trying to defend karma as a genuine theory of moral desert, not just cosmic cause-and-effect. At a dinner party in the book, two other characters get into it, and I want the argument to feel real rather than strawman. Here's roughly what they said.

Rosa said karma can't really be about desert if reincarnation wipes the slate clean psycholgicaly. The person suffering now isn't the one who stole or lied — they're a different consciousness with different memories. Moral desert has to stick to the agent who earned it, otherwise it's just random bad luck wearing a moral mask.

Jules answered that this assumes identity is all about memory and psychology, which is already controversial. If you take a causal or narrative view of the self — like a river that keeps its name even though the water changes — then the karmic thread is still the same agent. The desert travels with the thread, not with the mental content.

Rosa pushed back: even if the thread is the same river, desert requires that the subject can understand the connection between what they did and what's happening to them. If the reincarnated person has no recall of the past life, they experience the suffering as meaningless misfortune. That breaks the moral logic of desert, which is supposed to be about justice being done, not just energy balancing out.

Jules said that's mixing up two things — the experience of punishment and the fact of punishment. A society can execute justice on someone who doesn't understand why, and it's still justice. But Rosa said that's exactly the problem: without awareness, karma becomes indistinguishable from natural law. Gravity pulls you down without caring who you are; if karma works the same way, calling it moral desert is just poetry.

Then Jules brought in this idea he called the desert-transfer asymmetry — the notion that in karmic systems, merit and demerit don't attach to a psychological ego but accumulate in a kind of moral field tied to the causal stream. The stream inherits the balance, not because it deserves it in the personal sense, but because the field needs discharge, and the stream is the appropriate vessel. So karma is moral desert at the level of the system, not the individual moment.

Rosa said that sounds clever but it's just relabelling causation. If the vessel doesn't personally deserve the hit, then you've abandoned moral desert for cosmic bookkeeping. She also said Jules is missing something: if karma is really about desert, there should be some reason why suffering is the right currency — why not just have the wrongdoer learn a lesson without pain? The fact that karma uses suffering suggests it's either retributive or it's not about morality at all.

Jules replied that suffering might be the only thing that actually reshapes the causal stream — that pleasure doesn't correct imbalance, it just adds to it. So suffering isn't arbitrary punishment; it's the specific mechanism by which the field restores equilibirum.

They went back and forth a bit more but that's the core. Can you go through there exchange and say who's right about each specific point? And tell me what both of them are missing — especially about whether karma can survive without either full psycholgical continuity or collapsing into pure natural law?
