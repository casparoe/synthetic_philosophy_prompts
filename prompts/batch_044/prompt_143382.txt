Hey, I'm a retired chemist who started reading philosophy of science after I left the lab, and I think I cooked up an argument that eats its own tail. Maybe you can tell me where I tripped?

(1) Popper's answer to the demarcation problem is falsifability: a claim counts as scientific only if there's some possible observation that could show it's false.

(2) But in my old field we constantly use models we know are literally false, like treating atoms as hard spheres in crystal packing. We don't reject them when quantum mechanics contradicts them; we just restrict their domain and keep using them.

(3) So if falsifiability means "can be shown literally false," then these crystal-packing models aren't scientific. But they clearly are, so that can't be right.

(4) Maybe Popper means "falsifiable within its claimed domain." But then astrology can claim its domain is only "broad personality patterns" and dismiss precise failures as outside that domain.

(5) Meanwhile, any theory can retreat to a domain restriction. So the criterion becomes "does the practitioner draw boundaries?" which feels sociological, not logical.

(6) Therefore either falsifiability fails as demarcation, or we have to say chemisty's hard-sphere models are pseudoscince, which is nuts.

(7) I still feel there's a real difference between how we defend crystal models and how astrologers defend horoscopes, but I can't pin it in the logic. Which premise should I drop, and what's the cost? I'd rather save the intuition that science risks being wrong in a way astrology doesn't, but I'm not sure which step buys me that.
