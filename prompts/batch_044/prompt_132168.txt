I was discussing causation by omission with friends after skimming some older philosophy of science threads, and we kept circling a case that never appears in the standard textbooks. Imagine an automated regional climate-adaptation pipeline in which a critical aerosol-forcing variable is systematically excluded—not by any present agent's choice, but because an upstream feature-selection heuristic, originally designed for a different low-variance domain, silently drops it. Years later, when the pipeline produces dangerously optimistic projections, can we genuinely say the omission caused the harm? One of us defended a structural-interventionist view: in distributed socio-technical systems, omissions become causally loaded when they are locked in by prior design commitments, producing what we might call institutional omission that interventionist counterfactuals can still track. The other insisted this is causal misattribution—only the positive acts of building and deploying the pipeline are causes, and treating absence as causal in automated contexts confuses our epistemic gap with metaphysical reality, particularly since no agent was present to "fail" to act. Please write a dialogue between two technically competent interlocutors—one sympathetic to structural or interventionist causation, the other to a stricter Humean or regularist framework—who debate whether this kind of embedded omission in algorithmic infrastructure counts as genuine causation by omission. They should engage each other's actual points about distributed responsibility, counterfactual dependency, and whether structural absence differs from individual negligence, rather than falling back on generic examples like a gardener neglecting to water plants.
