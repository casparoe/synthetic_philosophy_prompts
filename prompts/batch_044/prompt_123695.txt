I'm in a philosophy of well-being seminar and we've been stuck for like three weeks on tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering actually carries intrinsic value, and that positive mental states are at best instrumentally useful. One camp keeps saying it's just negative utilitarianism with a buddhist gloss and that we're not being precise about "absence"; another camp insists it's structurally different because it denies any axiological weight to pleasure or flourishing, not just that suffering outweighs them. Then there's this one grad student who keeps arguing that "equanimity" is itself a positive state that tranquilists smuggle back in, so the position collapses into a weird kind of hedonism about calmness. We've all read the same three papers, nobody's moved an inch, and I'm honestly not sure if we're arguing over the word "value" or over hidden empirical claims about whether desire-less contentment is psychologically possible. Is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive one about the ontology of mental states, or are we answering different questions — one about welfare economics and one about the phenomenology of craving — under one heading? And if the stall is structural, what kind of distinction or thought experiment would actually unstick it, or should we just admit it's bedrock intuitions about whether a life with zero desire is even coherent?
