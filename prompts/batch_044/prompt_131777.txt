I am designing a 90-minute graduate workshop on practical rationality for 14 advanced philosophy students. I want an in-class game that makes participants feel the force of escalation of commitment within a dollar-auction structure—not merely grasp it intellectually, but experience the precise moment when continuing to bid feels locally rational even as global losses accumulate, and when quitting feels like a betrayal of prior moves rather than an arithmetic correction.

Design a fully runnable activity with the following architecture. Use a classic dollar-auction payoff scheme: participants are paired into 7 simultaneous pairs. Each pair competes for a fixed prize worth 10 points. Bids escalate in increments of 1 point. The highest bidder wins the prize and pays their final bid; the second-highest bidder pays their final bid and receives nothing. Run the bidding for up to 12 rounds or until both withdraw. Use no real money—only points that feed into a small-stakes course-ranking system—while preserving enough emotional weight that withdrawal stings.

Inject this philosophical twist: before bidding begins, each player secretly draws one of three practical-reason profiles—Instrumentalist (justify each move solely by expected point yield), Identity-Consistent (justify each move solely by coherence with prior public commitments made in this game), or Rule-Follower (justify each move solely by adherence to a self-announced stopping threshold, which they must state aloud in round 1). Every bid must be accompanied by a one-sentence written rationale on a visible index card, constrained strictly to the reasoning mode of their drawn profile. A player may switch profiles at any time, but doing so costs 3 points, requires them to physically tear up their old justification card in front of their partner, and forces them to begin a fresh card. The rules and the visibility of the cards should be arranged so that the justification mechanism itself becomes part of the escalation trap: players defend continued bidding not because they still want the prize, but because tearing up the log or violating their announced rule feels like a public admission of failure or irrationality.

Please provide: (1) complete rules, materials, and room setup; (2) the point/payoff scheme and how it sustains emotional stakes without real money; (3) a timeline dividing the 30-minute game phase from the 60-minute debrief and discussion; (4) the debrief mapped precisely onto moments in the game—the first bid that exceeds half the prize value, the round in which a partner’s glare or silence replaces calculation, the moment a player considers tearing their card but bids instead—connecting each to the formal mechanics of sunk-cost escalation and to the philosophical tension between instrumental and identity-based practical reason; (5) an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., pairs quitting too early out of social embarrassment, one partner refusing to bid aggressively, or the profile-draw feeling arbitrary) and how to correct for it in facilitation; and (6) a frank statement of which feature of real-world commitment escalation this activity cannot reproduce—such as the absence of genuine financial ruin, or the fact that profiles are assigned rather than authentically adopted—and how you will supply that missing feature verbally during the debrief.

The idea should emerge from what the rules and incentives cause participants to do, not from what you explain afterward. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation to any author. Do not ask participants to read anything in advance. Write as if I will run this tomorrow.
