I'm presenting in an interdisciplinary humanities seminar a thesis about Orwell's political essays—principally Politics and the English Language and The Prevention of Literature—that moves beyond the familiar euphemism reading. I argue that Orwell advances an implicit functionalist account of what I term conceptual attrition: not merely that degraded language obscures reality, but that specific syntactic patterns—the systematic replacement of active verbs with nominalizations paired with passive constructions—gradually erode the grammatical and lexical resources required to formulate moral objections to bureaucratic violence. On this reading, Orwell treats language degradation not as stylistic failure but as a structural mechanism that suppresses agency attribution, and this distinguishes his position from later critical discourse analysts, who treat language as reflecting rather than causally shaping institutional violence. I also claim that this reading avoids reducing Orwell to a crude Sapir-Whorf determinist, since he locates the damage in syntactic habits that are learnable and therefore resistible, not in an inescapable linguistic prison.
