You are a professional philosopher of moral psychology and the philosophy of history, with specific expertise in the historiography of collective violence and the explanation of perpetrator behavior. Before drafting, think step by step and spend at least a brief internal reasoning stage: identify the core empirical claims shared by situationalist explanations (associated with Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101) and cultural-character explanations (associated with Daniel Goldhagen’s thesis about eliminationist antisemitism), then isolate exactly where they diverge on motivation, peer dynamics, and ideological predisposition. Then draft the joint adversarial-collaboration statement.

Your deliverable is a formal document that two opposing scholars—one defending a situational-institutional model and one defending a culture-character model—could both sign. Structure it in four parts:

1. Agreed empirical claims: A numbered list of statements about the documented behavior of Reserve Police Battalion 101 during the Józefów massacre in July 1942 and its subsequent actions that both sides accept without qualification. Cover observable patterns—rates of refusal, patterns of voluntary participation, expressions of psychological distress, and expressions of satisfaction or enthusiasm—while strictly avoiding interpretive framing about what caused them.

2. Neutral formulation of disagreement: A table with two columns. In wording each defender would accept, one column should state the situationalist claim that institutional pressure, peer conformity, incremental escalation, and division of labor are sufficient to explain perpetration without presupposing a prior, widespread ideological commitment to killing Jews. The other column should state the Goldhagen-style claim that a culturally embedded, eliminationist antisemitism was a necessary condition for the scale and willingness of the killing, and that situational factors alone are explanatorily insufficient.

3. Cruxes: Identify exactly two cruxes. The first must apply an injected conceptual framework called the counterfactual stability test: for each of three explanatory layers—situational pressure, peer-culture reinforcement, and characterological predisposition—state whether perpetrator behavior would remain stable if that single layer were removed while the other two were held constant. Both sides must agree that the test is meaningful, even if they predict opposite outcomes. The second crux should address whether moral injury (psychological trauma resulting from killing) and ideological enthusiasm (positive affective satisfaction from killing) are compatible co-occurring explanations or mutually exclusive indicators of underlying motivation.

4. Predictive commitments and residual disagreement: For each crux, specify one concrete historical detail or hypothetical scenario both sides agree bears on the question, and record in advance what each side predicts. Then address whether there is any evidence both would agree could actually move the debate, or whether part of the dispute is verbal—specifically, whether the phrase willing executioner functions descriptively (about subjective motivation) or normatively (about moral culpability)—and state clearly which part.

Write in formal, neutral academic prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not use verbatim quotation marks for any source material; paraphrase all references to texts, testimony, or historical records. Be explicit about one feature this adversarial format cannot reproduce: the emotional and phenomenological weight of encountering primary testimony, which the facilitator or reader must supply directly. Finally, include a brief predictive note on what will go wrong if this document is used in a seminar without preparation: participants will likely collapse the counterfactual stability test into a simplistic nature-versus-nurture framing, and the facilitator must correct this explicitly before discussion begins.
