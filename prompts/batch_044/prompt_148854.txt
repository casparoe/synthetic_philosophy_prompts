For a graduate bioethics workshop of ten participants with seventy-five minutes, design a structured role-play that makes the group feel why the authority of an advance directive can collapse not through a change of person but through what you will call a “value-translation failure”: a situation in which the later self genuinely understands every term in the directive yet can no longer map those terms onto any live evaluative framework. Specify three rotating roles—directive-author (bound to defend the document’s original normative vocabulary), present-self (who retains emotional coherence but operates with an incommensurable evaluative vocabulary revealed only after Phase 1), and surrogate adjudicator (who must decide using only arguments available within the assigned framework)—together with concrete rules and small stakes, such as pooled recommendation points that the triad must defend to the full group. Provide a three-phase timeline: fifteen minutes for drafting directives under either an identity-continuity or a semantic-continuity rule; twenty-five minutes for triad negotiation with hidden “value-translation” cards that alter the present-self’s evaluative vocabulary without altering memory or emotional life; and fifteen minutes for a “disruption event” in which the facilitator reveals a simulated neuropsychological profile showing selective erasure of the framework that authored the directive but preservation of the emotional life it was meant to protect, forcing an immediate vote with justification. Include a debrief that maps negotiation moments onto the distinction between identity-based authority and semantic-continuity authority, predicts the predictable failure mode (participants will conflate disagreement about values with failure to understand the directive), and explains how to handle it. End with an honest note on which feature of value-translation failure—the first-person phenomenology of finding one’s own past norms not wrong but literally unintelligible—the activity structurally cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply through guided reflection rather than rules. Do not ask participants to cite philosophers or texts; the force should emerge from the incentives, hidden information, and the voting constraint.
