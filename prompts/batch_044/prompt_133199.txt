The debate over whether large language models genuinely hold beliefs—framed through Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance—has stalled in a way that feels conceptually gridlocked rather than empirically open. On one side, philosophers and interpretability researchers argue that attributing beliefs such as “Paris is the capital of France” to a transformer-based system is at best a predictive fiction: the model lacks persistent representational states governed by truth-conditions, shows no stable calibration asymmetry between belief and disbelief (it can affirm and deny the same proposition across prompt variations without any internal commitment change), and possesses no defeasible commitment structure—human beliefs are withdrawn in response to specific social and evidential challenges, whereas model “outputs” are revised only through parameter updates or prompt engineering, not through reasoning about reasons. On the other side, proponents of the intentional stance reply that belief attribution was never intended to track internal mechanisms but to capture behaviorally stable patterns that resist mechanistic explanation at the relevant explanatory grain; they cite emergent consistency in chain-of-thought reasoning and the practical failure of purely mechanistic descriptions to predict outputs in complex domains, insisting that if a system behaves in ways that are best predicted by treating it as having beliefs, then for the purposes of that predictive practice it does.

Yet neither camp denies the other’s descriptive claims: the mechanists grant the predictive utility, and the stance-advocates do not assert that the model contains belief-box neurons. Is this disagreement substantive—about what conditions must actually obtain for belief—or verbal, with “belief” functioning as “mechanistically realized, defeasible cognitive commitment” for one group and “predictively stable information-bearing state” for the other? Or are they answering different questions—one about ontological constitution, one about explanatory strategy—under a single heading? Diagnose the stall using what each side actually commits to, and determine whether a hidden empirical premise is doing the work (for instance, whether future architectures could close the calibration and defeasibility gap), or whether bedrock intuitions about biological minds as the only proper subjects of psychology are driving the impasse.

My own working hypothesis is that the dispute is largely verbal, compounded by different explanatory goals, but I am genuinely uncertain whether the mechanists’ appeal to the defeasibility asymmetry identifies a missing necessary condition for belief or merely stipulates a biologically loaded criterion.
