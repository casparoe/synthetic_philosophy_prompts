The conflict has led me to reconsider the Western default of treating personhood and agency as properties anchored in individual bodies or minds. I have been drawn to the Ubuntu tradition, with its central idea that a person is constituted through reciprocal relational recognition rather than through intrinsic, self-standing features—often summarized by the proverb that one becomes a person through other people.

Please argue for the following position: if Ubuntu's relational conception of personhood is taken seriously, then in contexts of sustained collaborative cognition—such as the co-development of an open-source scientific framework or a distributed artificial intelligence system—moral and epistemic credit should not be partitioned among individual contributors according to their discrete labor inputs. You should defend not merely the ethical claim that attribution norms should change, but the metaphysical claim that individualist attribution is conceptually mistaken when the conditions for personhood and agency are genuinely distributed across a functional collective.

In developing this argument, please state how confident you are in each of its major steps—particularly in the analogy between neural ownership and collaborative attribution—and explicitly identify the point at which you are least certain. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; construct the reasoning from the conceptual resources available to you. I am especially interested in whether you think the argument forces us to abandon all individual recognition, or whether there remains a defensible role for partial attribution within an essentially relational ontology.
