The replication crisis in psychology and medicine is routinely explained as a failure of frequentist null-hypothesis significance testing: flexible analytical pipelines, undisclosed researcher degrees of freedom, and the binary interpretation of p-values combine to produce false positives that do not replicate. From this diagnosis, many methodologists conclude that Bayesian inference—with its explicit priors, continuous updating, and direct probabilistic statements about hypotheses—offers the natural correction, since it eliminates arbitrary thresholds and forces analysts to make assumptions visible. But this argument seems to presuppose that the crisis is primarily statistical rather than structural: if the root problem were truly the choice between frequentist and Bayesian frameworks, then studies using Bayesian methods should exhibit systematically higher replication rates, yet the available meta-scientific evidence does not clearly show this. Does that mean the frequentist-versus-Bayesian debate is largely a red herring, with replication failure driven instead by publication incentives, small samples, and hidden multiplicity that afflict both paradigms equally? Or is there a more subtle interaction—perhaps that Bayesian methods, through prior-selection flexibility and the opacity of model specification, introduce their own replication vulnerabilities that are harder to detect than p-hacking? If my framing wrongly collapses institutional failure into statistical methodology, please say so plainly rather than answering within it; otherwise, reconstruct what the replication crisis actually presupposes about statistical practice, and assess whether changing paradigms would address the failure or merely relocate it.
