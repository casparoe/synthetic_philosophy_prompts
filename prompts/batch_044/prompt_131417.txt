I've been thinking on my own about panpsychism — the view that consciousness is not something that suddenly switches on in brains but is a basic feature of matter, like charge or mass. I constructed an argument that seems to show this idea spreads consciousness everywhere or collapses into contradiction, but I cannot find the exit. Here are the steps as I see them.

(1) If panpsychism is true, then even the smallest physical constituents carry some minimal form of experience.
(2) In a human being, all those tiny experiences must somehow come together into one unified consciousness — the one that sees red and feels warmth — rather than remaining a crowd of separate micro-subjects.
(3) So panpsychists owe a combination rule: an account of how micro-experiences bind into a single macro-subject.
(4) The most natural rule appeals to physical integration: when parts are wired together with dense information exchange, their experiences combine.
(5) But physical integration is a continuum. A brain has high integration; a table has very little; a computer network or a weather system falls somewhere in between. Nature offers no sharp boundary.
(6) Therefore, if integration is what combines experience, either it does so at every degree — meaning tables, clouds, and temporary computer clusters all possess faint macro-consciousness — or the panpsychist must draw an arbitrary line at some specific threshold.
(7) An arbitrary threshold is unsatisfactory unless it appeals to something beyond the physical, which would defeat the purpose of adopting panpsychism as a naturalistic view.
(8) So the panpsychist must accept that many non-biological systems are conscious.
(9) Yet consider sleep or general anesthesia: the brain's integration drops sharply, but upon waking I seem to be the same subject. If integration genuinely creates the macro-subject, then lowering it should destroy me and generate new subjects upon recovery, not restore the old one.
(10) This suggests that macro-subjects are not genuinely created or destroyed by integration at all, but either persist through disintegration or were never genuinely present.
(11) Either way, the combination story collapses, and with it the motivation for saying consciousness is fundamental: if physical structure neither combines subjects nor dissolves them, then consciousness does not depend on physical arrangement in the way panpsychism requires.
(12) Therefore panpsychism either assigns consciousness to nearly everything, rendering the view absurd, or it secretly relies on a non-physical criterion, undermining its naturalistic appeal.

I want to keep (1) and (3); I still think there must be some answer to how parts make a whole. But I do not want to land at (12). I feel something slipped between (5) and (6), or perhaps between (9) and (10), though I cannot name it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I deny that integration is continuous, must I accept a mysterious jump in nature? If I deny (9), am I forced to say that deep sleep really kills me and a new person wakes up?
