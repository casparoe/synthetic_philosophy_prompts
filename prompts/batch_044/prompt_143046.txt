I've been stuck in this decision-theory reading group for like three months now, and we're circling the same drain with the Pasadena paradox — you know, the predictor game where the payoff sequence is arranged so the expected value literally doesn't converge, it just oscillates in that annoying undefined way. One camp says that if EU theory spits out undefined, that's just a boundary condition: fall back on stochastic dominance or pick the option that doesn't hang on the tail, full stop. The other camp insists you can regularize the series — Abel summation, truncation-invariance, whatever — and once you do their's a clear rational choice, so the paradox dissolves into a math problem with a definite answer. We've hashed out every formal move and nobody moves an inch.

I'm definately starting to think the disagreement isn't about the calculus at all, but about what counts as being inside the domain of rational choice in the first place: is this a physical scenario where infinite payoffs are impossible, so undefined expectation is just a sign the model is misspecified? Or is it a pure normative construct where applying a convergence norm is part of being rational, not an add-on? Or maybe it's verbal — we're using "rational" to mean consistency-with-dominance in one room and coherence-with-credences in the other, and we don't realize we're answering different questions under the same heading. I want an honest diagnosis, not a verdict: is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work here, like whether truncation-invariance is actually required for consistency? Are there bedrock intuitions about infinite risk that neither side can argue for? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress — a distinction I haven't seen, a result that shows regularization conflicts with dominance, or is this just one of those stalls where we should accept that no argument will shift anyone?
