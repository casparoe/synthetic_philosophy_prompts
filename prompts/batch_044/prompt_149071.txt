I am currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of language seminar—about fourteen students, one seventy-five-minute session—where we have been working through Wittgenstein’s account of language games and family resemblance. I want to stage an in-class exercise that lets the group experience family resemblance as a positive, structural feature of conceptual use rather than as an admission that a category is too sloppy to have an essence.

To prevent anyone from falling back on memorized examples, I want to inject a niche, invented domain: I will present ten brief vignettes describing practices I call “binding acts,” defined as social practices in which one party undertakes a commitment to another through an explicit or implicit marker, but with extreme formal variation. The vignettes should describe invented cases—such as a silent token exchange in a described ritual, a deliberately ambiguous gesture within a fictional merchant culture, a digital acknowledgment with no verbal component, a physical posture adopted only under specific lighting conditions, and others that share no single observable feature—rather than real-world institutions like contracts or promises. Working in pairs, students will have fifteen minutes to sort the ten vignettes into “genuine binding acts” and “not binding acts,” but they must defend their sorting by identifying the one feature they take to be necessary and sufficient for membership. After the pairs present, I will record each proposed essential feature on the board. In a second round, the group tests every proposed feature against all ten vignettes and discovers that each feature is absent from at least one case they want to include and present in at least one they want to exclude.

The rules and incentives should be arranged so that the pressure to save essentialism comes from the students’ own commitments, not from my framing. Please design a scoring structure—perhaps points for internal consistency between a pair’s stated feature and their sorting, with a bonus round in which pairs may revise their feature only if they can defend it against a randomly drawn counterexample—so that the frustration builds organically. Then the whole group compares lists and sees that overlap, not shared essence, holds the category together.

Please provide the full design: the exact pairing and comparison rules; a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes; the materials I need, including either the ten vignettes themselves or precise instructions for constructing them without relying on familiar real-world analogues; and the incentive structure. Include a debrief that maps each phase—the initial confidence in finding an essence, the accumulation of counterexamples, the temptation to propose ever more abstract features, and the eventual recognition that a network of overlapping similarities sustains the concept—onto Wittgenstein’s view. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong: for instance, that one pair will declare the category meaningless rather than family-resemblance-governed, or that another will propose an essential feature so abstract it becomes explanatorily useless, and advise me how to handle each failure in the moment without lecturing.

Finally, be explicit about which aspect of Wittgenstein’s broader framework—perhaps the embedding of language games in specific forms of life, or the normative dimension that meaning is use within a rule-governed practice—this activity necessarily cannot reproduce, so that I know exactly what I must supply through direct discussion rather than through the exercise itself.
