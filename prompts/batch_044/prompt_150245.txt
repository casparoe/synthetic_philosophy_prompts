I just finished a long argument with a friend about Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology in On Certainty. She insisted that our basic commitments—such as that the earth existed long before my birth—are not knowledge-claims that happen to lack justification, but grammatical rules or conditions of sense that stand outside justification altogether, so they are neither true nor false and not genuinely open to doubt. I pushed back that if they are neither justified beliefs nor rules we have chosen, they must be arbitrary dogmas, which means someone living within a "world-created-five-minutes-ago-with-false-memories" hinge-system would be equally rational, merely playing a different language-game. She replied that hinges are not hypotheses we adopt but practices we inhabit, so talk of "choosing" them is a category mistake. I am still stuck on what looks like a hidden contradiction: Wittgenstein repeatedly calls these commitments "certain," which sounds like an epistemic achievement, yet he denies they have evidential support or ordinary propositional content. So here is my question: if hinge commitments are neither justified beliefs nor selected rules, but also not reducible to mere psychological habits, what exactly distinguishes a legitimate hinge from a delusional framework? And does not Wittgenstein's claim that they are "not open to doubt" secretly presuppose the very kind of rational-constraint theory he claims to have dissolved—one that can separate sane hinges from madness without appealing to evidence?
