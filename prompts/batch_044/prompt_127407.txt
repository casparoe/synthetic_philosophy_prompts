I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for an advanced undergraduate philosophy reading group that knows Stoicism and basic Buddhist ethics but has no background in analytic axiology. I want them to grasp tranquilism, taken here not as generic calmness but as the specific axiological claim that only the absence of craving and suffering carries value, while positive mental states—including joy, aesthetic fulfillment, love, and achievement—are strictly axiologically neutral rather than merely subordinate goods. Don't explain the view to me; plan how I should teach it. First, what conceptual distinction must be secured before tranquilism can be heard as distinct from stoic apatheia and from negative utilitarianism, and why must that distinction precede the introduction of the view itself? Second, specify the single carrying comparison that should anchor the session: describe the two lives or states being compared, what properties the favorable case must possess (for instance, complete absence of craving and suffering paired with complete absence of positive affect) and what properties it must lack (so students do not read it as mere risk-aversion or hedonism in reverse), and justify why this case rather than a religious ascetic example should carry the idea. Third, map the misconceptions in the order the students will likely form them—I expect first that this is just extreme Stoicism, then that it collapses into negative utilitarianism, then that it licenses suicide or total withdrawal—and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off and with what conceptual move. Fourth, design one question I can pose near the end that would reveal whether they understand tranquilism's distinctive commitment to the axiological neutrality of positives, rather than merely its emphasis on eliminating suffering. Fifth, state openly which dimension of the view—perhaps its metaphysics of craving, its historical lineage in specific traditions, or its relationship to welfare economics—I should refuse to open up at this level and explicitly say so. The deliverable should be a dependency-ordered teaching plan with a misconception map, not an explanation of the view itself, and it should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
