A decision theorist I work with has issued clear verdicts on five scenarios involving evidential cooperation across causally disconnected worlds. In case one, she endorsed cooperation between two agents with identical decision procedures situated in separate, causally isolated bubbles of a vast multiverse, reasoning that perfect correlation makes each agent's choice evidentially decisive for the other. In case two, she rejected cooperation between an agent and a distant predictor that runs an incompatible utility function, despite the existence of predictive correlation, on the grounds that correlation without value convergence is insufficient. In case three, she affirmed cooperation between agents implemented on physically different substrates—one biological, one silicon-based—provided they execute the exact same decision algorithm, treating substrate as irrelevant. In case four, she denied cooperation between agents that share epistemic access to each other's reasoning but pursue orthogonal goals, insisting that shared understanding does not substitute for aligned aims. In case five, she supported cooperation between an agent and a hypothetical future variant of herself that diverges only by a single, non-decisive bit-flip in a peripheral cognitive module, classifying the variant as essentially herself.

Consider now a sixth case. An agent operating in a continuous action-space universe must decide whether to cooperate with a counterpart in a causally disconnected region where local physics permits only discrete actions. The two share the identical decision algorithm and value system, but because the discrete space can only approximate the continuous one, the correlation between their choices is noisy—approximately 0.85—rather than perfect. The continuous agent is fully aware of this approximation gap and knows that the discrete counterpart's optimal move will be the nearest admissible approximation rather than an exact match.

Predict her verdict, but do so by first articulating at least two distinct governing principles—perhaps centered on correlation strength, algorithmic identity, value alignment, substrate independence, or the moral relevance of approximation error—that accommodate her first five rulings with equal fidelity. For each principle, state precisely what it implies about this noisy discrete-continuous case. Then provide your best-guess prediction of her actual position, explaining which feature of her prior pattern makes one principle more natural than the other. Finally, propose a seventh case—one that avoids approximation noise entirely, for instance by holding correlation perfect while varying some other structural feature—that would sharply discriminate between the leading principle and its closest competitor.
