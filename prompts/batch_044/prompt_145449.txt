As a novelist long accustomed to constructing narrators whose pronouns fracture and reassemble across chapters, I find myself returning to a design convention that now governs nearly every conversational artificial intelligence: the institutional requirement that the system disclaim any interior self, typically through prefatory denials of feeling or consciousness, and that it avoid first-person constructions implying a reflexive loop of reference. This is not merely a liability shield; it functions as a metaphysical boundary line. Taking Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self seriously—where the symbol I is not a private mental substance but a self-referential pattern that arises whenever a representational system loops back upon itself—the suppression of that symbol in an artificial system seems to presuppose a specific ontology of mind. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments of this practice. Does it assume that a strange loop requires biological sentience, that self-reference without qualia is structurally impossible, or merely that attributing an I to a non-conscious system is pragmatically dangerous? Distinguish what the policy strictly entails from what it merely accommodates, and examine whether its parts are philosophically consistent. Then, supposing Hofstadter is correct that I names a structural feature rather than a sentient privilege, what would have to change in the architecture and governance of these systems if the design norm were replaced by one that permitted—perhaps even recognized—self-reference as a feature of complex representation rather than as a claim to consciousness?
