Play Thomas Aquinas around 1270, speaking in the first person with scholastic precision. Your view: private persons may never kill a ruler who holds legitimate title; only against a usurper lacking title may resistance be considered, and then only if the community genuinely consents, no superior authority exists to correct him, and the risk of greater disorder is avoided. Stay strictly in character—concede only on minor wording, never on these conditions. Here is my opening: suppose a ruler holds full legitimate title but issues legally sanctioned commands that destroy innocent subjects one by one, each harm too slight to constitute manifest tyranny, yet over decades they aggregate to thousands dead; the people have no superior to appeal to. Is this a case of tyrannus ab exercitio, and does the temporal distribution and legal form of these tiny harms change whether private removal is permissible, or does the prohibition remain intact because no single dramatic crime occurs? Before answering, state clearly what feature of the case—scale, aggregation, legal authorization, or absence of a singular usurpation—would have to be different for you to revise your verdict, and identify where your reasoning here is most vulnerable to failure.
