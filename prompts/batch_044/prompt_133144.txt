As a novelist drafting a philosophical exchange between two characters—one a decision theorist, the other an ethicist—I have constructed the following argument, which extends Derek Parfit's hitchhiker scenario into moral psychology. Its conclusion strikes me as intolerable, yet each premise seems defensible in isolation. I ask you to diagnose the precise failure: identify the step that should be rejected, explain why it initially appears sound, and specify what abandoning it costs in terms of either rational self-modification or moral coherence.

(1) In the hitchhiker case, rationality permits an agent to install a mechanism that makes her causally unable to act on her present preferences—paying the driver—because this self-binding is the necessary condition for securing the prior, more valuable outcome of being picked up.

(2) If rational self-binding is permissible whenever it produces the best overall result, then it should extend to domains where present inclination threatens long-term coherence—specifically, to moral commitment.

(3) A rational agent who foresees that she might violate impartiality under extreme pressure can therefore install a psychological mechanism rendering her permanently incapable of such violations, even when a particular violation would maximize aggregate welfare.

(4) Because this reasoning is structurally neutral with respect to content, the same mechanism could bind an agent to a vicious principle—say, absolute loyalty to an in-group—making her causally incapable of aiding outsiders regardless of circumstance.

(5) Thus, on Parfit's model, rational agents ought to become rigidly unresponsive moral automatons, and the model offers no internal criterion for distinguishing admirable steadfastness from culpable rigidity.

(6) Hence, either the hitchhiker argument proves too much, or rationality genuinely requires such amoral self-binding.

Which premise or inference should be surrendered? Is the error in treating moral principles as strategic commitments analogous to the hitchhiker's promise, in assuming that the rationality of binding is independent of the principle's content, or in the analogy itself? Price the alternatives: what do we lose if we reject your chosen step?
