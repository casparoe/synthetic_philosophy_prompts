Take the argument from illusion, but run it inside the framework of predictive processing rather than classical sense-data theory. (1) In hallucination the brain produces a perceptual prediction—say, of a pink elephant—that is not constrained by bottom-up input; in matching veridical perception the same hierarchical model issues an identical prediction, only now weighted by sensory precision. (2) Because subjective character is determined by the prediction's content, not by its causal origin, both cases contain a shared mental factor: the decoupled representation. (3) Hence direct realism is false; we are never directly aware of physical objects, only of our own predictive models. Now extend the reasoning: (4) the brain can generate decoupled predictions about its own states—I could be "hallucinating" my pain, my belief, or even my conscious awareness—since introspection also relies on hierarchical predictions with variable precision weighting. (5) So the argument yields global skepticism: no direct awareness of world or self. (6) The disjunctivist denies (2), insisting that veridical and hallucinatory experiences share no mental kind whatsoever; they differ not in weighting but in fundamental nature. (7) But if predictive processing describes all cognition, then perception and introspection share the same machinery in both cases, making the disjunctivist distinction look like an ad hoc dualism. So either predictive processing must be rejected, or disjunctivism collapses, and the illusion argument still proves too much. Which step should be rejected? If you keep (2), can direct realism survive in any form? If you reject (4), what principled boundary stops the slide from external perception to self-awareness?
