I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. A 62-year-old patient, now in a prolonged disorder of consciousness after an anoxic brain injury, left an advance directive fifteen years ago refusing artificial nutrition, hydration, and any life-prolonging intervention should she ever enter a state she described as "permanent absence of the kind of self-awareness that makes life valuable to me." The document contains an unusual clause: if clinical evidence ever showed that she retained a form of present-moment emotional responsiveness without autobiographical memory or future-oriented planning, the directive should be treated as a strong but rebuttable presumption rather than an absolute instruction.

She now exhibits exactly that profile—consistent affective orientation to familiar voices and aversive responses to invasive care, but no demonstrable self-reference or temporal continuity. Her spouse insists the directive applies literally and demands withdrawal of nutrition within seventy-two hours; her adult child argues that the rebuttable clause has been triggered and that her current experiential welfare should govern.

Our institution has no policy for interpreting conditional directives of this kind. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a specific time frame (for example, whether to convene an independent neuropsychological assessment panel, continue or withdraw treatment pending that evaluation, or seek judicial clarification), and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument for honoring the earlier directive—namely, that allowing present welfare to override a deliberately conditional instruction collapses the distinction between respecting autonomy and substituting our own judgment—rather than merely citing legal precedent or clinical definitions of consciousness.
