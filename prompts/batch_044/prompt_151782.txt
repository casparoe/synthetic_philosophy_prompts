My colleague runs a small forecasting collective in which members submit anonymous predictions about upcoming events, and the group produces a final forecast through a weighted aggregation algorithm that assigns influence according to each member’s past accuracy. They now face an important investment decision that hinges on whether this aggregation method can be trusted over simple averaging. The difficulty is that, for many of the past events used to calibrate the weights, there was no independent ground truth available; the group assessed accuracy by comparing individual predictions against the algorithm’s own prior outputs. My colleague argues that this is not viciously circular—he compares it to using eyesight together with a mirror to check eyesight—whereas I suspect the parallel conceals a deeper problem about how a method can justify itself without access to an external check. He is asking me whether the group can rationally claim to know that its system works, or whether its confidence is structurally bootstrapped in a way that undermines any such claim. I want to help him see precisely what kind of epistemic failure, if any, is involved here, and what conditions—if any—would allow this form of self-validation to yield genuine knowledge rather than empty circularity. What is actually at stake in this structure of justification?
