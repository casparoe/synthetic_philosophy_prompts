I'm an editor at a small regional history quarterly and we keep recieving submissions that mix solid archival work with heavy narrative framing -- the kind where the author picks a shape first and fits the documents to it. I need a reusable decision procdure I can run in about fifteen minutes per manuscript, grounded in the philosophy of history debates about objectivity: Ranke's aim of showing things as they actually happened versus Carr and Hayden White's scepticism that every narrative imposes its own structure.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions I ask in order. For each one say what counts as passing, what counts as failing, where failure means reject, and where it just means I have to make a judgment call about values rather than facts. It needs to work when I haven't read the primary sources, and it should catch framing that is implicit rather than announced. Include the points where it will definitely misfire.

Then apply it to three hypothetical submissions:

1. An article on the 1848 revolutions arguing they collapsed mainly because telegraph networks broke down, not because of ideological disagreement. The author cites some telegram logs but never addresses the well-known factional splits.
2. A microhistory of one factory worker in 1923 that describes her inner resistance to management in her own voice, with no diary, letter, or interview to back up those mental states.
3. A local founding history that sticks strictly to land deeds and census rolls, framing settlement as orderly and legalistic, but it leaves out entirely the documented indigenous displacement that our magazine covered in a previous issue. I want this one to come out acceptable, but I need the procedure to flag it anyway.

Please organize like this:

- The procedure (around 350 words)
- Where judgment replaces the checklist (around 100 words)
- Case 1 (around 120 words)
- Case 2 (around 120 words)
- Case 3 and the unwanted flag (around 150 words)
- What the procedure flat-out cannot do (around 80 words)
