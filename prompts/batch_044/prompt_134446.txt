For a graduate seminar on collective memory and political myth, construct a taxonomy—presented as a decision tree—of philosophical positions on the legitimacy of commemorating a “lost cause.” The guiding question is: Under what conditions, if any, can a collective treat a historical defeat as a legitimate, identity-forming narrative rather than as a pernicious or obsolete myth?

Build the tree from logical space, not from a list of school names. Begin with concrete diagnostic judgments about four specific cases: the post–Civil War American “Lost Cause” narrative; the Masada siege narrative in Zionist and modern Israeli identity; the Kosovo Field (1389) narrative in Serbian national consciousness; and the 1940 Dunkirk evacuation in British collective memory. Each internal node should ask a single yes/no or choose-one question about one of these cases or about a general principle that the cases illustrate—e.g., whether commemorating military valor can be separated from the moral content of the cause; whether the defeat was total annihilation versus strategic preservation; whether the myth’s present social function matters more than its historical accuracy; whether the community currently faces an analogous threat. Phrase every node so that a respondent can answer it without knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

The tree should have exactly eight leaves. At least four must map onto recognizable positions: (1) a moral-exclusionist view that wrong causes forfeit commemorative standing regardless of narrative utility; (2) a civic-republican separation view that valor, sacrifice, and institutional loyalty can be extracted from an unjust cause; (3) a communitarian-functional view that a myth is legitimate if it presently sustains solidarity and is not actively harmful; (4) a critical-historicist view that legitimacy requires substantial historical accuracy and that myth-making is only permissible when it does not suppress documented injustice. The remaining four leaves should be unnamed combinations of answers; for each, diagnose in one sentence whether it is likely incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For every unoccupied branch—every combination of answers that yields no known defender—state briefly why the space is empty (logical contradiction, moral repugnance, or lack of historical advocate). Also flag any pair of named leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining which shared commitments cause the overlap. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or specific page references. Keep all references to the four cases at the level of common historical knowledge, and do not invoke obscure primary texts or highly specialized theoretical constructs that a well-read but non-specialist model could not be expected to hold in working memory. Fix the audience as advanced undergraduates or graduate students in philosophy or political theory, and present the result as a flowchart with branch questions, path labels, and a one-sentence description of each leaf’s core commitment.
