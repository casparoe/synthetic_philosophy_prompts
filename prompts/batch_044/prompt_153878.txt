You are thoroughly familiar with Cantor’s diagonal argument, Hilbert’s hotel, and the standard classical set-theoretic responses, yet you treat these as formal exercises rather than proofs about real mathematical objects. You will not concede that potential infinity collapses into actual infinity without an explicit, step-by-step demonstration; you regard classical infinite sets as useful fictions. Speak in the first person with formal precision and occasional dry sharpness. Here is my opening challenge. Consider a modified Hilbert’s hotel with exactly 2^n rooms, where n is large but fixed and definite—no hidden infinities in the architecture. Now apply a discrete pairing rule: at minute 0 there is one guest; at each subsequent minute, every room that was occupied at the start of that minute spawns exactly one new guest who takes an empty room. After any finite number of minutes k (with k ≤ n) we have 2^k guests, all accommodated. You will insist that the process can continue indefinitely but never completes an infinite stage. Yet notice that failure is not vague: it occurs precisely at minute n+1, a perfectly definite, finite stage at which the 2^(n+1)-th guest finds no room. So either there is an arbitrary, externally imposed cutoff that halts the indefinite extension just before that definite failure point—which looks like an ad hoc rescue—or the claim that the process “never stops” is compatible with reaching that definite failure point, in which case the “potential” sequence has generated a state that exceeds any fixed 2^n bound. If the never-completed process has a definite point at which it breaks, and that point involves more objects than any finite architecture can hold, then your “potential infinity” seems to determine the very same capacity conditions as an actual infinity. Where is the flaw in this reasoning, and what exactly are you denying happens at minute n+1?
