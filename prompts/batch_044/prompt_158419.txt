In debates over religious arguments in democratic politics, a frequently invoked analogy treats Rawlsian public reason as a translation protocol for political speech: a religious comprehensive doctrine is like a film spoken in a language only its adherents understand, and the duty of civility requires providing secular subtitles—publicly accessible reasons—before the argument can be projected onto the legislative screen. Proponents deploy this to claim that religious citizens are not censored but accommodated, just as foreign films are shown rather than banned; critics reply that the analogy masks an asymmetry, since secular doctrines rarely seem to need subtitles, and that translation may distort the original message beyond recognition. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy's reach. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the original dialogue, the subtitle writer, the theater audience, the projection apparatus, and the censorship that the framing is meant to distinguish itself from? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the normative conclusion from those that do not: is there actually a neutral target language of political liberalism, or does every translation embed substantive commitments? Does the analogy quietly import the idea of reversible encoding—that the religious speaker can recover the original meaning intact after public translation—or that there exists an objective standard of correct subtitle, neither of which clearly applies to democratic deliberation? Assess whether the defender's conclusion—that religious participation survives fully intact—depends on these imported features, and whether it collapses once they are removed. Finally, if there is a more accurate analogy—perhaps not translation but the distinction between private diary entries and testimony given under oath, or between speaking a minority language in a genuinely bilingual assembly versus being required to speak only the majority language—present it and say whether it strengthens the critic's case, the defender's, or dissolves the framing altogether.
