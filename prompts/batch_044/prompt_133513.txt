At a student-government forum about whether to disinvite a speaker accused of causing “psychological harm” to listeners, a committee member ended discussion with the line “free speech ends where harm begins.” I suspect that sentence is doing a lot of hidden work, but I cannot tell which version of it they actually meant. Could you disambiguate the slogan into a menu of precise claims—starting with the near-tautological (speech that literally injures someone physically) and running through intermediate readings (offensive speech that causes documented mental-health damage; speech that creates a hostile environment; speech that undermines equal dignity regardless of listener reaction) up to the maximally expansive (any speech that produces discomfort, disagreement, or perceived disrespect)—and for each say: (a) what conception of “harm” it uses and whose harm counts, (b) whether it is consistent with Mill’s harm principle as usually interpreted, (c) whether it is a platitude, defensible but substantive, false, or self-undermining, and (d) whether it actually supports disinviting a speaker in advance rather than addressing specific unlawful acts or direct incitement? I do not want a history of free-speech law or a bibliography; I want the precisification map so that, at the next meeting, I can ask which entry on the menu they are defending and whether that entry genuinely gets them the conclusion they drew.
