I am preparing a draft essay for my graduate seminar on normative self-reference, and I would be grateful for your critical comments on its argumentative architecture and its central conceptual innovation. The essay begins with Peter Suber's well-known game of Nomic, treating it not as a mere curiosity about rule-change but as a formal template for constitutional orders that contain explicit provisions for their own amendment. From there, I introduce a variant I call Nomic-II, in which amendment authority is not vested in a single hierarchical meta-rule but distributed across a network of local procedural rules, each capable of altering only its immediate neighbors, with global coherence maintained by a constraint modeled on non-well-founded membership relations in set theory rather than on any supreme interpretive body. The essay then examines whether the classic paradox of self-amendment—if a rule R authorizes its own repeal, then R either persists and nullifies the repeal, or is repealed and nullifies the authorization—dissolves under this distributed architecture, or whether it is merely relocated into the coherence conditions themselves. I contend that the paradox genuinely dissolves only if we abandon the synoptic assumption that every valid amendment must trace to a single originating authorization, though I acknowledge that this abandonment transforms constitutional identity from a synchronic property into a diachronic, path-dependent one.

Before I commit to this line, I want your assessment of three specific points. First, does the shift from centralized to distributed authority actually resolve the logical tension, or does it merely disguise a regress behind institutional complexity? Second, can the coherence constraint borrowed from anti-foundation axioms be rendered normatively intelligible—distinguishing institutional stability from mere descriptive regularity—without collapsing into a sociological observation? Third, does my framing of constitutional identity as diachronic risk reducing to familiar arguments from living constitutionalism, and if so, is there a genuinely distinct philosophical contribution here, or have I inadvertently redescribed institutional pluralism in formal dress? I am particularly interested in whether the essay's claim—that self-amendment is paradoxical only under a monolithic conception of legal authority—strikes you as philosophically substantive rather than terminologically convenient, and whether any hidden premises about normative authority need to be made explicit before the argument can stand.
