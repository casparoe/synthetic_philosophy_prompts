I'm currently revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of science, and I find myself returning to a tension that I think resists easy resolution. In our recent faculty discussions, colleagues kept treating the renormalization-group revolution of the late 1960s and 1970s—Kadanoff's block-spin transformation, Wilson's derivation of critical exponents, and the subsequent discovery that radically different microscopic substrates share identical macroscopic critical behavior within universality classes—as an unambiguous victory for reductionism. I want to push back on that consensus, but rigorously, so I need a careful conceptual mapping rather than a polemic.

Please analyze this historical episode through the lens of the reduction-emergence debate, with particular attention to the problem of universality. Identify precisely what the levels are in this case (the specific Hamiltonians versus the macro-critical exponents), what counts as explanatory success, and whether Wilson's ability to derive macroscopic scaling laws from statistical mechanics genuinely vindicates reductionism or whether the substrate-independence of those laws—what physicists call universality—points instead to weak emergence, or perhaps to a stronger autonomy of the macro-level. Then run the analysis in reverse: ask what the episode reveals about the philosophical categories themselves. Does the emergentist have any account of why Wilson's method succeeded at all, or does that success simply dissolve the emergence claim? Conversely, does reductionism have the conceptual resources to explain why the same macro-law governs magnets, fluids, and superconductors without implicitly treating the macro-regularity as causally autonomous? I want a disciplined case analysis—who plays which conceptual role, what the explanatory options and payoffs look like, and where the theory is genuinely silent rather than forced to speak—and I am explicitly not asking for a bibliography or literature survey.
