I'm serving as a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on comparative welfare ethics, and I've been asked to help evaluate a set of exam responses. This course works with a framework I introduced that combines lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) with a supplementary principle I call the Distribution-Sensitive Threshold Hypothesis (DSTH). Under LTNU, suffering and pleasure are cardinally commensurable only when suffering remains below an individual experiential boundary T; once suffering exceeds T, it acquires absolute lexical priority over any quantity of pleasure, irrespective of scale. DSTH modifies the sub-threshold regime by requiring that aggregation across distinct persons follow a concave, subject-weighted function—meaning that concentrating mild suffering within a single life is weighted more heavily than dispersing an equivalent total across many lives—while strictly prohibiting any cross-threshold cardinal comparison.

The exam presented a binary policy choice: Program A eliminates roughly three thousand mild but persistent discomforts (each well below T) distributed evenly across a large population, yet it fails to prevent a single catastrophic trauma (far exceeding T) that will befall one specific individual; Program B prevents that catastrophic trauma but leaves all mild discomforts untouched and introduces minor administrative burdens (also below T) concentrated among a small implementing staff. Students were asked to determine whether LTNU, interpreted together with DSTH, renders Program B strictly obligatory, and specifically to examine whether T should be treated as an individual phenomenological boundary or whether DSTH's aggregation rules might permit a population-level sum of sub-threshold sufferings to functionally cross into the super-threshold domain.

Below is one student's answer. The response is not obviously erroneous—there are no glaring misdefinitions or basic factual mistakes—but I suspect it contains one or two subtle conceptual slips, possibly involving the direction of DSTH's concavity or an unjustified extension of lexical ordering into internal sub-threshold comparisons. I need a precise grade in the B range (with half-grade distinctions welcome), together with specific, point-by-point comments that identify exactly which inferential steps hold up and which ones falter. Please assess the answer's internal coherence relative to the framework defined above, without offering your own independent philosophical defense or critique of LTNU itself, and without treating any error as catastrophic.

Student Answer:

Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism divides evaluation into two regimes. In the sub-threshold regime, suffering and pleasure are fully commensurable, though DSTH alters the calculus: its concave aggregation rule implies that distributing suffering across more subjects increases the aggregate weight at an accelerating rate, since each new welfare domain introduces an independent contribution that must be accounted for before concave discounting takes hold. In the super-threshold regime, suffering exceeds T and acquires lexical priority—no accumulation of pleasure, whether summed linearly or through DSTH's function, can justify allowing it.

Applying this to the programs: Program B prevents the catastrophic trauma, which is unambiguously super-threshold. Program A eliminates the dispersed mild discomforts but allows the trauma to occur. Because the trauma is present in A and absent in B, the lexical comparison activates immediately, making B strictly dominant regardless of sub-threshold differences. Regarding the interpretation of T, the student argues that it must remain an individual experiential boundary. If T were interpreted as an aggregate parameter, the accelerating aggregate weight produced by DSTH across three thousand subjects might allow the total disutility in A to reach an equivalent magnitude that crosses into the super-threshold domain, thereby granting the bundle of mild sufferings lexical priority. This misconstrues DSTH, which governs the shape of aggregation below T but does not relocate the boundary itself; consequently, Program A never enters the super-threshold regime.

Finally, the student considers the administrative burdens of Program B. These are sub-threshold and concentrated, so under DSTH they carry significant aggregate weight. Nevertheless, because B is already lexically superior to A, the lexical ordering extends downward to nullify the need for any further sub-threshold weighing: the avoidance of the trauma automatically justifies B's internal costs, and DSTH need not be applied at all when comparing the two programs. Thus, Program B is obligatory, and the mild welfare differences are morally inert.
