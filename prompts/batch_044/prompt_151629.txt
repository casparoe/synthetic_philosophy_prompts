Consider a counterfactual in which Edward Gibbon, retaining the historiographical commitments of his History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire but granted access to modern paleoclimatology, ancient DNA analysis of the Justinianic Plague’s Yersinia pestis strains, and settlement-pattern archaeology, is asked to evaluate the current scholarly consensus that the Western Empire did not experience a singular “fall” but rather a rapid, exogenously driven reconfiguration of socio-political complexity between roughly 536 and 640 CE—the convergence of the Late Antique Little Ice Age with sustained pandemic mortality and fiscal contraction. Would Gibbon maintain that internal moral decay—his account of luxury, superstition, and the erosion of civic virtue—remains the primary explanatory variable, or would he concede that these climatic and biological disruptions functioned not merely as accelerants of decline but as necessary conditions without which his internalist narrative collapses into a post hoc description? More specifically, ask him to adjudicate between two competing modern framings: first, a “systems-collapse” interpretation that treats imperial dissolution as a rapid phase transition in logistical and tax-network connectivity (sometimes analogized to a Seneca-style rapid collapse applied to Roman state infrastructure), and second, the “transformation rather than fall” thesis that emphasizes institutional continuity, regional adaptation, and the persistence of Roman legal and cultural frameworks well into the early medieval period. In his response, he should address whether his original moral psychology can be reconciled with a multi-causal model in which exogenous environmental shocks and endogenous institutional fragility operate as jointly sufficient conditions, or whether he would reject such reconciliation as a category error that confuses proximate environmental triggers with the ultimate historical cause he originally identified. He should also consider whether the archaeological evidence for continued urban life and trade in parts of Gaul, Italy, and the Eastern Mediterranean undermines his narrative of universal civilizational darkness, and if so, whether he would restrict his thesis of decline to specific administrative and military structures rather than to Roman civilization as a whole.
