A colleague laid out this case for evidential cooperation in large worlds, and now it strikes me as proving too much. (1) Evidential decision theory recommends the act that provides the best evidence about outcomes you care about. (2) In a sufficiently large universe, your decision procedure correlates with those of distant copies facing identical prisoner's dilemmas, so cooperating gives you good news that they cooperate too. (3) If correlation alone justifies cooperation, then it justifies it regardless of your posterior beliefs about those specific copies, including after you have observed them defecting. (4) But then evidential reasoning becomes insensitive to conditioning: you should cooperate even with direct observational evidence that your copies have defected, which collapses into an unconditioned policy like updateless decision theory and abandons Bayesian updating. (5) So either evidential cooperation applies only when you lack such observations, which requires an ad hoc boundary, or it applies universally and licenses obviously futile cooperation. (6) Yet no principled restriction on correlation reasoning can distinguish these cases without begging the question. (7) Therefore the argument either proves that cooperation is rational even against direct counterevidence, which is absurd, or it relies on a hidden exception that undermines its own foundation. I would prefer to preserve (2), but the slide between (3) and (4) feels suspicious. Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost the evidential cooperationist?
