After a long, circular argument with a friend last night about whether populism is genuinely democratic or merely demagogic, I realized we were both assuming that “the will of the people” is something that can be discovered rather than constructed. I want to stage that exact confusion in a workshop so that participants feel the conceptual tension in their own negotiation rather than hearing it summarized. For an upper-level political philosophy workshop—fourteen participants, seventy-five minutes, one session—design a structured role-play with rules, roles, and a token-based payoff scheme that forces the group to experience the conflict between treating popular will as an inventory of pre-existing individual preferences and treating it as an artifact produced by the very aggregation mechanism that claims to discover it. Call the exercise “The Constituent Ledger.” Provide the facilitator with materials that can be printed on standard paper: fourteen Constituency Cards (each listing a fictional constituency’s ranked preferences over four policy options on three separate issues, with contradictions engineered so that no option holds a stable pairwise majority across cards); three Aggregation Mechanism Cards specifying not only counting rules but also built-in “exclusion protocols” (for example, a Borda-style mechanism that permits the group to vote one constituency’s preference out of the tally as “externally influenced,” and a deliberative-unanimity mechanism that allows any single blocker to claim they represent a “silent majority” outside the room); one rotating “Voice of the People” badge; and individual token sheets. The session should run in four phases without any up-front lecture naming populism or collective choice theory. Phase one (fifteen minutes): each participant receives one Constituency Card and must advocate for its top preference without revealing the full ranking, while the group attempts to compile a visible “inventory” of demands on a board; the facilitator should quietly note that different pairwise comparisons yield different winners, but should not name the paradox. Phase two (fifteen minutes): the group must select both an aggregation mechanism and a final policy for Issue A by voting on the mechanism first and then applying it; the mechanism cards should make the procedural choice consequential, not cosmetic. Phase three (fifteen minutes): the participant wearing the Voice badge—who was excluded from the Phase Two vote—delivers a two-minute interpretation declaring which included preferences represent the “authentic will” and which excluded ones were “distortions”; the group then votes to ratify or reject this interpretation, with strict time enforcement. Phase four (twenty minutes): the group repeats the process for Issue B, but now each participant also holds a hidden “Legitimacy Token” that can override the mechanism once, provided the override is framed as “restoring the true will against procedural corruption.” The payoff scheme should use small, non-monetary stakes: each participant starts with five legitimacy points; they gain two if their constituency’s preference is the final outcome and is labeled authentic by the Voice, gain one if it is the outcome but labeled inauthentic (victory without recognition), and lose two if it is excluded and labeled inauthentic; the Voice gains four points if ratified and loses three if rejected; the group collectively loses one point for every minute Phase Three exceeds its limit. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that controlling the narrative of authenticity is strategically more valuable than winning the vote, and that any singular “will” requires suppressing the very heterogeneity that Phase One made visible. After the exercise, provide a debrief that maps Phase One onto the myth of a pre-political people, Phase Two onto mechanism-dependence, Phase Three onto the performative nature of representation (the Voice does not describe the will but purifies it), and Phase Four onto the populist maneuver of declaring procedural corruption to justify override. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run: the Voice may dominate the room, participants may fixate on token optimization and forget the conceptual stakes, and some groups may resolve the mechanism choice too quickly; advise the facilitator to treat tokens as proxies rather than goals, to use a visible timer, and to intervene only to enforce the exclusion protocols, never to explain them. Finally, include an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot generate the historical, affective, or media-driven construction of “the people” as a social identity over time, so the facilitator must supply the point that mechanism-dependence is only half the story—the other half is that the category “the people” is itself historically contingent—and must avoid citing specific theorists or texts by name, instead letting the structural experience carry the argument.
