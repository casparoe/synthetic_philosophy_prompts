In my interdisciplinary reading group, a fellow doctoral student summarized Frankfurt’s attack on the principle of alternative possibilities roughly as follows: Frankfurt demonstrates that an agent can be morally responsible even when she could not have done otherwise, using the Black-Jones thought experiment in which an intervener stands ready to compel the decision but never actually does so because the agent acts on her own. The student then claims that Frankfurt’s “prior sign”—the intervener’s cue to step in—maps neatly onto recent hierarchical predictive-processing accounts of motor control, in which prefrontal “shadow” simulations of alternative actions are automatically suppressed before conscious awareness. From this, he concludes that the brain never genuinely entertains alternatives at the point of decision, so the principle of alternative possibilities was never a psychologically realistic condition for responsibility and Frankfurt cases merely make this explicit. I am unsure whether this synthesis is accurate. Is it a fair characterization of Frankfurt’s argument? Please distinguish (i) what correctly captures the structure of the hierarchical mesh model and the role of the prior sign, (ii) where the summary conflates Frankfurt’s own view with the “flicker of freedom” objection and with empirical claims about motor planning, and (iii) whether the predictive-processing gloss genuinely supports the stronger conclusion that PAP is psychologically empty, or whether it imports commitments—such as a deterministic interpretation of suppression—that Frankfurt’s conceptual argument does not require.
