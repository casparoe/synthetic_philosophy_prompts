I'm preparing my position for a workshop on the ontology of artifacts, and I want to check whether the following commitments are jointly tenable. Here is my working profile:

(a) At any time when both exist, the statue and the clay lump are not two numerically distinct objects sitting in the same place; they are "one thing without being strictly identical," in the sense that they share every material part and do not form a layered or composed whole, yet I resist reducing the relation to bare Leibnizian identity because their sortal descriptions genuinely differ.

(b) Despite that, they have different persistence conditions: the clay can survive being flattened, melted, or divided into pieces, whereas the statue essentially cannot survive the loss of its specific artistic form.

(c) I reject four-dimensionalism and temporal-parts theories; I think both the clay and the statue (if there is one) endure through time as three-dimensional entities that can gain and lose parts.

(d) The relation between them is best described as asymmetrical material constitution—the arranged clay realizes or makes present the statue, and the statue depends on the clay, not conversely—but I deny that this requires a layered ontology in which artifacts are literally composed of matter-level entities as distinct mereological sums.

(e) I hold a principle of maximal material coincidence: if two putative objects share all of their parts at a given time, then either one is a proper part of the other, or they are the same object; they cannot be co-located but numerically distinct wholes.

(f) A statue's existence and its conditions of persistence depend partly on collective artistic recognition: if all relevant art-historical and curatorial practices vanished, the object would revert to being a merely shaped lump, even though its physical form and material parts had not changed.

(g) I treat "statue" and "lump of clay" as analogous to "child" and "adult"—phase-sortals that apply to a single enduring substance—so that asking which one is "really there" is like asking whether a thirty-year-old is really a child or an adult.

Please audit this set. Also identify at least one pair that looks like it should conflict but in fact dissolves on careful inspection, explaining exactly why. Then tell me which single change—dropping or significantly revising one commitment—would clean up the most tension overall, and what I would have to give up in exchange.
