I run a postgraduate philosophy seminar — about 14 students, 75 minutes, they've done the standard just-war reading but nothing too specialised. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel why the moral equality of combatants starts to wobble once you inject what I'm calling 'epistemic partitioning': the idea that a soldier's liability to be harmed shouldn't depend just on whether her cause is just, but on what she can reasonably be expected to know about her command's reliability, and whether that knowledge is shared across enemy lines. Design a role-play or structured deliberation with real but small stakes — point allocations, forced choices with token costs, something physical — so they run into the tension themselves before anyone names it.

Split them into four 'command cells' of 3-4 people. Each cell recieves a hidden profile card: just or unjust cause, plus high or low epistemic reliability of leadership (i.e. do they have good reason to trust what command tells them?). They face three targeting scenarios involving enemy soldiers only — direct strike, issue warning/shield, or stand-down. Within each cell they vote and allocate points representing operational success and moral risk. The rules should reward internal consistency but penalise cross-cell agreement unless they justify it on grounds other than epistemic position. Crucially, they then have to negotiate a unified 'rules of engagement' treaty across all four cells, with each cell having veto power over provisions that apply to them.

Then a debrief: map each voting round onto parts of the equality thesis, say what will predictably go wrong the first time it runs — probably the unjust-high-reliability cell dominates the negotiation — and tell me how to handle that without shutting down the exercise. Be frank about which feature of moral equality the activity can't reproduce — I think it's the abstract normative claim that liability is cause-independent regardless of belief-state — and explain how I should supply that in the discussion afterwards. Keep it runnable with just paper, pens, and a timer. No lecture up front, don't name-check specific philosophers during the rules, and they shouldn't hear the phrase 'moral equality of combatants' until after the treaty round.
