I am writing after retiring from a long career in observational astrophysics and, over the past two years, auditing graduate seminars in the philosophy of science at a nearby university. In my research I constantly moved between observational data and theoretical models, yet I never examined the logical architecture of that movement with any precision. Recently, a fellow auditor—an undergraduate with a background in economics—offered me an elaborate summary of Inference to the Best Explanation (IBE), which she said she had synthesized from lecture notes and a widely used introductory text. She presented it with considerable confidence, and I find myself uncertain how much of it to accept.

According to her account, IBE is not merely a heuristic but a deductively valid rule: among competing hypotheses, one calculates a composite explanatory score across three dimensions—predictive accuracy, structural coherence, and ontological economy—and if one hypothesis exceeds all rivals in every dimension, then belief in that hypothesis is logically compelled. She further maintained that this framework dissolves the problem of empirical underdetermination, because ontological economy always operates as an automatic tie-breaker; therefore, two theories that make identical predictions can never genuinely tie for best explanation. To formalize this, she introduced what she termed a “tri-level model of explanatory depth,” which holds that any inference to the best explanation must simultaneously satisfy conditions at the phenomenological, mechanistic, and unificatory levels, and that the absence of any one level causes the entire inference to collapse. From these premises she concluded that IBE constitutes the complete logic of scientific discovery, rendering purely statistical or probabilistic methods essentially redundant.

I have the strong intuition that several of these claims are overstated—particularly the assertion of deductive validity and the dismissal of statistical reasoning—but I lack the philosophical vocabulary to identify precisely where the distortions lie. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Please distinguish, point by point, what aligns with the mainstream philosophical treatment of IBE, what represents a defensible but simplified gloss, and what is a subtle misrepresentation—especially regarding the logical status of explanatory virtues, the actual handling of underdetermination, and whether that tri-level model belongs to the standard framework or is an external imposition. I would also like to know whether her concluding claim about the redundancy of statistical inference follows from the accurate portions of her account, or whether it depends on one of the errors you identify.
