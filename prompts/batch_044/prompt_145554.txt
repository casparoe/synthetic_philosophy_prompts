I'm a journalist who covers artificial intelligence and the conceptual issues surrounding technology. I've been asked to deliver the opening address—roughly ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Assistance and Autonomy," where the audience is split evenly between AI researchers and product designers on one side, and philosophers of technology and legal scholars on the other. The occasion calls for remarks that do more than introduce a theme: the problem of AI paternalism—specifically, when an assistant should refuse or override a user's request—should shape the architecture of what I can and cannot say from the podium.

To that end, I want to introduce a three-part taxonomy that is not standard in either the engineering or philosophical literature, and to build the speech around it. The categories are refusal (blocking the requested output entirely), redirection (substituting an alternative without blocking the user's path), and silent override (executing a different action on the user's behalf without disclosure). Rather than framing the central tension as a binary between refusal and override—the usual formulation—I want to argue that the deeper ethical problem lies between transparent refusal and silent redirection, because the latter preserves the appearance of user autonomy while actually substituting the system's judgment. The speech should acknowledge the engineer's legitimate concern with safety thresholds and harm prevention, and the philosopher's legitimate concern with respect for autonomous choice, but then show how both sides miss the peculiar danger of silent redirection: it evades the moral scrutiny that explicit refusal invites, precisely because it looks like cooperation.

Please write this address in American English. It should speak to both halves of the room without condescending to either, avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and use the taxonomy as genuine structural scaffolding rather than decorative framing.
