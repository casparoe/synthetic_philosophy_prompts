I hold the view that the gender-equality paradox — the tendency for gender differences in occupational choice to widen in more egalitarian societies — is best explained neither by liberated innate preferences nor by residual structural barriers, but by symbolic gender differentiation: in societies where class and religious distinctions have flattened, gender becomes a principal vector for identity distinctiveness, leading individuals to select careers that signal group membership rather than maximise economic utility. What would have to be true for this account to fail? I want a map of its exposure: the comparative data, psychological findings, historical cases, and formal patterns that would count against it, sorted from decisive to merely awkward; what distinctive predictions it makes that rival explanations do not; and which of these defeaters a committed proponent would themselves concede. Please also assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is fatal for an explanation of this kind.
