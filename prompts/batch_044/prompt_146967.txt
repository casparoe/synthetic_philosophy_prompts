I hold a version of the dirty-hands thesis that departs from the standard framing: not merely that political necessity sometimes forces moral agents to do wrong, but that the unavoidable moral residue from such acts is structurally productive in democratic governance. Specifically, I claim that the persistent affective burden—guilt, moral unease, or a sense of taint—experienced by officials who authorize necessary but wrongful actions functions as an irreplaceable epistemic brake against bureaucratic rationalization; that attempts to eliminate this residue through full procedural transparency, algorithmic decision rules, or maximally distributed responsibility actually increase systemic moral risk by removing the signal that keeps powerholders viscerally aware of the gravity of their choices; and that a well-functioning polity should therefore preserve, rather than design away, conditions in which decision-makers personally bear the weight of unavoidable wrong. What would have to be true for this to be false? I want the evidence and arguments that should genuinely move me, sorted by how decisive they would be: empirical cases where moral residue produced decision paralysis or corruption rather than prudence; conceptual arguments that affective burden is an unreliable indicator of moral seriousness or that it can be replaced by structural checks without loss; institutional designs that achieve the same epistemic function without concentrating personal moral cost; formal results in collective decision theory that undermine the claim that distributing responsibility removes necessary safeguards; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by treating any apparently successful transparent system as merely displacing the residue onto invisible actors. For each category, say whether defenders of this structural-productivity claim would themselves concede it as damaging or could absorb it with minor adjustments, and distinguish evidence that would be fatal from evidence that would merely be awkward. I am especially interested in whether rival accounts—say, that moral residue is purely pathological and should be minimized—explain the same institutional phenomena without positing functional value, and what specific observation or design outcome would force a choice between them. Finally, assess honestly whether the position risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated by qualifications in a way that is either a strength or a defect for a thesis of this kind.
