I am a high school senior writing an independent paper for my philosophy seminar on the conceivability argument for philosophical zombies. Rather than rehearsing the standard debate about whether conceivability entails metaphysical possibility, I want to try a more specific line: I argue that Chalmers-style zombie conceivability actually presupposes the very phenomenal properties it tries to subtract. To imagine a being physically and functionally identical to me but lacking consciousness, I must first deploy my own phenomenal concepts—what it is like to see red, to feel pain—to specify exactly which quality is missing from the duplicate. But if phenomenal concepts are genuinely reference-dependent in this way, then the "zombie" is not a fully coherent absence; it is defined negatively against a phenomenal backdrop I have already smuggled in. So the argument does not show that physical facts do not entail phenomenal facts; it only shows that I cannot bracket my own consciousness without using it as the frame of reference.

My essay develops this in three parts: first, a reconstruction of the conceivability argument; second, my "reference-dependence" objection, which I contrast with the more familiar "phenomenal concept strategy" but distinguish by focusing on the act of conception rather than on concept acquisition; and third, a response to the obvious counter that I could describe the zombie purely in physical-functional terms and then notice the absence. I worry my reply there is too quick, and that I have not adequately addressed whether a third-person scientific description could do the work without phenomenal reference.

Could you please comment on the logical coherence of the objection, on whether it genuinely differs from existing phenomenal-concept replies, and on whether my reply to the functional-description counter succeeds or collapses into a claim about my own cognitive limits rather than about metaphysical possibility? In your assessment, please say explicitly what consideration or argument would change your mind—whether a refined version of the functional-description reply, an empirical point about concept formation, or a logical flaw in the reference-dependence premise—and flag the part of your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
