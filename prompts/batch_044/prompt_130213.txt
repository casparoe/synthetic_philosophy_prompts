ok so i've been stuck on sen's liberal paradox for a bit and the debate around it feels like it's been spinning its wheels for decades. on one side you have people who treat it as a knockout result: if you grant anyone even a minimal personal domain — like deciding whether they read a specific book — then you can't have a social choice rule that respects both that domain and pareto efficiency. on the other side people say that's only fatal if you assume preferences are fixed and exogenous, and that once you allow preference transformation or procedural rights the tension dissolves because pareto just shouldn't apply across rights-boundaries. but in every reading group i've been in the two camps just restate these positions with the same prudish/lewd example and nobody moves. my hunch is that part of the stall is verbal: both sides use 'domain' but one means a sovereignty sphere and the other means a weighting parameter in an aggregation function. but there's also this formal thing i keep wondering about — what happens if you let people vote on who holds the decision rights in the first place? i sketched a recusive delegation model where individuals assign meta-preferences over authority allocation, and the contradiction doesn't vanish; it just occures at the second order. so is the disagreement really about whether rights are constraints on profiles versus weights, or is it bedrock intuition about veto power? and more concretely, if the paradox survives recusive delegation, does that mean any representation theorem for liberal rights has to allow infinite regress, or does it show the question is ill-posed? i want a diagnosis of what's actually blocking progress — hidden formal question, semantic slip, or unarguable intuition — and what kind of result or distinction would actually shift anyone.
