I am a PhD student working between metaphysics and philosophy of art, and I have become convinced that the material-constitution debate—the statue and the clay—has reached a peculiar kind of stasis. For decades the same three positions have circulated: the pluralist insists that the statue and the lump are numerically distinct but spatiotemporally coincident objects with different modal and causal profiles; the monist treats the statue as constituted by, but not identical to, the clay, allowing for temporary coincidence; and the eliminativist denies that there is any object over and above the arranged particles. Even after the introduction of formal mereology, artifactual essentialism, and various persistence conditions, the arguments in recent literature feel like well-rehearsed choreography: each side produces the same puzzle cases—squashing, gradual replacement, fission—and each side answers with the same distinction. In my own reading group, which includes both metaphysicians and conservators, the disagreement has not moved in two years despite shared agreement on the physical facts.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a failure of ingenuity but a structural misalignment: the debate is being treated as a single metaphysical question about identity and persistence, when in fact it conflates at least three different inquiries. One is a semantic or explanatory question about what our artifact-terms track—functional role, intended form, or continuous material substrate. Another is an aesthetic-practical question about what museum conservators are preserving when they restore a sculpture, which seems to depend on art-historical continuity rather than on strict material sameness. A third is a metaphysical question about whether there can be co-located objects with different essential properties, which may depend on prior commitments about ontological parsimony rather than on the statue case itself. I also wonder whether recent practices—digital 3D scanning that produces functional twins without material continuity, and conservation ethics that authorize replacing substantial portions of original material—have quietly dissolved the presupposition that artistic identity requires uninterrupted substrate, without anyone in the metaphysical literature acknowledging that the ground has shifted.

So I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication. Using what each side actually says about modal differences, causal powers, and constitution relations, is this a substantive disagreement about the world, a verbal disagreement about the word 'object' or 'statue', or are different parties answering different questions—one about semantics, one about art practice, one about ontological economy—under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, perhaps about how we individuate artifacts in practice, that neither side has made explicit? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal distinction between artifactual persistence conditions and material persistence conditions; a reconceptualization that separates preservation ethics from identity metaphysics; a demonstration that the question is ill-posed once we recognize that digital reproduction severs artistic identity from material continuity; or an honest argument that no resolution should be expected because the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about ontological parsimony? Please engage the debate concretely, address whether the injection of conservation practice and digital reproduction changes the structure of the disagreement or merely adds noise, and tell me what, if anything, could actually move it.
