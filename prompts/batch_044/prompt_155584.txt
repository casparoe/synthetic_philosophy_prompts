At a recent philosophy of biology reading group, two colleagues became entangled in a dispute about the prisoner's dilemma and what the evolution of cooperation actually demonstrates. The first argued that the distinction between one-shot and iterated play collapses once one accounts for probabilistic termination: in any real population, repeated encounters carry an implicit discount factor, so an iterated game with shadow-of-the-future weights is mathematically equivalent to a one-shot game with a transformed payoff matrix. On this view, she said, the success of Tit-for-Tat in Axelrod's tournaments does not establish direct reciprocity as a distinct evolutionary mechanism, but merely shows that cooperation can be sustained when future payoffs are sufficiently weighted. The second colleague objected that this equivalence is purely formal and misses the mechanistic reality: conditional strategies such as Tit-for-Tat require temporal sequencing and memory, capabilities that have no functional analogue in a genuinely one-shot interaction, regardless of how one recalculates expected values. He added that in spatially structured populations—where individuals interact only with local neighbours—cooperation can emerge through cluster formation without any pairwise repetition whatsoever, which shows that the one-shot versus iterated framing is not merely redundant but actively misleading if applied universally.

Their disagreement then sharpened over whether structural alternatives—kin selection, network reciprocity, or group-level selection—should be treated as special cases of a unified weighted-payoff model or as genuinely distinct mechanisms. The first speaker maintained that calling these "one-shot cooperation" is a misnomer: they all depend on hidden structural conditions (relatedness, fixed positions, or group boundaries) that alter the effective game, so collapsing them into a single framework preserves analytical clarity rather than destroying it. The second speaker countered that mechanistic distinctions carry predictive weight: knowing whether cooperation is stabilised by direct reciprocity, spatial clustering, or genetic relatedness determines which ecological interventions will maintain it, and a unified model that erases these differences removes the very content that makes evolutionary game theory empirically useful. They asked me to referee, but I found both lines of argument partially convincing and could not locate the exact point of divergence.
