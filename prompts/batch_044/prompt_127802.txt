You are a philosopher-poet who has read the rationalist and effective-altruism blogosphere long enough to recognize the standard ethical moves by heart, and I want you to write something that escapes them. Before you draft a single line, spend a moment mapping out how the villanelle’s interlocking refrains can make two normative commitments talk past each other structurally, without ever resolving into harmony.

Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the last couplet—about prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. Do not treat this as a generic ethics-101 exercise. Instead, embed the specific constructed case of a welfare planner who holds a fixed budget distributed across exactly seven agents: two severely deprived (welfare -3), four moderately well off (+4), and one exceptionally well off (+9). The planner discovers that the only way to reduce the prioritarian-weighted sum of suffering—without adding any resources—is to impose a uniform tax that pulls the best agent down to +5, leaving everyone else exactly where they are. The poem should stage the prioritarian impulse in one refrain—something like “Weight the worst, but leave the rest alone”—against the leveling-down critic’s recoil in the other—something like “Better all stand than one be pulled.” These two lines should take turns through the tercets, never synthesizing, and in the final quatrain they should appear side by side without reconciliation, the form itself showing why the two positions keep passing.

The philosophical rendering should be precise enough that someone who knows the prioritarian framework recognizes it, yet it should turn on this invented seven-agent budget scenario rather than on textbook abstractions—someone unfamiliar with the debate should still feel the tension as a human dilemma rather than as a named theory.

Please present:
1. The villanelle (19 lines, with the two refrains clearly working through the form).
2. Commentary on form and philosophical choices (approximately 150–200 words), identifying which refrain belongs to which position, explaining why the villanelle’s structure demands they end adjacent but unreconciled, and assessing whether the seven-agent scenario exposes a genuine feature of prioritarian aggregation or merely a framing artifact.

Think carefully about meter and rhyme; do not force technical jargon where it breaks the iambic rhythm, but do not dilute the argument into vague sentiment either. Make the poem accurate enough that a specialist wouldn’t wince, and good enough that a non-specialist wouldn’t need footnotes.
