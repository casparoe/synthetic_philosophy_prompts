The exam question was: "Maya sculpts an artefact called The Recluse out of one lump of clay. Over thirty years she gradually replaces every particle with synthetic polymer that looks and feels identical. The original clay is kept in a sealed jar, still arranged roughly like the figure was originally. Is the gallery statue the same object as the original clay-lump? Is it the same object as the jar of particles? Discuss with reference to at least one theory of material constitution."

Here's the anomymised student answer:

"The statue and the original lump aren't identical, since they have different modal properties: the lump can be squashed into a ball and survive, but the statue can't. This supports material constitution over strict identity. I think Baker's constitution view solves it neatly — the statue is constituted by the lump at t1, but is a distinct object with its own persistense conditions, so there's no contradiction.

As for the gradual replacement, I think the statue survives so long as its form and artistic function stay intact, analogously to the Ship of Theseus. The polymer gallery piece retains Maya's original intention and the same visible shape, so it is numerically the same statue, just constituted by different matter. The jar of original clay particles, however, isn't The Recluse because although it contains the right matter arranged in roughly the right shape, it lacks the unified form required for artistic status — it's merely debris in a container that happens to look like a figure.

One might object that hylomorphists think form can only inhere in appropriate matter, so replacing clay with polymer should destroy the statue. But this actually supports Baker: on her view, constitution only requires that there be matter arranged statue-wise at each time, not that the matter remain clay. So the polymer version counts, and the jar is excluded because its particles aren't arranged statue-wise in a unified whole — they're dispersed inside glass."
