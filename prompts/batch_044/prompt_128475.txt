I am writing a seminar paper for my advanced philosophy of religion course on Hilary Putnam's resistance to naturalistic reductions of religious experience, particularly his view that authentic religious life involves an irreducible relational and participatory dimension rather than mere propositional assent. For my analysis, I am considering a recently developed system—fine-tuned on medieval mystical texts and contemporary pastoral theology—that generates personalized contemplative exercises and spiritual direction. Practitioners report experiencing these interactions as genuinely transformative despite knowing the system possesses no consciousness, belief, or communal embeddedness. Given Putnam's framework, do you think he would have argued that such algorithmically mediated experiences necessarily lack the essential relational quality he associated with real religious encounter, or could he have allowed that the functional and experiential effects on the practitioner satisfy the criteria for authentic religious practice regardless of the system's internal mental absence?
