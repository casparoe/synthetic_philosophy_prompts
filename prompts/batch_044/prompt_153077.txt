I've been drafting a short essay for my history of ideas course about hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in explaining the fall of Contantinople in 1453. My basic claim is that historians usually present Mehmed II's commissioning of the Orban cannon as this almost inevitable strategic masterstroke, but in reality the decision was way more contingent—he'd botched other sieges, the cannon was an expensive gamble that nearly exploded on its first use, and there was a real window where Byzantine diplomacy or a shift in Ottoman court politics could have delayed or derailed the assault entirely. Once we know the city fell, though, we smooth all that noise out and construct this tidy causal chain where technological superiority + political will = victory, which feels like explanation but is really just a coherent story told backward.

I'm trying to argue that this collapses two different things: the actual causal factors (aging walls, depleted manpower, blocked aid) and the retrospective attribution of strategic genius to Mehmed specifically. I wonder if I'm running together hindsight bias—where outcomes feel predetermined—with Taleb's idea of the narrative fallacy, where we mistake a well-structured story for genuine predictive or causal power.
