Here is a set of commitments I've arrived at about Spinoza's democratic theory, and I want to know if they form a stable package. (1) Democracy is the most natural and absolute form of sovereignty for Spinoza because right equals power, and only in democracy does the multitude retain its collective power without alienating it to a monarch or an aristocratic council. (2) His exclusions of women, dependents, foreigners, and criminals from citizenship in the Political Treatise are best interpreted as contingent sociological judgments about whose will is determined by another, not as essentialist claims about natural rational incapacity. (3) Genuine democratic rule still requires institutional mediation—councils, legal frameworks, and structured deliberation—that filters popular passion and channels the multitude toward rational common interests. (4) Democracy is more stable than monarchy precisely because it distributes power, yet Spinoza consistently warns that without widespread education in reason democratic assemblies degenerate into faction, sedition, and eventually tyranny. (5) If sovereignty is genuinely collective and inalienable, then excluding any agent whose judgment is not actually determined by another—regardless of gender, economic status, or origin—violates the core democratic principle that power and right coincide.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like conflicts, and how severe each real tension is. For each tension, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs. In particular, address whether (1) and (5) can stand together given (2), and whether (3) and (4) pull in opposite directions about how much institutional design can substitute for education. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them equally. Do not lecture me on Spinoza generally; audit this specific package.
