I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on the boundaries of moral patienthood, and I'm trying to articulate the principle—if there is one—that governs my own judgments across cases where biological taxonomy, cognitive capacity, and synthetic origin come apart. Here are my verdicts, which feel mostly consistent but contain at least one tension I can't resolve.

First, regarding conventional animals: I judge that a factory-farmed pig suffering in confined conditions is a direct object of moral concern, and that harming it requires strong justification; however, regarding a wild deer with a broken leg in a remote forest, I do not think I have a direct duty to intervene, though if I caused the injury my obligation changes. Turning to humans at the margins: an anencephalic human infant who will never develop consciousness still strikes me as possessing full moral status—harming her for convenience is gravely wrong—whereas a non-biological artificial system that reports distress but lacks any evolutionary or neural substrate does not, in my view, fall within the scope of direct moral obligations, even if its reports are behaviorally indistinguishable from suffering. The tension emerges with a hypothetical chimeric organism—say, a being with human-grade neural architecture and self-awareness housed in a porcine body, created for agricultural research—where I find myself judging that it should be accorded rights commensurate with its cognitive capacities rather than its species origin, which seems to pull toward capacity-based criteria. Yet that conflicts with my judgment about the anencephalic infant, where capacity is absent but status remains intact. Meanwhile, I hold that future persons who will exist only if we mitigate climate damage have a place in our moral reasoning—our actions can wrong them in a derivative sense—but they do not generate the same immediate duties as present suffering beings; and I deny that ecosystems or species themselves are moral patients, though we may have indirect duties to preserve them.

What principle, if any, accommodates all of these verdicts? Does it require abandoning the capacity-based intuition about the chimera, or the status-preserving intuition about the anencephalic infant, or can a multi-factor account save both at the cost of simplicity? I also want to know what the resulting account predicts for a case I haven't listed: a genetically modified octopus with human-equivalent problem-solving abilities but no mammalian neural architecture, living in a laboratory environment. Please state any candidate principle precisely, indicate which of my listed verdicts it must overturn, and explain whether the principle treats "species" as morally arbitrary or as carrying indirect weight.
