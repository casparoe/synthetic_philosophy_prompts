Analyze the 1945 Trinity nuclear test and the subsequent global deposition of plutonium-239/240 isotopes as a proposed boundary marker for the Anthropocene epoch, treating the episode through the lens of formal stratigraphic periodization rather than through environmental or technological history. First, map the Trinity event and its radioactive fallout signature onto the three canonical criteria for ratifying a Geological Boundary Stratotype Section and Point: a synchronous global signal, clear distinctiveness from Holocene background values, and long-term preservability in the sedimentary record. Second, contrast this event-based boundary with the competing proposal that the epoch should begin instead with the mid-twentieth-century "Great Acceleration"—the sharp, sustained rise in carbon emissions, concrete use, and nitrogen fixation—treating that proposal not merely as an alternative date but as a different kind of stratigraphic logic altogether. Third, examine what this disagreement reveals about authority: who is entitled to declare a new geological epoch—international geological commissions, climate-system scientists, or historians of technology—and whether that authority derives from control of the rock record, of planetary-system models, or of causal narratives about human agency. Finally, identify the precise point at which the stratigraphic framework itself becomes strained when applied to an epoch defined not by evolutionary turnover or tectonic reconfiguration but by the geochemical footprint of a single technological species, and say whether that strain undermines the Anthropocene as a geological unit or instead exposes a limitation in how geology conceptualizes rapid, agent-driven change.
