Question: Daniel Dennett argues for varieties of free will worth wanting rather than a single metaphysical property. Explain how his compatibilist framework distinguishes the capacities that ground moral responsibility from the libertarian unconditional could have done otherwise, and show why he believes evolutionary explanations of layered self-control support rather than undermine this distinction.

Student answer: Dennett rejects the idea that free will requires violating physical laws. Instead, he says we care about evitability—the ability to avoid an outcome if we want to—which is a conditional power, not an absolute one. Evolution built this through layers of control, like being able to pause and reason before acting. This is essentially the same position as Harry Frankfurt's, where freedom means having second-order desires that match our first-order ones. So moral responsibility just requires that actions come from our own values, not from nowhere. Dennett thinks there is basically one variety of free will worth wanting—the skill of responding to reasons—and that this is sufficient for judging even adolescents, who are still developing these control layers but already possess them in basic form.
