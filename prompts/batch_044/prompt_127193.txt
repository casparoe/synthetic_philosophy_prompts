Below is a draft philosophical essay of approximately 550 words. I am asking for critical comments on it as an argument, not for a rewrite or for external references.

The standard Aristotelian account of weakness of will (akrasia) treats the phenomenon as a failure within a unified agent: practical reason correctly identifies the good, but appetite or passion overwhelms it. In this framework, strength of will is the capacity of reason to maintain governance over non-rational desire, and weakness is a temporary defeat of that governance.

Hyperbolic discounting offers a different structural picture. Rather than a single agent with competing faculties, it suggests that an agent at time t evaluates rewards at time t+n using a discount factor that declines hyperbolically rather than exponentially. This produces preference reversals: the agent at t=0 prefers $100 in one year to $50 in eleven months, but at t=11 months prefers $50 immediately to $100 in one more month. The descriptive psychology is well established. What is less examined is its conceptual consequence for akrasia.

If we take hyperbolic discounting seriously as a feature of practical reasoning rather than merely a bias, then the Aristotelian unitary agent fragments into a sequence of temporal stages, each with its own evaluative framework. The judgment "X is best all things considered" is not stable across these stages, not because appetite interferes, but because the weighting of future goods is structurally different from the present perspective. This suggests that akrasia is not best understood as reason being overpowered by passion, but as one temporal stage of the agent acting according to its own evaluative framework in conflict with the framework endorsed by another stage.

On this view, precommitment devices—Ulysses contracts, commitment strategies, removing temptations—are not expressions of "strength of will" in the Aristotelian sense. They are not cases where present reason successfully binds future appetite. Rather, they are mechanisms by which one temporal stage (t=0) attempts to constrain the actions of another temporal stage (t=n) that operates with genuinely different preferences. The "will" that binds is not a faculty of reason overcoming desire; it is an institutional or technological bridge between evaluative frameworks that lack a common metric.

This reconception has two implications. First, moral evaluation of weakness should shift from assessing the agent's rational control to assessing whether the agent has installed adequate intertemporal governance structures. An agent who fails to precommit and then succumbs to present preference is not weak-willed in the traditional sense; she is administratively deficient. Second, the concept of "all-things-considered judgment" becomes temporally indexed. There is no atemporal standpoint from which to adjudicate between the preferences of t=0 and t=n; there are only negotiations, contracts, and power relations between temporal selves.

The objection will be that this collapses normative practical reason into descriptive psychology. But the point is not that hyperbolic discounting describes how we actually reason and therefore determines how we ought to reason. Rather, it reveals that the Aristotelian model of a single agent with a unified practical reason was always an abstraction. Once we recognize the temporal multiplicity of evaluative frameworks, we must reconstruct practical philosophy not around the governance of appetite by reason, but around the design of institutions—both internal and external—that allow different temporal stages to coordinate without appealing to a non-existent neutral authority.

Please comment specifically on the following, without providing bibliographies or reading lists:

1. Does the argument illicitly slide from descriptive hyperbolic discounting to a normative reconception of practical reason? Identify any unearned "is-to-ought" or descriptive-to-conceptual moves.

2. The claim that precommitment is intertemporal governance rather than an exercise of will: is this distinction coherent, or could an Aristotelian defender absorb it without abandoning weakness of will?

3. Is the idea of a "temporally indexed" all-things-considered judgment a genuine philosophical contribution, or does it merely rename familiar problems of diachronic consistency?

4. Assess the overall rigor and interest: does this framing depend on treating hyperbolic discounting as more conceptually revolutionary than it is, or does it open a productive line of inquiry?
