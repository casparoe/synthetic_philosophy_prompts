I'm drafting a literary novel in which a philosopher of mind serves as an ethics consultant to an artificial intelligence alignment laboratory, and in researching the conceptual terrain I have become absorbed by the epistemic architecture of inverse reinforcement learning—specifically, the problem of inferring stable human preferences from behavior that is already shaped by the instruments designed to measure it. I want to understand which open conceptual lines in this domain are genuinely upstream of the others, rather than merely technically intractable.

If you were advising a small interdisciplinary group—two philosophers and two decision theorists, with roughly three years of conceptual work and no empirical mandate—how would you rank the urgency of these four questions: whether observed reward signals can be disentangled from the measurement apparatus that elicits them; how to represent preferences directed at entities or ontological categories that do not yet exist within the agent's framework; whether inverse reinforcement learning implicitly presupposes a single coherent utility function, or whether it can accommodate genuinely fragmented or contradictory preference structures; and what normative weight should be assigned to inferred preferences if they are treated as authoritative rather than merely predictive? Please defend your ordering with explicit criteria: dependency—what must be settled before the rest can be resolved; tractability for conceptual rather than experimental work; and practical significance—specifically, which implications for democratic governance of automated decision systems or for personal autonomy within recommendation architectures would genuinely alter practice if adopted, and which remain philosophical ornament. I am not seeking a survey of the literature, but a defended agenda that identifies what should come first, what may be deferred, and what conceptual developments over the coming years would cause you to reorder the priorities. Please respond in American English.
