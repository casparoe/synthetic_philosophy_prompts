Two interlocutors—call them Maya and Jonas—are debating whether lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism can be sustained without either collapsing into weighted aggregation or relying on an ad hoc boundary. Their disagreement introduces a hypothetical conceptual device: an inter-phenomenal exchange rate, defined as a non-linear scaling function that attempts to map units of extreme suffering onto units of intense pleasure across a putative lexical divide.

Maya opens by arguing that the lexical boundary is defensible only if pleasure and pain are genuinely incommensurable across it. To test this, she proposes that we try to specify the exchange rate precisely. If we can coherently assign a scaling factor—however steep—that relates catastrophic suffering to massive well-being, then the lexical claim is not a qualitative discontinuity but merely an extreme quantitative weighting. In that case, lexical negative utilitarianism collapses into standard negative utilitarianism with a very sharp discount curve, not a true lexical priority.

Jonas responds that the exchange rate is a category error. Pleasure and pain, he claims, differ not merely in magnitude but in kind, particularly regarding their relation to agency, narrative integration, and the subject’s capacity for meaning. Nevertheless, he concedes that within a lower “domain of relevance”—where suffering is severe but not catastrophic—partial commensurability holds, allowing weighted trade-offs. He therefore defends a tiered lexical view: absolute priority for suffering above the threshold, but aggregation within the lower tier, bounded by the subject’s integrative capacities. He insists this is not ad hoc because the threshold tracks a qualitative shift in how experiences relate to personal identity.

Maya pushes back. If Jonas allows aggregation within the lower tier, then he already accepts a metric there. Once such a metric exists, she argues, there is no principled reason it cannot be extended upward with diminishing returns rather than an absolute block. She also challenges his appeal to narrative identity as the boundary-marker: it seems contingent and psychologically variable, which makes the moral principle implausibly sensitive to differences in cognitive architecture. She asks whether an infant or a severely demented adult, with diminished narrative capacity, would therefore fall outside the protection of the lexical boundary.

Jonas replies that the boundary should be anchored not to actual narrative capacity but to the potential for meaning-making, which even minimal subjects possess. To illustrate the relational nature of the boundary, he asks us to imagine a being lacking any integrative consciousness—pure momentary awareness. For such a being, he suggests, there might indeed be no lexical boundary, only continuous scaling. This shows, he claims, that the incommensurability is not intrinsic to hedonic physics but is relational, tied to the structure of person-level moral standing. He also notes that Maya’s exchange-rate proposal, if applied consistently, would eventually license the conclusion that an enormous population of mildly happy lives could outweigh one instance of extreme torture, provided the scaling factor is steep enough—precisely the repugnant conclusion the lexical view was designed to prevent.

Maya counters that if the lexical boundary depends on contingent relational features rather than an objective, mind-independent intensity scale, then negative utilitarianism loses its robustness as a universal moral principle. She argues that Jonas’s “potential for meaning-making” is too vague to do the work of a sharp boundary, and that his tiered structure ultimately smuggles in the very quantitative reasoning it purports to reject at the top tier.
