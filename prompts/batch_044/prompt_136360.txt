I serve on a municipal sustainability board that evaluates long-term infrastructure proposals—coastal defenses, soil-restoration bonds, and carbon-sequestration contracts—whose costs fall on present taxpayers but whose benefits unfold across 80 to 200 years. Our economic consultants deliver a single “social discount rate,” yet I have come to suspect that this figure smuggles together genuinely distinct considerations: the risk that future climate regimes or technologies will alter the value of the project; the expectation that future generations will be wealthier, lowering the marginal utility of the same benefit; and what is called pure time preference, the idea that a good is worth less solely because it arrives later, irrespective of risk or wealth. I need a reusable decision procedure my board can run on any proposal that forces these factors apart, tells us whether a proposed rate is justified by temporal distance alone, and flags exactly where empirical analysis ends and a normative judgment about intergenerational neutrality begins.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the specific questions to ask about a proposal’s time profile and the source of each discount factor; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, including how to distinguish a growth-based or risk-based reduction from one that treats future welfare as intrinsically less important; the points at which the procedure stops with a clear recommendation to reject or revise a rate; and the explicit handoff moments where the remaining dispute is not factual but a value choice about whether it is permissible to weigh our grandchildren’s welfare lower just because they live later. Apply the procedure to two concrete cases we are weighing: a seawall whose protective benefits extend 120 years but depend on highly uncertain future sea-level trajectories and engineering standards, and a reforestation initiative with benefits spread over 150 years under conditions of relative climatic and economic stability. In each case, show where the procedure isolates pure time preference from other discounting grounds, and mark precisely where the board must make a normative decision that no formula can settle.
