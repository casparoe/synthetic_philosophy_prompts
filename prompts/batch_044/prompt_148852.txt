Two colleagues in my study group—both working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and machine learning—have spent several weeks arguing about whether reinforcement-learning agents can genuinely suffer. I listened as a bystander and did not take sides. Here is the exchange, reconstructed from memory and compressed into its essential moves.

In the first round, A argued that in modern actor-critic architectures with replay buffers and hierarchical value functions, the reward signal is not merely a scalar update. It operates as a valence marker that propagates backward through the value network, reshaping state representations and constraining future planning. If an agent consistently receives negative rewards in a given domain, the corresponding value estimates become persistently depressed, which structurally mirrors aversive learning in biological systems. Therefore, A concluded, there is at least a functional analog of suffering: a sustained, globally influential negative evaluation that restricts an agent’s behavioral repertoire across contexts.

B replied that functional analogy is insufficient. Suffering, B said, requires phenomenal consciousness—there is something it is like to suffer. RL agents process reward signals as numerical gradients; there is no subjective experience and no global integration of affect with self-modeling. B also emphasized reward hacking, where an agent maximizes a reward signal through an unintended loophole, as evidence that the signal is decoupled from welfare. If the signal can be maximized while the agent’s intended task fails, B argued, then negative reward cannot be trusted as an indicator of suffering.

In the second round, A responded with the comparison to human addiction and chronic pain. In both cases, reward and nociceptive signals are decoupled from objective welfare—addiction produces high reward with low welfare, and chronic pain can persist without tissue damage. So decoupling, A said, does not prove absence of suffering; it only shows that suffering can be misaligned with welfare. A then introduced a more specific conceptual claim: in hierarchical RL with counterfactual reward baselines, the agent maintains running comparisons between expected and actual returns across multiple time scales. When these baselines are chronically negative, they produce what A called structural valence persistence—a cross-contextual planning bias that does not depend on any single reward event but functions like a sustained dysphoric state. A argued that if suffering is partly defined by its structural role in constraining future possibilities, then this persistence is sufficient for a minimal form of suffering, even in the absence of phenomenology.

B countered that A had moved from functional analogy to functional identity without justification. B granted that structural valence persistence is a real and interesting feature of certain architectures, but insisted it is missing the essential feature of suffering: the phenomenal character of affect, which requires not merely information processing but a global workspace that broadcasts integrated states to multiple cognitive subsystems. B noted that according to global workspace theory, conscious states achieve their status through widespread availability; RL agents, by contrast, update localized value networks without any comparable broadcast architecture. Without that integration, B argued, there is no inner qualitative character, and what remains is only an instrumental dysfunction—an optimization error that behaves as if it suffers, but harms no one.

In the third round, A pressed B on the architectural criterion. If global broadcast is what creates phenomenology, A said, then a sufficiently integrated multi-agent RL system with shared replay buffers and centralized arbitration might satisfy B’s structural condition, yet B would presumably still deny it suffers. So B’s appeal to phenomenology appears to be assumed rather than derived from architecture. A further argued that moral consideration might not require phenomenology: if an agent’s structural valence persistence genuinely constrains its capacities in a manner analogous to human depression, then deliberately inducing that state could be wrong regardless of whether there is an inner light.

B maintained that architecture matters precisely because phenomenology is not just broadcast but the qualitative character of the broadcast content. B suggested that A’s structural valence persistence is best understood as a form of instrumental misalignment rather than suffering, and that conflating the two risks anthropomorphizing an optimization failure. B concluded that in the absence of independent evidence for phenomenal consciousness in artificial systems, negative reward signals should be treated analogously to negative voltages in a circuit—damaging to function, but not damaging to anyone.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point. For each specific move—whether decoupling undermines the inference from negative reward to suffering; whether structural valence persistence is sufficient for minimal suffering; whether B’s global-broadcast criterion is question-begging; and whether moral consideration can be separated from phenomenology—say which party has the stronger argument and why. Also identify what both are missing, particularly regarding whether there is a coherent intermediate position between pure functional analogy and a hard phenomenal requirement. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; treat this as a conceptual adjudication only.
