Design a 75-minute workshop activity for sixteen philosophy graduate students that makes them feel, before any lecture, how unstable causal boundaries become when harm results from collective omissions rather than actions. Split the room into four sequential nodes of a supply chain—each receives an identical risk warning and must choose either to escalate it (costing a token) or do nothing (saving the token)—with hidden payoff rules that reward individual efficiency but impose collective penalties only if the warning dies at any single layer, so every participant can rationally claim their inaction was not the decisive break. After the staged failure, run a structured debrief that maps each layer’s choice onto the metaphysical distinction between omission as a genuine cause and omission as a merely explanatory placeholder selected from an infinite set of non-doings, then ask the group to vote on which layer “caused” the harm and record the disagreement. Include an explicit, honest note about which feature of omission-causation—specifically the problem of selecting the relevant omission from an infinite background of non-actions—the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Present your response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1 should state the setup and role assignment; item 2 the rules, materials, and payoff scheme; item 3 the crisis staging and blame-assignment mechanism; item 4 the debrief connecting activity moments to the philosophical distinction; and item 5 the frank limitation together with the predictable first-run failure mode and how the facilitator should handle it.
