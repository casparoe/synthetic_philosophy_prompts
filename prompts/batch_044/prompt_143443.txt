I'm preparing a seminar on comparative epistemology and found myself stuck on an institutional feature of Jain monastic debate that seems to presuppose more than mere rhetorical politeness. In the traditional disputation protocol, a speaker is required—by community norms that treat omission as an epistemic fault rather than bad manners—to articulate his opponent's thesis through the full seven-fold schema of predication (affirmation, negation, conjunction, inexpressibility, and their combinations) before advancing his own position. The underlying rationale is that to present only one mode is to suffer from ekānta, a one-sided vision that mistakes a partial aspect for the complete reality of the object.

What theory of truth and cognition does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly require the metaphysical doctrine of anekāntavāda, according to which reality is irreducibly manifold and every predicate captures only one aspect of an object, or can the same institutional rule be licensed by weaker commitments—say, a pragmatic pluralism about linguistic frames, a fallibilist humility about human cognition, or even a purely procedural norm designed to prevent sectarian violence? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely accommodates comfortably, and tell me what a debate institution built on pure fallibilism—without the Jain ontology of manifold reality—would have to change or abandon in its rules.
