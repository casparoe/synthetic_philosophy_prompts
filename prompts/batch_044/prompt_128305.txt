I'm giving the commencement address at my university next month, and I need your help drafting it. The occasion is complicated: our institution just adopted what we're calling the Nested Demos Charter, which grants formal deliberative standing not only to enrolled students and faculty, but also to three other groups—the permanent residents of the watershed that supplies our campus water, the lineal descendants of the laborers who constructed our original buildings in the 1890s, and a randomly selected future cohort of local teenagers who will not attend university for another fifteen years but whose lives will be shaped by the research priorities we set today. Roughly half the audience will be democratic theorists and political philosophers who have spent decades arguing about the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos, whether affected interests or being subject to coercion should determine membership, and whether people who do not yet exist can have present standing—while the other half will be graduating students, their families, and local residents who have never read a page of democratic theory. The philosophical problem needs to do real structural work in the speech: I cannot simply declare that we have solved the boundary problem, because the charter is explicitly provisional and contains internal tensions—for instance, the watershed residents have veto power over environmental policy but not over curriculum, while the future cohort can propose long-term research agendas but cannot vote on tuition. I need to honor the seriousness of the charter without pretending it resolves the theoretical debates, and I need to comfort and inspire the graduates without saying anything that a careful theorist in the room would recognize as false or vacuous. Please write the address as continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a formal letter or spoken manuscript. Keep it suitable for oral delivery, about five to seven minutes when read aloud, and make sure the boundary problem shapes the arc of the argument rather than serving as decorative reference.
