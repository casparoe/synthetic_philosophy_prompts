As a philosophy professor consulting for a national heritage authority, I ask you to translate the philosophical analysis of vagueness and the sorites paradox into a reusable decision procedure that preservation officers—who have no philosophical training and must defend their rulings in administrative review—can apply when determining whether a gradually restored historic structure still qualifies as "the original" for protected-status designation. Ground the steps in the distinctions that actually matter—tolerance conditions, the possibility of sharp but unknown boundaries, epistemic versus ontic vagueness, and the failure of bivalence—without requiring the officer to cite any literature. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist: the precise questions to ask about material continuity, functional design, temporal interruption, and community identification; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; the point at which the procedure issues a definitive "protected" or "not protected" determination; and the explicit nodes where judgment is unavoidable because competing theories of vagueness give opposite answers. Be honest about which philosophical commitments are operative at each node (for example, whether adopting a supervaluationist framework blocks a boundary case that epistemicism permits). Apply the procedure to three test structures: (i) a wooden bridge in which sixty percent of planks have been replaced but the original arch, foundation, and alignment remain intact; (ii) a bridge destroyed by fire and rebuilt from original blueprints using salvaged timber plus concealed modern reinforcement, never having been fully absent from the site; and (iii) a bridge maintained continuously for two centuries such that no single plank is older than fifty years, yet it was never demolished and local records consistently identify it as the same structure. For at least one case, show that the procedure produces a verdict the authority will dislike, and conclude by stating exactly what classes of borderline case the procedure cannot settle and must be referred upward to a values-based review board.
