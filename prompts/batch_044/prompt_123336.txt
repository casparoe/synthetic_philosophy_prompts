I'm trying to work out whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, by testing my gut reactions against a range of concrete cases. Here is what I think, case by case.

A hiker three hours into a mountain trail realizes she has no water left. Pushing to the summit takes another two hours and risks dehydration; turning back means the distance already covered yields no payoff. I think she should turn back — treating the miles already walked as a reason to continue is irrational.

A composer spends a decade on an opera she now despises. She finishes and premieres it because she has come too far to abandon the project. That strikes me as the same error: irrational.

A couple has been together fifteen years, mostly unhappy, but they stay because of everything they have already endured together. Here honoring the sunk emotional cost does not feel irrational; if anything, walking away from the shared history feels like a failure of loyalty.

A city council approves another five hundred million dollars for a structurally flawed bridge because three billion is already spent. Clearly irrational.

A reader is three hundred pages into a five-hundred-page novel she dislikes. She finishes it so she does not waste the time already invested. Irrational, though I feel less judgmental about it than about the bridge.

A research lab continues a failed experiment for a third funding cycle not because anyone expects success, but because the grant is already awarded and staff salaries depend on maintaining it. This feels different — not purely rational, but not the same kind of error as the hiker's.

A soldier holds a position he knows is lost, not for strategic gain, but because leaving would betray the comrades who already died there. Honoring their sunk sacrifice feels morally required rather than irrational.

An investor refuses to sell a plummeting stock because he needs to recover what he lost. Irrational.

The pattern I think I see involves whether the sunk cost has become part of a standing commitment to others, rather than remaining a purely instrumental investment for future gain. But I cannot state the boundary precisely, and several pairs trouble me: the couple's shared misery and the soldier's fallen comrades both invoke loyalty, yet the hiker's wasted miles invoke none; the lab's grant and the bridge's budget are both institutional, yet I treat them differently.

Can you state the principle — or the smallest family of principles — that best captures all eight verdicts? For each clause, say which case motivates it. Then predict my verdict on a case I have not listed: a patient who has endured two years of painful chemotherapy that has not worked considers a third year solely because of the suffering already endured. Finally, be explicit about which of my eight judgments the principle cannot accommodate and therefore forces me to drop or revise.
