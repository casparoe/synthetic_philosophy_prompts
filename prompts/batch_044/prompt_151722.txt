I'm taking a course on philosophy of science and we're stuck on the same argument every week. Someone points out that a macro model assumes representitive agents with perfect foresight, or that climate models leave out cloud feedback loops because they're too messy, and the defenders say all models are wrong and cite Friedman's essay about unrealistic assumptions not mattering if predictions hold. But the move feels like it's smuggling in different commitments in different cases. Can you lay out what philosophical commitments about science, explanation, and representation are actually being presupposed when someone invokes the F-twist to defend an unrealistic model? Is it just instrumentalism about theories, or does it hide a specific view about what explanation is, or about whether false premises can yield true conclusions? And is there a hidden commitment about which unrealistic assumptions are harmless versus fatal — maybe something about tractability or modularity that nobody says out loud? I'm especially confused about whether this practice treats models as pure prediction tools, or whether it secretly needs them to be explanatory in some other sense, say for policy guidance. Break down the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it just fits with, and tell me what would have to change in how economists defend their models if we dropped the instrumentalist part but kept the predictive focus.
