I've been comparing Montaigne's essay on cannibals with the sixteenth-century travel narratives that informed it—especially the first-hand reports of Tupinambá social life that circulated in France at the time—and I keep encountering what looks like a systematic tension. One common interpretation takes Montaigne to be advancing a radical cultural relativism: European revulsion at cannibalism is mere ethnocentric prejudice, since the Tupinambá regard their own ritual practices as honorable and European religious warfare as far more barbaric. If that is his position, then moral judgment across cultures should be incommensurable. But in the very same essay he praises the cannibals for their natural courage, fidelity, and freedom from artificial needs, and he condemns European torture and hypocrisy by comparison. If moral standards are genuinely culture-bound, on what basis can he rank the Tupinambá as nobler or European cruelty as worse? Is he smuggling in a universal conception of human excellence, or is the praise purely strategic—a rhetorical inversion meant to shame his own society without committing to any cross-cultural evaluation? I am also struck by the fact that his account relies on translated eyewitness descriptions of Tupinambá customs. If he treats those reports as accurate rather than as culturally constructed narratives, doesn't that presuppose that some observations can be objectively true across cultural frameworks—precisely the claim that radical relativism seems to deny? So is the essay best read as a failed attempt at consistent relativism, or is the appearance of contradiction itself the point?
