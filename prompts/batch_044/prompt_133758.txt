I'm writing up a conceptual profile for a philosophy seminar on political ontology—specifically on whether the left-right distinction is real and what binds each side together—and I want a consistency check before I present. My commitments are these. First, I think the left-right axis tracks a real and stable cleavage in political psychology, but I deny it is a natural kind or that either pole represents a coherent philosophy derived from shared axioms. Second, I propose that what actually holds each cluster together is what I call negative coherence: the internal unity of a side comes not from logical derivation among its commitments but from shared opposition to the symbolic and normative markers of the other side. Third, I maintain that there are objective, mind-independent facts about distributive justice, legitimate authority, and the scope of collective obligation, and that individual policies favored by the left or the right can be evaluated against these facts independently of cluster membership. Fourth, I hold that for the vast majority of individuals, adopting a left or right identity is primarily an expressive or signaling behavior—an affiliation with a social group—rather than the outcome of reasoning about those objective normative facts. Fifth, despite that signaling thesis, I believe reflective equilibrium remains a valid method for revising political commitments, including abandoning left-right framing altogether, because the social origin of a belief doesn't necessarily vitiate its justifiability.

Please audit this package. Which commitments pull against each other genuinely, which conflicts are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. I'm especially concerned that negative coherence in (2) might undermine the possibility of objective evaluation in (3), and that the signaling account in (4) might reduce reflective equilibrium in (5) to mere post-hoc rationalization. But I also want you to flag any hidden tension—particularly between (1)'s claim that the axis tracks real psychological patterns and (2)'s denial of positive philosophical coherence—that I may have overlooked.
