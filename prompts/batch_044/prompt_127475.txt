Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Dr. Alistair Vane and Dr. Renata Coss—debating whether every coherent hierarchical system necessarily rests on an "inviolate level," a base that is not itself constituted by the system it grounds, or whether tangled hierarchies (exemplified by M. C. Escher's Drawing Hands, in which each hand draws the other with neither being ontologically prior) can be fully self-sustaining without collapse.

To keep the exchange away from well-worn formal-systems clichés, ground it in a specific invented case: the "Archive of Concurrent Histories," a hypothetical institution whose records are not stored in any external medium but are inscribed solely within the events they document. In this archive, altering a record requires altering the historical event it describes, and altering the event rewrites the record; there is therefore no "base layer" of raw data separate from interpreted history, only a loop of mutual constitution.

Dr. Vane should defend the inviolate-level thesis. He should argue that even this archive secretly relies on a non-recursive substrate—whether physical laws that permit inscription, a meta-rule preventing contradiction, or a pre-archival act of institution—and that without such a substrate the loop either spins into arbitrariness or collapses under its own circularity. He must respond directly to Coss's claims about interdependence, not merely restate foundationalism.

Dr. Coss should defend the tangled-hierarchy view. She should argue that the archive is structurally sound precisely because its levels are interdependent rather than linear; that demanding an external substrate is like demanding an artist outside Escher's picture to hold the pencil; and that "grounding" is a relational achievement distributed across the loop, not a unidirectional foundation. She should address Vane's specific appeals to contradiction-avoidance and substrate-dependence, showing why she thinks they mischaracterize the stability of tangled structures.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other's actual last point rather than delivering parallel monologues. Neither should be a strawman. Do not resolve the dispute with a clear winner; instead, conclude by pinpointing the precise disagreement—whether the absence of an inviolate level entails a fatal instability, or merely a different, non-hierarchical kind of stability that requires no external rescue.
