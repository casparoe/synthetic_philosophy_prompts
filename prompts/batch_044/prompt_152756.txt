In ethics seminars on just war theory, a familiar analogy keeps surfacing: Harry Truman's authorization of the atomic bombings is said to map directly onto the classic surgeon case, in which a physician kills one healthy patient to harvest organs and save five dying ones. The comparison is meant to license a specific conclusion drawn from G.E.M. Anscombe's critique — that Truman intentionally directed lethal harm at innocent civilians as a means to ending the war, which makes the act categorically impermissible under the principle of double effect, regardless of the lives saved. I'm trying to assess how far this analogy actually holds, particularly because Anscombe was analyzing the direction of an individual agent's intention, not the distributed, bureaucratic intentionality of a modern state apparatus like the Manhattan Project.

Could you lay out an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the surgeon, the five patients, the one healthy victim, the scalpel, and the institutional administration — then identify precisely where the mapping fractures? I want particular attention to whether the collective, mediated nature of Truman's order breaks the analogy's model of direct bodily agency, and whether the analogy quietly imports a framework of individual bodily integrity that doesn't translate to strategic bombing campaigns. Does the conclusion — that the bombing was impermissible because innocents were used as means — survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the entire argument? If there is a more adequate analogy for capturing Anscombe's concern about directed intention in large-scale state violence, suggest one briefly and explain why it fares better.
