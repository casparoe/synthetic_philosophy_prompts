I am preparing a long-form feature on peer disagreement in philosophy and science for an audience that follows ideas closely but does not read the specialist journals. The standard framing pits conciliationism against steadfastness, yet in my reporting I have encountered a more granular picture: disagreement among experts often tracks not differences in evidence but differences in what I will call epistemic risk profiles—the calibrated threshold at which a researcher treats an anomalous result as noise rather than signal. For the feature, please rank the following four responses to peer disagreement by their adequacy for long-running methodological disputes, assigning each a tier from S (most adequate) to D (least adequate) and giving a one-sentence reason: (1) strict conciliationism, which requires splitting the difference; (2) steadfastness with first-person privilege, which permits retaining one's view; (3) divergence in epistemic risk profiles, which treats disagreement as a mismatch in anomaly-tolerance rather than belief-error; and (4) institutional-redundancy social epistemology, which treats sustained disagreement as a functional feature of distributed inquiry rather than an individual failure to be corrected. In a separate paragraph, provide a single score between 0 and 1 indicating how much you think the conventional equal-peer framing itself prejudices the debate toward conciliationism, with a brief justification.
