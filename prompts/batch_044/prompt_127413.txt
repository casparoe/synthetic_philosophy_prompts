In our seminar on political judgment, a fellow student summarized Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial like this: she argued that Eichmann was not a fanatical anti-Semite but a shallow careerist whose evil was banal — that is, it arose not from hatred but from a failure to think, an inability to take the standpoint of others, and a reliance on bureaucratic clichés. According to the summary, Arendt therefore denied that he possessed genuine moral agency, treated his thoughtlessness as an exculpatory condition rather than a culpable failure, and maintained that the trial was wrong to present him as a uniquely guilty monster. The summary further claims that because such thoughtlessness is widespread, Arendt saw the Holocaust as primarily revealing the dangers of modern administrative structures rather than the force of Nazi ideology, and that her controversial remarks about Jewish councils were meant to illustrate that victim compliance is an inevitable structural byproduct of those same bureaucratic dynamics.

How fair is this characterization to Arendt's actual position in Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil and her related essays on thinking and moral responsibility? Please separate what is accurate from what is distorted: does she deny Eichmann's agency and culpability, or does she relocate them? Is the idea of banal evil intended to excuse, or to name a specific form of moral failure that is precisely not exculpatory? Does her analysis of the Jewish councils serve the explanatory role assigned here, and is the bureaucratic-structural reading of the Holocaust consistent with her broader critique of totalitarianism? I am especially unsure whether she treats thoughtlessness as diminishing guilt or as constituting an unprecedented ground for it.
