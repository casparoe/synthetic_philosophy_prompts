Having spent thirty years in systems neuroscience before turning to philosophy of mind, I have developed a somewhat idiosyncratic stance on the zombie conceivability argument that I would like tested for coherence and vulnerability. I accept that we can imagine a being physically and functionally identical to me but lacking subjective experience; however, I contend that what we actually conceive in that exercise is not genuine phenomenal absence—what philosophers call a zombie—but rather a state I term phenomenal transparency, in which all cognitive processing occurs without meta-representational access to its own contents. On my view, true phenomenal absence is not positively conceivable at all, analogous to trying to imagine a color that occupies no region in color space; the zombie intuition therefore tracks a deficit in access consciousness, not a missing phenomenal quality. Consequently, I reject the inference from conceivability to metaphysical possibility, but not on the usual grounds of modal rationalism or empirical correlation—rather, because the premise of the argument misdescribes what is being conceived.

If this distinction holds, then standard two-dimensional reasoning collapses not at the modal step but at the descriptive step: the zombie world is conceptually identical to a world in which every agent possesses only first-order processing with perfect functional integration but no second-order modeling of that processing. This makes the argument vulnerable to findings from predictive processing and global workspace theory, but in a specific way—if empirical neuroscience demonstrates that phenomenal consciousness just is a particular form of meta-representational integration, then my view predicts that there can be no physically identical duplicate lacking it, which is precisely the anti-zombie conclusion I want. Yet this also commits me to the claim that any being with the right kind of recursive self-modeling necessarily has phenomenal experience, a consequence that strikes me as plausible but perhaps too permissive regarding artificial systems or non-human animals.

What I need is an honest audit of this position's exposure: what specific empirical results, conceptual distinctions, or formal arguments would genuinely threaten it, and which would defenders of standard conceivability arguments concede as damaging versus dismiss? For instance, would a demonstrated dissociation between global workspace broadcasting and subjective report in human patients undermine my identification of phenomenal consciousness with meta-representational access, or could I absorb it by refining the transparency-versus-absence distinction? Would a compelling case that we can positively conceive of phenomenal absence—not merely the absence of access—force me to abandon the descriptive-error diagnosis of the zombie argument, and is that fatal or merely awkward? I am particularly interested in whether there is a hidden commitment in my view that collapses it into either reductive physicalism about consciousness or a form of integrated-information panpsychism, and whether that is a cost I should accept or a reductio. Please rank these potential defeaters from decisive to merely inconvenient, and indicate which ones I, as the holder of this view, should be prepared to concede and which ones I could consistently reject without special pleading.
