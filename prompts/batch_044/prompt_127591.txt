I have been tracking the public positions of Dr. Lena Vasquez, a philosopher of technology who advises our university’s digital-ethics oversight panel, across five cases she has addressed in her advisory reports and seminar papers over the past two years. In each instance she delivers a clear verdict with minimal qualification. First, she held that employers using keystroke dynamics to infer workers’ emotional fatigue violate privacy, even when the raw data is anonymized at collection, because the inference penetrates pre-reflective mental states. Second, she approved a municipal study that used fully anonymized and aggregated subway mobility traces solely to optimize traffic flow, on the grounds that movement through public space carries no intimate expectation of concealment. Third, she rejected a consented, de-identified genetic database designed to predict individual disease risk for institutional health planning, arguing that forecasting bodily destiny for external management erodes personal sovereignty over one’s future narrative regardless of anonymity safeguards. Fourth, she condemned a commercial “mood-forecasting” service that synthesized public social-media sentiment, weather patterns, and local event calendars to predict individual emotional trajectories over the coming week for workforce allocation, even though the service never stored legal names. Fifth, she endorsed a public library’s use of anonymized borrowing aggregates to guide acquisitions, noting that reading choices expressed within a public institution do not constitute intimate disclosure.

A new proposal has now reached the board: an “ambient anticipatory infrastructure” that fuses anonymized public-transit card taps, open Wi-Fi probe counts, and meteorological feeds to generate forty-eight-hour neighborhood-level predictions of stress-related resource needs. The raw inputs are automatically deleted within twenty-four hours; no individual is named in the output; no algorithm explicitly infers mental states; yet the model’s predictive accuracy is high enough that anyone possessing minimal external knowledge—say, two public social-media geotags—could in principle cross-reference the neighborhood forecasts to reverse-engineer a specific resident’s probable location and emotional condition. The system operates under a broad public-interest exemption with no universal opt-out mechanism, though citizens may request exclusion of their Wi-Fi probe signatures.
