In teaching and discussing moral responsibility, I have repeatedly observed an asymmetry in how interlocutors deploy the reactive and objective attitudes associated with Strawson’s framework, though I am unsure whether this specific configuration has received its own name. The pattern is this: when an agent’s harmful action is explained by reference to a mechanistic or clinical cause—say, a neurological disorder, severe trauma, or pharmacological influence—listeners typically suspend resentment and moral blame, shifting to a clinical or managerial stance. But when the very same action is explained by reference to a fully agential, rational cause—for instance, that the agent calculated it was the optimal strategy to maximize social status or economic gain—listeners do not suspend reactive attitudes; on the contrary, they often reinforce blame, treating the rational structure of the explanation as confirming rather than undermining responsibility. The asymmetry persists even when both explanations are deterministic, both lie outside the agent’s immediate control, and both are offered as complete causal accounts rather than as mere background conditions. I have seen this in classroom debates about corporate malfeasance versus addiction-related harm, and in discussions of political violence explained either by ideological conviction or by psychological conditioning. What interests me is not the general Strawsonian distinction itself, but this selective triggering: the objective attitude seems activated by explanations that deny agency, yet inhibited by explanations that affirm it, regardless of whether the latter actually preserve the conditions for genuine moral address. Does this selective pattern have an established label in the literature on moral psychology or the philosophy of responsibility—perhaps as a variant of the fundamental attribution error, or as a distinct bias in our practices of excuse? If not, how precisely should we delimit it? In particular, does the pattern hold when mechanistic and agential explanations are combined, as in cases where an agent rationally exploits a psychological vulnerability that was itself caused by trauma? I would welcome a careful conceptual mapping: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and whether it reveals an unacknowledged commitment in our reactive practices.
