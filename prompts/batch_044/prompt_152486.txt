So at the dept coffee hour yesterday, two of my grad students got into it about chaos and determinism. Maya said that if the universe is deterministic but chaotic, determinism is basically empty — the future is fixed, but because any measurement error explodes exponentially, there's no meaningful sense in which present conditions "determine" outcomes for anyone actually inside the system. She called it "determinsm without grip." Leo pushed back: determinism was never about epistemic access, it's an ontological claim about the closure of physical law, and chaos actually makes determinism more interesting because it shows how rigid laws produce wild unpredictability. Maya countered that this just redefines determinism to avoid criticism — if determinism doesn't constrain predictions or explanations, it's not doing philosophical work, it's just a metaphysical label. Leo said that confuses determinism with predictability, and that Maya's view makes determinism depend on our cognitive limits, which is backwards. Maya then added that promises and moral responsibility also seem to require determinism to have some kind of "grip," not just be true in some inaccessible way, so Leo's view severs determinism from the practical contexts where it actually matters. Leo replied that practical relevance is a separate issue and that conflating them is exactly the mistake that makes people reject determinism for bad reasons. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please explain who's right about what specific point, and say who is more right overall. Also tell me what argument or consideration would change your mind here, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
