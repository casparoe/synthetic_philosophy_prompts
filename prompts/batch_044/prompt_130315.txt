I'm delivering the opening remarks—roughly five minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Everyday Obligations," attended by moral philosophers, anthropologists, local community organizers, and a cohort of international graduate students who arrived only days ago. The workshop's guiding question is whether everyday conventions—tipping practices, queuing norms, and local jaywalking expectations—constitute genuine moral obligations, and whether visitors are bound by them. I want the welcome to do real philosophical work rather than merely decorate the occasion: as host, I need to address the newcomers without either imposing false universalism ("when in Rome, do exactly as Romans do, because it is right everywhere") or collapsing all norms into arbitrary preference ("it is just culture"). To structure the remarks, please work with the distinction I have been developing between coordination conventions—practices such as queuing or turn-taking that depend on shared expectation to function, so that outsiders have direct reason to participate—and deference conventions—practices such as tipping hierarchies or unwritten pedestrian rules that encode local status relations and may be questioned without necessarily breaking cooperation. Do not let that distinction collapse into a simple "rules versus culture" dichotomy; instead, show where it frays, for instance when a queuing norm also functions as a deference norm, or when refusing a local tipping expectation disrupts the very coordination it was meant to smooth. The speech should be fully accessible to the community organizers and new students, yet precise enough that the philosophers in the room recognize the stakes, and it should frame the workshop's purpose—determining which everyday obligations travel with us and which remain locally anchored—without pretending the answer is already settled.
