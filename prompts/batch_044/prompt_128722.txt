I am working through the conceptual geography of societal collapse, particularly the tension between demographic-catastrophe narratives and institutional-continuity accounts. Give me a concrete example—ideally situated in the transition from the Late Classic to the Terminal Classic in parts of the southern Maya lowlands, or in the post-1200 BCE eastern Mediterranean and Levant—where the same archaeological body of evidence sustains both a collapse reading and a transformation reading, and where the disagreement is genuinely conceptual rather than empirical. I want the case to turn on specific material ambiguities: the simultaneous cessation of large-scale limestone monument construction and long-distance exchange in obsidian and jade, paired with the uninterrupted persistence of local ceramic traditions, domestic architecture, and low-level agrarian rituals across the same chronological horizon.

Specifically, frame the scenario so that one interpretation reads these patterns as the disintegration of a centralized tributary state—an extraction system that collapsed when drought and soil exhaustion undermined its capacity to mobilize labor—while the other reads them as the deliberate or adaptive decentralization of that same population into dispersed, self-sufficient agrarian communities that no longer needed monumental architecture or long-distance prestige goods. Then identify the precise conceptual hinge—whether it lies in whether hierarchical governance is a necessary condition for societal continuity, in whether urban population density functions as a normative criterion for success, or in whether institutional death can be distinguished from institutional migration—at which the two accounts diverge despite agreeing on every observable fact. The deliverable should not be a historical verdict about what actually happened, but a sharply drawn illustration of exactly where the category of collapse performs explanatory work that reorganization or adaptation cannot replicate, and where it risks imposing a teleological narrative of failure onto a process that might be better described as structural reconfiguration.
