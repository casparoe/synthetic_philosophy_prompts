I'm trying to work out what I actually think about higher-order defeat and being epistemically akratic — like, when you've got reason to think your own reasoning is messed up but you still can't shake the belief. I'm in a philosophy course and we keep running into the same tired examples about sleep-deprived mathematicians and philosophers on drugs, so I want to test my gut on some weirder cases before I commit to a view.

Here are my verdicts. Case one: you pull an all-nighter, carefully check a long proof twice and get the same answer, then learn you were severely sleep-deprived and that people in that state miss errors constantly. I think you should suspend belief in the theorem, even though the proof still looks valid to you. Case two: you're a climate modeler, your model predicts outcome X, but then you find out 80% of peers with your training disagree. You can point to exactly the simplifying assumptions they make that you reject. I think you should keep believing X, peer disagreement be damned. Case three: you take a pill you know causes irrational reasoning, you feel totally clear-headed, and you derive some conclusion about free will. I think you should reject it flat — no provisional belief, nothing. Case four: you realize you have generic "motivated reasoning" tendencies about politics, and you find yourself believing a politically convenient claim that also has decent first-order support. I think the higher-order worry should barely move you — maybe lower confidence a little, but don't get akratic. Case five: you discover you're in a domain where you've consistently overestimated your competence — like a domain-specific Dunning-Kruger situation — and you form a belief that requires complex reasoning but just "feels" right. I think here you should be fully akratic: hold the belief for practical use but withhold full assent, like keeping it at arm's length.

So is there one principle that captures all five? Maybe something about whether the defeat targets your specific reasoning process versus being generic, or about whether you can reconstruct the argument clean? And what does it say about the case I keep flip-flopping on — where you have good first-order evidence for p, plus specific higher-order evidence that this particular reasoning chain was corrupted, but then you reconstruct the argument from scratch and it still works? Is there a coherent account here, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
