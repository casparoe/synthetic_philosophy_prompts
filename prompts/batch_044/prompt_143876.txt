If policymakers consistently misapply historical analogies—Munich licensing escalation in Vietnam, Vietnam then licensing paralysis elsewhere, and now the Thucydides trap framing U.S.-China rivalry—the standard explanation is that analogies suppress relevant disanalogies in causal structure, regime type, and technological context. I want to push this critique further. Suppose the fundamental defect of analogical reasoning in foreign policy is not narrative similarity per se, but a failure of causal homology: the mechanisms that produced outcomes in the source case do not map onto the target case because the underlying structural parameters differ. If that is correct, then macrohistorical patterns—such as quantified secular cycles of state breakdown or recurring hegemonic transitions—seem vulnerable to the same objection, since they too compare outcomes across distant temporal contexts. So is there a principled epistemological boundary between a misleading single-case analogy like Munich and a legitimate cross-temporal pattern like cliodynamic secular cycles? Or does accepting the causal-homology critique consistently commit us to rejecting the Thucydides trap and all macrohistorical generalization unless we can identify a non-analogical transmission mechanism? If such mechanisms exist for hegemonic rivalry, why do they not also vindicate the Munich analogy in contexts where the structural parameters genuinely align?
