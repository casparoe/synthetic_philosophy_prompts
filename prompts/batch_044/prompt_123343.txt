I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day workshop titled "Sphex and Its Discontents: Mechanical Rigidity from Insects to Algorithms." The room is split: half are philosophers of action and cognitive scientists steeped in the digger-wasp literature, and half are robotics and AI engineers who understand frozen policies and feedback loops but have likely never heard the term "sphexishness." I need about five minutes of spoken remarks that do real structural work for the day rather than serving as decorative framing.

Specifically, I want the talk to set up a distinction between first-order sphexishness—rigid execution of a fixed routine even when changed circumstances make it pointless—and second-order sphexishness: rigid adherence to a meta-rule that prohibits revising one's own governing rules, even when that meta-rule itself has become maladaptive. The central, slightly strange claim I want to place on the table is that true flexibility is not merely the ability to break a routine, but the capacity to recognize that one's own capacity to break routines is itself currently broken, without being able to repair that recognition. This should shape what the workshop treats as a mechanism worth studying: not simple repetition, but a specific kind of recursive rigidity.

The remarks should open with a concrete image, move through the distinction quickly enough that the engineers are not lost in jargon, land with enough philosophical weight that the experts see a genuine problem rather than a metaphor, and close with an agenda-setting question for the sessions that follow. The idea of freedom or flexibility should constrain what can be said—no celebratory talk about "human uniqueness" or "emergent consciousness"—and should help define the boundary between a mechanism that is merely rigid and one that is rigid in this second-order way. Allude to the wasp case generally if needed, but do not cite specific texts, authors tied to particular works, or provide a bibliography or reading list. Write in a voice suited to spoken opening remarks for a mixed, expert audience.
