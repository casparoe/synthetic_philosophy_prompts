I run a small rationalist-adjacent discussion group—mostly people with technical backgrounds who have read some effective-altruism and decision-theory literature but have never taken a formal philosophy course. Next session I want to teach the inefficacy objection in collective-harm cases (climate emissions, meat consumption, voting), but I do not want to retread the standard “one vote doesn’t matter” script. Instead, I want to build the explanation around a framing I have not seen formalized in the usual literature: the distinction between causal redundancy in the actual world and what I will call counterfactual fragility—the property of being a necessary link within a sufficient set across nearby possible worlds, rather than being merely substitutable in the world that actually obtains. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Please structure the plan as follows. First, the dependency order: what idea must be in place before the redundancy/fragility distinction can even be posed, and what must come immediately after so the distinction does not collapse into naive expected-value reasoning? Second, the carrying example: one concrete scenario—preferably not voting or a single hamburger, since those trigger pre-cooked intuitions—that illustrates both actual-world redundancy and counterfactual fragility, and explain exactly what features the example must possess and what it must lack in order to serve this double purpose. Third, the misconception map: the audience will likely arrive with at least three errors—probably that this is just small-probability expected utility, that institutional mediation makes individual causation moot, and that the problem is purely epistemic rather than modal. For each, specify where in the sequence it will surface and the precise move to head it off without derailing the arc. Fourth, one check-for-understanding question that specifically diagnoses whether they have grasped counterfactual fragility rather than just the general idea that collective harm is structurally complicated. Fifth, state openly what should be left out at this level—threshold deontology, institutional-design literature, or formal game-theoretic models—and explain why omitting it is pedagogically necessary rather than a gap. The audience is technically fluent but philosophically naive; the goal is that they leave seeing the inefficacy objection as a modal-structure problem, not a scale problem.
