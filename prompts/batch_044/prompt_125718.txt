I'm writing a paper for my course on Early Modern Philosophy of Education, and I'm stuck on a passage from Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693). Locke compares the young mind to white paper and soft wax, and he insists that habits of virtue—courage, temperance, and respect—must be impressed through repetition, example, and gentle correction long before a child can grasp any rational argument for them. He even warns that reasoning with young children is useless because their understandings are not yet developed. At the same time, he claims that the ultimate goal of education is to produce a rational, self-governing agent who acts from an internal sense of duty rather than from fear of punishment or desire for reward.

I'm trying to understand whether there's a deep tension here: if moral dispositions are formed through pre-rational conditioning—what we might now call heteronomous scaffolding—how does Locke avoid reducing the mature moral agent to a well-conditioned automaton? Does his view anticipate the modern idea of scaffolded autonomy, in which external control is gradually internalized, or does it instead commit him to a kind of moral behaviorism incompatible with the rational freedom he prizes? Could you help me parse the argument in the passage and say whether the tension is real, and if so, how Locke might resolve it? Please respond in American English.
