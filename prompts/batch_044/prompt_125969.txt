You are an expert in political philosophy and institutional design. Think step by step: first identify which conceptual distinctions must survive simplification, then build the reusable procedure, then stress-test it on hard cases.

I was recently appointed to a municipal zoning appeals board that hears objections to multi-family and mixed-use developments every month. I need a thirty-minute decision procedure my board can actually run—ordered questions, what counts as sufficient answer to each, clear stop points—to determine which objections carry legitimate procedural weight under a "right to the city" framework. The framework I want to use distinguishes domiciliary standing (claims grounded in current proximity, use, and local attachment) from civic standing (claims grounded in membership in the urban polity and on distributive effects across the housing system). Because future residents who would benefit do not yet exist, assigning them any form of standing also forces an implicit choice between person-affecting and impersonal theories of justice; the procedure must flag where that choice is being made rather than hiding it.

Please construct the procedure: the sequence of tests, which answers are factual versus normative, where it stops with "no standing," where it mandates full board deliberation because values conflict irreducibly, and where it will predictably misfire. Be honest about what it cannot settle.

Then apply it to three proposals:

1. A neighborhood association objects to a 40-unit affordable housing project near a historic low-rise district, citing shadow, traffic, and erosion of "neighborhood character."
2. A renters' coalition supports the development but demands the board reject it unless 50% of units are permanently affordable, arguing that 20% falls short of distributive justice.
3. A fifteen-year-old informal community garden—untitled, actively tended, the only green space in a dense ward—would be eliminated by a mid-rise development that increases total housing but removes the garden entirely.

In case 1, show the procedure granting procedural weight to an objection I will personally find parochial. In case 3, show it denying weight to an objection I find morally compelling because the gardeners hold no formal title. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly regarding trade-offs between aggregate housing supply and specific, non-substitutable local goods, and regarding the standing of people who do not yet exist.
