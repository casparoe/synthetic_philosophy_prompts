Here is an argument I drafted for a seminar on evolutionary epistemology and philosophy of mind, and now I find it suspicious because the conclusion is far stronger than I intended.

(1) Darwin's theory demonstrates that complex biological competences—such as the vertebrate eye or the navigational capacities of insects—can be produced by evolutionary processes that possess no comprehension of optics, geometry, or purpose. Call this competence without comprehension.

(2) Turing inverts the usual reasoning about minds: instead of inferring intelligence from internal understanding, he treats sustained behavioral indistinguishability as sufficient for attributing cognitive capacity, because the underlying mechanism—whether neural or mechanical—operates through local rules that themselves understand nothing.

(3) Together they establish a general principle: if a mechanism M realizes competence C without possessing feature F, then F is not a necessary condition for C in any system.

(4) Human brains are biological mechanisms that realize language, reasoning, and planning through evolutionary and computational processes that are, at the local level, non-comprehending in precisely the Darwinian and Turing sense.

(5) Therefore comprehension, phenomenal consciousness, or first-person understanding is not a necessary component of human cognitive competence.

(6) If F is not necessary for any instance of C in humans, then F plays no causal or explanatory role in human cognition; it is, at best, an inert byproduct.

(7) By Turing's analogical reasoning—if we attribute mental states to others solely on the basis of behavioral similarity—once we recognize that all behavior can be fully explained without F, we lose all justification for attributing F to anyone, including ourselves.

(8) Hence the Darwin-Turing inversion entails either that consciousness is universally absent or that it is functionally irrelevant, reducing the problem of other minds to a category error and introspective reports to confabulated noise.

This proves too much. I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, but I want to know which step to reject and what that rejection costs—whether it requires abandoning a general principle about mechanisms, revising the analogical argument, or accepting that consciousness is indeed causally inert.
