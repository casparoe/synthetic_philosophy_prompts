I encountered a summary of Milton Friedman’s argument about unrealistic assumptions in scientific and economic modeling—commonly called the “F-twist”—in a philosophy-of-science forum post. The author wrote:

“Friedman held that the realism of a model’s assumptions is entirely irrelevant to its scientific value. He believed unrealistic assumptions are actively beneficial, because stripping away complicating details isolates the core mechanism. This applies uniformly: physics treats planets as point masses, economics treats agents as rational maximizers, and in both cases the falsehood of the premise is harmless so long as predictions hold. Therefore Friedman was a strict instrumentalist: scientific theories are mere prediction-tools with no descriptive ambitions whatsoever. And this entails that if a model predicts well, there is literally no scientific reason ever to revise its assumptions, even once we learn they are false.”

I have a nagging sense that this captures the broad outline but subtly misrepresents Friedman—perhaps by overstating how broadly he applies the claim, by turning a modest predictive thesis into an active endorsement of falsehood, by conflating “as if” selection reasoning with ontological denial, or by ignoring constraints on when unrealistic models are acceptable. But I am unsure exactly where the distortions lie. Could you audit this summary? Specifically: (1) Which parts are faithful to Friedman’s position, which are common simplifications, and which are misrepresentations? (2) Does Friedman claim that unrealistic assumptions are a virtue because they isolate mechanisms, or is his claim more restricted—centered on selection pressures that produce behavior “as if” the assumptions held? (3) Is the inference that he is a “strict instrumentalist” with “no descriptive ambitions” warranted by his argument, or does it conflate predictive success with a denial of ontological commitment? (4) The summary claims predictive success permanently immunizes a model’s assumptions from revision. Is that Friedman’s view, or does he leave room for other scientific considerations—such as whether an unrealistic model is “de-idealizable,” meaning its false assumptions can be relaxed step-by-step without destroying predictive power—to motivate revising them?
