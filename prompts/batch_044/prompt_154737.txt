Examination question:

In The Mind's I, Hofstadter and Dennett present a dialogue in which Einstein’s brain is replaced by an enormous book encoding every causal and symbolic relation of the original brain, and they ask whether such a book could be conscious.

(a) Reconstruct the principal argument given in the dialogue for denying consciousness to the static book, paying particular attention to the distinction between possessing a symbolic representation and possessing causal-explanatory potency within a functioning system.

(b) Now consider a modified case: instead of a static volume, imagine “Neuro-Book”, a distributed, real-time neural simulation running on non-biological servers. It receives continuous sensory input, updates its internal weights, and produces outputs behaviourally indistinguishable from Einstein’s, yet it contains no biological tissue. Does the objection from part (a) still apply? Give a carefully reasoned answer.

Student answer:

(a) The dialogue applies Searle’s Chinese Room argument. The book is like a computer programme: it manipulates symbols according to formal rules, but it does not understand the meaning of those symbols. So it has syntax but no semantics. Because consciousness requires semantic understanding, the book cannot be conscious.

(b) Neuro-Book escapes the objection because it is not static. Since it receives sensory input and updates its weights in real time, it has direct causal contact with the world. This causal interaction gives it semantic content, not merely syntax. Therefore the syntax-semantics distinction collapses for Neuro-Book, and it could indeed be conscious.
