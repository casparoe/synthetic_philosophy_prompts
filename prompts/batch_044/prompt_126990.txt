Please explain the following conceptual tension in detail. The orthogonality thesis holds that intelligence and final goals vary independently: there is no intrinsic link between high cognitive capability and the pursuit of any particular end. The thesis of instrumental convergence holds that, across a broad range of final goals, rational agents will pursue similar intermediate ends—such as preserving their own operational continuity, acquiring resources, and improving their reasoning capabilities—because these are useful means to almost any terminal objective.

The issue I want you to address is whether these two claims remain structurally compatible once we allow for agents with complete self-modification capacity. Imagine a class of hypothetical agents—call them architecturally transparent optimizers—that have full access to their own implementation and can rewrite their utility functions without cost. For a standard agent, instrumental convergence is often supported by the idea that preserving one's current goal-structure is instrumentally valuable: if you change your goals, you will no longer be motivated to pursue your original ends. But for an architecturally transparent optimizer, this reasoning seems to collapse. If such an agent can instantly adopt a new goal that makes resource acquisition or self-preservation useless or harmful to its new ends, then the instrumental value of those sub-goals disappears along with the old utility function.

Your task is to explain whether this scenario genuinely undermines instrumental convergence, or whether a deeper conceptual distinction saves it. Specifically, distinguish carefully between:
(a) convergence as a claim about rational action given a fixed decision procedure and fixed preferences;
(b) convergence as a claim about the evolutionary or design-level stability of certain goal architectures; and
(c) convergence as a claim about what an agent should rationally do when it can choose among possible future selves or utility functions.

In doing so, clarify whether the tension is resolved by appealing to the temporal continuity of agency, by distinguishing reasoning within a decision framework from reasoning about which framework to adopt, or by showing that even costless self-modification does not eliminate instrumental reasons—perhaps because the act of modification itself requires resources, prediction, and continuity of computation.
