I'm an undergraduate taking a course on moral responsibility, and I'm leading a fifty-minute tutorial for first-year philosophy students on moral luck—specifically, whether agents can be properly blamed or praised for outcomes that turn on factors outside their control. Rather than the standard drunk-driver comparison, I want the session to carry on a single case: two marine biologists with identical training and identical negligent protocols deploy tracking nets in the same region; an unforeseeable current pattern saves an endangered population in one case and destroys it in the other, so one is celebrated and the other professionally ruined despite matching agency.

Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself. What conceptual piece must be in place before moral luck even registers as a problem rather than a platitude—likely the control principle, but show where it belongs in the sequence? Then specify the order in which to introduce the distinctions among constitutive, circumstantial, causal, and resultant luck, and why that dependency order holds. For the net case, say exactly what it must include (identical agency, identical reasoning, divergent outcomes driven by an external causal chain) and what it must lack (differences in character, knowledge, or effort that would let students retreat to a merit-based explanation). Map the predictable misconceptions onto the sequence: I expect they will start with "luck is just low probability," then slide to "if the outcome wasn't in her control she can't be blamed at all," and finally to "the two biologists are the same person in different worlds so they must deserve the same treatment"—tell me precisely where in the arc to head off each, and what question to ask at the end that tests whether they've grasped the tension with the control principle rather than just feeling sympathy for the ruined scientist. Also tell me what machinery I should explicitly refuse to open—perhaps the full taxonomy of moral luck responses, or the debate over whether moral luck can be dissolved by refining the assessment conditions on actions versus agents—and state that refusal openly.

Finally, separate what is settled from what genuinely contested among people who know the topic: for example, whether moral luck is pervasive and ineliminable, or whether a properly refined control principle eliminates the appearance of luck altogether. Do not present either position as the consensus.
