Below are notes for an essay I am drafting on Davidson's radical interpretation. The central claim is that Davidson's account of radical interpretation contains an implicit but undertheorized version of the boundary problem familiar from democratic theory: the interpreter must decide whose verbal behavior belongs to the linguistic community being interpreted before charity can be applied, yet Davidson offers no principled criterion for drawing that boundary. I argue that this matters because it threatens to make the principle of charity circular—if we exclude anomalous speakers as not part of the interpretive demos, we risk insulating our scheme from disconfirmation. I also suggest that Davidson's holism about belief and meaning exacerbates the difficulty, since excluding a speaker removes not just one data point but an interlocking web of attributions. I am particularly unsure whether Davidson's response to Quinean indeterminacy already addresses this, and whether importing a political boundary concept distorts the philosophy of language rather than illuminating it. Could you comment on the accuracy of the Davidson exegesis, the coherence of the boundary-problem analogy, and whether the circularity worry is genuinely distinct from familiar concerns about interpretive charity? I would especially welcome suggestions on where the argument overreaches or needs sharper distinction from standard worries about radical translation.
