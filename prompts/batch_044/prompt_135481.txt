Exam question: Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett both reject the commonsense claim that dreams are conscious experiences occurring during sleep, but for different reasons. First, explain Malcolm's verificationist argument that dream reports fail to describe in-sleep events. Second, outline Dennett's view that dream narratives are constructed upon waking rather than replayed from storage. Third, consider whether a predictive-processing framework—in which REM sleep involves strong top-down generative models with suppressed bottom-up sensory error correction—better supports Malcolm's denial of in-sleep content or Dennett's reconstruction thesis.

Student answer:
Malcolm argues that dreams are not experiences because there is no possible way, even in principle, for an observer or the sleeper to verify that a particular mental episode is occurring during sleep. Since meaningful talk about experience requires verification conditions, and sleep removes all criteria for checking, the phrase "dream experience" lacks application to the sleeping state. This fits with Malcolm's broader behaviorist outlook: mental states just are behavioral dispositions, so unobservable inner episodes during sleep do not count as real mental occurrences.

Dennett also denies that dreams happen during sleep, but he explains this with his cassette theory: the brain records dream content like data on a tape during REM, and upon waking the mind plays that tape back and weaves it into a narrative. Dennett later revised this into his multiple-drafts model, but the core idea remains that some stored informational trace must exist to be reconstructed. So for Dennett, there is content, but it only becomes experiential after waking reconstruction.

If predictive processing is right, REM involves the brain generating predictions from the top down without bottom-up error correction. Because there is no sensory input to correct or encode the predictions, nothing is actually stored in memory during sleep. This undermines Dennett's claim that there is a trace to replay, and it supports Malcolm instead: the generative model runs without producing any real phenomenal content, so there is genuinely nothing there to verify. Therefore, the predictive-processing picture favors Malcolm's view over Dennett's reconstruction account.
