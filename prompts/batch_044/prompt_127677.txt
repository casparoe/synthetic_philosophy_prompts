hey im taking a course on medical ethics and my study group got stuck on this chain about advance directives that feels like it proves too much but i cant tell where the gears slip. (1) If a living will is binding, it must be because the earlier self has authority over the later demented self. (2) But authority over another rational being only makes sense if that being is not fully autonomous in that domain, otherwise its just one autonomous agent coercing an equal. (3) So if the directive binds the patient, then the demented patient is not fully autonomous with respect to medical refusal. (4) Now autonomy isnt like a switch that only turns off for ventilators - its a general status. (5) So if the patient lacks autonomy in medical refusal, they lack it everywhere: eating, walking out, talking to family. (6) But in practice we dont treat dementia patients as globally non-autonomous; we let them choose lunch and we only invoke substituted judgment for big medical stuff. (7) So either autonomy is domain-specific, which kills premise (4), or we should actually be controlling every aspect of thier lives based on the old will, which is obviously wrong. (8) But if we reject (4) and say autonomy is domain-specific, then there's no reason the medical domain is special - why does the old self get authority there but not over lunch? (9) So it looks like either we go full authoritarian with the directive, or we admit the directive has no special authority at all. Where do I jump off? I really want to keep that dementia can reduce authority without wiping out the person completely, but I dont know how to block the slide to (9).
