I am preparing an essay for my advanced political philosophy course, and I want to understand why the long-running dispute between cosmopolitans and nationalists over whether compatriots have special moral claims has reached a seeming impasse. The standard positions are familiar: cosmopolitans deny that national membership generates distinctive obligations, while nationalists defend partiality through analogies to family or friendship. What interests me is whether the deadlock is substantive. I suspect the two sides are operating with different conceptions of what makes a relation reason-giving—nationalists treating shared citizenship as an associative bond that structures practical identity, cosmopolitans treating it as a distributively irrelevant feature like eye color—without either side offering a non-circular criterion for when membership generates duties rather than merely permissions. Is this disagreement best understood as verbal, with both sides using special to mean different things, as substantive but masked by shared vocabulary, or as a case in which the parties are actually addressing different questions—individual moral obligation versus institutional design—under one heading? Given your diagnosis, what kind of conceptual distinction, institutional case study, or formal framework for reason-giving relations would count as progress, and is there any reason to expect the debate to move?
