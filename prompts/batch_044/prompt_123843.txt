I'm working through a problem in practical philosophy with a small study group—about eight of us, meeting weekly over dinner—and we've reached an impasse on whether honoring sunk costs can ever be genuinely rational, rather than always falling prey to the fallacy. We've covered the standard economic framing, which treats past expenditure as dead weight for forward-looking agents, but one member pressed a less-discussed challenge: that some goods—narrative unity, loyalty to a long practice, the integrity of a commitment—seem to depend precisely on not treating prior investment as disposable. The cost, on this view, isn't sunk in the sense of being irrelevant; it is partly constitutive of the very thing being valued. For our closing session, I want a poem that takes a definite side in this dispute, not a survey of options. Write a sonnet in iambic pentameter with a strict ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme scheme and a clear volta at line 9. In the octave, establish the orthodox position with concrete imagery: rational agents should look only forward, past losses are phantom weights, continuing out of loyalty to what has already been spent is a kind of bondage to dead accounting. At the volta, pivot sharply to the counter-argument: that there are commitments whose value is diachronic, existing only across time and through loss, and that to abandon them prospectively is to misunderstand the kind of good they are. The closing couplet must commit unambiguously—either defend honoring such sunk costs as rational because they preserve the continuity of the self or the practice, or reject the counter-case and insist that even narrative goods must be weighed by future benefit alone, with prior commitment carrying no special authority. Make the structural turn mirror the philosophical one: the rhyme scheme's shift should feel like a change in the direction of reasoning, from calculation to continuity. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with debates in diachronic rationality recognizes the stakes, but compelling as verse on its own. After the poem, include a brief, frank paragraph noting exactly where the argument takes liberties with philosophical precision—particularly regarding whether personal identity over time can bear the normative weight the poem assigns it—and stating clearly which contested view of the self, say a narrative or reductionist picture, the poem implicitly relies on.
