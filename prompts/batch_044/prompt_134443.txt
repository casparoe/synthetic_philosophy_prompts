I am a PhD student in philosophy of economics leading a 75-minute graduate workshop (about ten participants) on pure time preference—the claim that future goods may be rationally weighted less heavily solely because they occur later, not because of risk, growth, or epistemic uncertainty. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, state the conceptual dependency: what distinction must be secured before pure time preference can appear as a substantive normative thesis rather than a tautology or an economic convention? Second, propose one carrying scenario or model—explicitly not the standard exponential discount curve—that should carry the idea; specify what features it must possess and what it must avoid so that participants feel the pure rate as a live ethical choice. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: conflation with risk aversion, uncritical spatial-distance analogies that treat temporal location as ethically analogous to spatial separation, and the assumption that any positive rate requires instrumental justification. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted. Fourth, design a brief diagnostic exercise for the final ten minutes that reveals whether participants have grasped the pure/impure distinction rather than merely repeating it. Fifth, incorporate a framing device that is not standard in introductory treatments: present intertemporal neutrality as a symmetry principle analogous to spatial neutrality in ethics, then show where the analogy structurally breaks because temporal position is indexical in a way spatial position is not. Be explicit about what this framing illuminates and what feature of pure time preference it risks distorting. Finally, state what should be explicitly omitted at this level—such as formal aggregation over infinite populations or social-choice frameworks for intergenerational welfare—and acknowledge that omission.

The session should not require prior reading of specific texts and should avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
