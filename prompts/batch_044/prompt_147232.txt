I'm running a ninety-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates and computer-science graduate students who arrive convinced that talk of "moral responsibility" is mostly folk-psychological confusion. I want them to grasp Strawson's claim that reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, forgiveness—are not rewards or sanctions appended to libertarian freedom, but the very relational medium in which moral responsibility lives, and that the "objective attitude" is not a moral upgrade but a shift in how one relates to another being. Don't explain the theory to me; plan how I should teach it.

First, lay out the dependency order: which idea must be secured before reactive attitudes can be introduced, which must come before the objective attitude can be distinguished from mere "cool-headedness," and what must follow so they don't collapse the distinction into "emotional versus rational." Second, specify the single carrying example: I want to build it around a hiring algorithm that rejects a qualified candidate because of an opaque, unreviewable weighting error, asking whether we can resent the system, its designers, or neither. Say exactly what this case must include (opacity, human oversight failure, direct harm) and exclude (a single identifiable wrongdoer with clear intent) to do the philosophical work, and explain why a classic interpersonal betrayal—say, a friend breaking a promise—would fail as the central case for this audience. Third, map the misconceptions I should expect and intercept: that reactive attitudes are switchable emotional states; that the objective attitude is just clinical analysis; that determinism forces us to adopt the objective attitude toward everyone; and that Strawson is offering a compatibilist rescue of responsibility by redefining freedom. For each, identify the point in the sequence where it will surface and how to defuse it without derailing the arc. Fourth, give me one end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether they understand moral responsibility as anchored in the quality of a relation rather than in a metaphysical freedom condition. Fifth, state explicitly what I should refuse to cover—whether the full taxonomy of reactive attitudes, the comparison with Kantian autonomy, or the later literature on moral luck—and justify why leaving it out protects the core idea rather than simplifying it away. The plan should fit ninety minutes with no advance reading.
