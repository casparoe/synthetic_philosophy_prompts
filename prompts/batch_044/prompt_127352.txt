I'm preparing the opening address for my school's interdisciplinary symposium on causation, scheduled for next Thursday in the main auditorium. As a senior who has been studying Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism in an independent philosophy course, I have been asked to set the frame for the day's panels — but I want to avoid the standard classroom summary that merely notes God as the direct cause of every event. Instead, I want to structure the address around a less familiar comparison: treating Al-Ghazālī's denial of secondary causation alongside a specific reading of statistical interventionism, in which causal claims depend on an agent's capacity to intervene on one variable while holding others fixed, and in which natural objects possess no such capacity. The audience will be mixed — philosophy faculty, skeptical science teachers, and fellow students — so the speech must be formal, accessible, and about six minutes long, with occasionalism doing genuine structural work rather than serving as decoration. Specifically, it should determine why I speak cautiously about scientists discovering causal laws rather than describing invariant habits, why I question whether human moral responsibility can be grounded in causal chains if those chains are not genuinely ours, and why I close with an open question about whether any non-divine agent can truly do anything. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions; I want a polished speech that uses this framing to shape what can and cannot be said about explanation, blame, and agency.
