Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in the philosophy of urban land use: (A) proponents of a “spatial justice” view that treats restrictive zoning as an unjust exclusion from a collective good of urban accessibility, drawing on a “right to the city” framework modified by a claim-right versus liberty distinction in property theory; and (B) defenders of a “localist proceduralist” view that treats neighborhood-scale democratic control over land use as a non-commodifiable aspect of collective self-determination, resisting both pure market allocation and centralized planning, but accepting a land-value-tax framework for fiscal capture as a separate issue. The document should include: a numbered section of agreed claims about the empirical dynamics of zoning (e.g., supply constraints, displacement effects, fiscal zoning incentives) and the normative premises both accept (e.g., that adequate housing is essential, that local knowledge has value, that racial and class exclusion has occurred through zoning); a disagreements table with precise formulations of their divergence, phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position, focusing on whether zoning rights are claim-rights held by residents or liberties held by owners and developers, and whether “the city” is a single moral community with distributive obligations or a federation of neighborhoods with associative rights; the cruxes—specifically, whether naturalness or historical continuity of neighborhood character carries independent moral weight, and whether predictive models of displacement can be trusted enough to justify preemptive upzoning; and for each crux, a concrete hypothetical case or empirical argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s predicted evaluation of that case recorded in advance. Format as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Ensure the formulations are neither vacuous nor slanted, and identify any portion of the dispute that may be verbal rather than substantive.
