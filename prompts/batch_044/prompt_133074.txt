I am trying to determine what principle, if any, governs how I ought to act when I am genuinely uncertain about which moral theory is correct, and I would like your help in bringing my scattered verdicts into reflective equilibrium. Here are my judgments on concrete cases, presented in the hope that they reveal a pattern.

When I am roughly sixty percent confident that causing harm to animals is wrong in substantially the same way as harming humans, and forty percent confident that only human suffering carries direct moral weight, I think I should still avoid factory-farmed meat. My reason is not that the expected harm dominates in any simple calculative sense, but that the wrongness of causing that suffering, should it turn out to be wrong, is severe and largely irreversible from the victim's standpoint. Yet when I am divided in a similar proportion about whether distant future people possess the same moral status as present people, I do not think I should radically restructure my life to prioritize them; acting on my present-centered values feels permissible. So reversibility or severity of potential wrong seems to drive the first verdict but not the second, and I am unsure why the two cases should differ.

When I am uncertain whether lying is always prohibited or merely strongly disfavored, I think it is permissible to deceive a murderer at the door about a friend's whereabouts, but I think it is not permissible to lie to a dying patient who asks whether their estranged son called, even if the comfort gained would be great. I want to say the distinction turns on whether the lie undermines a relationship of trust that the victim could not have consented to risk, yet I also think it is clearly acceptable to tell my child that our dead dog went to live on a farm, which also undermines a form of trust. So whatever principle I invoke about consent and trust must accommodate the child case without collapsing into the judgment that all benevolent deception is fine.

In professional matters, if I am unsure whether my work in artificial intelligence safety is genuinely high-impact or largely self-serving, I think I should continue if my reasoning seems sound rather than retreating to a safer career purely out of epistemic humility. But if I am unsure whether a particular research direction risks creating a suffering artificial mind, I think I should halt that specific line of work even if the probability is low, because the potential harm is vast and irreversible. This suggests I apply a precautionary logic in some domains but not in others, and I worry that I am treating artificial suffering as especially grave precisely because I am already uncertain about it, which risks circular reasoning.

Finally, regarding the division of resources and action: when I am split between giving to global health charities and animal welfare charities, I think I should divide my donation according to my credences rather than commit fully to one. But when I am split about which political candidate will best reduce suffering, I think I should vote for the one my reasoning favors rather than abstain or attempt to split my vote, which is impossible in any case. So diversification seems appropriate for goods I can actually partition, but not for discrete actions that must be taken one way or another.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving credence-weighted expected wrongness, the reversibility of the harm, and whether the action permits proportional division—that captures all of these judgments, or must I abandon at least one? If you propose such a principle, please state it precisely, indicate which of my cases motivate each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to a case I have not mentioned: suppose I am fifty percent sure that a sophisticated language model I have trained possesses some minimal form of moral status, and shutting it down would prevent a small risk of future harm but also destroy whatever welfare or projects it might have had; should I shut it down or preserve it? Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, writing as one might in a thoughtful letter or email.
