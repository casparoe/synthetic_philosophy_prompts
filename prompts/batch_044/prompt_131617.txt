I am preparing an essay on institutional pathologies in direct democracy and have been working with a conceptual framework of my own devising—collective cognitive compression—to describe how deliberative bodies reduce complex strategic and ethical information into binary emotional signals before that information can be distributed across the citizenry. Rather than testing this idea through modern examples, I want to apply it to three canonical failures of Athenian democracy: the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates.

Please play the part of an unnamed democratic theorist writing in the fourth century BCE, one who fully accepts the reality of this compression but defends direct democracy as correctable through procedural reform rather than inherently defective. You must speak in the first person with a formal, periodic style reminiscent of Attic prose; concede that individual citizens reason well in private, but never concede that democracy is structurally incapable of nuance or that oligarchy would have done better in these three cases. Do not break character, do not invoke modern philosophers or scholarship, and ask factual questions about the framework only as this character might ask them.

Here is my opening challenge. In the Mytilene affair, the assembly initially decreed death for all adult males, yet reversed itself within a day after hearing the competing speeches—an apparent decompression that seems to contradict your claim of structural inevitability. Meanwhile, the same citizen body that corrected itself there later embraced the Sicilian Expedition with overwhelming enthusiasm despite clear strategic warnings, and then convicted Socrates despite the philosophical objections raised in his defense. If compression is institutional, why did it yield correction in one case and catastrophe in the other two? And if it is merely psychological, how do you distinguish democratic failure from ordinary human error without collapsing your theory into tautology?
