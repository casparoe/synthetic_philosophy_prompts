As someone who has recently taken on responsibility for assessing escalation dynamics in long-term institutional commitments, I find myself returning to the logic of the dollar auction. Please analyse the 1965–1968 escalation of United States ground commitment in Vietnam through the lens of escalation-of-commitment and dollar-auction theory. I want a disciplined mapping: identify the effective bidders, specify what prize was being contested, set out the payoff rules that made withdrawal more costly than continued bidding at each stage, and show how the Gulf of Tonkin resolution and the 1968 Tet Offensive altered the auction structure rather than terminating it. Then, with equal rigour, tell me where the model breaks: does it explain the Johnson administration's refusal to withdraw despite private pessimism, or does it miss bureaucratic incrementalism, domestic audience costs, and — crucially — the possibility that the North Vietnamese leadership was running a parallel domestic escalation game? In particular, examine whether this was not a single bilateral auction but a nested structure in which both sides were locked in separate domestic bidding loops. Present the answer in British English, without bibliographies, citations, or reference lists; I want the conceptual analysis and its limits, not a survey of the literature.
