I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy of history, and I find myself caught between two images of the historian's task that seem to borrow from scientific practice. In seminars and popular accounts, I often hear historical research compared to the reconstruction of a damaged mosaic: the fragments are the archival traces, the grout is the historian's interpretation, and the picture that emerges is either the original scene — Ranke's aspiration that history should reveal how things actually happened — or merely one coherent pattern among many that could be assembled from the same pieces — the scepticism of Carr and Hayden White, who stress selection, emplotment, and the narrative construction of meaning. The analogy is usually deployed to defend the idea that, although perfect objectivity is impossible, history remains a constrained reconstruction rather than fiction — or, conversely, to argue that if mosaic-building is always underdetermined, then Rankean objectivity is an illusion.

Recently, however, I have begun to wonder whether a different analogy — drawn from my own background in evolutionary biology — captures the problem more precisely, and I would like you to audit both. In phylogenetic reconstruction, we infer ancestral states from extant lineages and fragmentary fossil data using models that are explicitly underdetermined: the same morphological evidence can support multiple trees, and the choice among them depends on prior assumptions about evolutionary rates, convergence, and the weight given to molecular versus morphological signals. The reconstructed ancestor is not found like a buried object but computed as the best-supported node in a theoretical space, and different scientific communities can defend incompatible reconstructions without anyone being dishonest.

Can you lay out, first, how far the mosaic analogy holds: what corresponds to the fragments, the grout, the original image, and the viewer's eye; where it breaks down — particularly regarding whether the historian's connective interpretation is merely filling gaps or actively shaping the fragments' significance; what normative or epistemic commitments the mosaic smuggles in about the existence of an original whole; and whether the analogy actually supports the intermediate conclusion that history is a constrained reconstruction, or whether it collapses into either Rankean realism or Carr/White-style narrative constructivism once its disanalogies are exposed.

Then, second, do the same for the phylogenetic-reconstruction analogy: map the archival traces to the data, the historian's theoretical framework to the evolutionary model, Ranke's aspiration to show how things actually happened to the inferred ancestral state, and Carr's emplotment to the choice of phylogenetic priors. Where does this second analogy break — for instance, in the fact that evolutionary models aim at predictive or explanatory coherence across living lineages, whereas historical narratives aim at particularistic understanding of non-repeating events? Does it quietly import a scientific realism that privileges one tree over others in a way that historiography cannot justify? Crucially, does either analogy survive the audit well enough to license a specific position on historical objectivity, or does the comparison itself carry the argumentative weight? If one analogy is superior, say which; if both fail, suggest what a more honest analogy would look like.

Please be precise about which features of the source domains — mosaic assembly, phylogenetic inference — lack counterparts in historiography, and avoid treating either analogy as a mere illustration.
