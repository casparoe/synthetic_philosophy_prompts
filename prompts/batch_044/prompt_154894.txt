I am drafting a chapter that situates early modern accounts of well-being within current analytic debates about objective-list theories versus modified hedonism. I keep returning to Émilie du Châtelet's treatise on happiness, particularly her claim that illusion is not merely an epistemic failure to be overcome but in some sense constitutive of happiness for finite, embodied beings. Some interpreters treat this as a conventional synthesis of Epicurean moderation and Stoic resignation, while others argue it reflects a distinctive metaphysical position informed by her reading of Leibnizian pre-established harmony and her explicit rejection of Wolffian rationalist optimism.

Write an essay that weighs whether du Châtelet's position is better reconstructed as a form of prudential hedonism qualified by necessary self-deception, or as an anticipation of objective-list well-being in which certain metaphysical illusions operate as irreducible goods. In reaching a conclusion, attend closely to her treatment of love, ambition, and the passions, but do not treat the issue as purely historical exegesis: defend a substantive verdict about the normative architecture of her view, and address the strongest objection—namely, that her account either collapses into pure subjectivism or commits her to an implausible metaphysical realism about happiness itself.
