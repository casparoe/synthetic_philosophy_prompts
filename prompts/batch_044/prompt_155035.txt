I run a 75-minute graduate seminar in political philosophy with about 20 students. I want them to experience, before I name it, the central tension in the socialist calculation debate: a central board can adjust prices iteratively in response to aggregate surpluses and shortages, yet still fail to capture the dispersed, partly inarticulable local knowledge that producers hold about particular conditions of time and place. The mechanics—not my explanation—should make this failure tangible.

Design a runnable in-class simulation. Split the group into a Central Pricing Board (3 students) and Sector Managers (the rest, each running one factory). Each manager receives a hidden “local condition” card describing an unobservable bottleneck or opportunity specific to their site—information that can affect optimal output but cannot be fully encoded in a message to the board. The board sets prices for two intermediate goods (steel and coal) at the start of each round and can revise them after observing only aggregate order totals and inventory levels, not the hidden cards. Managers then choose production quantities for a final consumer good, earning points for meeting a private local target (tied to their hidden card) plus a “profit” calculated with the board’s prices. The board earns points for minimizing aggregate imbalance and for reaching a socially optimal total output. In round two, introduce an “innovation” option: a manager may switch to a more efficient technique, but only if they reveal part of their hidden condition to the board—doing so improves price accuracy for everyone but costs that manager private points.

Please structure your response as follows:

1. Simulation rules and round structure (about 250 words): roles, materials, sequence of three rounds, and exactly how information is partitioned between board and managers.
2. Payoff scheme and strategic tension (about 150 words): the point system, why managers face a real choice about revelation, and how the board’s adjustments systematically miss the mark.
3. Debrief mapping (about 200 words): link specific moments in play—e.g., the board “fixing” a shortage by raising a price that was already wrong for local conditions—to the logic of iterative price adjustment on one side and the dispersed-knowledge objection on the other; state clearly what students should come away seeing.
4. Predictable failures and fixes (about 150 words): what will go wrong the first time (e.g., managers revealing everything to be cooperative, the board solving too quickly), and how to correct it without destroying the point.
5. Necessary simplification (about 100 words): identify one feature of the actual debate—such as the epistemic status of entrepreneurial discovery or the computational limits of general equilibrium—that this simulation necessarily flattens, and tell me exactly what I should say verbally to supply it.

Keep everything self-contained: no bibliographies, no assigned readings, and no citations. Do not quote Hayek, Lange, or Mises verbatim; paraphrase their concepts in your own words. Avoid references to obscure secondary literature that depend on precise scholarly attributions.
