I've been trying to understand the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: if a system is powerful enough to encode arithmetic, Löb's theorem shows it cannot consistently assert its own soundness without contradiction, which is often taken to mean that sufficiently capable AI cannot rationally endorse its own reasoning. Recently I encountered the claim that this obstacle extends even to defeasible, meta-cognitive policies—specifically, any belief-revision rule whose reflective closure includes something like "if I can derive that my reasoning is sound, then I should increase my confidence in my outputs." The argument is that once an agent's belief states are arithmetized, such a policy collapses into a Löbian loop regardless of whether the self-trust is probabilistic or heuristic. But this seems to blur the line between formal derivability and empirical self-monitoring: couldn't an agent treat its own track record as ordinary inductive evidence, maintaining graded confidence without ever encoding that confidence as an arithmetized axiom? Where exactly does the arithmetization necessarily creep in to block this kind of non-formal self-trust, and does the obstacle genuinely show that rational self-endorsement is impossible for strong agents, or only that it cannot be self-certified within the formal system?
