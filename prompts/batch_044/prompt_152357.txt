I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been attempting to read philosophy of language and science with the same rigor I once applied to experimental design. I have composed a brief essay—really a set of structured conceptual notes—on deflationary theories of truth, and I would welcome direct critical comments on its coherence, accuracy, and philosophical interest.

The essay proposes an analogy between deflationary accounts of truth, particularly those that treat the truth predicate as redundant or purely expressive, and the concept of gauge symmetry in theoretical physics. In physics, a gauge symmetry indicates a redundancy in mathematical description: two states related by a gauge transformation are physically identical, and the transformation itself corresponds to no observable difference. I suggest that deflationary truth functions analogously at the level of language. To assert that a proposition is true adds no empirical content beyond the assertion of the proposition itself, just as applying a gauge transformation adds no physical content beyond the state already described. On this view, the truth predicate is not a property that tracks an independent feature of reality, but a conventional coordinate—similar to the choice of gauge—necessary for logical coordination but metaphysically inert.

From this analogy I derive three claims for evaluation. First, that deflationism should not be interpreted as either realism or anti-realism about truth, but rather as a claim about linguistic economy, comparable to the physicist's claim about descriptive redundancy. Second, that the distinction between redundancy theories and minimalist theories—those that allow substantial disquotational principles without reifying truth—may be less significant than usually supposed, since both can be understood as identifying different degrees of gauge freedom within discourse. Third, I tentatively propose that truth operates like a calibration convention in measurement science: indispensable for comparing results across contexts, yet not describing an intrinsic feature of the measured system, much as the definition of the meter has shifted from a material artifact to a constant of nature without altering its practical function.

Please comment specifically on whether this analogy illuminates or distorts the philosophical issues. Does comparing truth to a gauge transformation illegitimately import a mathematical structure into a linguistic practice? Have I mischaracterized the difference between redundancy and minimalism, or collapsed distinctions that philosophers have good reason to preserve? And does my calibration-convention conclusion avoid substantive metaphysics, or does it conceal a commitment to conventionalism about reality that requires independent defense?

Treat the essay as an amateur but serious attempt: point out conceptual mistakes, identify where the physics analogy misleads, and indicate whether any part of the argument strikes you as genuinely worth developing rather than merely confused. I do not require bibliographic suggestions, reading lists, or citations—only direct philosophical criticism.
