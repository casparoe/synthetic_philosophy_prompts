I've been thinking about trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect, but specifcally in how we design fail-safe mechanisms in distributed systems - like when a load balancer has to drop one request to save the cluster, and the question is whether it's okay if that specific request dies as a side effect versus being the target of the kill signal. I think the DDE distinction actually maps pretty cleanly onto software: intended harm is when the casualty is literally in your optimiztion target, while foreseen-but-not-intended is just a constraint boundary being hit. So my position is that for algorithim ethics, DDE isn't just philosophical fluff - it's a structural feature you can verify by inspecting the loss function and the call stack. What would count against this? I want to know if there's formal work showing that in practice you can't separate the causal chain from the optimiztion target in real code, or if the distinction collapses once you factor in maintainance updates and emergent behavior. Also, has anyone argued that in human moral psychology the DDE distinction doesn't actually track intent but just tracks physical proximity or something? Rank the defeaters for me - fatal versus just awkward - and say whether defenders of DDE in software ethics would concede any of them. I'm especially curious if this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, where every counterexample gets explained away by redefining what counts as 'intended' in the architecture.
