I have been participating in a seminar that takes up the old question of whether the Enlightenment should be understood primarily as a source of modern progress—democratic politics, empirical science, and universal human rights—or as the intellectual origin of modern domination—colonial administration, racial taxonomy, and the instrumental rationalization of nature and society. After months of discussion, the debate has reached an impasse that feels structural rather than merely stubborn. Both sides routinely invoke the same broad archives: the tension within Kantian universalism between cosmopolitan aspiration and racial classification, the statistical and cartographic projects that served both public health and imperial governance, and the revolutionary declarations of liberty that coexisted with plantation slavery and the violent suppression of colonial uprisings. Yet no interpretation shifts, and the same objections circulate unchanged.

My working hypothesis is that the stall arises because the two camps are operating with incompatible but largely unstated models of how ideas relate to institutions. One camp treats Enlightenment reason as essentially a normative capacity—critical self-reflection and universal moral judgment—that was historically corrupted by slavery, empire, and patriarchy but remains conceptually separable from them. The other camp treats reason as inseparable from its institutional practices of classification, measurement, and documentation, so that the production of universal categories was simultaneously a technology of control that made populations and territories administratively legible. If that is right, then the dispute is not about whether colonial violence occurred—everyone grants that—but about whether reason can be conceptually purified from its embedded practices of ordering the world. At the same time, I wonder whether the disagreement is partly verbal: both sides use Enlightenment and reason as umbrella terms, but one is asking a genealogical question about the conditions under which these concepts gained authority, while the other is asking a normative question about whether we should still endorse them.

Given that framing, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a hidden empirical disagreement about causation—did Enlightenment ideas cause domination, or did domination instrumentalize them? Is it a substantive metaphysical disagreement about whether normative concepts can be abstracted from their material histories? Or is it a case of two different inquiries—one in intellectual history, one in normative philosophy—being forced under a single heading? Please engage the positions concretely, not as a survey of familiar names, and tell me whether any conceptual clarification, historical finding, or shift in framing could actually move the debate. In particular, would it help to distinguish procedural rationality, understood as a method of justification, from structural rationality, understood as a mode of governance? Would progress require separating the evaluation of Enlightenment ideals from the genealogy of Enlightenment institutions? Or is the impasse a sign that the framing itself is ill-posed, and that asking whether the Enlightenment was good or bad collapses distinctions that should be kept apart? Finally, if the disagreement is indeed about what counts as data—whether institutional outcomes should weigh in the assessment of conceptual content—then what, if anything, could resolve that meta-level conflict?
