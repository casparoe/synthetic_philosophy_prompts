I am designing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy seminar on narrative ethics and the representation of the dead, and I need one essay question that students could answer in about ninety minutes, carrying thirty marks. I want the question to be unanswerable by anyone who has merely memorized standard objections about consent, defamation, or artistic license in biopics—since the dead cannot legally be defamed and never signed release forms—so it should center on a specifically engineered tension. Present this scenario: a filmmaker releases a biopic of a real, recently deceased climate scientist, using her actual name and public reputation; the script invents a private marital crisis that never occurred and uses it to “explain” her decision to suppress a controversial dataset, even though her real motives were complex and partly epistemic. The film is marketed as “inspired by true events,” carries appropriate disclaimers, and is artistically coherent, but it systematically misrepresents the structure of her scientific reasoning for dramatic effect. Ask students to determine whether a distinct wrong occurs here—one that is not reducible to harm to the deceased (who cannot suffer), harm to viewers (who are warned of fictionalization), or a generic duty to historical accuracy—and to analyze whether that wrong is best understood as a moral failure of “narrative restitution” with respect to her self-interpretive authority, an aesthetic failure of distorting the epistemic logic of scientific dissent, or an irreducibly hybrid failure. They should also address whether any such wrong scales differently for widely known public figures versus relatively private ones, and whether the filmmaker owes a specific duty to the dead scientist’s intellectual legacy rather than only to living audiences.

Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit conceptual moves, with marks allocated for each (totaling thirty) and a one-line note on the typical student error that costs marks at that point. In both the model answer and the scheme, clearly separate what is philosophically settled—such as the legal non-status of posthumous defamation and the absence of direct experiential harm to the dead—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether deceased persons retain robust moral standing or “dignity interests,” and do not present any contested thesis (for example, that narrative restitution is a genuine moral category) as though it were the consensus view.
