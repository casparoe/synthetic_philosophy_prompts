Wittgenstein's remarks in On Certainty identify a class of hinge propositions—such as that one has two hands or that the earth existed long before one's birth—that are not themselves objects of knowledge but instead constitute the fixed framework within which knowledge-claims operate. Because these hinges are not justified by other beliefs, they appear to be epistemically inert: they carry no evidential weight of their own, yet they bear the entire load of empirical justification. This raises a puzzle about what I will call distributed hinges: in a scientific community, different subgroups may treat different propositions as hinges, for instance the reliability of electron microscopy versus the stability of statistical sampling. If hinges are genuinely framework-constitutive rather than belief-constitutive, then a disagreement between two research programs about which propositions stand fast is not a disagreement about evidence but a disagreement about the conditions under which evidence counts at all. But does this not collapse Wittgenstein's distinction between hinges and ordinary empirical beliefs? After all, if two physicists can rationally disagree about whether quantum field theory functions as a hinge or as a revisable hypothesis, then the hinge-status of that theory seems to depend on rational assessment rather than on its being taken for granted. So is Wittgenstein forced to admit that hinges are, after all, subject to rational evaluation—merely evaluation of a different kind—or does he have the resources to maintain that such disputes are always, at bottom, expressions of different forms of life rather than internal epistemic disagreements?
