I have been working through an argument about collective memory that seems to dissolve the very idea of a duty to remember. (1) Following Margalit’s framework in The Ethics of Memory, societies have a moral duty to remember injustices done to victims, grounded in solidarity rather than utility. (2) Because this duty is collective, it must be distributable: no single person can hold an entire society’s past in active consciousness. (3) Distribution permits specialization—archivists, educators, and descendants bear the load—while others contribute only indirectly. (4) In conditions of perfect digital preservation, the institutional framework can maintain records with virtually no ongoing human cognitive investment; the archive becomes effectively self-sustaining. (5) If the institutional framework is intact, the collective duty appears satisfied even if every living person has personally forgotten, since the memory remains structurally available and the solidarity is preserved in form. (6) But this severs the duty from its relational core: Margalit’s solidarity requires living agents who stand in ethical relation to the dead, not merely functioning servers. (7) To block this reduction, one might require a minimal quantum of active personal remembrance—say, that some living members must actually know and care. (8) Yet any specific threshold seems arbitrary: why must remembrance be distributed among five percent rather than one-tenth of one percent of the population? The figure appears administratively, not morally, determined. (9) So the duty either collapses into an institutional maintenance obligation that could be fulfilled by automated systems alone, or it demands an unprincipled quota of human awareness. (10) From this it seems to follow that there is no coherent collective duty to remember—only a choice between bureaucratic storage and arbitrary mnemonic rationing. I want to keep (1) and (6), because the relational intuition feels right and the digital-reduction worry is genuinely troubling, but I am unsure whether (5) or (8) is the weaker link. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does Rieff’s case for forgetting in In Praise of Forgetting help stabilize the argument, or does it accelerate the collapse?
