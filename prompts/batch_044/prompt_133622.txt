Construct a diagnostic decision tree for a philosophy-of-mind seminar that classifies positions on Jackson's knowledge argument without relying on labels that a student could simply pick from a list. The root node should ask whether Mary, on leaving her black-and-white room, acquires genuinely new propositional knowledge about color experience, as opposed to only gaining a recognitional ability or practical skill. From there, build branches that distinguish: (i) non-physicalist views that take the new knowledge to reveal a non-physical property of experience; (ii) physicalist phenomenal-concept strategies that treat the knowledge as old physical information grasped under a novel, directly referential mode of presentation; (iii) the ability hypothesis and its variants, which deny any propositional novelty altogether; and (iv) an injected comparison case—replace color with a purely structural property such as the chiral topology of a molecule that Mary can fully describe physically but never perceptually encounter—to test whether the knowledge gap collapses when phenomenal vocabulary is removed. Add a further branch that asks whether the residual gap, if any, is best explained by an indexical shift (analogous to learning "I am here" rather than learning a new fact about location) rather than by missing descriptive content. Each leaf must carry a named or unnamed position, a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, and a flag if two positions your tree cannot separate. The tree should contain at least six leaves and should be phrased so that each node is a concrete claim or case comparison a student can answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for external sources; the instrument should be usable by someone who understands the debate but has no web access.
