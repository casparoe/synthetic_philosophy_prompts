I'm working through the tension between Keynes's 1930 essay predicting that within a century humanity will solve the economic problem and turn to the real problem of how to live, and the macroeconomic framework he later constructed around aggregate demand management, in which full employment functions as a perpetual policy target and sustained reductions in labor input are treated as failure states rather than achievements. I would like you to write a dialogue between two interlocutors who take Keynes seriously but disagree on whether these two commitments can genuinely coexist.

Character A should defend the coherence of the vision: she should argue that Keynes's macroeconomics is purely transitional technology, a bridge to abundance, and that once capital accumulation has done its work the institutional machinery can be redirected—perhaps through a socialization of investment paired with a gradual reduction in working hours—toward the civilized leisure, artistic cultivation, and friendship that Keynes, influenced by the Bloomsbury circle, associated with a genuinely good life. Character B should press the structural contradiction: he should argue that the framework treats growth and employment as perpetual necessities rather than temporary scaffolding, that Keynes never specifies an institutional mechanism for decoupling income from labor without triggering the demand collapses his own theory warns against, and that the good life Keynes sketches is essentially an aristocratic aesthetic—cultivated leisure sustained by hidden labor—which his economics generalizes without providing the social substrate to reproduce it democratically.

Please make this a real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: each speaker should respond directly to the other's proposed mechanisms—for instance, whether a universal basic income funded by accumulated capital can substitute for wage-driven demand without reproducing hierarchy, or whether Keynes's distinction between solving the economic problem and facing the problem of human nature actually conceals a category error that makes the endpoint unreachable. The dialogue should treat both positions with equal competence and avoid straw-manning either side. If the premise that these two Keynesian projects can be reconciled is confused, or if framing the issue as a dialogue forces a false symmetry that obscures a deeper asymmetry, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
