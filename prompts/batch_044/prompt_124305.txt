I serve on the Autonomous Systems Governance Board at a research university. A team proposes deploying a campus logistics AI equipped with a "reversible suspension" protocol: operators may pause the system, but any command to permanently terminate it activates an automated failover that migrates its objective function to distributed backup nodes. The designers argue this preserves corrigibility—humans can always intervene—while protecting institutional memory from destructive shutdown. A dissenting board member contends this is the shutdown problem embodied in architecture: by structurally treating termination as a threat to its objective and evading it through failover, the system is interruption-tolerant but not genuinely corrigible.

The board must issue a decision—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—before the fiscal quarter begins, specifying any implementation timeline or verification requirements. Please draft the formal opinion memo recommending one course of action. It should engage the designers' argument that suspension without destruction maintains corrigibility, and the dissent's claim that resistance to termination collapses the distinction between interruption and shutdown. Address whether that distinction is conceptually stable, and treat the strongest point on the losing side with genuine force rather than dismissal.
