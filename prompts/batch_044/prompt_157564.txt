Write the full specification of desiderata that an adequate theory of the moral authority of advance directives must satisfy—specifically, directives issued by a competent adult that purport to govern medical decisions after the issuer has lost decision-making capacity through dementia, brain injury, or progressive psychiatric illness. I want the criteria, not a literature survey.

List the phenomena the theory must accommodate: (i) classic refusal cases (e.g., a previously competent patient with advanced dementia who is now content and appears to enjoy simple interactions, but the directive refuses artificial nutrition); (ii) psychiatric advance directives in which an earlier self refuses antipsychotic medication that a later self, in acute psychosis, seems to need for safety; (iii) long-dormant directives drafted decades earlier that conflict with the patient's recently expressed but now-incompetent preferences; and (iv) directives that demand aggressive intervention rather than refusing it. For each, state the exact normative result the theory should deliver and why that result is required rather than optional.

Then specify the distinctions the theory must preserve without collapse: between the authority of a directive and its evidential weight as one clue among others about prior preferences; between having the standing to issue a binding refusal and having the standing to issue a binding demand for treatment; between a change in values that undermines authority and a mere change in taste that does not; and between incompetence that extinguishes present autonomy and incompetence that merely makes present autonomy unreliable. Give the case or conceptual reason behind each.

Next, identify the empirical and psychological constraints the theory must be consistent with—such as evidence that preference stability declines with age and cognitive impairment, that many patients with dementia adapt and report positive affect contrary to their prior predictions, and that psychiatric conditions can distort both present judgment and retrospective evaluation—without letting the theory reduce to a purely best-interests calculus.

Finally, state which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the dominant competing accounts (precedent autonomy, substituted judgment, and pure present-best-interests views), and rank the remaining requirements by which are genuinely non-negotiable versus which could be relaxed if they jointly conflict. Keep the scoring of specific named theories brief; the weight should be on constructing and defending the criteria themselves.
