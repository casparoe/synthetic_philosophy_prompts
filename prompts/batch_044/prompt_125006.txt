I'm writing a feature on the theoretical architecture behind predictive processing, and I need to unpack a specific tension for readers who already grasp the basics. In the free-energy formulation, an agent selects policies by minimizing expected free energy, which combines pragmatic value—preference satisfaction—with epistemic value—uncertainty reduction. The conceptual difficulty arises when we consider policies that restructure the agent's own generative model: for instance, undergoing intensive training that temporarily spikes prediction error across hierarchical levels, or integrating a neural prosthesis that reweights the precision of sensory channels. Can the framework coherently represent an agent that deliberately accepts a short-term increase in variational free energy—because the restructuring is projected to lower expected free energy over longer horizons—without implicitly invoking a second-order evaluator that stands outside the generative model? More precisely, does the formal distinction between variational free energy (the current bound on model evidence) and expected free energy (the projected cost of a policy) genuinely do the work of licensing such self-modifying choices, or does it collapse once the model being evaluated is identical to the model being altered? I want a rigorous, intuitive explanation of where the conceptual fault line lies.
