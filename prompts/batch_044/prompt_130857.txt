I am preparing a graduate seminar on determinism and chaos, and I have constructed an argument that strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to swallow the very reasoning that produced it. I should be grateful if you could diagnose precisely where it fails.

(1) If determinism holds, then every present state of a physical system is fixed by prior states together with the laws of nature.
(2) Chaotic dynamics are deterministic: they evolve according to precise differential equations.
(3) Nevertheless, in such systems the separation between initially close trajectories grows exponentially, with a characteristic Lyapunov time beyond which predictive access is lost.
(4) Any actual cognitive agent — human or artificial — is physically embedded and therefore subject to the same chaotic dynamics at the microscopic scale of neural or computational substrates.
(5) Consequently, the specific trajectory that produces any given act of reasoning (say, the derivation that chaos undermines determinism) is exponentially sensitive to fluctuations that lie permanently beyond the agent's epistemic horizon.
(6) If a reasoning process is such that its specific conclusion is fixed by fluctuations that are, in principle, inaccessible to the reasoner, then the reasoner has no rational basis for preferring that conclusion to any other that might have emerged from a neighbouring trajectory.
(7) Therefore, if determinism is true, no reasoning process — including the argument for determinism itself — carries normative force; it is merely the inevitable output of a dynamical path.
(8) But if an argument lacks normative force, it cannot provide a reason to accept its conclusion.
(9) Hence determinism, if true, undermines the rational warrant for believing determinism.
(10) The same reasoning applies to every domain of inquiry, since all physical cognition is embedded in chaotic substrates.
(11) So determinism proves too much: it entails that no belief, scientific or otherwise, is ever rationally justified.

My instinct is to resist (11), but I am unsure whether the fault lies in the slide from (5) to (6), in the identification of deterministic causation with the absence of normative structure, or elsewhere. Which step should be rejected? Please explain why it initially appears plausible, and spell out what accepting or rejecting it costs for our understanding of rational agency within a deterministic — and specifically chaotic — physical world. I should like the response in British English, with clear attention to whether the flaw is an equivocation, a scope error, or a modal confusion.
