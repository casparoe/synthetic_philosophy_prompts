Construct a taxonomy of positions on the demandingness objection to act-consequentialism, arranged as a branching tree in which each split is labeled by a concrete claim or case rather than by a philosopher's name. Begin with whether morality can ever require an agent to bear a genuinely burdensome sacrifice; if yes, split on whether the central problem is the sheer magnitude of that sacrifice (cost-based demandingness), the lack of any stopping point for moral optimization (structure-based demandingness), or the crowding-out of special relationships and personal projects (prerogative-based demandingness). Next, branch on the proposed remedy: whether one resolves the problem by adopting a scalar or rule-based form of consequentialism, by appealing to an institutional or collective division of moral labor, or by accepting the high demands and revising our expectations of moral agency altogether. The leaves must include the standard agent-centered prerogative view, the scalar consequentialist position, the institutional-division revisionist, the view that demandingness is a feature revealing our moral cognition is calibrated for small-group cooperation rather than global impartiality, and at least one combination that is unoccupied or rarely defended—note it and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every node should be phrased as a question or case that a respondent could answer without knowing the philosophical labels, and the instrument should contain no bibliographies, citation requests, or author-based sorting.
