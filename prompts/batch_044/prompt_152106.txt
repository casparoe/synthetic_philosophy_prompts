Question: The paradox of fiction asks why we experience authentic emotional responses—such as pity or dread—toward characters and events we firmly believe to be non-existent. One proposed resolution introduces the idea of "epistemic insulation": the claim that our belief in a fiction's unreality is housed in a practical-reasoning cognitive module that is temporarily disconnected from the affective module during narrative immersion. Explain how this proposal attempts to dissolve the paradox without invoking pretend-beliefs or Kendall Walton's make-believe account. Then evaluate whether this resolution succeeds, paying particular attention to whether modular separation actually removes the philosophical tension or merely relocates it.

Student answer:

The student begins by stating that Radford's paradox consists in a direct contradiction within our belief-attitude structure: we believe Anna Karenina does not exist, yet we feel pity for her, and these two states cannot rationally coexist. The epistemic insulation model resolves this by assigning the belief to one module and the emotion to another, so the two never interact; there is therefore no contradiction, only parallel processing. This allows us to reject both pretend-beliefs and Walton's theory, since we do not need to pretend the story is real—the belief remains genuinely held, it is simply blocked from reaching the affective system in time to suppress the response. The student then extends the model to predict that first-person narration produces stronger emotional effects, because presenting a character's thoughts in the first person lowers the insulation barrier between the practical-reasoning module and the affective module, allowing mental-state information to flow directly into emotional processing.
