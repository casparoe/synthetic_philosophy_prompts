I'm grading exams for an advanced philosophy-of-mind course and I'm trying to calibrate rigor the way one calibrates belief updates: penalizing subtle missteps that survive a quick scan but don't hold up under closer inspection. The exam asked students to explain, in about 250 words, how Dennett's multiple drafts model addresses temporal integration, whether extending his critique of the Cartesian theater to a so-called Cartesian calendar—the assumption that conscious experience requires a globally synchronized present—is textually supported, and how fame in the brain relates to the apparent unity of the present moment. Here is one submission: Dennett rejects the Cartesian theater because there is no central observer in the brain where experience comes together. Instead, he proposes the multiple drafts model, in which parallel sensory streams generate distributed narratives that are never fused into one canonical version. This also eliminates the Cartesian calendar, since without a central theater there can be no global now that binds all inputs together. The color phi phenomenon demonstrates that the brain can construct temporal order retrospectively, which proves synchronization is unnecessary. In Dennett's account, content becomes conscious by achieving fame in the brain—widespread access across cognitive modules—which means temporal unity is just a case of one draft gaining enough fame to dominate the others. The drafts therefore vote on which timeline becomes the experienced present, though there is no central counter tallying the votes. This explains split-brain cases, where two streams of awareness persist without a calendar to reconcile them. Overall, Dennett dissolves both spatial and temporal Cartesian errors by treating consciousness as distributed, asynchronous, and narrative-dependent.

Please assign a specific grade and justify it with point-by-point comments, separating settled interpretations of Dennett from genuinely contested ones and never presenting a disputed reading as consensus. Deliver the assessment as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, and avoid meta-hedging about grading difficulty.
