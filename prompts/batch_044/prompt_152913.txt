I take von Neumann's argument in "Can We Survive Technology?" (Fortune, 1955) to heart: technological progress is irreversible, and societies that cannot adapt to the destructive potential of their own capabilities tend to perish. From this I derive a stronger, less familiar claim—call it strategic opacity: democratic societies facing existential-level technologies (advanced synthetic biology, superintelligent systems, etc.) must deliberately conceal certain research trajectories and capabilities from both adversarial states and their own publics, because in these domains transparency is not a civic virtue but an evolutionary liability. What would count against strategic opacity? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness: historical analogues where openness or secrecy actually determined survival, formal arguments about innovation under concealment, institutional evidence that democratic accountability can survive partial opacity, and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability (e.g., "any failure of opacity was insufficient opacity"). For each, indicate what a defender of the view should honestly concede versus dismiss, and assess whether insulating the position this way is a problem for a claim of this kind. Please answer in American English.
