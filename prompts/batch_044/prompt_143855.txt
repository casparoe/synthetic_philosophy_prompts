hey so i was at the natural history museum last weekend and there was this exhibit layout that kept bugging me. there's one whole wing about "the evolutionary origins of morality" - kin selection, reciprocal altruism, social instincts getting selected in primates, the usual evolutionary psych stuff. they present moral behavior like just another adaptive trait, with phylogenetic trees and diagrams showing when cooperation branched off. then there's a totally seperate wing about religion and human culture, framed like religion is basically a cognitive byproduct or a spandrel, nothing to do with ethics. the two never talk to each other.

but i was reading about darwin's own views, and apparently he thought the moral sense was literally the highest distinction between humans and lower animals, yet he was also worried that explaining morality through natural selection might weaken its authority or make obligations feel less absolute. so the museum is presenting evolutionary ethics as totally straightforward and value-neutral, while sticking religion in its own box, almost like they assume that explaining where morals come from doesn't touch whether they're objectively binding, and that religion has no part in that question.

what philosophical commitments does that curatorial separation actually presuppose? does keeping the morality exhibit and the religion exhibit apart commit them to a view where evolutionary origins and normative authority just don't interact? or is there an implicit naturalism about ethics that quietly needs some non-natural foundation to do the work darwin thought religion might do? reconstruct the implicit theory behind the layout: what does it assume about moral knowledge, about whether origins affect validity, and about whether darwin's own tension was a real philosophical problem or just a personal hangup? and tell me whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined - could several rival theories license the same separation - or whether it strictly rules out views where morality and religious belief are intertwined. if darwin's worry was legit, that evolutionary explanations might undermine the sense that moral obligations are absolute, then what would have to change in how museums or textbooks present this? would they need to intergrate the two wings, or just be more honest about the gap between explaining morality and justifying it?
