I was chatting with two old colleagues from my physics days — well, one’s now a mathematician and the other’s doing a philosophy doctorate — and they got into a proper row about expected value fanticism. I’ve only just started an evening course on practical rationality since I retired, so I was mostly listening with my coffee going cold. The mathematician said that if you genuinely take unbounded utility seriously, then any action carrying a nonzero chance of producing infinite wellbeing dominates all ordinary moral choices, which collapses ethics into a kind of lottery-ticket madness. The grad student pushed back saying this only bites if you reject a stability constraint on utility functions — she called it the thermodynamic ceiling idea, where just as entropy statistically forbids macroscopic reversals, a rational decision rule should scale the weight of tail outcomes by the informational resources needed to realise them.

The mathematician said she was committing a category error: decision-theoretic probabilities are epistemic, not physical frequencies, and her ceiling just smuggles in an arbitrary cutoff that solves the problem by fiat. She came back that she wasn’t proposing a hard cutoff at all, but a renormalised expectation where each world-state is weighted by its algorithmic complexity, so wildly implausible utopias get downweighted not by prejudice but because they require more bits to specify. He replied that this is just Bayesianism with an extra complexity penalty, and if the penalty is finite then in the limit the fanatical conclusion returns anyway. They both refused to budge. Could you adjudicate point by point who is right about which specific claim, and what, if anything, both are missing?
