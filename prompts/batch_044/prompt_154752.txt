I've been trying to understand why the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution gets used in climate bargaining models instead of Nash. From what I gather KS splits the surplus so each side gains the same proportion over their disagreement point, which sounds fair. But I also read KS satisfies individual monotonicity — meaning if one player's fallback option improves, the solution can't make them worse off. So if a poor country suddenly develops cheap carbon capture and their disagreement payoff jumps, KS protects them from being squeezed, whereas Nash just reweights the product and lets the stronger fallback dominate. That makes KS sound conservative in a wierd way: it anchors on the status quo fallback rather than the actual feasable frontier. Am I getting monotonicity backwards? Or is the argument for KS in climate talks exactly that outside options shouldn't drive the bargain?
