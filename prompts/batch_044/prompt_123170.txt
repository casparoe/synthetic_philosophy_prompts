For a philosophy of social science seminar, examine the institutional practice of publishing global happiness rankings—where nations are ordered by aggregate scores drawn largely from self-reported life-evaluation surveys, and where policymakers are encouraged to treat a higher rank as a legitimate objective of governance. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments this practice presupposes, distinguishing those it strictly requires from those it merely accommodates. First, what theory of well-being is embedded in the choice to privilege subjective life-satisfaction metrics over objective-list indicators, capability measures, or preference-satisfaction accounts? Does aggregating individual responses into a national score presuppose that well-being is interpersonally comparable in at least an ordinal fashion, and what epistemological view licenses cross-national comparison? Second, what conception of state legitimacy is implied by treating happiness maximization, rather than justice or autonomy, as an appropriate policy target? Consider whether the practice assumes liberal neutrality: could a government legitimately improve its rank by altering citizens' adaptive preferences or distributing mood-altering interventions without changing objective conditions, or does the ranking implicitly depend on distinguishing authentic from inauthentic well-being? Identify internal tensions—such as between promoting cross-national competition for rank and simultaneously acknowledging deep cultural variation in conceptions of the good life. Finally, determine whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a perfectionist, a capability theorist, or a hedonist each endorse the same methodology without contradiction, or does the metric quietly rule out non-subjectivist accounts? And should a country actually try to top such rankings, or does the very framing of governance as rank-optimization presuppose a reductive view of political purpose?
