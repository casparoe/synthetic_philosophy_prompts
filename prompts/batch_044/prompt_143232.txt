For a 90-minute graduate seminar of roughly twelve participants, design a structured simulation that makes the force and the fragility of historical materialism tangible—specifically the problem of reverse determination, in which superstructural categories (legal, scientific, or ritual) do not merely reflect an independently given economic base but actively redefine what counts as part of that base. Divide the group into three estates—producers, administrators, and interpreters—each with hidden incentives. Over three rounds, they must collectively set a resource-extraction rule, choose a research priority, and establish a ceremonial obligation, but the facilitator silently introduces a “base condition” (a technological constraint and a scarcity threshold) that shifts between rounds based on the prior round’s superstructural choices; for example, the legal framework adopted in round one may retroactively classify a previously non-extractable material as a productive resource, thereby altering the very “economic base” the group thought it was responding to in round two. Provide explicit roles with private information, a strict timeline (20 minutes deliberation, 10 minutes decision, 10 minutes revelation), and payoff rules tied not to individual victory but to collective coherence. After the simulation, include a debrief that maps each round onto Marxist concepts—base, superstructure, relative autonomy, determination “in the last instance”—and ask participants to identify the exact moment they felt causation running downward versus upward. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the long diachronic span across generations that historical materialism typically requires, and the macro-statistical patterns that no small-group game can generate; also warn that participants will likely confuse the facilitator’s engineered rule shifts with genuine emergent causation, and advise how to correct that misreading without undermining the conceptual point. The goal is not to prove or disprove that the economic base explains everything else, but to make participants feel why the claim is either too strong—because the base is never available independently of the categories used to describe it—or too vague to apply cleanly to discrete institutional choices. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the conceptual rigor should come from the rules and the debrief alone.
