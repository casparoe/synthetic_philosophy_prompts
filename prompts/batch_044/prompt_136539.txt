You are a professional philosopher of history and social science with expertise in causal explanation and conceptual analysis. Please think carefully and, before giving your final diagnosis, briefly outline the main positions as they actually appear in the literature and in seminar debate. I am currently taking an advanced course on the philosophy of social explanation, and our discussions have stalled in a frustrating way over whether Max Weber's thesis linking the Protestant ethic to the development of modern capitalism offers a genuine causal account or merely describes an elective affinity between cultural values and economic structures. The empirical historians in the room insist that capitalism had material precursors in Northern Italy and that correlation is not causation; the Weberians reply that Weber never claimed monocausality and that his notion of adequate causation operates at the level of meaningful action rather than law-like regularity; and the philosophers keep asking whether values can enter the causal order of material events without committing a category mistake. No one changes anyone's mind, yet no one thinks the issue is settled.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily empirical—there are no hidden data that would resolve it—but conceptual and disciplinary, with both sides using the word cause to answer different questions: one about the mechanisms behind a specific historical transition, another about whether cultural meanings can be constitutive of the very phenomena they explain. I want you to diagnose this disagreement concretely: is it verbal, is it about hidden standards of evidence, are the sides answering different questions under a single heading, or is it bedrock intuition about the autonomy of culture? And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress—would it require a conceptual distinction between efficient and constitutive explanation, a formal model of how values interact with material conditions, or an admission that the question is ill-posed? In particular, please address whether there is a substantive disagreement disguised as methodological—say, about whether social explanation must reduce to individual actions, or about whether values can be overdetermining causes in the same ontology as technological change.

I am also struck by a framing that has not come up in our course readings: the idea that Weber's defenders and critics might not be disagreeing about history at all, but about whether explanation with values requires values to function like physical forces in an efficient-causal chain, or whether they can operate as what we might call frame-conditions that make certain economic behaviors intelligible without pushing them like levers. If this distinction captures part of the impasse, does it resolve anything, or does it simply relocate the disagreement—perhaps to the question of whether intelligibility itself is a kind of causal power? Give me an honest assessment of whether this framing moves the debate, whether it collapses back into standard interpretations of Weber, and whether any real progress should be expected on this front given your diagnosis.
