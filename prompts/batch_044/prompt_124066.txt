hey, im writing a short essay for my phil seminar comparing german and american folk morality — specifically how each culture weights rule-following vs individual conscience when they clash. my working thesis is that german folk morality runs on what im calling "normative intertia": the idea that breaking a rule carries moral cost not just because of harm done, but because it degrades a shared "coordinated expectation space." american folk morality, by contrast, tends to treat rules as coordination tools with a "default permitted" logic: actions are okay unless they cause direct harm, and conscience can override bureaucratic rules.

im trying to avoid the usual weber/haidt summaries and instead look at everyday scenarios: queue-jumping, minor workplace violations with no victim, and whistleblowing on technically legal but unethical practices. my claim is that german intuition sees the first two as morally loaded even without harm, while american intuition asks "who got hurt?" and treats whistleblowing as heroic precisely because it breaks rules for conscience.

here are my rough notes / draft structure:

1. german "normative intertia" treats rules as having standing, not just instrumentality.
2. american model treats rule-breaking as neutral or positive if aligned with internal conscience.
3. comparison applies to queue-jumping, minor policy violations, whistleblowing.
