I'm taking a seminar on post-war political philosophy, and we've been working through Karl Jaspers's well-known distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. A fellow student has built an argument about intergenerational collective guilt that strikes me as both compelling and suspicious, because if it holds it seems to prove far too much. I'd like to know exactly which step fails, and what it costs to reject that step, especially since she wants to preserve certain premises.

(1) If a state commits systematic wrongs and its citizens identify with that state's continuity, then the citizens bear political guilt—not merely legal liability, but a genuine moral burden requiring acknowledgment and repair. (2) Political guilt attaches to membership in a collective moral subject rather than to individual causal contribution; a citizen need not have voted for the regime or directly participated in its acts. (3) States are diachronically persistent entities: the political community of 1960 is the same collective subject as that of 1940, just as a corporation survives the replacement of every employee and shareholder. (4) Therefore, citizens in 1960 inherit the political guilt of 1940, since they belong to the same guilty collective subject that committed the wrongs. (5) If guilt can be inherited through membership in a diachronic collective, then it attaches to every successive generation that maintains membership, including those born long after the wrongs ended. (6) But if someone born in 1980 is genuinely guilty for 1940 atrocities—despite having no causal role, no capacity to have prevented them, and having been born into the collective involuntarily—then moral responsibility no longer requires agency or causal contribution. (7) If agency and causation are not necessary conditions for guilt, then membership in any persistent collective—ethnic, religious, national, or even human—makes one guilty for all wrongs ever committed by that collective. (8) So every person alive today is guilty for every historical atrocity committed by groups they belong to, which collapses moral guilt into an indiscriminate, universal stain and destroys its normative function. (9) Therefore, either collective political guilt does not exist, or it cannot be transmitted across generations.

My classmate says she is most attached to (2) and (4): she thinks collective subjects can genuinely be guilty and that membership in a persisting state matters more than individual action. She rejects (8) as absurd. I want to understand whether the error lies in the diachronic identity claim in (3), in the inheritance principle in (5), in the agency condition in (6), or elsewhere—perhaps in an unnoticed slide between different kinds of guilt. Where exactly does the argument slip, and if we block that slide, can we still maintain that a nation can be guilty across generations without proving that every human being is guilty for everything?
