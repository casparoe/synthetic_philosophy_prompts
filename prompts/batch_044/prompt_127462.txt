I'm working through my intuitions about when non-human forces—pathogens, climate regimes, volcanic events—ought to count as genuine "historical actors" rather than as background conditions or as determinist explanations, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments or whether some of them have to be abandoned. Here are my concrete verdicts.

The Black Death in fourteenth-century Eurasia: I think treating Yersinia pestis as the actor that "ended feudalism" is environmental determinism and should be rejected. The pathogen was a trigger that exposed pre-existing structural tensions over labor and land; the genuine actors are the human institutions that renegotiated obligations in the presence of scarcity.

The 1783 Laki eruption in Iceland, whose sulfuric haze caused crop failures and famine in France during the years immediately preceding 1789: I think the volcano is not an actor in the French Revolution. It was an exogenous stressor, but revolutionary agency belongs entirely to the political actors who interpreted and responded to scarcity.

The 1815 Tambora eruption and the "Year Without a Summer" of 1816: I think the volcano counts as an actor, but only within cultural and literary history—specifically, in causally shaping the confined conditions that led Mary Shelley to write Frankenstein—whereas in political or economic history it remains mere background.

The 1918 influenza pandemic in the United States: I think the virus acts as an agent in the transformation of gendered labor patterns, because its age-specific mortality depleted the male workforce in ways that pre-existing structural conditions alone would not have produced. It is not determinism, since human institutions mediated the response, but the virus is not reducible to background either.

The prolonged drought of the Classic Maya collapse around 900 CE: I think treating climate as the "cause" of state fragmentation is determinism I want to reject, yet I also feel that without the drought the specific trajectory of collapse—particularly the rapid abandonment of lowland centers—would not have occurred. So climate is neither pure background nor a full agent.

The coupled shock of the 536 CE volcanic winter—likely from a high-latitude eruption—and the first Justinian Plague pandemic: I think neither the volcano nor the plague is individually the actor. Rather, the compound event functions as an actor only because it crossed a mortality and agricultural threshold that neither shock alone would have reached. I am unsure whether this is a principled exception or whether it undermines my other verdicts.

Finally, a case I keep revisiting: if an El Niño–Southern Oscillation event contributed to the 1876–79 global famine, should it be treated on the model of Laki (background stressor), on the model of Tambora (domain-specific actor), or on the model of the 536 compound (threshold-dependent agent)?

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving institutional mediation, domain restriction, substitutability, or threshold-crossing—that captures all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause governs which case, and predict its verdict for the 1876–79 El Niño famine. If not, offer the smallest family of principles that accommodates them, and be explicit about which of my judgments must be overturned. In either case, flag the case among those listed that your account handles least comfortably.
