I'm part of a small agricultural cooperative that has relied for fifteen years on the planting and pest-management protocols left by our former coordinator, Elena. Elena had no formal training in agronomy or ecology; she developed her schedules through decades of trial and error, adjusting crop rotations and beneficial-insect releases by what she called "listening to the plot." Her notebooks are mostly illegible—sketches of root architectures, weather notations, and cryptic phrases like "when the clover stalls, move the brassicas." She could never articulate a general principle behind a decision, yet her yields and soil-health metrics consistently outperformed neighboring farms that followed university extension guidelines. After she retired, we digitized her protocols as best we could, but the system remains essentially her opaque intuition made procedural.

This year, one of our members—who has a background in software engineering—introduced an optimization tool that generates planting schedules through evolutionary computation: it breeds candidate schedules against historical yield data, selects for performance, and iterates across generations. The outputs are similarly opaque; not even its designers can fully reconstruct why a particular rotation pattern was selected, only that it performs well in simulation and, now, in our pilot beds. The cooperative is divided over whether we should gradually replace Elena's legacy protocol with the algorithmic one. A vocal faction argues that since both are black boxes—neither Elena nor the software can explain its reasoning—there is no epistemic difference between them, and we should choose purely on efficiency metrics. I feel this equivalence misses something crucial, but I cannot say whether my resistance is just romantic attachment to Elena's human touch, or whether there is a deeper distinction between a competence that evolved organically without comprehension and one that was engineered to simulate competence without comprehension. How should we think about the authority of these two systems? Is there a principled reason to privilege one form of unreasoned success over the other, or should I accept that my discomfort is merely sentimental?
