Consider an institutional risk-management practice—call it the Damascus Protocol—adopted by several logistics and safety boards. Under this protocol, if a predictive model issues a conditional forecast that a specific, identifiable action (for example, routing a shipment through a particular corridor or deploying a system update) will result in catastrophic failure, that action is automatically prohibited. Crucially, the model's accuracy is then audited only against the set of actions that were permitted and executed; the prohibited branch is never observed, so the forecast is never directly falsified. The protocol treats the avoidance itself as evidence that the model "worked," yet it also assumes that had the action been taken, the disaster would indeed have occurred.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of prediction, causal counterfactuals, and rational avoidance does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it implicitly endorse a specific view of self-defeating prophecies, or does it conflate the epistemic and causal roles of forecasting? Reconstruct the implicit theory of predictive authority embedded in the protocol, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, say, with both a causal-interventionist reading and a purely evidentialist one. Write your answer in American English.
