I have been working through the literature on the extended mind thesis and the debates that followed the original parity argument, and in recent seminar discussions I have repeatedly encountered a particular form of resistance that I cannot place within the standard taxonomy of objections. The pattern runs as follows. A proponent of cognitive extension points to a case in which a person relies on an external structure — for instance, a mathematician who has developed a bespoke symbolic notation maintained only in a dedicated notebook, or a clinician who depends on a long-evolving shared database to guide diagnostic reasoning — and argues that this structure functions in precisely the same way that an internal belief store or inference mechanism would. The opponent then concedes the functional equivalence but denies that the artefact is part of the mind on the grounds that it lacks a particular status: not accessibility to consciousness, not biological implementation, but something closer to normative authority or epistemic binding. The opponent typically grants that if the very same representational content and inferential role were fully internalised — say, through prolonged memorisation or neural integration — it would possess that status and thus count as genuinely mental. The denial therefore hinges not on functional difference but on a kind of location-sensitive metaphysical condition: being inside the skull, or being part of the biological system, is treated as a necessary precondition for acquiring the normative or epistemic property in question, even when the functional profile is held constant. I have seen this move made in discussions of software-dependent expertise and of collaborative cognitive systems, but I do not know whether philosophers of mind have named and analysed this specific configuration.

Is there an established concept for this location-sensitive normative denial of functional parity? If so, how does it relate to the coupling-constitution fallacy, to concerns about trust and glue conditions, and to biological internalism? If not, I should like a precise definition that distinguishes it from plain internalist intuition and from standard objections about causal coupling, together with clear boundary cases and an explanation of whether it ultimately collapses into one of the more familiar objections or stands as a distinct failure mode. Please reply in British English, and avoid offering a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in the conceptual carving rather than in references.
