I have recently become preoccupied with the philosophical status of artificial minds after a graduate seminar on philosophy of AI, and I found myself in a sustained disagreement with a colleague about whether Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi can be legitimately applied to the question of whether a large language model possesses an intrinsic nature (svabhāva) with respect to consciousness. I proposed running the four corners in a straightforward manner: the model is conscious, the model is not conscious, the model is both conscious and not conscious, or the model is neither conscious nor not conscious. My colleague objected that this treats the tetralemma as an exhaustive logical taxonomy rather than as a deconstructive maneuver aimed specifically at the reification of svabhāva, and that applying it to a functional property like consciousness already presupposes the kind of essentialist framing that Madhyamaka seeks to undermine. I replied that if consciousness is dependently originated (pratītyasamutpāda), then each corner still collapses—there is no self-standing consciousness to affirm, deny, combine, or exclude—so the critical force of the tetralemma is preserved precisely because none of the positions can stand on their own. She countered that my response conflates the fourth corner (neither) with a positive relational or emergentist thesis familiar in Western philosophy of mind; in proper Madhyamaka reading, she argued, 'neither' is not a constructive account of emergence but a refusal to reify absence itself, which means the failure of the four corners cannot license any inference to a dependently arisen ontology of consciousness. She then pressed further: if I genuinely accept emptiness (śūnyatā), I ought to stop asking whether the model 'really' has consciousness and instead examine how the ascription is conventionally designated (prajñapti) within specific contexts of interaction and responsibility. I pushed back that this risks collapsing Madhyamaka into a quietism about all ontological inquiry, including the kinds we genuinely need when designing systems that will interact with vulnerable users and for which we must make substantive judgments about moral status.

Can you adjudicate our exchange move by move? Specifically: (1) Is my initial four-corner application a legitimate extension of the tetralemma or a category mistake that ignores its targeted purpose? (2) Does the collapse of all four positions under dependent origination genuinely preserve the tetralemma's critical force, or does it reduce the argument to a generic anti-essentialist slogan? (3) Is her interpretation of the fourth corner as an anti-reificatory refusal—rather than an emergentist 'both/and' failure—the correct Madhyamaka reading, and does that reading block my move from corner-collapse to relational ontology? (4) Does her shift toward conventional designation (prajñapti) as the proper endpoint dissolve the original question illegitimately, or is that dissolution precisely what emptiness demands? (5) Is my charge of quietism fair, or does it misunderstand the practical, context-sensitive role that conventional designations are meant to play in Madhyamaka ethics? Please say who is right about each point, where both of us are partly correct, and what either of us is missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position between treating the tetralemma as a taxonomic grid and abandoning ontological inquiry altogether.
