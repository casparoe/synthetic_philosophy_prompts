After an extended and rather heated discussion last night with a close friend about whether nations have any genuine pre-modern roots, I want us to formalize our disagreement into a constructive adversarial-collaboration document rather than continuing to talk past each other. Our debate concerns the invention of tradition and the imagined-community thesis, but it has settled on a specific crux that standard treatments often glide over.

Please draft the joint statement that a Radical Modernist and a Mnemonic-Substrate Perennialist could both sign after good-faith collaboration.

Camp One, the Radical Modernist, holds that nations are essentially modern political artifacts consolidated after the long eighteenth century; that traditions are deliberately fabricated by elites in response to bureaucratic and print-capitalist demands; and that any appearance of deep historical continuity is an illusion produced by selective archival preservation and retrospective narration. This camp accepts that collective identification can be mass-produced, but insists there is no meaningful mnemonic substrate prior to modernity that constrains which inventions succeed—state power and media technology are sufficient explanations.

Camp Two, the Mnemonic-Substrate Perennialist, agrees that nations take modern political form and that traditions are actively constructed, but denies that invention occurs in a vacuum. This camp posits durable mnemonic substrates—long-cycling ritual calendars, vernacular religious geographies, linguistic isoglosses, and territorial cult structures—that persist across centuries as structurally stable palimpsests. On this view, an invented tradition achieves mass adherence only when it maps onto these substrates, and collapses when it does not; national identity is therefore not ex nihilo but a crystallization of older memory-forms that are not themselves national yet become nationalized.

The document should include the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—covering the constructedness of national tradition, the role of print and administrative rationalization, the inadequacy of purely primordialist accounts, the observable variance in how quickly different invented traditions take root, and the fact that some subnational regions resist national identification despite identical state policies.

2. A precise formulation of the residual disagreement, with Camp One’s position phrased in wording Camp One would accept and Camp Two’s in wording Camp Two would accept. The core divergence should be whether mnemonic substrates have independent causal-constraining force or are merely post-hoc rationalizations selected by successful state projects.

3. The cruxes—I take there to be at least three:
   a. Whether the differential success of national narratives across regions correlates more strongly with pre-existing substrate alignment than with state capacity and coercive resources alone.
   b. Whether archival silence in pre-modern periods indicates an actual absence of large-scale collective identification or merely reflects preservation biases that systematically erase non-elite mnemonic practices.
   c. Whether regions where long-cycling ritual calendars were disrupted by colonial or revolutionary intervention show measurably weaker national identification under otherwise identical institutional conditions.

4. For each crux, specify the concrete comparative case, natural experiment, or evidentiary pattern that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the finding will be.

5. A brief concluding section identifying whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether disagreement over the word substrate can be dissolved by re-description without loss of predictive content—and, if so, stating exactly which part and why.

Format the agreed claims as a numbered list; present the disagreements in a table with columns for Issue, Camp One Formulation, and Camp Two Formulation; and present the cruxes as a numbered list with sub-items for the shared evidentiary case and the paired predictions. Maintain a rigorous, neutral, formal tone throughout. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers; treat Hobsbawm and Anderson only as conceptual touchstones, not as textual authorities to be quoted.
