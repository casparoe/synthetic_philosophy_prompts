Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps interpreting T.M. Scanlon’s contractualism in relation to the aggregation problem. Name the parties precisely: Camp A, the Pairwise Baseline Contractualists, who hold that the reasonableness of rejecting a principle is assessed for each affected individual against a baseline that does not include other people’s parallel burdens, so that multiplying small harms across a population never aggregates into justification for imposing a large harm on one; and Camp B, the Structural Interdependence Contractualists, who hold that in institutional and collective-choice contexts the baseline for each person already includes the structural presence of others, so that evaluating whether a principle can be justified to each requires comparing patterns of burden across the affected group, and what looks like aggregation is actually pairwise comparison against interdependent baselines.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims—shared commitments about what contractualism requires (justification to each, not maximization of aggregate welfare; the role of reasonable rejection; the rejection of pure utilitarian trade-offs; the distinction between harming one to prevent harm to others versus distributing unavoidable burdens). Follow this with a disagreements table: for each remaining dispute, state the precise formulation that Camp A would sign for its own position and the precise formulation that Camp B would sign for its own position, using neutral wording for the framing of each row.

Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case or line of reasoning both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts or concludes about that case. Include at least these three cruxes: (1) whether the baseline for measuring burden in Scanlon’s formula can include the presence or absence of other burden-bearers without collapsing into utilitarian aggregation; (2) whether an individual’s knowledge that she is one of many similarly situated can enter into what she can reasonably reject, or whether such knowledge is irrelevant to pairwise justification; (3) whether there is a principled distinction between “counting numbers” and “counting structural patterns” in institutional design, and if so, what formal feature distinguishes them.

For the concrete test case, use the following constructed scenario, which both camps should treat as genuinely bearing on the dispute: the Distributed Archive Protocol, in which a shared institutional system must either preserve 10,000 individually minor but collectively irreplaceable cultural records (each tied to one specific community) at the cost of one researcher’s life-saving project being halted, or preserve the researcher’s project at the cost of all 10,000 records being lost. Both camps should state whether the contractualist can justify the first option to the researcher, whether she can justify the second option to any one record-holder, and whether the multiplicity of record-holders changes what any one of them can reject.

Conclude the joint statement by indicating, for each disagreement, whether it is substantive or partly verbal—specifically, whether the dispute over “aggregation” dissolves once both sides accept that the structural-interdependence view does not license sacrificing one for the greater good in the utilitarian sense, and whether the pairwise-baseline view can accommodate institutional reasoning without contradiction. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific page numbers. Do not ask the camps to agree on a final verdict; the goal is an exact map of shared ground and precisely located residual disagreement.
