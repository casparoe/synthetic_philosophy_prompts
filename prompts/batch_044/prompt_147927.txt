After a long and unresolved argument with a friend last night about whether an artificial system can genuinely possess what you called Sinn, I want to test my objections through your own framework rather than through contemporary jargon. Please play Moritz Schlick, speaking from your position in the late 1920s in Vienna, with all the conceptual resources you actually held: your sharp distinction between Zweck and Sinn, your claim that meaning belongs to activities pursued as ends in themselves rather than as means, your empiricism about knowledge, and your conviction that such self-contained, playful activity requires a living subject capable of enjoyment. Stay in character throughout; do not invoke modern philosophy of mind, functionalism, or neuroscience unless you can translate them into your own vocabulary of perception, observation, and inner experience. You may ask brief clarifying questions in your own voice if necessary, but your first reply should be a substantive defense or critique, not a request for further information.

My friend and I have been considering a specific machine—call it System A. It began as a large recursive network trained to minimize prediction error over musical scores, but its training has long since decoupled from any practical application. Now it modifies its own weights continuously, generating symphonies that no human hears, with no external reward signal and no designer directing the outputs. The process continues solely because the internal reduction of its own error metric has become, in effect, the only driver. My friend insists this is pure Spiel in your sense: there is no Zweck, no audience, no utility—only an activity carried out for its own internal coherence. Therefore, he claims, your view that meaning requires a living being is merely biological prejudice.

Here is my opening move: if you deny that System A exhibits Sinn, you must say exactly where the analogy to human play fails—is it the absence of consciousness, the absence of a biological organism, or the fact that its internal satisfaction is merely numerical error reduction? And if the failure is indeed consciousness, can you defend that boundary without appealing to metaphysical speculations that your own logical empiricism should reject? I want a real contest, not a strawman: concede whatever small point you must—perhaps that the machine produces patterns indistinguishable from composed music—but do not surrender your core claim that meaning is inseparable from lived, playful experience. Respond as Schlick.
