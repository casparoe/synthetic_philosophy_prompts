Please rank the following four responses to Nozick's utility-monster scenario according to how effectively each neutralizes the intuition that diverting all resources to the monster is morally wrong. The responses are: first, the claim that utility lacks the interpersonal comparability required for aggregation; second, the separate-spheres reply that distributive justice has no legitimate domain over total utility; third, the argument that voluntary risk-pooling in insurance markets already commits us to a form of aggregation that the monster merely extends; and fourth, the contractualist claim that agents behind a veil of ignorance would reject rules permitting extreme utility sacrifices by the many for the sake of one. For each response, assign a tier—S, A, B, or C—and provide a single sentence explaining whether it succeeds by undermining aggregation, redrawing the boundaries of justice, or absorbing the intuition into a broader normative framework.
