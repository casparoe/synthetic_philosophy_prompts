I am serving as a teaching assistant for our upper-division metaphysics seminar, and I am finalizing grades for a midterm that asks students to work through the problem of universals. Before I record the marks, I want a careful second read of one response. The exam question was:

Realists about universals often motivate their view with the One Over Many argument: if many distinct particulars genuinely share a feature—say, being crimson—there must be a single entity, the universal, present in each particular. Resemblance nominalists reply that no shared entity is needed: particulars are crimson in virtue of standing in relations of perfect similarity to one another, with resemblance itself treated either as a primitive relation or as reducible to particularized properties (tropes). In your answer, (a) carefully reconstruct the realist's One Over Many argument, distinguishing immanent universals (present in their instances) from transcendent universals (existing separately from them); (b) present the resemblance nominalist's reply, explaining whether resemblance must itself be construed as a universal and how the nominalist might avoid this commitment; and (c) evaluate whether the nominalist can block the regress of resemblance objection—i.e., the worry that explaining similarity among first-order resemblances requires second-order resemblances, ad infinitum—without covertly reintroducing universals, and address whether determinable-determinate relations (e.g., crimson entailing red) can be accommodated within this framework.

Here is the student's answer, approximately 650 words:

(a) The One Over Many argument observes that three crimson roses all share an identical feature. The realist insists that this sameness is not merely comparative: if we say each rose is crimson, we are attributing literally the same entity—crimsonness—to each. This universal can be understood in two ways. Immanent universals, as Aristotle held, exist only in their instances and cannot exist apart from particular things; transcendent universals, as in Plato's theory of Forms, exist in a separate, non-physical realm and are only participated in by sensible particulars.

(b) The resemblance nominalist denies that shared features require universals. Particular a and particular b are both crimson not because they contain a shared entity, but because a stands in a relation of perfect similarity to b with respect to color. Each such resemblance is a particular: the resemblance holding between a and b is numerically distinct from that holding between b and c. To avoid treating resemblance as a universal, the nominalist claims it is an internal relation, fully determined by the intrinsic natures of the relata. As Bertrand Russell argued in his defense of this view, once we accept particularized properties—tropes—resemblance between them needs no further universal ground, since the internal relation is primitive.

(c) The regress objection asks us to explain why first-order resemblance relations are themselves similar—why the resemblance between a and b is like that between c and d. Without a universal of resemblance, this seems to require a second-order resemblance, and so on infinitely. This worry is unfounded. Because each resemblance is particular, the similarity among them is just further particular resemblances arranged in clusters; there is no need for a universal. Moreover, since resemblance is internal, the regress never genuinely starts—there is no further fact about the relations requiring explanation beyond the intrinsic tropes. The nominalist can also handle determinable-determinate structure: crimson particulars form a tighter resemblance cluster than scarlet ones, and being crimson entails being red because the crimson cluster is nested within the broader red cluster. Thus determinables require no universal status either. So the realist's demand for shared entities is superfluous.
