I have been working through the boundary problem—who properly belongs to the demos—by testing my intuitions against concrete membership cases rather than deriving them from a single prior principle. Here are my verdicts, which I present for coherence-testing.

First, long-term non-citizen residents who are fully subject to domestic law should eventually acquire full voting membership; mere administrative category seems insufficient to justify permanent exclusion. Second, citizens who have permanently emigrated and are no longer subject to domestic law should eventually forfeit membership, even if they retain nationality, cultural identity, or property interests; being outside the jurisdiction should eventually outweigh being inside the nationality. Third, outsiders severely affected by a polity’s decisions—downstream water users, for instance—merit consultation or weighted consideration, but not full membership; affectedness alone does not confer democratic standing. Fourth, future generations should be institutionally represented through proxy or constitutional mechanisms, yet they are not present members of the current demos; representation is not membership. Fifth, in territories that are governed but not incorporated—where residents bear all obligations without corresponding representation—I hold that full membership is required; the absence of incorporation is a contingent institutional failure, not a justification for exclusion.

Two judgments, however, pull in opposite directions. A temporary worker who remains for fifteen years, building a life but retaining formal non-citizen status, strikes me as eventually entitled to membership; but a citizen abroad who continues to pay taxes and hold property strikes me as eventually not entitled, even though the latter maintains stronger formal ties and economic stakes. This suggests that being subject to law is doing more work than either nationality or economic stake, but I worry I am weighting jurisdictional presence ad hoc to save both verdicts.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing jurisdictional subjection from affectedness, introducing a temporal threshold for incorporation, or separating membership from representation—that captures all five verdicts without collapse? Which of my judgments must be abandoned for the sake of coherence? In particular, can my differential treatment of the emigrant citizen and the long-term temporary worker be derived from a single account of what democratic membership is for, or does it depend on an unstated normative premise?

Please lay out the candidate principles precisely, state which verdicts each saves and which it sacrifices, and apply them to at least one further case I have not considered—say, digital residents who participate economically and socially via remote presence without ever entering the physical territory. Be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable and what specific counterexample or theoretical commitment would lead you to revise the principle you defend.
