I'm working through the logic of superrational decision-making in symmetric, non-communicating populations, specifically comparing the Platonia dilemma—the telegram game with twenty participants in which exactly one sender receives a large reward, with the superrational prescription being to send with probability 1/20—to the Luring Lottery, in which entrants submit postcards designating a number of entries and share a fixed prize divided by total entries, producing a continuous strategy space where the superrational response seems to scale with the expected submissions of others and risks an unbounded runaway. What I find conceptually elusive is not the derivation in either isolated case, but the structural reason superrationality appears to yield a clean, symmetric point solution in the discrete binary-choice setting yet fails to stabilize in the continuous intensity-setting without either collapsing to zero or diverging.

To probe this, I want to understand whether the instability is intrinsic to continuity or to the particular payoff scaling. So consider a hybrid variant I have been constructing: each of N symmetric agents independently decides first whether to enter at all (a binary choice), and if entering, selects a continuous intensity s > 0 representing resource commitment; the total pool is fixed, but the payout to a randomly selected entrant is divided not linearly by total intensity but by its square root, so that marginal dilution is concave. The agents are superrational, share common knowledge of rationality, and cannot observe others' choices before acting. In this framing, does a well-defined symmetric strategy pair (entry probability p, intensity distribution over s) exist? More precisely, is there a unique fixed point in which each agent's expected payoff from entering with intensity s equals that from abstaining, and if so, does it require p to be strictly between 0 and 1 with s collapsing to a point, or does the continuous dimension allow a continuum of equilibria that undermines the very symmetry-breaking that makes the Platonia prescription determinate?

What I am after is an explanation of the conceptual boundary: does superrationality require either a discrete symmetry-breaking mechanism (like the probabilistic coin-flip in Platonia) or a continuous self-limiting feedback loop (like the entry-scaling in the Luring Lottery), but become ill-defined when both are present together? If the hybrid case genuinely lacks a unique prescription, does that reveal that superrational reasoning depends on the strategy space being one-dimensional in a specific sense—either purely categorical or purely quantitative—but not both? I would like the analysis to stay at the level of the decision-theoretic structure rather than empirical predictions about actual human contestants, and to clarify whether the failure of determinacy, if it occurs, is a defect in the concept of superrationality or merely a signal that the concept applies only to specially structured games.
