I teach a 90-minute graduate seminar on moral philosophy and cross-cultural pragmatics, 12 students, with no prior reading assigned on this topic. Design a runnable in-class simulation—complete with roles, hidden rules, symbolic stakes, and a structured debrief—that makes participants feel the moral tension between Dutch-style directness and Japanese-style indirectness, specifically about whether white lies are ever wrong. Before anyone names a philosophical view, the rules and hidden incentives should generate the conflict themselves.

Build the simulation around a distinction you should embed in the hidden architecture but not announce at the start: shielding lies (withholding or softening information to protect the hearer from harm) versus smoothing lies (using polite fiction to preserve interactional flow without altering what the hearer takes to be true). The scenario should be a concrete, ambiguous workplace evaluation—say, a collaborative project review in which one member’s contribution was genuinely weak but salvageable, and the group must decide what to say to her and to the external sponsor.

Specify the following:

- Roles and materials: assign each student one of three hidden orientation cards—Transparency (Dutch-style frankness as respect for autonomy), Harmony (Japanese-style indirectness that protects relational face), or Mixed—plus an observer with a scoring sheet. Include any physical props needed (e.g., sealed envelope instructions, reputation tokens).
- The interaction rules: a 20-minute structured exchange in which the evaluator must deliver feedback, the recipient must respond, and observers record whether transparency, emotional protection, or interactional smoothness was prioritized.
- A hidden payoff scheme revealed only after the interaction: points are awarded for epistemic accuracy, for absence of expressed discomfort, and for preservation of working relations, with weights that make it impossible to maximize all three. Include a forced-choice ballot (10 minutes) in which each participant must vote to condemn or permit one specific utterance drawn from the role-play.
- A timeline for the full 90 minutes: setup, interaction, revelation of scoring weights, silent ballot, and debrief.
- The debrief: map each phase onto the shielding/smoothing distinction; connect transparency to the Dutch notion of bespreekbaarheid (frankness as respect for the hearer’s autonomy) and indirectness to the Japanese gap between honne (true feeling) and tatemae (public presentation); and show how each orientation condemns a different kind of white lie. Be explicit about the broader question of whether white lies are morally permissible.
- Predictable failures: participants will likely read directness as aggression or indirectness as deception, and they will often collapse shielding and smoothing into a single category of “being nice.” Say exactly how the facilitator should correct these misreadings in the moment.
- An honest limitation: state which feature of long-term relational ethics (e.g., the cumulative effect of repeated smoothing on trust over months) the one-shot simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally.

Make sure the participants experience both stances as internally coherent moral positions before the debrief names them, and ensure that avoiding both kinds of lie appears, within the rules, to violate either sincerity or care.
