As a student in my university's seminar on moral philosophy and international institutions, I'm drafting an essay on asylum obligations and would value comments on its coherence, its hidden philosophical commitments, and where it might be most vulnerable.

The standard framework distinguishes refugees—those with a well-founded fear of persecution on protected grounds—from other displaced persons. I want to propose a further distinction within refugee status itself, organized not around the nature of the threat but around the causal structure of any particular state's obligation to admit. Call them direct-refuge cases and distributed-causation cases. In direct-refuge cases, a specific state's actions or treaty-bound omissions are the predominant cause of the flight risk. In distributed-causation cases, no single state's actions predominate; instead, the risk emerges from interactions among climate dynamics, economic structures, and non-state violence, where any receiving state's contribution is probabilistic and diffuse.

I then argue that the duty of non-refoulement applies with full stringency in direct-refuge cases, but in distributed-causation cases it transforms into a duty of equitable admission allocation rather than an absolute bar on return. This does not license returning people to danger; it relocates the moral burden from a negative duty not to return to a positive duty to coordinate admission quotas across states according to each state's structural contribution to the displacement dynamics.

I'm worried this collapses into a cost-benefit analysis disguised in causal language. I also wonder whether I'm implicitly committing to a version of collective responsibility that requires identifying specific state intentions, which seems implausible for climate-linked displacement. Could you comment specifically on whether the causal-structure distinction actually does independent moral work, whether my framing of non-refoulement is conceptually consistent with the 1951 Convention's general purpose without requiring legal exegesis, and whether there's a hidden assumption that obligations must be traceable to identifiable agents? I'd especially value suggestions on whether the argument needs a supplementary account of how to measure structural contribution without reducing it to carbon-footprint accounting, since I want to avoid that reduction, and on whether the direct-refuge versus distributed-causation split risks obscuring the individual claimant's urgency in favor of an overly systemic view.
