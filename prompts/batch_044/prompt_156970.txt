I'm running a grad seminar on well-being and we got totally sidetracked arguing about Nozick's experience machine — but not the usual would-you-plug-in replay everyone does in intro classes. I was talking with a friend who works in clinical ethics about this protocol they're piloting in some Scandinvian hospitals, something called phenomenal substitution therapy. The idea is that patients with severe, treatment-resistant anhedonia spend 48-hour blocks inside a fully immersive VR environment calibrated to there own counterfactual biography — not just pleasure, but living out the life they would have led if the illness hadn't derailed them. The clinic frames it as restoring narrative coherence, yet the patients are literally inhabiting a world that is, by design, more subjectively valuable than anything they can access in reality. Meanwhile the same hospitals ethics board blocks long-term opioid maintenance for the exact same cohort, saying that well-being has to be anchored in real achievement, not just feeling good.

So here's what I'm actually puzzled by: what implicit theory of well-being makes both policies make sense together? Are they presupposing some weird hybrid of objective-list goods plus hedonism, or is there a hidden commitment to biographical authenticity that explains why simulated achievement counts but chemical pleasure doesn't? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether it's actually coherent or just an ad hoc mess, and tell me what a policy built on pure experience-machine hedonism would have to look like — would the VR become mandatory, or would the refusal of opioids collapse?
