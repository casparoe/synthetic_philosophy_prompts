Draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on the implications of Libet-style experiments for free will. Name the camps precisely: Camp A holds that the pre-conscious readiness potential (RP) demonstrates that conscious mental states do not initiate voluntary action, thereby undermining libertarian free will; Camp B holds that the RP reflects preparatory dynamics that are causally compatible with conscious mental causation, particularly through conscious modulation or veto, and that the experiments therefore do not refute free will.

The document should contain four parts, in this order.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Include agreement about the basic empirical pattern: a measurable RP in pre-supplementary motor area precedes the reported time of conscious intention by several hundred milliseconds; there is a subsequent window in which a conscious veto might occur; averaging across trials obscures single-trial variability; and the experiments concern simple, pre-planned wrist flexions rather than deliberative choice. Also include agreement on what needs explaining: whether neural precursors are causally sufficient for the specific action executed, and whether conscious mental states play any causal role in determining which action is performed or whether it is performed at all.

Second, a disagreements table. State each residual disagreement in wording that its own camp would accept. At minimum cover: (a) whether the RP represents an unconscious decision that fixes the action, or merely a preparatory bias that remains open to conscious influence; (b) whether the subjective report of the time of conscious intention (the W-judgment) is a reliable temporal marker of the relevant mental event; (c) whether a conscious veto, if it occurs, is itself predetermined by prior neural activity; and (d) whether free will requires that conscious intention be the first cause in the neural chain, or only that it exercise a non-epiphenomenal causal role at some stage.

Third, the cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect two central cruxes. Crux One: whether high-accuracy predictive decoding of single-trial pre-SMA activity can determine the specific motor choice (e.g., left versus right) well before any reportable awareness, and whether such predictive success leaves causal work for a conscious veto. Crux Two: whether a multi-level model of agency—in which conscious evaluation operates on a slower integrative timescale than rapid motor preparation—can be empirically distinguished from an epiphenomenalist model in which all conscious experience is a post-hoc construction. For each crux, specify a concrete study design, hypothetical result, or argument that both camps agree would bear on it. Then record each camp's advance prediction: what specific finding or logical result would cause them to revise their position, and what outcome they expect.

Fourth, a brief concluding section that distinguishes genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones. If part of the dispute turns on different definitions of "decision," "conscious cause," or "free will," say exactly which part and explain why the remaining disagreement is substantive.

Keep the formulation neutral, precise, and formal. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for them. Do not invoke obscure theories or technical frameworks that are not broadly familiar in philosophy of mind and neuroscience; however, inject one precise conceptual distinction of your own—such as the distinction between "initiation closure" (whether the RP fixes the specific motor program) and "authorization closure" (whether any mental state can still authorize or abort it)—and require both camps to state whether they accept it and how it affects their position.
