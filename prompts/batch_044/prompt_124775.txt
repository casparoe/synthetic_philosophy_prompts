Consider the following framing of Leibniz's moral philosophy: he holds that justice is the charity of the wise, that moral evil arises from limitation rather than positive malice, and that the perfection of rational beings consists in knowledge of and love for the divine order. Within this framework, there appear to be at least three persistent open problems. First, whether his account of moral obligation can be separated from his theological commitment to a benevolent creator without collapsing into a form of rational egoism. Second, how his deterministic monadology accommodates the kind of deliberative freedom required for moral praise and blame. Third, whether his distinction between metaphysical, physical, and moral evil provides a coherent taxonomy for modern discussions of systemic injustice, or whether it forces all social harm into the category of metaphysical limitation.

You are advising a small research group that can devote three years to only one of these lines. Which open question should they prioritise, and in what order should they treat the remaining two? Defend your ranking using criteria that include tractability for philosophical rather than historical scholarship, the extent to which resolving the question would illuminate other parts of Leibniz's system, and whether the answer would have meaningful implications outside purely Leibnizian exegesis. Please write your response in British English, and avoid offering bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific manuscript editions; I want the reasoning and the defended agenda, not a survey of the literature.
