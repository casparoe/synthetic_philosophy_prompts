I serve on the planning committee of a town of roughly fifteen thousand residents, where a low, crumbling granite wall—waist-high, partly collapsed, and overgrown—has separated the old commons from a former mill yard now redeveloped into housing. Modern surveys place the wall entirely on public land; it retains no soil, holds no preservation status, and obstructs no valued view. The neighborhood association petitions to dismantle it and pave a continuous greenway connecting two districts. To most committee members this seems uncontroversial: the wall is an aesthetic obstacle and a barrier to accessibility. One longtime resident, however, urges us to leave it untouched. She does not invoke heritage law or claim the wall is beautiful; rather, she observes that for generations its presence has quietly structured pedestrian movement, creating an unmarked but stable convergence point between two flows of foot traffic that rarely conflict precisely because the wall forces them into a predictable pattern. She warns that removing it—without grasping why that informal coordination matters—could dissolve a social order we rely upon but have never formally recognized or documented. The committee is inclined to treat her objection as mere nostalgia. Should we approve the dismantling, and on what grounds ought we to evaluate her warning?
