I’m a novelist and I watched two people in my writing group — not me, I just listened — argue about what authorial intent actually does to a text. Jordan said that if a writer leaves a private note saying the blue door in chapter four symbolizes death, then thats the meaning, period, even if the published sentences are ambigious about it. Sam said once its published the text stands on its own feet; the note is biography, not part of the work. Jordan came back: okay, but what if the note is scribbled in the margins of the actual proof copy, making it part of the material artifact? Sam said readers still only get the printed page, and proof copies arent in the reading contract. Then Jordan raised a collaborative case: Author A writes a scene, Author B writes the ending, and A’s private outline says the ending should be ironic, but B writes it straight with no ironic markers anywhere. Is B wrong because they violated A’s intent? Sam admitted thats trickier, but said the published version is still the unified text we interpret; hidden outlines dont override the sentences on the page. Jordan said that just proves intent is always operating, sometimes buried under collaboration. Then Jordan threw in this synthetic twist: imagine the dead writer’s journals are used to train a language model that writes a sequel, and the model reproduces the private blue-door note verbatim inside the new text. Since the model has no real intention, does that fix the symbol’s meaning or does it dissolve the whole point? Sam said that shows intent never mattered, Jordan said it means we need a new category for synthetic authorship. They stayed stuck. I dont want writing advice — I want you to adjudicate point by point: who is right about which specific claim, what are both missing, and how does that language-model twist change or fail to change the core disagreement?
