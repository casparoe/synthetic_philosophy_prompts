Question: Machiavelli compares fortune to a river you can dyke against, and also to a woman who must be beaten into submission. A student argued these two images imply opposite roles for virtù: one predictive/engineering, the other violent/domineering. Is that contradiction real, or does Machiavelli have a unified account? Also, could either image accommodate collective or distributed virtù — like a council of advisors functioning as one virtuos agent?

Answer:
Machiavelli uses both in The Prince around ch.25, saying fortune controls half our actions and we the rest. The river means build dykes early, the woman means strike first. So the contradiction is only apparent — both just show virtù being active and dominating fortune. There's no real tension. About collective virtù, Machiavelli rejects it: virtù is always individual, like Cesare Borgia. Also fortuna isn't random but cyclical, so if you grasp the cycle you can predict it, which is another form of virtù.
