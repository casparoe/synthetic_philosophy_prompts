I serve as the clinical ethics coordinator at a neuro-palliative unit that practices coupled identity preservation therapy (CIPT), a niche intervention in which long-term caregivers of patients with severe frontotemporal degeneration are trained to maintain the patient’s narrative preference structures and self-referential patterns within their own cognition. Drawing on Douglas Hofstadter’s account of the self as a strange loop — a pattern that need not be confined to a single brain but can persist in other minds that have internalized its structure — our unit sometimes treats a caregiver-patient pair as hosting one distributed “I” for purposes of informed consent and treatment planning. I face this decision repeatedly, about twice a week, on proposals to alter sedation, withdraw artificial nutrition, or enroll a patient in a terminal trial; I have roughly twenty-four hours to produce a recommendation that must be co-signed by the attending neurologist and a designated family representative, yet the underlying philosophy offers no direct clinical algorithm, and the family dynamics are often volatile.

Please write a reusable decision procedure — an ordered checklist of operational tests — that translates Hofstadter’s idea of distributed selfhood into criteria my team can actually run. For each step, state the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a hard “no,” and where it must hand the decision to a human ethics board because the issue is one of values rather than pattern continuity. Be explicit about where judgment is unavoidable and where the procedure is likely to misfire. Apply it to three cases: (a) a minimally responsive patient whose spouse, deeply trained in CIPT, refuses withdrawal of a feeding tube that the patient had previously requested; (b) a patient with no detectable neural response whose two adult children hold equally strong but conflicting internalized versions of the patient’s preferences; and (c) a case where the procedure authorizes a sedation change that the patient’s closest caregiver finds morally intolerable, so the procedural output conflicts with a human refusal. Trace the exact path through your steps for each. Then state concisely what the procedure fundamentally cannot settle — for example, whether a loop instantiated in a non-consenting brain carries independent moral authority — and flag the precise nodes where an ethics board must override. Lead with the bottom line: a brief summary of the procedure’s core logic, its three hard-stop conditions, and its irreducible judgment gaps. Keep the entire response under 600 words.
