I was arguing with my friends last week about whether ethics boards can ever really say that helping person A is 'more important' than helping person B, and then I realised my actual job has this exact problem. I sit on a regional health authority's allocation committee, and we have to approve funding splits between programs that serve totally different populations — chronic pain patients vs acute trauma survivors, mental-health liaison services vs surgical capacity — and the board keeps asking whether we're comparing apples to apples when we claim one group gains more welfare than another loses.

I don't want a grand theory. I need a reusable decision procedure — like a flowchart in words — that my non-philosopher collegues can run in a meeting. The rules we already have are: no cardinal utility scores (no QALYs or point scales), and a 'recognition boundary' clause that says if a proposal touches whether people are treated as the kind of person they are (say, gender-affirming clinical intake vs a generic protocol), that recognition value can't be folded into a health-outcome tradeoff. So build the procedure: the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, where it stops with 'these are incommensurable,' and where it forces a human vote. Ground it in whatever philosophy actually knows about interpersonal comparison without assuming we can read minds. Then run it on two proposals we actually recieved: splitting rehab slots between chronic pain and acute trauma, and funding a cultural liaison program that improves mental health metrics but doesn't change mortality stats. Make sure one of those runs produces an answer the committee will hate. And flag the misfires — the spots where the checklist can't settle the issue and someone just has to decide.
