I spent the better part of last night in a long argument with a close friend who works in evolutionary biology. She kept insisting that if a behavioral regularity is evolutionarily stable, then it already is a convention in the relevant normative sense, and that David Lewis's emphasis on common knowledge and arbitrary alternatives adds nothing but philosophical ornament. I think she is wrong—specifically, she is collapsing the normative "ought" of a Lewisian convention into the descriptive stability of an equilibrium—but I need to be able to show exactly where the collapse happens, and why it matters. So I am asking for a teaching plan, not an explanation: I will lead a single ninety-minute session with a reading group of cognitive-science graduate students. They are fluent in evolutionary game theory and signaling models, but they have no background in philosophy of language or action theory.

The concept I need them to grasp is Lewis's analysis of convention as a solution to coordination problems that carries genuine normative weight, together with the evolutionary dynamics that can stabilize but do not by themselves constitute such conventions. I need the dependency structure laid out precisely: which component—the payoff structure of a coordination problem, the notion of an arbitrary alternative, the common-knowledge condition, or the normative "ought"—must be secured before the next is introduced, and what breaks if the order is reversed?

For the carrying example, I want to avoid both the overused telephone and driving-on-the-right illustrations. I am considering the spontaneous stabilization of functional roles—healer, tank, damage-dealer—in a cooperative multiplayer raid environment where no central authority assigns positions, and where newcomers are socially expected to learn and adopt the established pattern. What properties must this example possess, and what properties must it deliberately lack, in order to carry the distinction between a merely stable regularity and a full Lewisian convention? And why should an explicit institutional contract or legal rule not serve as the parallel case?

Then I need a misconception map tailored to this audience. I anticipate three specific errors: that convention requires an explicit agreement or contract; that evolutionary stability is sufficient for convention; and that the common-knowledge condition involves an impossible infinite regress of beliefs about beliefs. For each, I need to know exactly where in the sequence it will arise, what form it will take given their scientific training, and the precise point at which I should intervene to head it off—especially the second misconception, which is the one my friend advanced.

What should I explicitly leave out at this level? I am tempted to introduce later work in evolutionary dynamics—perhaps signaling-game models or accounts of normative emergence in social science—but I want to know whether bringing them in helps or undermines the distinction I am trying to protect.

Finally, I need one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that reveals whether they have genuinely understood the difference between a stable evolutionary regularity and a Lewisian convention with normative force, rather than whether they can merely list the formal conditions.

Please structure your response as a teaching plan: the ordered introduction with dependency justifications; the carrying example with its required and excluded features; the misconception map with intervention points; the omissions with reasoning; and the check-for-understanding question. End with a single line beginning "Core principle:" that summarizes the central pedagogical insight in one sentence.
