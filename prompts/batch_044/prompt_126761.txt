I'm coordinating a small study group that reads rationalist-adjacent work on collective risk, and we recently got stuck arguing about whether the unilateralist's curse applies to what one member calls "interpretability leakage" — the publication of partial mechanistic explanations of frontier models that lower replication barriers without providing corresponding defensive tools. The discussion started with a casual reference to Bostrom's framing, but quickly moved into whether information goods can trigger the same aggregation dynamics as physical actions. I want a move-by-move adjudication of the actual exchange, not just an overall winner, because neither party was convinced and I need to see whether the framework is being misapplied or genuinely extended.

Person A opened by claiming that publishing a novel red-teaming protocol is a clear instance: one lab releases the method unilaterally, other actors cannot retract it, and the aggregate effect is a raised competitive floor that accelerates capabilities research without a matching safety gain. Person B objected that the curse requires actions with direct physical or causal externalities summed across agents, whereas publishing a protocol is non-rivalrous and does not automatically cause anyone to build a more dangerous system — it merely supplies information that competitive actors might use. Person A countered that in a competitive market, information supply is causally sufficient for replication: if Lab A demonstrates that architecture X achieves capability Y, Labs B through N are strategically compelled to reproduce it, so the publication operates like an emission that each actor adds to a shared risk pool. Person B then distinguished strategic compulsion from causal aggregation, arguing that the curse depends on each contributor's action being individually negligible and therefore escaping moral accounting, whereas a high-profile publication is large, identifiable, and made with full strategic awareness — which makes it an arms-race decision, not a curse-driven externality. Person A conceded the identifiability point but insisted the dilution still holds: each researcher feels their single paper is a drop in the bucket, yet summed across hundreds of publications the information environment becomes genuinely hazardous, so the psychological structure of the curse is preserved even if the causal mechanism differs. Person B closed by saying that if you relax the causal mechanism that far, the concept loses its distinctive predictive power and collapses into a generic warning about competitive dynamics, which suggests the framework is being stretched beyond coherence.

Please adjudicate each of these six moves — A's opening claim, B's initial objection, A's counter about strategic compulsion, B's distinction about identifiability, A's concession about dilution, and B's closing collapse charge — rating each as clearly correct, partially correct but missing something, or clearly mistaken. Say which party is more right overall, identify what both sides are missing about how the unilateralist's curse interacts with epistemic goods, and propose a modified principle — you may call it "epistemic unilateralism" if it helps — that captures whatever is genuinely right in both positions without reducing the curse to either pure information theory or pure strategic interaction. Present your adjudication in a table with these exact columns: Move, Speaker, Core Claim, Rating, Brief Justification, Critical Omission. After the table, provide a brief synthesis paragraph summarizing the overall verdict and spelling out the modified principle in one or two sentences.
