I serve as the clinical ethics officer at a residential treatment facility specializing in severe substance-use disorders with co-occurring impulse-control conditions, and our state recently mandated that every eligible admission include an evaluation for a voluntary precommitment protocol—essentially a Ulysses-style contract in which a patient, during a period of assessed capacity, binds their future self to specific clinical restrictions (refusing discharge, accepting medication holds, blocking supplier contact) that activate automatically during a predicted crisis window. At present my team drafts these protocols case-by-case, but legal review and patient advocates have begun challenging them on inconsistent grounds: some contracts are attacked as disguised coercion, others are voided in crisis despite proper execution, and we have no shared framework for deciding when a binding mechanism genuinely preserves autonomy rather than substituting the institution’s judgment for the patient’s. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit stop conditions and judgment-call flags—that staff can run in approximately thirty minutes per case, without requiring on-site legal counsel but with a mandatory sign-off line, translating the philosophical distinction between a Ulysses contract (a future-self-binding instrument designed to protect a long-range preference from temporary distortion) and constitutional precommitment (a structural rule that constrains available options regardless of present preference) into operational criteria that a non-philosopher can apply consistently.

The procedure should ask, in sequence: (1) whether the patient currently demonstrates procedural autonomy—understood here not merely as cognitive capacity but as the ability to revise preferences through a legitimate reflective process—versus whether the contract merely locks in a past substantive preference; (2) whether the binding mechanism targets a specific anticipated failure mode (e.g., acute withdrawal, manic escalation) rather than imposing a blanket restriction; (3) whether the contract includes a dead-hand provision—an irreversible institutional hold that outlasts any change in the patient’s values or circumstances—versus a reversible trigger with a defined cost or delay; (4) whether revocation during crisis is structurally possible and, if so, whether it requires a second-order evaluation rather than an instantaneous override; and (5) whether the protocol, if enforced, would produce an outcome that the patient’s current crisis-state self rejects but that aligns with their stable, pre-crisis value system, with clear markers for when this alignment is ambiguous. At each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a clear do-not-implement or refer-to-ethics-board decision, and where judgment cannot be eliminated—particularly in weighing whether a future preference reversal reflects authentic value change or merely symptomatic distortion. Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, a patient with alcohol-use disorder who binds herself to a thirty-day discharge-block and disulfiram administration upon readmission of alcohol on breath; second, a patient with bipolar I disorder in remission who contracts to maintain lithium and block discharge requests during manic phases, but who now, in a manic episode, demands immediate release; and third, a case where the procedure yields an answer the clinical team will resist—perhaps a contract formed during a prolonged depressive episode that binds the patient to refuse all future antidepressant changes, where the procedure identifies a dead-hand structural error and mandates voiding despite the team’s belief that enforcement would prevent harm.

The procedure must be honest about its limits: it cannot resolve deep metaphysical disputes about the persistence of personal identity across severe mental-state shifts, it cannot substitute for legal review of statutory rights, and it should flag rather than conceal the point at which autonomy-preservation becomes paternalism masked as precommitment. It should also distinguish between soft precommitment (reversible with a cooling-off period) and hard precommitment (institutionally enforced regardless of present objection), and it should include a final sign-off line that records which steps were satisfied by evidence and which were satisfied by clinical judgment alone. Please present this as a practitioner-facing flowchart in words, with the test questions in order, the decision nodes, the three worked applications—including the unwelcome result in the third case—and an explicit statement of what the procedure is not designed to do.
