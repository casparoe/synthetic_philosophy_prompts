The particularist-generalist debate in moral philosophy—between those who hold that moral reasons are irreducibly context-sensitive and that no exceptionless principles govern them, and those who hold that qualified, hierarchical, or ceteris-paribus principles do capture genuine moral structure—has been at an impasse for decades. Particularists routinely invoke feature-shifting cases: kindness that is virtuous in one setting becomes patronizing or cruel in another, suggesting that the moral valence of a property is not intrinsic but depends on the whole gestalt of the situation. Generalists respond that this demonstrates only our epistemic difficulty in stating fully qualified principles, not that no such principles exist; they propose layered, defeasible, or weighted rules that accommodate context without abandoning generality. The standard moves are now completely familiar—particularists cite the shape of the moral landscape, generalists reply with hidden conditions or weighting schemes—yet neither side has conceded ground, and no new formal result or empirical finding has broken the symmetry.

My working suspicion is that the stall is not really about whether principles exist, but about a deeper, largely unacknowledged disagreement over whether moral explanation is compositional. Particularists seem to treat a reason—say, that an act causes suffering—as never explaining the wrongness of that act except in conjunction with an ineliminable contextual background; the explanatory work is done by the situation as a whole. Generalists, by contrast, treat reasons as modular: suffering counts against an action in a way that can be isolated, weighed, and subsumed under a principle, even when other reasons override it. If this diagnosis is right, the two sides are not disagreeing about the metaphysics of principles at all, but about what a moral explanation must look like—whether citing a feature like unnecessary harm ever genuinely explains a judgment, or whether explanation always requires citing the full contextual shape.

But I am unsure whether this is correct. The disagreement might instead be verbal, with one side using 'principle' to mean exceptionless universal law and the other to mean any generalizable pattern. It might be that the sides are answering different questions—one about the ontology of reasons, one about the psychology of moral learning—under a single heading. Or there might be a hidden empirical question about how human moral cognition actually operates, masked by apparently conceptual arguments. There may also be bedrock intuitions about whether moral judgment is more like perceptual recognition or like inferential reasoning, which neither side can argue for from shared premises. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of positions and not a verdict on who is right: is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of moral reality, a methodological dispute about what moral theory should aim for, or an ill-posed question that should be dissolved? Given your diagnosis, what exactly would count as progress? Would it require a formal model of reason-combination that demonstrates the failure of compositionality, a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates ontological holism about reasons from epistemic anti-codifiability, an empirical study of moral cognition, or should we conclude that no progress should be expected and that particularists and generalists ought to abandon their shared vocabulary? Be concrete about what each side is committed to and about what, if anything, could actually move the debate.
