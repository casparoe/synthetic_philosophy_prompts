Please construct a rigorous argument for the view that macro-historical contingency is best explained by institutional branching rather than by analogies to mathematical chaos theory. Begin by clarifying why the familiar proverb about a lost nail and a fallen kingdom—where a trivial micro-event cascades into world-historical change—rests on a category error: social systems are high-dimensional, open, and institutionally buffered, so they do not exhibit the sensitive dependence on initial conditions characteristic of low-dimensional dynamical systems. Defend the claim that only events occurring at specific branching points, where governing rules are genuinely ambiguous and authority is contested, generate path-dependent outcomes; elsewhere, structural absorption neutralizes micro-variation. In doing so, carefully distinguish epistemic unpredictability, which reflects our cognitive limits, from ontological chaos, which requires actual exponential divergence of trajectories, and explain why historians have often conflated the two. Please also identify which parts of your reasoning rest on settled findings in chaos theory and philosophy of history, and which parts depend on contested interpretive judgments—such as the criteria for identifying an institutional branching point—without presenting those judgments as the consensus among specialists.
