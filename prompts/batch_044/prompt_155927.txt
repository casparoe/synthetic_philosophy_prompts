The philosophical debate over whether the self is a center of narrative gravity—an abstract organizational point rather than a substantial entity—has persisted for decades without meaningful resolution. One camp treats narrative coherence as constitutive of identity, holding that the self is a useful fiction generated by storytelling; the other either dismisses narrative as a superficial overlay on primitive experiential continuity or rejects it as an oppressive fragmentation of an otherwise unified consciousness. As a novelist, I am struck by the fact that I routinely construct characters who possess full narrative gravity—clear biographies, motives, and interpretive arcs—without attributing to them any inner phenomenological stream comparable to human awareness. This suggests that the stalemate may not concern the ontology of consciousness at all, but whether philosophers and novelists are using the same concept of "gravity" for different phenomena: one measuring the social and interpretive conditions that make a life intelligible to others, the other measuring first-person experiential binding. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or driven by hidden empirical assumptions about autobiographical memory and attribution? More specifically, if narrative exerts a kind of retroactive binding—making past mental states "mine" only through subsequent storytelling—does that dissolve the opposition or deepen it? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, together with an honest answer about whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a reframing of the question could actually move the debate, or whether the impasse is structural.
