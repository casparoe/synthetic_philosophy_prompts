Consider the record of a conservative institutional theorist who appeals to Chesterton's fence and Burkean arguments about embedded social wisdom. Her rulings: she opposed removing an unwritten convention that ministers who mislead parliament must resign, because its enforcement depended on tacit norms not captured by law; she favored retaining a hereditary legislative chamber stripped of veto power, since its selection mechanism cultivated a distinctive deliberative temperament; she endorsed abolishing a guild entry monopoly once state certification duplicated its explicit quality-control purpose and no residual social role was visible; she resisted replacing customary local arbitration with a centralized tribunal despite efficiency gains, citing unarticulated community-binding effects; and she accepted separating civil marriage registration from religious ceremony, leaving both intact, on the grounds that bureaucratic and ritual functions could be disentangled without loss. Now she faces a proposal to replace a mandatory multi-day legislative recess—instituted to cool partisan passion—with an algorithmic amendment-filter that scores proposals for coherence and advances only high-scoring ones, eliminating the human delay while sharpening the reflective purpose. Predict her verdict. Lay out at least two rules consistent with her prior judgments—say, a strict principle that every hidden purpose must be mapped and replicated before an institution is altered, versus a functionalist principle that permits explicit-purpose substitution when hidden social roles are either absent or relocatable. State what each rule predicts here, give your best guess with reasons, identify the closest prior analog, and name the further case—perhaps a documented but technologically irreplicable embodied ritual—that would separate the two rules.
