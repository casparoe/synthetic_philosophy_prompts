I am drafting a chapter on what my supervisor terms the “competence-comprehension inversion” — the paired insight, running from Darwin’s evolutionary reasoning to Turing’s mechanistic thinking, that structured, adaptive competence can arise in a system that possesses no internal grasp of the task it performs. Over the course of our seminars she has delivered clear rulings on six borderline cases, and I want to test whether her judgments trace a single principle or whether they remain jointly consistent with competing interpretations. Her verdicts are these. First, a beaver building a dam that regulates water flow: she counts this as a pure Darwinian inversion — the animal behaves with hydraulic competence whilst lacking any concept of fluid dynamics. Second, a pocket calculator computing square roots: she denies this is an inversion at all, describing it instead as a transparent extension of human mathematical comprehension rather than an autonomous competence. Third, the evolutionary emergence of the vertebrate eye: she treats this as the paradigmatic Darwinian case, in which functional design appears without a designer’s understanding of optics. Fourth, a contemporary large language model generating fluent legal argumentation: she rejects this as an instance of the inversion, arguing that its linguistic competence is borrowed from human-comprehended training data and lacks the reproductive stakes that render evolutionary competence genuinely blind. Fifth, a swarm of simple robots that, via purely local interaction rules, collectively sort waste into recyclable categories without any individual robot representing the concept “recycling”: she accepts this as the Turing-style inversion, where mechanistic rules generate global competence that none of the constituent parts understand. Sixth, a native speaker’s intuitive grammatical judgement — knowing that a sentence is malformed without being able to articulate the governing rule: she regards this as contaminated rather than pure; although the syntactic mechanism resembles an inversion, the speaker’s semantic comprehension and reflective awareness prevent it from being an unambiguous case.

The new case is a synthetic evolutionary simulation in which digital organisms compete and reproduce within a virtual environment. Over hundreds of generations they evolve a cooperative strategy that solves collective navigation problems. Crucially, however, the simulation contains a hard-coded “fitness bonus” parameter, set once by the programmers, that explicitly rewards cooperative behaviour; this parameter never mutates, and the organisms’ present cooperative competence, though entirely opaque to them, depends causally on this embedded specification of the global goal. The organisms do not comprehend navigation, yet their competence is not fully blind either, since the programmers’ comprehension of cooperation is structurally encoded in the selection environment itself.

Please predict her verdict on this digital-organism case, but I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning behind it. Identify at least two distinct rules that fit her earlier six verdicts equally well, state what each rule predicts about this case, and explain which further case — perhaps a modified robot swarm or a variant of the language-model scenario — would best discriminate between them. Also name the closest analog among her precedents and justify the comparison. Write in British English, and conclude with the following exact line: Verdict: [your best-guess prediction, e.g. “accepts as Turing-style inversion”, “rejects as contaminated”, or “borderline”].
