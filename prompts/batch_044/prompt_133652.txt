I am a novelist who has been invited to deliver the opening remarks at the “Porous Minds” workshop—an interdisciplinary gathering of philosophers of mind, roboticists, and practicing artists held next month at the Institute for Situated Inquiry. The audience is deliberately split: half are specialists in embedded and extended cognition who will recognize every move in the argument, and the other half are writers, choreographers, and designers who live inside these questions but do not speak the jargon. I need you, acting as both a professional philosopher of mind and an experienced ceremonial speechwriter, to think carefully before composing. First, in no more than three sentences, brainstorm the structural tension I must navigate: embedded agency dissolves the neat boundary between agent and environment, yet without some boundary we cannot make sense of responsibility, regret, or creative authorship. Then write the remarks.

The philosophical idea must do real structural work, not decorative work. I want the speech to turn on a specific, somewhat niche conceptual problem I am calling the “retroactive scaffolding dilemma”: an agent constructs a cognitive environment—perhaps a collaborative protocol, a memory system, or a responsive tool—that then generates outputs the agent ratifies, but the environment was configured by an earlier version of the agent who may no longer fully exist. This should determine what I can and cannot say: I must not comfort the room with talk of a stable, continuous self that merely “uses” tools; but I also must not dissolve speaker and listeners into pure process, because we are gathered precisely to ask where praise and blame land when cognition is distributed across time and matter.

The occasion demands warmth, welcome, and intellectual honesty. I should acknowledge that some in the room will hear this as an ethics of disappearance, while others will find it obvious. I should not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; this is spoken, not archival. Please avoid direct quotations from named philosophers—paraphrase any referenced ideas without quotation marks, and do not invent publication dates, titles, or attributions. The voice should sound like a novelist speaking: precise sentences, occasional concrete imagery, respectful of abstraction without drowning in it.

Format: begin with one sentence stating the bottom-line tension. Then deliver the remarks themselves, which together with that opening sentence must not exceed 600 words. Include at most three brief bracketed notes for pacing or gesture. Make sure the speech lands for the specialists without losing the practitioners, and let the retroactive scaffolding dilemma shape the welcome, the warning, and the closing invitation.
