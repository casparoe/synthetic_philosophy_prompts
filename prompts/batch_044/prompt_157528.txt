Question: Clark and Chalmers defend extended cognition with a parity principle—if an external process functions like an internal one, we shouldn't deny it cognitive status just because it's outside the skull. Explain how this applies to Otto and his notebook. Then consider two variants: (a) a violinist who has practiced so long that adjusting intonation is done by finger-pressure feedback loops that bypass conscious representation—she doesn't calculate, her hand just 'finds' the pitch; and (b) a researcher who uses a custom AI scratchpad that doesn't store answers but generates counter-arguments to her drafts in real time, which she then accepts or rejects. For each variant, say whether extended mind applies and why. Then discuss Adams and Aizawa's coupling-constitution fallacy: does it apply with equal force to (a) and (b), or does one case invite it more?

Student answer:
The parity priciple says that if something plays the same role as memory or reasoning inside, location doesn't matter. Otto's notebook is available, reliable, and automaticaly endorsed, so it's like his biological memory.

For (a), this is embodied cognition, not extended. The feedback loops are in her hands and ears, so they're inside the boundary. Clark and Chalmers care about things outside the body, so (a) doesn't count as extended mind—it's just skill.

For (b), the AI is outside and actively processing, so it's coupled in a strong way. Because it's generating reasoning steps, not just storing them, it's actually part of the researcher's cognitive process. Adams and Aizawa say coupling isn't constitution, but that objection works best for passive storage like Otto's notebook. With active generation in (b), the coupling is so tight that it becomes constitutive, so the fallacy doesn't apply here.

Also, letting in (b) means cognitive science has to study the AI too, which breaks the autonomy of psychology.
