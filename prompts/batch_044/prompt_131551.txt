I've been arguing with a friend about David Pearce's hedonistic imperative — you know, the biotech abolitionist idea that we should genetically engineer away suffering in all sentient beings, not just humans. She thinks it's obviously the right endgame, but I keep wondering wether wiping out negative affect completely also wipes out the conditions for a specific kind of moral recognition. Like, if compassion is just a preference for wellbeing gradients, is it still compassion? Anyway, I want a short story that plays with this.

Set it sixty years after global abolition succeeded. The protagonist is a junior ecologcal calibrator — someone who tunes wild ecosystems so predators and prey don't suffer but still fill their niches. She's always been a true believer. Then during a survey she finds an uncalibrated pocket where old-style suffering still happens: a predator tearing into prey, real fear, real pain. For the first time she feels this sharp, directed moral urgency that isn't aesthetic or preference-based — it feels like it's responding to something actually wrong in the world. The story should dig into whether that feeling is just evolutionary nostalgia, or if it shows that compassion needs suffering as its object in a way pure hedonism can't replicate. Let her be genuinely torn — maybe she reports the pocket for immediate recalibration, maybe she hides it. Either way, end with her realizing that the hedonistic imperative solved suffering but may have dissolved the very conditions for recognizing moral urgency. Don't be preachy, just let the tension sit there.
