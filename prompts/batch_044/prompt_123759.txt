For a philosophy of science seminar at my school, I have been trying to articulate a pattern I observe in inductive reasoning that does not seem to match the classic examples precisely.

Consider two situations. First, a biological generalization holds that all members of a broad class reproduce in one manner. When a counterexample is discovered, the class is not abandoned; instead, it is subdivided, and the counterexample is placed in a narrower subclass. The original generalization survives in a qualified form, and the discovery is treated as refining taxonomy rather than falsifying induction.

Second, consider a predictive model in meteorology that asserts a specific climatic boundary will not be crossed during a defined season. After the boundary is crossed, the model is not rejected. Rather, an implicit exception is appended—referring to an unspecified anomalous condition—and this exception is applied retroactively to the very case that violated the prediction. The model's structure remains unchanged, but its domain of application is now protected by a hidden clause that did not exist in the original formulation.

Both situations protect an inductive claim after failure, yet they operate differently: one revises the categories being observed, while the other preserves the categories but inserts an unstated limitation. I am aware of the well-known grue paradox, in which a predicate shifts meaning at a predetermined time, and I understand the general concern about saving theories through auxiliary hypotheses. However, neither case involves a time-indexed predicate like grue, and in the second case the protective clause is constructed using knowledge of the failure itself rather than being an independent theoretical addition. Does this specific configuration—inductive protection achieved either through taxonomic subdivision or through retroactive domain restriction—have an established name in the philosophy of science? If not, how should one distinguish these two mechanisms from each other and from ordinary ad hoc adjustment, and is there a principled reason to treat them as the same underlying phenomenon or as genuinely different failures of inductive reasoning?

I would appreciate an answer that clarifies whether I am describing one concept with two manifestations, two separate concepts that happen to look alike, or something for which no standard term yet exists, and that explains where the boundaries of each lie.
