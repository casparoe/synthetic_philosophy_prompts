I hold that the so-called sunk cost fallacy is systematically overdiagnosed whenever reasoning moves outside pure, anonymous market exchange. The view I want tested distinguishes between separable sunk costs—irrecoverable expenditures that leave the agent’s preferences, capabilities, and the social meaning of abandonment essentially unchanged—and constitutive sunk costs, investments that have become woven into one’s practical identity, cooperative commitments, or path-dependent institutional fabric, such that walking away now does not restore a prior status quo but actively dissolves a self, a partnership, or a shared structure. On this account, honoring constitutive costs is not a cognitive bias but a form of practical rationality, sometimes even an obligation; only honoring separable costs is genuinely fallacious. What would count against this? Rank the defeaters by decisiveness: empirical or experimental findings that blur or collapse the separable/constitutive boundary in actual judgment; philosophical arguments—perhaps from diachronic personal identity or from decision-theoretic formalization—showing that all sunk costs are in principle separable because the agent can always be redescribed without them; ethical objections that appeals to “constitutive commitment” are usually post-hoc rationalizations of loss aversion or status-quo bias; and concrete cases, in long-term personal projects or institutional policy, where honoring a past investment clearly destroyed net value without plausibly altering anyone’s identity or cooperative obligations, which defenders of my view ought to concede as genuine counterexamples. I also want an honest assessment of whether my distinction risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every recalcitrant case can be redescribed ex post as “constitutive”—and whether that insulation is a theoretical vice for a view that claims to correct, not merely rename, a widespread reasoning error. Which of these objections would merely be awkward to absorb, and which, if sustained, should force me to abandon the distinction altogether?
