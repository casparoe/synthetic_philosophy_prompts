Following a long argument with a friend about whether transcendental arguments are genuinely a priori or merely well-disguised empirical psychology, I want to crystallize our disagreement into an exam question for my upper-level epistemology seminar. We kept circling the scope problem: the move from claiming that condition C is necessary for human experience of type E, to claiming that C is necessary for any experience of E whatsoever, and whether that move smuggles in an empirical premise about our cognitive architecture or instead articulates a modal claim about what can count as intelligible experience at all. I need a question that forces students to navigate this ambiguity without allowing them to retreat into a summary of Kant's transcendental deduction or Strawson's descriptive metaphysics.

Please write one essay exam question suitable for a 75-minute sitting. The question should present a hypothetical counter-case—an artificial cognitive system that synthesizes spatial representations without any temporal ordering, and that apparently tracks object permanence without causal expectation—and ask whether this system's conceivability undermines a transcendental argument that temporal succession is a condition for the objectivity of experience. The prompt must require students to distinguish three responses: (i) treating the system as an empirical counterexample; (ii) treating it as proof that the argument is only transcendental-psychological; and (iii) treating the system's description as conceptually incoherent because objectivity without temporal synthesis is unintelligible.

Then provide a model answer of approximately 600 words that takes the third position, arguing that the transcendental claim concerns the conditions under which the denial of C can be intelligibly entertained rather than a universal law of mental causation, and that the hypothetical system either fails to be a genuine subject of experience or tacitly presupposes the temporal conditions it claims to eliminate. Finally, provide a marking scheme built around four criteria, each worth 5 marks (total 20): precise identification of the scope ambiguity; accurate application to the hypothetical artificial system; defense of the modal or intelligibility-based reading; and an honest recognition of where the empirical-psychological interpretation genuinely threatens the argument. For each criterion, include a one-line note on the characteristic failure that separates a B-level answer from an A-level one.

Keep the prompt formal and polished, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
