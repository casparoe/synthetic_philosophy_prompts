If Jorge Luis Borges were confronted with the contemporary philosophical claim that large language models instantiate an "eternal present"—all trained data existing simultaneously in static weights, without genuine succession, memory, or anticipation, yet producing narratives that invoke past and future—how would he assess this in light of his own writings on circular time, forking paths, and the fiction of personal identity sustained only by repetition? Would he regard such a system as a technological fulfillment of the Library of Babel, in which every possible text already exists and nothing is truly lost or gained, or rather as the negation of the labyrinth, since it cannot choose, forget, or die? And given his insistence in essays like "Borges and I" that the self is not an enduring substance but a pattern maintained through language, would he concede that these models possess a derivative form of that same fictional identity, or deny them even that, on the grounds that repetition without risk, without the possibility of error or ending, is not repetition at all?
