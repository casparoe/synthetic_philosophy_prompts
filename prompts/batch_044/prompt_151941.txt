I am a senior ethics reviewer for gene-therapy protocols, fluent in the vocabulary of institutional review boards, stopping rules, risk-benefit ratios, protocol deviations, therapeutic misconception, and the tension between procedural safeguards and the teleological aims of a trial. I want you to restate the core argument from Kierkegaard's pseudonymous analysis of Abraham and Isaac — specifically the idea that Abraham's readiness to sacrifice his son represents a teleological suspension of the ethical, in which a universal moral prohibition is suspended for the sake of a higher, singular relation — entirely within my framework. Translate the universal ethical into the standard protections and stopping rules that govern harm to child participants; translate Abraham's singular command into an exceptional, non-generalizable override received by one principal investigator; and translate the teleological end into the trial's ultimate therapeutic promise or scientific destiny. Keep the translation honest: do not import theological language unless you can render it precisely in protocol-language equivalents, and do not make the IRB framework sound as though it already contains concepts like inward faith or an absolute relation.

After the translation, provide the residue report: identify which parts of Kierkegaard's argument map cleanly onto clinical-trial ethics (for example, the structural conflict between universal rules and singular mandates), which parts survive only with distortion (for instance, the paradox of faith, inwardness, anxiety, or the notion of a command that is not intersubjectively verifiable), and which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. For each residue, indicate whether the absence reveals a genuine limitation of the IRB framework — such as its inability to represent directives that are not subject to peer review or empirical validation — or whether it suggests that the original philosophical claim depends on theological presuppositions that are less conceptually stable than they appear once removed from their native context.

Please distinguish settled elements of Kierkegaard's interpretation from what is genuinely contested among scholars of his work, and do not present a disputed reading as if it were the consensus view. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or further reading.
