I've been thinking for a long time about what an emotion actually is, and I keep encountering three answers that all sound partly right but never fully cover what I experience. One view says emotions are judgments—like reaching a verdict that something is dangerous, unjust, or lost. Another says they are perceptions—ways of picking up value in the world without first thinking about it, similar to seeing color. A third says they are just feelings—bodily changes and sensations that give experience its tone. Each account explains some cases well. Grief seems like a judgment that a loss is devastating, yet it also carries a physical heaviness that doesn't feel like a verdict. Fear of a sudden loud noise feels like pure perception, but fear of failing an exam seems full of beliefs about the future. So far, this is the familiar divide.

What keeps me stuck is that people have argued about this for generations without the disagreement visibly shrinking, even when they agree on basic brain science and on the same everyday examples. My own guess is that the stall isn't about hidden laboratory results, but about which experiences each side treats as the central ones. Those who start with moral feelings build theories around judgment; those who start with sudden startle responses build theories around perception or bodily change. They may be answering slightly different questions under the same name.

But I also keep noticing something that none of the three views captures comfortably: diffuse moods that seem to have no clear object and no sharp bodily signature—like the vague, unsettled feeling of walking down an empty street at dusk and sensing that something is off, without being able to say what is wrong or feeling any specific heartbeat or tension. If an emotion must be about something specific, or must feel like a definite bodily state, then this kind of experience doesn't belong. Does that mean it isn't an emotion at all, or does it show that all three theories are missing a category?

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of every position. Is this disagreement mainly verbal—do the sides mean different things by "emotion"—or is there a real substantive conflict about facts, about which cases count as data, or about what an explanation must include? Could a new conceptual distinction, a shift in which examples are treated as central, or a formal result about explanation actually move the debate forward? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, and if so, why? I am especially interested in whether the existence of these objectless, bodily-unclear moods reveals a hidden empirical question about how the mind works, or whether it simply exposes that the original three-way framing was too narrow from the start. Please address the structure of the disagreement itself, and be honest about whether any kind of progress should be expected.
