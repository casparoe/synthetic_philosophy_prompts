I have just become eligible to vote in my city’s local referenda after purchasing a small apartment, despite remaining a non-citizen resident who pays municipal income tax; my neighbor, a citizen who has rented in the same building for fifteen years, is still ineligible, and the municipal charter appoints a “guardian for future generations” who may block infrastructure projects on behalf of unborn residents, though it grants no comparable voice to present non-citizen taxpayers. What conception of the demos—of who properly belongs to the people entitled to rule—does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that can simultaneously justify property-based enfranchisement, long-term residency exclusion, and intergenerational representation while excluding present affected non-citizens. Is that theory internally coherent, or does the charter quietly combine inconsistent commitments—perhaps one rooted in ownership, another in temporal community, and a third in citizenship status? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, note where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say exactly which provisions would have to be rewritten if we replaced the underlying view with the principle that democratic standing belongs to all whose lives are substantially shaped by collective decisions, regardless of property, citizenship, or date of birth.
