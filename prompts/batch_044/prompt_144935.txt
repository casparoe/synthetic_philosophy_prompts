I hold the view that when novelists and filmmakers dramatise real dead figures, the governing ethical constraint ought not to be factual accuracy but what I call axiological continuity—the preservation of the subject's documented system of values, even when that requires inventing dialogue or compressing chronology. On this account, an invented scene is permissible if it expresses a moral or aesthetic commitment the subject actually held, and impermissible only when it imports values foreign to their framework, regardless of whether the event itself occurred. What would genuinely count against this position? I should like the objections sorted from fatal to merely awkward: documented cases where a subject's own actions flagrantly violated the values they professed, and whether suppressing such contradictions to maintain continuity amounts to hagiography; arguments that the dead possess no posthumous interests and that our obligations run entirely to living audiences and descendants; any results in narrative ethics or philosophy of art showing that coherence conditions on fictional representation collapse when source testimony is contradictory; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by treating every contradiction as merely apparent—and whether that insulation is a vice for a normative claim of this kind. Please distinguish the defeaters that a defender of axiological continuity would themselves accept as damaging from those they would reject, and say whether the position risks anything substantive or has been rendered immune by its own qualifications.
