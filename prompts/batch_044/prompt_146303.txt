I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active research, has taken up moral philosophy with the same attention to formal structure that I once reserved for measurement theory. I am currently working through Kant's 1797 essay on the supposed right to lie—particularly the scenario in which a murderer inquires whether a friend is concealed in one's house, the host answers falsely that he is elsewhere, and by an unfortunate coincidence the friend has independently moved to precisely that other location, where he is discovered and killed. In the passage, Kant seems to argue that the lie is unconditionally wrong and that the liar is causally implicated in the death, since truthful disclosure might have diverted the murderer or alerted the victim. From a scientific standpoint, this reasoning puzzles me: the friend's relocation functions like an exogenous intervening variable, and assigning moral responsibility through such a contingent causal chain seems empirically fragile, quite different from assigning responsibility for a direct, deterministic effect.

Could you help me understand whether the argument's real force lies elsewhere—perhaps in a formal contradiction that arises when one universalizes the maxim of lying for benevolent ends—rather than in this particular counterfactual narrative? I would also like to know whether scholars treat the causal claim as essential to the argument or as a rhetorical illustration, and whether there is a genuine dispute about how this essay relates to Kant's later Doctrine of Virtue, where some readers detect a more qualified account of veracity. Please be careful to distinguish what is well established about Kant's formal universalization procedure from what is actively debated among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretive reconciliation—such as the view that the Doctrine of Virtue implicitly modifies the 1797 prohibition—as if it were the consensus position.
