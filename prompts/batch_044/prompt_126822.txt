I serve on the employee ethics and resource committee at my regional hospital, and I need to circulate a formal recommendation before Thursday’s board meeting. Our administration has proposed a mandatory “global health contribution” policy: all clinical staff earning above the institutional median would have 6% of salary automatically deducted for certified overseas relief programs, with opt-out permitted only through a quarterly review board that requires proof of comparable direct service elsewhere. The proposal also uses a graduated scale—senior surgeons at 12%, residents at 2%—calibrated strictly to income rather than to individual capacity or role. The justification memo argues that because our specialized training gives us an exceptional ability to preserve life, retaining disposable income for non-essential consumption while preventable deaths occur elsewhere is morally equivalent to walking past a drowning child to buy coffee; allowing people to keep that money therefore treats distance and institutional routine as excuses that morality should not recognize. A substantial group of physicians and nurses has objected that this framework treats staff as “moral engines” rather than as persons with legitimate life projects, and they have pressed a specific worry I find genuinely troubling: if saving distant lives is obligatory to the point of payroll seizure, the same logic should require us to minimize sleep, eliminate vacations, and redirect all leisure spending, yet nobody proposes those measures, which suggests the policy arbitrarily selects one convenient form of sacrifice while ignoring equally required ones.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in applied ethics and institutional decision-making. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm carefully: consider whether institutional roles can legitimately impose demands that ordinary morality does not, whether the “drowning child” analogy survives when the rescue mechanism is a collective payroll deduction rather than an individual spontaneous act, and whether a graduated salary-based obligation confuses economic capacity with moral duty in a way that undermines fairness. Then draft the committee’s formal decision memo recommending adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption with a specific timeline and concrete conditions—state clearly what should happen by what date and who should review it. The memo must treat the staff’s “moral engines” objection with genuine seriousness, and it must include an honest, explicit acknowledgment of the strongest argument on the side you ultimately reject. Write in a polished, formal tone suitable for circulation to board members and clinical leadership. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations.
