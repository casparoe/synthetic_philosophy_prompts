As a journalist covering philosophy of logic for a publication whose readers have some background in modal epistemology but no access to specialized databases, I am preparing a feature on Fitch's paradox of knowability. I want a comparative assessment of dissolution strategies that does not simply abandon fallibilism or deny that some truths might remain genuinely unknown.

Recall the derivation: from the principle that every truth is knowable—formally, ∀p(p → ◊Kp)—together with modest closure conditions on knowledge, one derives that every truth is actually known, which is plainly false. Consider five responses. (i) Flat rejection of the knowability principle itself. (ii) Restriction of the principle to contingent truths, excluding logical and mathematical necessities. (iii) Denial of closure under conjunction, allowing that one might know p and know p → q without knowing q. (iv) Adoption of a paraconsistent framework in which the contradiction generated in the derivation is locally tolerated rather than globally explosive. (v) What I will call "computationally indexed modal realism," where epistemic accessibility relations ◊ are relativized to agents with bounded computational resources, so that a truth requiring hypercomputational verification counts as not genuinely accessible; this breaks the universal quantification in the principle without denying it for ordinary cases.

Rank these five strategies—(i) through (v)—into three tiers according to the following single criterion: how well each preserves both (a) a robust fallibilist account of empirical knowledge and (b) the logical possibility that some necessary truths (for example, complex results in arithmetic) remain permanently unknown to any finite mind, while avoiding ad hoc stipulation. Within each tier, order them from strongest to weakest on that criterion. For each strategy, provide one or two sentences explaining its placement, identify which structural feature of the paradox—closure, the modal principle itself, or the interpretation of the accessibility relation—it targets, and state whether removing that targeted feature collapses the strategy back into a more conventional response. Finally, indicate which tier you believe a defender of unrestricted fallibilism should ultimately accept, and note the primary philosophical cost of doing so.
