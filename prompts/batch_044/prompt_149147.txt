I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the revival of John Dewey's political philosophy, and a colleague has shared with me the following summary of Dewey's democratic experimentalism, drawn from her reading of the Lippmann-Dewey debate. She writes: 'Dewey's democratic experimentalism holds that politics should be organised as a collective form of scientific inquiry, in which citizens treat policy proposals as falsifiable hypotheses and test them through direct democratic deliberation. In his exchange with Walter Lippmann, Dewey rejected the idea that modern societies are too complex for ordinary people to understand, arguing that with adequate education in observation and reasoning, democratic publics can reach objective consensus on social problems without relying on expert intermediaries. It follows that representative institutions are largely redundant: democracy works best when it operates like a laboratory, with the public itself designing experiments in living, measuring outcomes, and revising beliefs according to evidence rather than tradition or authority.'

I suspect this captures Dewey's anti-authoritarian spirit but may overstate his hostility to representation, conflate his notion of social intelligence with a literal laboratory protocol, and perhaps import a Popperian falsificationism foreign to Dewey's own conception of inquiry. Before I rely on this for publication, I need a precise audit. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specialising in American pragmatism and democratic theory. Begin by brainstorming the distinctions Dewey actually drew between scientific inquiry and democratic politics—particularly regarding the formation of publics, the role of experts, the meaning of consensus, and the difference between the great society and the great community—before turning to the summary itself. Then proceed sentence by sentence: identify what is broadly accurate, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what subtly misrepresents Dewey's view of expertise and representation. Finally, assess whether the policy conclusion—that direct citizen assemblies should replace representative bodies—actually follows from the accurate parts of her summary, or whether the analogy between democracy and a laboratory collapses precisely at the point where her recommendation requires it.
