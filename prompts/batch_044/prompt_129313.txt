I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance, and I found myself stuck on a point that seems rarely pressed: if family resemblance is meant to dissolve essentialism by showing that members of a category share only overlapping similarities, then the category "language game" itself ought to be susceptible to the same treatment. My friend insisted I was overreading the thread metaphor—where Wittgenstein compares family traits to fibers twisted into a rope with no single strand running through the whole—arguing that the image is merely illustrative and does not apply reflexively. I countered that once you apply the rope image back onto the concept of language games, the overlap between ordering, praying, measuring, cursing, and joking becomes so diffuse that the category threatens to collapse into mere nominalism, or worse, to conceal a hidden essence in the very act of denying one.

To settle the mood rather than the dispute, I would like a poem. Write a strict sestina: six end-words, rotated through six stanzas according to the classic pattern, with an envoi that gathers all six in the final three lines. Choose six concrete, distinct language-game terms—such as ordering, praying, measuring, cursing, guessing, and naming, though you may substitute others so long as they remain specific and non-synonymous. The rotation should perform the philosophy: in each stanza, an end-word should appear to share some feature with its predecessor, but that shared feature should dissolve before the cycle completes, so that by the envoi the six sit side by side with no common thread binding them. Do not resolve the tension with a closing definition, essence, or moral; let the adjacency in the envoi stand for the unreconciled family. The poem should be precise enough that a reader of the Investigations recognizes the rope and the reflexive instability I am describing, yet it should hold together as verse without requiring philosophical literacy to appreciate its music.
