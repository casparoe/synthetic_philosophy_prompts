I have been studying Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle in the context of formal epistemology, and I believe I grasp its formal content: my present credence in a proposition ought to match my expectation of the credence I will assign to it after further reflection. From this, however, I have drawn a practical conclusion that troubles me. If rationality requires that my current beliefs align with those I will hold once my reasoning is complete, then any active deliberation I undertake now seems not merely redundant but potentially disruptive, since my future self will already have arrived at the 'settled' view. It therefore appears that the rational strategy is to suspend present judgment altogether and wait passively to inherit my future opinions, even in cases where I anticipate that future reasoning might be distorted by cognitive bias or limited evidence. I am also concerned that if I reason now and then defer to my future self, I might be vulnerable to a diachronic form of Dutch-book vulnerability, as though I am betting against my own coherence.

Could you clarify whether Reflection actually functions as a prohibition on independent present reasoning — a kind of command for epistemic passivity — or whether I have misunderstood its status as merely a consistency condition? Please answer in British English, and I would particularly value an explanation of how present reasoning and future coherence can be reconciled without treating the agent's current deliberative capacities as obsolete.
