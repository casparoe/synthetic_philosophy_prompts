I have been covering the resurgence of interest in Erwin Schrödinger's metaphysical writings—particularly the essays in which he draws on Vedanta to argue that consciousness is singular, despite the apparent plurality of bodies and observers—and I am drafting a closing piece for an interdisciplinary salon on quantum interpretation and Eastern philosophy. Rather than ending with analysis, I want to open the session with a poem that treats Schrödinger's claim as an unresolved tension rather than a settled mystical doctrine: the wave function describes one reality, yet the spatial separation of bodies creates the illusion that there are many conscious subjects, and Schrödinger, reading the Upanishads, concluded that this separation is a kind of mask, with the identity of self and universal awareness pointing to something quantum mechanics describes but does not explain.

Please write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—using exactly these six end-words in this rotational order: body, wave, boundary, light, mask, one. The recursive circulation of these words should enact the repetition of individual experience across separated physical forms, while the envoi, where all six end-words must appear together in the final three lines, should perform the formal collapse of multiplicity into singular awareness. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with Schrödinger's quantum essays and his Vedanta readings recognizes the conceptual architecture—especially the notion that spatial separation produces the appearance of many observers for a single underlying reality—without reducing the argument to a slogan or invoking any specific text by direct quotation. It should maintain a voice that is skeptical, archival, and slightly journalistic: aware that the claim sounds mystical, unwilling to dismiss it as mere poetry, and honest about the fact that neither quantum mechanics nor Vedanta fully resolves the gap between physical description and the experience of being one among many. Approximately twenty-four lines total, no rhyme scheme required beyond the sestina's own repetition, and no resolution of the tension in the final lines—leave it open, as the salon intends to do.
