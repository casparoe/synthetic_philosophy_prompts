You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory, political philosophy, and environmental ethics. I am preparing a course module on the ethics of long-term technological commitment, and in discussing the material with colleagues I keep returning to a conceptual tension that I would like you to work through carefully from first principles rather than summarizing standard positions.

We have been using the idea of transformative experience—choices that restructure an agent's core values and preferences in ways that the pre-choice self cannot fully anticipate or rationally weigh—to analyze individual life decisions. I want to push this framework into less familiar territory. Consider a democratic legislature that must authorize, in the present, a multi-generational geoengineering program that will permanently alter climate patterns, ecosystems, and the cultural conditions under which future citizens will form their aesthetic, moral, and practical values. The future citizens are not merely unknown to us; they may be value-transformed relative to present voters in precisely the way that a person who becomes a parent is transformed relative to their childless self.

Please describe the most important implications of applying the transformative-experience framework to this collective authorization problem. Begin with some brief conceptual clarification—distinguish the epistemic barrier (the inability to simulate future values) from mere informational uncertainty—then address the following: Does the framework undermine the legitimacy of present-majority democratic authorization? Is there any institutional analog to individual informed consent that could bridge the epistemic gap? How does the collective nature of the decision—representation, generational succession, and the absence of a single continuing self—alter or dissolve the problem? Finally, identify the specific points where the individual-to-collective analogy breaks down, and tell me whether those disanalogies rescue democratic legitimacy or make the authorization problem even more severe. Think step by step, build the conceptual mechanics out explicitly, and avoid appealing to named authors or canonical texts.
