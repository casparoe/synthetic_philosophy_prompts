I am preparing a long-form feature on the moral architecture of everyday social coordination, and I keep returning to a tension that sits at the intersection of philosophy and anthropology. On one side is what might be called normative conventionalism: the view that practices such as tipping service staff, queuing in orderly lines, or observing local pedestrian norms are not merely efficient coordination devices but generate genuine moral obligations for anyone present in the social space, including temporary visitors. On the other side is what I think of as practice-embedded particularism, which holds that these same conventions only become morally binding when they are woven into a shared form of life—sustained by mutual recognition, tacit background knowledge, and reciprocal expectations that outsiders, by virtue of not inhabiting that form of life, do not share.

To sharpen the comparison, I want to introduce a framing I have been calling normative tourism: the tendency of visitors to treat highly visible conventions—like queuing protocols or greeting rituals—as optional cultural performances to be sampled or ignored at will, while remaining largely oblivious to the invisible conventions—such as embedded tipping economies or unmarked pedestrian-right expectations—that actually sustain the local moral order. Compare normative conventionalism and practice-embedded particularism with respect to how each framework accounts for the moral status of these invisible obligations, and whether normative tourism is best understood as harmless cultural consumption, a form of moral free-riding, or something structurally in between. In particular, I want to know which view offers the more coherent account of why certain rules feel obligatory to insiders yet remain genuinely opaque to outsiders, and whether either framework can justify demanding full compliance from visitors without collapsing the important distinction between moral obligation and mere social assimilation.
