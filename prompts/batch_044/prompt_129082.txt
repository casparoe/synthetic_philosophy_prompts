I spent much of last evening in a long argument with a close friend about whether the Late Bronze Age crisis, the Classic Maya transition, and the Easter Island socio-ecological transformation represent genuine societal collapses or are better understood as rapid reorganizations that we mislabel because we implicitly value centralized complexity. We kept returning to the same evidentiary tension: the archaeological and paleoecological record demonstrates striking continuity in local settlement patterns, ceramic traditions, and subsistence practices — populations persist, farming continues, and local social networks adapt — yet it simultaneously records the abrupt dissolution of palace economies, long-distance exchange, writing, and monumental construction programs. My friend maintained that if people survive and daily life persists, labeling the event a 'collapse' is merely a normative judgment about institutional scale disguised as historical taxonomy. I replied that if administrative literacy, inter-regional integration, and large-scale public works can vanish without leaving a demographic rupture, then defining collapse out of existence by pointing to surviving agriculturalists is like denying a bankruptcy because the debtor is still breathing. But I then noticed a more specific structural feature of our disagreement. Each framework seemed calibrated to render the other side's central evidence conceptually inert: within my friend's continuity model, institutional disappearance was automatically reclassified as 'decentralization' or 'local autonomy,' while within my collapse model, demographic persistence was automatically relegated to the status of background noise against which the real event — the loss of complexity — occurred. This produced not merely disagreement but a kind of definitional immunity, where no conceivable archaeological finding could count as decisive for either position because the terms had been arranged in advance to absorb or dismiss the opposing dataset.
