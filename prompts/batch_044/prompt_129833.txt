I take the view that moral foundations theory is not normatively inert: specifically, that the descriptive fact that conservative moral cognition draws on all five of Haidt's foundations — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity — while liberal cognition tends to draw primarily on care and fairness alone, provides a defeasible, ceteris-paribus reason for thinking conservative moral frameworks are structurally closer to the truth. The reasoning is that a normative account which can accommodate a broader range of stable, cross-culturally recurrent human moral intuitions without reducing them to error is, other things being equal, less likely to be parochially distorted. What would have to be true for this view to fail? I want the counterarguments, observations, and formal results sorted by decisiveness: does the move from descriptive taxonomy to normative weight commit a genetic fallacy? Could liberal frameworks implicitly subsume loyalty, authority, and sanctity under reconceptualized versions of care or fairness? Do cross-cultural studies undermine the universality of the five-foundation structure, or show that the apparent liberal-conservative asymmetry is an artifact of Western sampling? Would evolutionary debunking apply symmetrically, undermining all foundations including those conservative cognition favors? And have defenders of this normative inference insulated it by retreating to merely defeasible or other-things-being-equal qualifications to the point where no empirical finding could refute it — and if so, is that insulation a defect for a view that claims to derive normative insight from empirical psychology? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed proponent should concede from what only critics allege, and assess honestly whether the view, in its current formulation, is well insulated or genuinely exposed.
