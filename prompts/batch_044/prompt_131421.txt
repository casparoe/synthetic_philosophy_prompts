I have been working through my own position on karma as a theory of moral desert after a recent period of personal reflection on whether suffering can ever be genuinely undeserved, and I want a consistency audit before I present the view in any formal setting. Here are my commitments:

(1) I take karma to be a substantive metaphysical thesis, not a psychological heuristic or social metaphor: there is an objective, law-like correlation between the moral quality of an agent's actions and the quality of that agent's subsequent experiences, which is not reducible to social causation or natural consequence.

(2) I reject any substantialist interpretation of the bearer of karmic fruit: there is no permanent self or ātman that transmigrates, so if karmic continuity operates across lives, it must be secured through some form of psychological or relational continuity rather than through the persistence of a metaphysical substance.

(3) I hold that moral desert is fundamentally non-instrumental: when suffering is karmically appropriate to a prior wrong, its normative significance lies in its fittingness to the act, not in its deterrent, rehabilitative, or social utility.

(4) I also think that collective or distributed karma is conceptually coherent and possibly actual: the moral properties of group actions can generate karmic consequences that attach to individuals within the collective in ways that are not traceable to those individuals' personal past deeds.

(5) I am committed to determinism about human agency in the sense that agents lack libertarian free will; actions flow from character, circumstances, and prior causes, though they remain genuinely expressive of the agent's psychological structure.

(6) I regard apparently radical cases of undeserved suffering—congenital disease in infants, victims of mass atrocity—as providing defeasible but serious evidential pressure against the universality of karma, rather than being automatically dissolved by reference to hidden past lives.

My question is whether this package hangs together. Specifically: does (2)'s denial of a substantial bearer undermine (1)'s claim that there is an objective correlation linking specific actions to specific experiences, since there may be no stable subject to bear the correlation? Does (3)'s non-instrumentalism sit comfortably with (4)'s distributive feature, which seems to detach desert from the individual agency that (3) presupposes? Is (5)'s determinism in tension with (3)'s idea of fittingness, or does fittingness survive without libertarian freedom? And does (6)'s willingness to treat undeserved suffering as counter-evidence conflict with (1)'s assertion of a universal metaphysical law—must I either weaken (1) to a statistical tendency or abandon (6)?

Please audit these tensions systematically. Rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally problematic. For each pair, say whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs, and identify which single revision, if any, would clean up the most conflicts without collapsing the overall view. Do not assume any commitment is obviously expendable: each is central to the position I am trying to hold.
