I'm a senior editor for a technical standards board and we publish huge regulatory docs made up of like five hundred individual clauses, each vetted seperately by a different working group. Right now my team has no real framework for deciding clause-by-clause whether we "accept" it into the standard, reject it, or punt it upstairs — and the problem is straight out of the preface paradox plus the lottery problem. Any single clause looks 99% likely correct, but with hundreds of them we know some errors will definitely get in. I want you to turn that philosophical mess into an actual decision proceedure my reviewers can run in about twenty minutes per clause, with ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand off to a human because it's a values call not a probability call. Don't flatten out the distinction between "accepting for practical reasoning" and "believing it's true" — that has to survive in the steps somehow. I also want you to build in a kind of document-level "error budget" idea, where acceptance of one clause affects the risk pool for the rest, since that's the only way the lottery logic makes operational sense here.

Apply this to three cases: a routine typography/formatting clause; a pedestrian-bridge structural-load clause where expert confidence is 99.2% but being wrong kills people; and a climate-adaptation clause that an industry group is pushing hard for, where the historical base-rate for similar clauses being wrong is high. One of these needs to come out in a way my team genuinely won't like — probably forcing rejection or an expensive delay despite the high confidence number. Then be explicit about what the procedure can't do: for instance, who sets the global error cap, whether we're ever required to reopen accepted clauses, or how to weigh political pressure against the budget.

Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — the single governing rule that replaces a flat belief-threshold — then lay out the reasoning, the checklist in order, the three worked cases (including the unwelcome one), and the hard limits, all with no preamble and without restating my question. Keep it practical, but don't pretend the philosophy disappears.
