Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and metaphysics, I find myself drawn to debates that sit at the boundary of physical symmetry and ontological commitment. I am particularly preoccupied with Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and with the classic challenge posed by Black's symmetric universe containing only two spheres. Rather than a conventional essay, I want you to construct a formal decision tree—or, if you prefer, a branching taxonomy—whose root is the question of whether one accepts any version of the identity of indiscernibles and whose internal nodes are concrete commitments about Black's case and its physical analogues. Each branch must split on a single diagnostic claim that can be answered without already knowing the metaphysical label to which it belongs.

Please observe the following structural requirements precisely.

First, the tree must contain at least six terminal positions, or leaves. Among them should appear named positions—such as a strict Leibnizian view, a Black-style rejection, a weak-discernibility defense, and a relational-coordination view—together with at least one unnamed or unoccupied combination. For that empty slot, provide a brief diagnosis: is it incoherent, merely unexplored, or unstable?

Second, one branch must explicitly address whether assigning a coordinate frame or orientation to the symmetric universe—analogous to selecting a gauge in physics—counts as revealing a genuine metaphysical difference between the spheres or only as introducing a descriptive artifact. This is the angle that matters most to me, given my scientific background, and it must be phrased as a concrete question about the case rather than as "do you accept relationism?"

Third, another branch should ask whether the quantum-mechanical indistinguishability of identical particles offers a real-world parallel to Black's spheres, a decisive disanalogy, or an irrelevant comparison. The answer should separate positions that treat symmetry as ontologically substantive from those that treat it as purely formal.

Fourth, every node must be formulated as a yes-or-no or either-or question about a specific case or claim. Do not ask "are you an animalist?" or "do you accept narrative identity?" The path through the tree should implicitly sort the respondent; do not append a separate scoring key that asks readers to self-label.

Fifth, at each leaf provide the name of the position, or "unnamed" if it lacks one, followed by exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—for example, being compelled to deny that the spheres could exist at all, or being compelled to accept that purely relational differences without any intrinsic grounding are sufficient for numerical diversity.

Sixth, please flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate given the available answer combinations, and explain briefly why they collapse into one another.

The deliverable should read like an instrument for an interdisciplinary seminar: formal, precise, and usable by someone who understands gauge choices and quantum statistics but has not yet memorized the metaphysical taxonomy. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific texts; I want the structure to stand entirely on its own logical space.
