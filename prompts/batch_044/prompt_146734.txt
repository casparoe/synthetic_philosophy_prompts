I'm a high school student in an advanced philosophy seminar on metaphysics and personal identity, and we've just begun discussing Buddhist anatta—the denial of any permanent, underlying self that persists through change. I keep arguing with my friends about whether the strong feeling that I am "the same person" who started this course is just a practical fiction we maintain for coordination, and I'd like an activity that lets about fifteen students, aged sixteen to seventeen, actually feel that tension rather than just hear it explained in a lecture. The session is a single ninety-minute seminar in a regular classroom with only paper, tokens, and a whiteboard; students should not need prior reading or access to any texts.

Please design a runnable game with rules and small real stakes—perhaps accumulated points that determine the group's next discussion topic or a shared snack budget—so that participants experience the pressure to treat a changing cluster of parts as one enduring subject. Frame the mechanics around the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—but present them as interdependent "departments" represented by cards that can be replaced, duplicated, or lost across rounds according to hidden dependency rules, such as altering a form card forcing an unpredictable shift in the consciousness card. Divide the fifteen students into three "custody committees" of five, each responsible for maintaining continuity for one cluster. Each round, after some cards change, the committee must vote on whether their cluster retains privileges—access to resources, narrative records, or voting rights—and they must invent and record their own continuity criteria. The hidden dependency rules should systematically undermine every essentialist criterion they try, pushing them toward a conventional, negotiated identity rather than an underlying substance.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto parts of the Buddhist analysis: where committees invented an essence to solve coordination failures, where clinging to that invented essence caused unfair exclusions or conflict, and how the card-swapping approximates impermanence. Be frank about what the game cannot reproduce—the direct, first-person immediacy of awareness without an owner—which the facilitator must supply through guided reflection. Also predict what will predictably go wrong—such as one committee refusing to dissolve a cluster despite total card turnover, or students conflating narrative continuity with metaphysical identity—and explain how to handle those moments without breaking immersion. The core idea should emerge entirely from the rules, payoffs, and forced choices; there should be no opening lecture naming anatta or personal identity until after the simulation and debrief are complete.
