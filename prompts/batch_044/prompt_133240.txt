There's this old move in philosophy of physics — the Rietdijk-Putnam argument — that uses special relativity's lack of absolute simultaneity to argue that all times are equally real, so presentism is dead. I get the mechanics: if two observers in relative motion disagree about which events share a "now", and there's no privileged frame to break the tie, then "the present" can't be a single global slice. But what I can't figure out is why this debate hasn't moved in decades. One side says it's a straightforward derivation from Lorentz symmetry; the other side says it's a non-sequitor that smuggles metaphysics into coordinate physics. And then there's a quieter position that says presentism can survive if you treat simultaneity as a gauge artifact and presentness as tied to worldlines rather than hyperplanes, but nobody on either main side seems to engage with that.

My hunch is that the stall isn't empirical — nobody expects a new experiment to settle whether "now" is frame-relative — and it's not exactly verbal, though people do slide between "real" as ontologically basic and "real" as explanatorily indispensable. Maybe it's that the two sides are answering different questions: one about the geometry of spacetime, one about the ontology of temporal passage. Or maybe there's a hidden disagreement about whether gauge freedom is a feature of our descriptions or a feature of the world itself, and that distinction is doing all the work without anyone naming it. So my question is: what's the actual structure of this deadlock? Is it bedrock intuition about whether spacetime is fundamental, a hidden formal issue about Lorentz-invariant dynamics, or just two fields using the same vocabulary? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new conceptual distinction, a proof that presentist dynamics can't be Lorentz-invariant, or just admitting the question is ill-posed?
