For an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, draft a single 90-minute final-examination essay question on performative utterances that cannot be answered by merely reciting standard taxonomies. Center the question on a hypothetical scenario I call the proxy performative problem: an individual explicitly authorizes a synthetic, algorithmically mediated agent—reconstructed from the authorizer's vocal and textual patterns—to issue specific performative utterances (for example, contractual offers, formal apologies, or institutional declarations) on their behalf, with recipients fully aware of the mediation yet treating the output as directly authorized. The exam question should require students to analyze whether the felicity conditions associated with Austin's account (particularly those concerning sincerity, uptake, and the absence of infelicity) and the distinction between illocutionary force and perlocutionary effect developed in the speech-act tradition can still apply when the physical speaker and the authorizing agent are separated, and whether this separation generates a novel category of infelicity rather than a familiar one. Provide three items: first, the exam question itself, framed to compel a reasoned verdict rather than a descriptive summary; second, a model answer of approximately 600 words that weighs competing considerations and defends a clear conclusion about whether proxy performatives are genuinely possible or structurally defective; third, a marking scheme built around five specific analytical moves that distinguish an excellent answer from a merely competent one, with point allocations that sum to 20 marks, and with a brief one-line note for each move identifying the common misconception or superficial move that typically costs candidates marks at that point. Write throughout in a formal, polished academic register appropriate to a graduate-level assessment document.
