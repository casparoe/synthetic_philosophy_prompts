Schelling argued that credible nuclear threats often depend on brinkmanship—deliberately leaving outcomes partly to chance—because a fully rational, fully controlled leader can always back down, which empties the threat. This seems to generate a paradox: if a leader rationally designs an automated retaliation system—say, a Dead Hand-style mechanism—to make the threat credible, they remove the very uncertainty that defined brinkmanship; if they retain manual override, they preserve the risk but undermine credibility. So rational deterrence appears to require an irrational commitment device, yet rationally installing that device eliminates the irrationality it was meant to supply. Doesn't this show that "rational brinkmanship" is incoherent? And if stable deterrence genuinely requires uncontrollable elements, must we conclude that Cold War survival was mostly luck rather than strategic design?
