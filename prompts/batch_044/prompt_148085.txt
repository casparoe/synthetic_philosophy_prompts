Question: Bayesian confirmation theory represents the support that evidence E provides for a hypothesis H by the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E). Explain why this generates the so-called problem of old evidence when E was already known before H was formulated, using Einstein’s 1915 general theory of relativity and the long-documented anomalous precession of Mercury’s perihelion—approximately 43 arcseconds per century, calculated in part by Urbain Le Verrier in the nineteenth century—as your central case. Then discuss two proposed responses: Colin Howson’s distinction between updating belief in the occurrence of E and updating belief that E supports H; and Clark Glymour’s bootstrapping account, according to which a theory can confirm the auxiliary assumptions used to derive its predictions, which can then in turn support the theory. Does either response fully resolve the difficulty? Identify any tension that remains.

Student answer: The difficulty is that in standard Bayesian conditionalization the posterior is proportional to the prior multiplied by P(E|H) divided by P(E). If the agent already knows E, then P(E) equals 1, and dividing by 1 renders the Bayes factor undefined, so H receives no update whatsoever. In 1915 Einstein explained the Mercury discrepancy, but astronomers had recorded the 43 arcsecond residual for decades. A rigorous Bayesian should therefore have assigned it prior probability 1, leaving general relativity with no confirmatory gain.

Howson solves this by noting that scientists rarely assign literal credence 1 to measurements; instrumental error and observational fallibility always leave residual uncertainty, so P(E) is realistically closer to 0.99. Once the prior is slightly below 1, the factor is well-defined and can exceed 1, allowing GR to be confirmed.

Glymour offers a Bayesian strategy called bootstrapping. Deriving Mercury’s orbit requires auxiliary assumptions about other planetary masses. If GR predicts the orbit correctly, we can use GR to confirm those auxiliaries, and then feed the confirmed auxiliaries back into supporting GR, breaking circularity. Thus we are not updating on an isolated old fact but on the success of an interlocking theoretical package.

Still, neither solution is fully satisfactory. Howson saves the formalism only by misrepresenting scientific confidence—Mercury’s orbit was not treated as a 0.99 guess. Glymour, being Bayesian, shifts attention to auxiliaries rather than explaining why a previously known observational result should boost credence in a new theory at all. Consequently, the core tension remains: if the perihelion value was genuinely known before 1915, then by the lights of conditionalization no later theory should be able to learn from it, however elegant the explanation.
