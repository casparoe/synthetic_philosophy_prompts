In my interdisciplinary working group on historical explanation, we have been circling a disagreement that feels increasingly immovable: whether evolutionary and technological history are genuinely path-dependent—locked in by early accidents—or whether they would converge on broadly similar outcomes if replayed from the start. The contingency camp insists that small initial perturbations cascade into radically different biotas and technological lineages, often invoking the replay thought experiment; the convergence camp points to repeated evolutionary solutions like camera eyes, echolocation, and social organization to argue that functional constraints make certain endpoints nearly inevitable. Both sides appeal to the same phylogenetic trees and archaeological records, yet after months of discussion neither has moved, and I am beginning to suspect that the deadlock is not primarily a lack of empirical data.

My working hypothesis is that much of the stall is masked by an ambiguous criterion for what would count as the same outcome arriving anyway. One camp appears to be asking whether a replayed history would yield organisms with recognizably similar phenotypes and ecological roles; the other asks whether it would reproduce the same deep homological structures, genetic architectures, or specific historical lineages. If that is correct, then the two sides may be answering different questions under the single heading of path dependence—one about functional convergence, the other about historical and genealogical identity—while also disagreeing about what counts as explanatory success, such as whether a model must predict particular clade membership or only statistical distributions of traits. Yet I am unsure whether this conceptual confusion is the full story, or whether there is also a hidden substantive disagreement about the dynamics of evolutionary search spaces, or even a bedrock normative divergence about whether history must be law-governed to be scientifically tractable.

I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a survey of positions or a simple verdict: is this impasse mainly verbal, mainly substantive, or a layered mixture in which an empirical dispute about evolutionary dynamics is being disguised by conceptual ambiguity about sameness? Given your diagnosis, what kind of progress should actually be expected—would a formal model of evolutionary accessibility resolve it, a sharper distinction between phenotypic and genealogical convergence, or a reframing that abandons the replay metaphor altogether? And if the disagreement ultimately rests on incompatible intuitions about whether history is fundamentally open or constrained, is there any reason to expect that further argument, as opposed to a shift in disciplinary framing or in what questions are treated as well-posed, could actually move the needle?
