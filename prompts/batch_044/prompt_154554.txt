Please construct a formal argument for a deliberately restricted form of eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. Rather than denying that brains cause behavior, defend the elimination specifically of the folk-psychological taxonomy—the partition of mental life into discrete propositional attitudes with fixed satisfaction conditions—on the ground that it fails a criterion we can call inter-subjective calibration stability. That is, argue that attributions of particular beliefs or desires to a subject cannot be reliably calibrated across observers (including first-person access) when context shifts even slightly, whereas coarse-grained neural-dynamical descriptions show greater cross-context stability. From this failure, conclude that neuroscience should not seek a reductive mapping of belief onto brain state, but should instead replace the folk kind-structure with a dynamical-systems taxonomy—e.g., trajectories of predictive-error minimization—that bears no one-to-one correspondence to belief or desire.

In developing the argument, address the standard self-refutation objection without covertly relying on the eliminated kinds: show how the thesis can be asserted using only the successor vocabulary (neural dynamics, error-minimization patterns) and without attributing a belief-in-eliminativism to the speaker. Then examine whether this restricted view genuinely differs from reductive identity theory, or whether the distinction collapses once we recognize that any successful replacement taxonomy will necessarily host functional analogues of belief-like and desire-like states.

Keep the argument entirely self-contained: do not request bibliographies, do not cite specific texts, and avoid named philosophers unless a specific logical point requires them; if you reference any work, paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not invoke exotic neuroscience or obscure theoretical frameworks that depend on specialist knowledge; build the case from conceptual analysis and broadly familiar ideas about mental attribution and brain dynamics.
