So I've been noticing this wierd patchwork in how institutions treat binding yourself against future weakness. State gambling boards let you put yourself on a self-exclusion list for life, and if you show up at a casino they can actually have you arrested for trespassing — your past decision is treated like a contract you can't break. But in contract law, if you sign something while in temporary mental distress or extreme emotion, courts often let you void it, protecting your present self from your past mistake. Meanwhile retirement accounts penalize early withdrawal but don't actually block it, sitting somewhere in the middle. What philosophical picture of akrasia and personal identity over time does that whole pattern presuppose? Does it assume there's a true self that makes the pre-commitment and a temporary, irrational self that needs restraining? Or can a hyperbolic discounting model — where it's just one continuous agent with wierd time preferences — explain why we enforce some locks but not others? And is the practice philosophically consistent, or does it smuggle in one view of weakness of will for addiction policy and a totally different one for commercial contracts? If we replaced the implicit theory with a straightforward hyperbolic-discounting framework, what would have to change in how these institutions are designed?
