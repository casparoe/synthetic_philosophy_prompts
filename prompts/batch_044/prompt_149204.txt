I am leading a fifty-minute discussion section in my school's junior-senior ethics elective—no prior philosophy coursework required—on the problem of moral luck. Rather than surveying the literature, I want to construct one sustained explanatory arc anchored in a single concrete scenario, and I need a teaching plan (not the explanation itself) that accounts for dependency order, misconception management, and assessment.

The carrying case is this: two engineers, A and B, independently design an identical autonomous-vehicle braking algorithm using the same testing standards and with the same conscientious intent to prevent harm. By geographic accident alone, A's system is deployed in a municipality with well-maintained roads and clear signage, while B's is deployed in a municipality with degraded infrastructure and obscured signals. B's system is involved in a fatal collision that A's system, facing identical inputs, never encounters; in the aftermath, B is professionally ruined and publicly condemned, while A is praised for an unblemished safety record.

Plan the session as follows. First, specify the exact sequence in which the conceptual pieces must appear—what must be established before the "control principle" (that we should only be blamed for what is within our control) can be introduced, and what must be in place before the scenario is presented so that it operates as a philosophical puzzle rather than a news story. Second, identify which features the scenario must possess and which it must lack in order to isolate resultant moral luck from circumstantial and constitutive luck, and explain why this engineered comparison is more pedagogically useful here than the familiar drunk-driver examples. Third, map the predictable misconceptions—specifically, that I am denying outcomes ever matter for blame; that B is entirely blameless because road conditions "were not her fault"; and that this is merely determinism or free-will skepticism in disguise—and state the precise moment in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose one check-for-understanding question for the final five minutes that distinguishes grasping the tension from merely sympathizing with B. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether the standard fourfold taxonomy of luck, the compatibilist-libertarian debate on responsibility, or the distinction between moral and epistemic luck—and justify why omitting it protects the core insight rather than simplifying it into error. The students have seen only a brief summary of Kant's claim that good will is the only unconditional good; I will have a whiteboard and no handouts. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need a plan executable without external sources.
