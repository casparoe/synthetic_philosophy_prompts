I'm working through debates in normative ethics for a seminar and have become fixated on a recurring argumentative move that appears whenever an absolute deontological prohibition is pressed by a catastrophic counterexample. The pattern runs like this: a defender maintains that some act—intentionally deceiving an innocent party, treating a person merely as a means, or breaking a solemn promise—is categorically forbidden. A critic then constructs an extreme scenario in which obeying the prohibition produces disaster on a massive scale. Rather than saying the rule is false, or that it contains an implicit exception clause activated by severity, the defender typically preserves the rule's formal status but quietly contracts its scope. They redefine what counts as "deception" or "mere means" so that the catastrophic action technically falls outside the prohibition, yet they do so in a way that seems driven entirely by the pressure of the counterexample rather than by independent conceptual analysis. Once this reinterpretation is accepted, it is often applied back to moderate cases, making the rule far narrower in practice than it was originally presented, while still being defended as the same unbroken principle.

I have seen versions of this in discussions of lying to prevent atrocity, of sacrificing one to save many, and of violating confidentiality under duress, and it strikes me as structurally different from a straightforward threshold exception—where one openly admits that the rule is overridden above a certain severity—because here the rule is never said to yield; it is said never to have applied. It also seems different from ordinary conceptual refinement, since the boundaries shift to accommodate disaster scenarios rather than tracking prior intuitions about the concept. Is there an established name for this specific pattern—the retroactive narrowing of a rule's application conditions driven by catastrophic testing, without formal suspension of the rule? How precisely does it differ from familiar threshold accounts and from strategies that preserve a rule by reinterpreting its terms rather than admitting an exception? And if the literature lacks a precise label for exactly this failure mode, what term and boundary conditions would best capture it?

Please take a definite position rather than laying out options neutrally: either identify the phenomenon with an established concept and defend that identification against nearby alternatives, or argue that it is genuinely distinct, propose a precise term with clear boundary cases, and explain where its edges lie—particularly how to distinguish it from cases where a rule is honestly refined versus cases where it is defensively narrowed to escape a counterexample.
