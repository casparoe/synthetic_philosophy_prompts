I am currently in my second year of a doctoral program in history, enrolled this term in a seminar on archival epistemology and the politics of record-keeping. Next week I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—approximately seven minutes—at a small interdisciplinary workshop convened under the title Reading the Blank Spaces: Microhistory, State Archives, and the Ethics of Reconstruction. The audience is deliberately split: one half consists of senior historians and archivists whose research depends on state repositories, census ledgers, and bureaucratic correspondence; the other half comprises independent oral historians, descendants of the communities being studied, and museum practitioners who work primarily with material culture, family papers, and embodied memory. I need remarks that genuinely welcome both groups without quietly privileging one form of knowledge over the other, and that do not treat the workshop as a diplomatic compromise between equals.

The conceptual problem I want to structure the speech—not merely decorate it—comes from our course unit on history from below and microhistory versus the history of elites and states, specifically refracted through a philosophical tension that has been troubling me: is honoring sunk costs always irrational? In this setting, the sunk costs are not financial but methodological and institutional. We have invested enormous energy in preserving, cataloguing, and learning to read state documents with extreme care, and there is a genuine rationality in continuing to honor that investment rather than discarding it as worthless. Yet if we treat those methodological commitments as binding, we risk interpreting the absence of non-elite records as a natural void rather than as an engineered omission produced by the very institutions we study. The speech must therefore sit inside this discomfort: it should be able to affirm the real value of archival rigor without implying that only archived lives count as history, and it should be able to acknowledge the fragmentary, often painful nature of counter-archives without romanticizing absence into presence or inventing voices to fill silence.

Please write these remarks in a polished, formal register appropriate to an academic workshop but fully accessible to attendees without university training. Do not include citations, reading lists, page references, or bibliographic suggestions, since part of the audience lacks library access and the event is intended as a conversational opening rather than a lecture. The philosophical tension should determine the actual architecture of what can and cannot be said—what claims must be held back, what acknowledgments are obligatory, and how the invitation to shared work can be framed not as a resolution but as a common problem. Both groups arrive with different sunk commitments—institutional and emotional—neither of which should be dismissed lightly, yet neither of which should be treated as beyond examination. End with an invitation that recognizes this discomfort as shared rather than solved.
