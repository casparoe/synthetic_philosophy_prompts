I've been lurking on rationalist forums for years and usually just read, but this one's been bugging me since a seminar on just war theory. People keep using the boxing-match analogy to defend the moral equality of combatants: both sides step into the ring, they both know the rules, there's a referee applying them equally, and punching isn't wrong just because your opponent is a jerk outside the ring. The idea is that jus ad bellum and jus in bello seperate cleanly, just like the cause of the match doesn't change the rules inside it. I'm trying to figure out how far this actually holds.

Can you map it out explicitly -- what corresponds to the gloves, the consent, the referee, the corner team, the bell? Then tell me where the mapping breaks: conscription vs voluntary entry, the fact that one side might be defending against agression while the other started it, whether the "referee" (laws of war) actually applies symetrically when one side is systematically violating them, and whether the analogy quietly imports the idea that both fighters have equal moral standing regardless of why they're fighting. I keep wondering if there's a better analogy -- maybe two people in a burning building where one lit the fire -- but I'm not sure that survives either. Does the conclusion that soldiers on both sides are morally equal actually hold up once the disanalogies are listed, or was the boxing frame carrying the whole argument?
