For the final meeting of my seminar on value theory, please compose a fourteen-line poem in blank verse, with a volta at the ninth line, spoken from the perspective of someone who has just recognized that treating both a particular long friendship and a particular beautiful object—say, a weathered stone garden—merely as instruments for producing satisfactions is structurally the same error. The poem should employ only two rhymes: one between line 3 and line 11, and one between line 7 and line 13, so that each rhyming word is separated by the volta and held in suspension across the turn, forcing the reader to hold the first term intact until the second arrives in altered relation. In the octave, build the reductionist accounting: the friendship catalogued as mutual advantage, the garden praised solely for the calm it produces, each part extracted from its context as if value were additive. At the volta, the speaker should reject this ledger not by substituting a new calculus but by noticing that the very act of separating the parts destroys what was valued—thus making precise Moore’s idea that certain goods are organic unities, possessing a value that is not the sum of any discrete elements and that belongs to the whole in a way that cannot be distributed back to its parts. The sestet should allow the delayed rhymes to arrive, pairing terms that name relations or shared contexts rather than isolated properties, and should conclude without resolving the tension into either moral instruction or mystical awe. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader acquainted with Moore’s ethics recognizes his distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value, and his use of friendship and beauty as paradigm cases of organic unity, yet it must contain no citations, no references to specific works by title or date, and no quotation marks around any philosophical claim. Keep the tone restrained and philosophical rather than confessional.
