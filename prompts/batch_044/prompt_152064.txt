I'm working through Keynes's Treatise on Probability in a study group on the philosophy of economics, and I've become stuck on a particular conceptual move that seems to underlie his later distinction between risk and uncertainty, yet I can't tell whether I'm reading it correctly. In the text, Keynes argues that not all probabilities are commensurable in magnitude; he introduces what he calls the "weight" of an argument, defined not as the probability itself but as the amount and balance of relevant evidence on which that probability rests. Crucially, he observes that an increase in weight—gathering more evidence—need not raise the assigned probability, and may even lower it if the additional information reveals alternatives previously excluded from consideration. This suggests that rational belief might have a two-dimensional structure rather than being reducible to a single numerical betting quotient.

What I'm trying to understand is whether this "weight" is meant as a second-order property of our confidence in the first-order probability assignment, or whether it is an intrinsic feature of the logical relation between the premises and conclusion of the argument itself. If it is intrinsic, then Keynes seems to be claiming that some pairs of probabilities are strictly incomparable, not merely that we are less certain about one of them. Could you help me parse this distinction? Specifically, does this framework provide Keynes with a principled reason to reject the reduction of all rational belief to measurable expectation, and how exactly does it relate to his later claim in The General Theory that "uncertainty" concerns situations where there is no scientific basis for forming any calculable probability? I want to avoid conflating the epistemic humility expressed by low weight with the ontological claim that some outcomes are genuinely non-measurable, but I'm unsure whether Keynes himself keeps these separate, and whether the passage I've described supports one reading over the other.
