I've been prepping for my punishment seminar next week and I keep getting stuck on a move that I think gets dismissed too quickly. There's a version of retributivism—call it the ledger view—that holds capital punishment is justified not by deterrence or rehab but because the state carries a duty to balence moral accounts: a deliberately taken life demands a forfiet life in return. On this reading, the familiar liberal objection about executing the innocent doesn't carry the same weight, since killing an innocent person is horrific but amounts mainly to a bookkeeping error (the wrong entry in the ledger) rather than a category rights-violation that makes the penalty uniquely monstrous. I think this is more coherent than people admit, though maybe I'm being too cute.

So I want an honest stress-test: exactly five considerations that would genuinely count against this ledger-version of retributivism, presented as a numbered list with no introduction and no conclusion, each point just a sentence or two. Please separate what's fairly settled in the philosophical literature from what's genuinely up for grabs, and don't present a contested reading as if there's consensus. I'm especially interested in whether any of these objections collapses the view into standard deterrence theory, or if they leave the ledger idea intact but just awkward.
