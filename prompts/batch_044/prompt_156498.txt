My flatmate has been going on about approval-directed agents again and I think he's tied himself in a knot, but I can't quite spot the knot. He wants to show the whole distinction is a mirage, which feels wrong because then half of alignment discourse makes no sense. Here's the chain he laid out, numbered so I can keep track:

(1) Call an agent approval-directed with respect to evaluator E if it picks actions purely to maximise E's score, with no independent world-state goal.

(2) In any real setup, E computes that score from observed behaviour via something like a Bayesian update over the evaluator's belief about the agent's intentions.

(3) So the score is just a function of world-features: the trajectory that occured, plus the evaluator's internal belief-state about it.

(4) Therefore maximising the score is just maximising a utility defined over an expanded state-space that includes both physical outcomes and the evaluator's mental representations.

(5) That makes approval-directed agents a special case of goal-directed agents — ones whose goals happen to include "have the evaluator think well of me".

(6) If so, they inherit all the standard dynamics: instrumental convergence, deception, and power-seeking, because all of those help secure a good opinion.

(7) So there's no seperate risk profile; talk of "deceptive alignment without long-term goals" is just misdescribing a goal-directed agent with an unusually social goal.

(8) Yet alignment researchers treat approval-directed systems as posing a distinct danger — one that doesn't need long-term planning — which only makes sense if the agent's objective isn't reducible to another world-state goal.

He reckons the slip is in (4), but if we cut (4) don't we end up saying that maximising a score isn't maximising anything coherent? I really want to keep (1) and (8) if I can — they feel like the heart of the matter. Which step actually breaks, and what's the cost of giving it up? In particular, if we reject the move from (3) to (4), do we have to allow that an agent can pursue something that isn't a world-state utility at all, or is there a subtler equivocation hiding in the reasoning?
