I have recently become a parent after undergoing preimplantation genetic testing that revealed a substantial risk of transmitting a late-onset, non-fatal but severely debilitating autosomal neurological condition. This experience has transformed Derek Parfit’s nonidentity problem from an abstract puzzle into an urgent personal question, and I am now preparing a proposal for a one-year interdisciplinary dissertation fellowship. I need you to write the proposal—approximately two pages, formal in tone—without including a bibliography or citation list, which I will supply myself.

The proposal should address a precisely stated question: does the standard nonidentity argument against harming future people still hold, or does it require fundamental revision, when reproductive choice is not a binary switch between existence and non-existence but a graded selection among embryos that share parental genetic lineage and differ only in the probability and timeline of expressing the same heritable condition? Existing philosophical treatments treat the counterfactual as “a different world with a different population,” which allows them to conclude that no numerically identical person is harmed. But clinical reality involves selecting among genetically related embryos where the rejected alternative is not absence but a sibling-like potential person with the same genotype expressed differently. The proposal must explain why this clinical framing leaves the standard answer unsettled, and why an obvious empirical or bioethical approach—simply surveying patient attitudes or regulatory guidelines—would not resolve the conceptual issue.

The method should be philosophical rather than empirical: analyze whether numerical identity remains the correct criterion for harm in graded selection cases, and whether we need an intermediate category of reasons—perhaps “lineage-affecting” or “family-affecting” obligations—that is neither fully person-affecting nor wholly impersonal. State what the possible conclusions are and which conceptual distinctions would decide among them. Describe a realistic deliverable for the fellowship year—likely a 6,000-word paper with a clearly demarcated philosophical thesis—and specify what would count as failure, such as finding that the graded-selection framing collapses without remainder into the standard binary framing. Give an honest assessment of scope: what can be achieved in one year, and where the project might become too large if it drifts into full-blown population ethics or clinical policy recommendation.

Write this from my perspective, maintaining the personal context as the motivating frame, and keep the tone appropriate for a fellowship committee in philosophy or philosophy of medicine. Do not invent references; refer to positions and arguments by name or description only.
