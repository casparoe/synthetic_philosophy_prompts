I have been discussing with a friend, who is taking a course on logic though I am not, the difference between two ways we use the word "if." She says there are conditionals that stay close to the actual world—for instance, if the match is struck in dry air it will light—and others that reach out into unreal possibilities, like if the match had been struck in a vacuum it would not have lit. What strikes me, and what I keep turning over, is an asymmetry she described: with the first kind you can keep adding conditions to the "if" part without overturning what follows—if the match is struck, and the air is dry, and the striker is intact, it still lights—whereas with the second kind, thickening the premise can reverse the conclusion. She gave an example about a missed train: if I had missed the train I would have walked; but if I had missed the train and it had been delayed, then I would have caught it; and if I had missed the train, it had been delayed, and I had been injured, then perhaps I would not have walked after all. I want a poem that makes this instability visible.

Please write a sonnet of fourteen lines in iambic pentameter, with a clear volta at line nine. In the octave, present an indicative claim about a seed falling on stone: it does not take root, and adding further conditions—no rain, no warmth, no soil—only reinforces the denial without changing it. In the sestet, turn to the counterfactual twin: imagine the same seed falling on stone in a world where it might have been carried to earth, but then strengthen the antecedent in stages—first with frost, then with shade, then with drought—so that each added premise pulls the outcome in a different direction and the conclusion never settles. The closing couplet should hold both positions side by side: the indicative refusal that remains steady, and the counterfactual promise that keeps dissolving under its own conditions. It should be precise enough that someone familiar with the logic of conditionals recognizes the parallel, but composed so that the structural tension is felt even by a reader who has never studied the subject formally.
