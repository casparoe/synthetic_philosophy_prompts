Please explain the conceptual tension at the heart of the gender-equality paradox: in societies with stronger institutional equality and lower economic coercion, career segregation by gender often widens rather than narrows. The usual framing treats this as either unmasking innate preferences or exposing residual socialisation, but both readings conflate structural opportunity equality with what we might call preference-autonomy equality—the absence of normative pressure that channels people toward gender-typical choices even when material barriers are removed. How should we understand the paradox if we keep these two dimensions separate? Could it be that high-trust, low-coercion environments do not reveal pure individual preference, but instead allow social signalling—status, identity, peer coordination—to operate without economic distortion, thereby amplifying gendered sorting that scarcity had previously suppressed? I want a conceptual map that avoids collapsing the mechanism into a simple nature-versus-culture dichotomy, and that clarifies what kind of equality is actually being measured.
