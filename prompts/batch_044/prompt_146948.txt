I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and I also sit on this small interdisciplinary grant board that gives seed funding in cognitive science and adjacent philosophy—like eight awards a year out of forty-some pre-proposals. I only have about twenty minutes per proposal and no access to citation databases in the room, so I need something I can actually run with paper and pen. The issue I'm seeing more often isn't direct plagiarism; it's what I've started calling framing herding. Applicants latch onto a methodological frame—predictive processing, causal inference from observational data, whatever was prominent in the last funded round—not because they've shown it solves their specific problem, but because the vocabulary signals they're part of the cascade. I was talking with my advisor about whether this is just rational convergence and she said probably not always, but I need a practical way to seperate the two without being able to check every citation.

So I'm asking for a reusable decision procedure my panel could run in that twenty-minute window: a sequence of questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we stop with a "this looks cascade-driven, send back," and where we have to admit the call depends on our risk tolerance rather than facts. It needs to cover the hard cases—when the frame genuinely fits, when an applicant rejects the cascade but sounds incompetent rather than independent, and when they cite the cascade papers correctly but don't actually depend on them. Then apply it to three hypothetical pre-proposals: one applying predictive coding to study aesthetic judgment in museum visitors, one rejecting all computational framing for social cognition in favor of pure ethnography, and one using do-calculus on archival letters to map influence networks in history of philosophy of science. Make sure one of those gets an answer I'd personally dislike—probably the ethnography one getting flagged unfairly?—and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checks, plus where the procedure will misfire and approve something hollow.

Oh, and please say how confident you are in the whole thing, especially in the claim that framing herding is actually distinguishable from legitimate convergence in this setting—point out the specific step you're least sure holds up. Also flag anything the procedure just can't do, like predicting whether rejecting the cascade will hurt an applicant's career regardless of merit. Don't ask me for citations or a reading list—half our board doesn't read the formal literature anyway and I wouldn't have time to check them in the meeting.
