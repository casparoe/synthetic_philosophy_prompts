As a doctoral student in evolutionary biology working at the intersection of philosophy of science, I am seeking a structural diagnosis of a disagreement that has stalled in my department’s working group for over a year. We have been debating the adaptationist programme—the view that natural selection routinely shapes phenotypic traits into finely tuned functional optima—against the Gould–Lewontin critique that many traits are spandrels, structural byproducts of selection acting elsewhere or of deep developmental architecture. Every participant can recite the standard moves: the charge of unfalsifiable just-so storytelling, the defense that null comparative models are now standard practice, the counter that spandrel claims are themselves often immune to refutation, and the canonical illustrative cases of human language capacity and the human chin. Yet positions have not shifted, and I suspect the impasse is not primarily empirical.

To sharpen the analysis, I want to introduce a framing I have been developing: the entanglement quotient, defined as the degree to which a trait’s ontogeny is mechanistically coupled to another trait that is the actual target of selection, such that the first trait is produced as an unavoidable structural byproduct rather than being independently modifiable by selection. If two researchers disagree about whether language is an adaptation or a spandrel, are they tracking different empirical predictions about this quotient—specifically, whether language-related developmental pathways can be decoupled from broader cognitive architectures—or are they operating with different standards for what counts as discharging an adaptationist explanatory burden?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what adaptationists and spandrel-advocates actually claim about concrete cases such as language and the chin. Is the standoff substantive (about evolutionary history, comparative genetics, or developmental mechanics), verbal (about the extension of terms like spandrel, exaptation, and adaptation), or a case of two research programmes answering different questions—one about optimal design, one about architectural constraint—under a single heading? Do the sides differ about hidden normative commitments, such as whether parsimony in evolutionary explanation requires a functional assignment for every prominent trait? Are there bedrock intuitions about naturalness or design that neither side can argue for?

Given your diagnosis, specify what would count as progress. Would it require a formal model of developmental entanglement that generates quantitative predictions about decoupling potential; a comparative method capable of distinguishing coupled structural byproducts from independently optimized traits; a conceptual distinction—say, between structural spandrels and functional exaptations—that dissolves part of the dispute; or is the disagreement genuinely intractable, and if so, why? I do not want a bibliography, a literature survey, or citations; I want an analysis of the architecture of the disagreement itself and an honest assessment of whether anything—empirical, formal, or conceptual—could actually move it.
