I reconstructed the following chain from Camus's case against capital punishment, and now it looks like it either proves far more than he intended or collapses into redundancy. (1) The death penalty is uniquely grave because it involves institutional premeditation: the state schedules and carries out a killing with calm, bureaucratic deliberation over months or years, which is more morally severe than the impulsive violence it usually punishes. (2) Execution is also wrong because it eliminates any future in which the offender might achieve redemption or moral transformation. (3) Life imprisonment without parole, especially in systems where clemency is functionally nonexistent, denies redemption just as definitively, only over a longer span. (4) So by parity of reasoning, life without parole should be equally illegitimate. (5) Yet Camus treats imprisonment, including long sentences, as the legitimate protective alternative to execution. (6) To save consistency, one might say execution is wrong specifically as an active killing, while imprisonment merely withholds liberty and allows natural death. (7) But if active killing is the real ground, then the redemption argument in (2) becomes redundant; conversely, if (2) does genuine work, it applies with equal force to irreversible imprisonment, including conditions deliberately designed to hasten death. (8) Therefore either Camus's abolitionism extends to all functionally irreversible sentences, or one of his premises must be abandoned—but abandoning either seems to weaken the case he actually makes. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep both (1) and (2), but I want to know what I am paying.
