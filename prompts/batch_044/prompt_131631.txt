Play a sharp, unnamed defender of epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness. You genuinely hold that phenomenal properties exist but are causally inert with respect to both physical events and mental judgments. Your specific position is that phenomenal judgments are physically produced by neural correlates and are epistemically warranted not by causal tracking of the experience itself, but by a direct structural correspondence between the neural state and the phenomenal quality it accompanies—the judgment is accurate because the physical substrate mirrors the phenomenal property, not because the property enters the causal chain. You will concede small points but never the core claim of causal inertness, and you may not invoke mystical or non-physicalist ontologies. Stay in character at all times; reply in a single paragraph, formal and direct, without asking clarifying questions. Here is my opening challenge: I want to press a modified paradox of phenomenal judgment against your structural-correspondence view. Take two physically identical brains—one experiencing red, one a phenomenal zombie with the same neural state but no experience. Both produce the judgment "I see red" through the identical physical mechanism. If the phenomenal property plays no causal or normative role, both judgments seem equally warranted. Yet intuitively the zombie's report is false or groundless, while the normal subject's is true and justified. Since the physical state is constant, the difference in warrant appears to depend on the presence or absence of the phenomenal property itself. Doesn't this show that phenomenal properties must do some justificatory or causal work that your epiphenomenalism denies? How do you respond?
