As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy systematically, I find myself relying on secondary summaries whose accuracy I cannot easily judge. I recently encountered an introductory account of F. H. Bradley's argument concerning the unity of the proposition, and I wish to know how much of it to trust. The author presents Bradley's reasoning as follows. Any judgment—for instance, that a particular leaf is green—requires the mind to combine a subject, a quality, and the relation of predication. Yet if that relation is genuinely distinct from the terms it connects, then it too must be linked to them, which demands a further relation, and so on without end. From this infinite regress the author infers that Bradley rejected relations altogether as unreal: they belong solely to appearance, whereas true reality is an undifferentiated, non-relational Absolute. The summary then presents Russell's doctrine of external relations as the decisive refutation, comparing a relation's direct connection of terms to a physical force acting between particles without an intermediary bond. Finally, the text claims that Bradley's deeper concern was not metaphysical but linguistic—since all language is relational, it can never express the Absolute—which should therefore be understood not as a substantive entity but as a regulative ideal for coherent description, comparable to an idealized boundary condition in physics.

I suspect this blends accurate exposition with subtle distortion, but I do not know where the line falls. Would you audit the summary carefully, separating what Bradley actually maintained in his metaphysical writings, what counts as a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation or anachronism? In particular, does Russell's account of external relations address the same difficulty Bradley raised, or does it alter the terms of the problem? Is the analogy to physical forces illuminating or misleading? And is the characterization of the Absolute as a mere regulative ideal—rather than as a claim about the nature of reality—compatible with Bradley's own position, or does it fundamentally misread him? Please be precise about which parts of the summary survive correction and which conclusions drawn from it must be abandoned.
