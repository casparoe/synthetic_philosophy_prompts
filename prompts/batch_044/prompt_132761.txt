Construct a thought experiment engineered to test whether a cultural value-orientation can operate as an independent causal factor in economic behavior, analogous to the role Weber assigns to the Protestant ethic but deliberately stripped of its theological content and applied under conditions that should break standard materialist explanations. Design a realistic, non-science-fictional scenario involving two isolated societies—designate them A and B—with identical technology, legal institutions permitting capital accumulation, initial resource endowments, and class structures. In A, a secularized "inner-worldly ascetic" value system prevails: productive labor and rational accounting are treated as moral duties irrespective of consumption needs, and social prestige attaches to demonstrable reinvestment rather than to leisure or display. In B, accumulation is legally permitted but culturally coded as morally ambiguous; prestige derives from ritual mastery and communal redistribution, and there is no stigma against non-productive leisure.

The scenario must contain an engineered inversion feature: both societies suffer an identical exogenous shock (for instance, a sustained ecological change) that makes further capital accumulation materially disadvantageous for all individuals—marginal returns become negative, and legal structures actually impose costs on reinvestment. Despite this, A continues disciplined rational accounting and reinvestment for at least two generations, even though the behavior is individually costly and offers no hidden consumption benefit. B, by contrast, immediately redirects surplus toward ritual expenditure and consumption.

Present the scenario in a single paragraph. Then demonstrate that it satisfies the design specifications: (i) state the verdict delivered by a Weberian "elective affinity" account (the value-orientation possesses independent causal efficacy through elective affinity with rational economic conduct, persisting even when material incentives invert); (ii) state the verdict delivered by a materialist-reductionist account (values are epiphenomenal to material interests and should dissipate once incentives turn negative, so the persistence in A either reveals a misdescribed hidden interest or a temporary cultural lag); (iii) state the verdict delivered by a functionalist account (values stabilize behavior that is adaptively functional, but cannot drive genuinely maladaptive conduct, so the functionalist must argue that A's persistence is not truly maladaptive—perhaps via long-term group-level selection).

Next, identify the precise structural feature that does the causal work—the combination of inverted material incentives with identical legal and institutional frameworks—and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed (if incentives remain positive, both the Weberian and materialist accounts can explain A's behavior, and the scenario collapses into an underdetermined correlation). Finally, explain how a reductionist defender would likely attack the scenario—probably by alleging that the "moral duty" framing in A disguises a continuing class interest or that the cost structure has been mischaracterized—and specify what explanatory cost that defense incurs. Provide the answer in American English.
