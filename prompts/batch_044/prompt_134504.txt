Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps—the Comparative Counterfactualists and the Conceptual Historians—would produce on whether the contrastive framing “why Britain and not China?” is conceptually well posed, and on whether Joseph Needham’s parallel question about the development of modern science suffers from the same defect or from a different one.

Position A, the Comparative Counterfactualists, maintains that disciplined counterfactual comparison is possible: one can hold constant a cluster of pre-industrial conditions—agricultural productivity, market integration, state fiscal capacity—while varying institutional arrangements such as property-rights regimes, coal accessibility, and colonial resource flows, and thereby derive genuine causal insight about why sustained industrial growth emerged in one case and not the other. They deny that this framing is teleological; they treat industrialization not as a predetermined endpoint but as an observable cluster of processes whose absence demands explanation.

Position B, the Conceptual Historians, maintains that the question is malformed in three distinct respects. First, it treats “Britain” and “China” as symmetric, unitary actors, eliding the vast internal heterogeneity of Qing territories and the composite, non-unitary character of the British state and its colonial periphery. Second, it requires holding constant the very social and institutional conditions that are themselves part of the explanation, so that the counterfactual “China with British institutions” is not a coherent possible world but a conceptual hybrid. Third, it imports a teleological frame by treating industrial capitalism as the implicit baseline against which both societies are measured, rather than as one contingent path among others.

Your deliverable should be a document both camps could sign. It must contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about the historical record and about the relationship between the Great Divergence debate and the Needham question—specifically including agreement that early-modern Chinese technological and economic capacities in several regions were comparable to those in parts of Western Europe; that the British case involved a specific, non-random conjunction of resource endowments and institutional changes; and that Needham’s question about modern science is not identical to the economic-divergence question, though the two are frequently conflated in popular discussion.

2. Precise, neutrally worded formulations of the residual disagreements, with Position A’s version phrased so that Position A would accept it as accurate, and Position B’s version phrased so that Position B would accept it. Explicitly identify any dispute that is merely verbal—such as whether “counterfactual” requires modal realism, or whether “China” in this context denotes a civilizational region, a state apparatus, or an economic zone—and state what substantive disagreement remains once terminology is fixed.

3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. For each crux, provide a concrete comparative case, thought experiment, or scenario both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case. One crux must address whether the Needham question and the Great Divergence question fail for the same reason—namely, that both illegitimately isolate one variable from a path-dependent institutional bundle—or whether they fail for different reasons. Another crux must address whether treating polities as “natural experiments” with isolable variables is methodologically defensible in comparative history, or whether configurational causation makes such isolation impossible without distorting the object of study.
