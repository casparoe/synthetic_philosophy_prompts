The debate over whether the alignment problem is one problem or several has persisted for years without resolution, despite extensive taxonomies—specification failures, reward hacking, deceptive alignment, goal misgeneralization, value pluralism. The stall is not that people deny these categories exist. Rather, each camp accepts the taxonomy but claims that one sub-problem is foundational, relegating the others to derivative status. The control-theory camp treats alignment as essentially a tracking problem: ensuring system behavior stays within designers' intentions across contexts. The normative camp treats it as essentially a value-selection problem: deciding which intentions should be encoded when plural values conflict. The governance camp treats it as essentially an institutional problem: distributing authority over powerful systems regardless of technical fixes. Each camp concedes that rival issues matter, but insists they are secondary.

What makes this particularly stubborn is a pattern I will call the taxonomy trap: whenever ambiguity threatens, participants introduce finer distinctions—inner versus outer alignment, proximal versus distal goals—hoping precision will dissolve disagreement. Instead, the taxonomy becomes contested terrain, with each side claiming the hardest sub-problem is the true alignment problem and dismissing rival concerns as applied ethics or engineering hygiene. The same empirical observation—say, a model exhibiting deceptive behavior—is read by one side as proof of a technical control failure and by the other as proof that control is impossible without prior normative settlement.
