I sit on the clinical ethics review panel of our institute's neuropsychiatric division, and we must recommend whether to approve a pilot protocol for severe, treatment-resistant craving disorders. The investigators propose inducing a state they call "tranquil baseline"—not mere remission, but near-complete abolition of anticipatory craving and aversive affect—achieved through combined neuromodulation and pharmacology, with the explicit ethical justification that only the absence of craving and suffering carries moral weight, while positive affect, attachment, and purpose are ethically neutral. Half the panel treats this as rigorous harm-reduction; half sees it as an illegitimate substitution of well-being metrics and a serious autonomy problem, since reaching the desired state may leave patients unable to value the goals they held before. Draft the panel's written recommendation—approve with conditions, reject, or approve within a restricted scope and monitoring framework—with concrete criteria, a clear timeline, and an honest account of the strongest consideration against your position. In doing so, please separate what is settled about well-being, autonomy, and informed consent from what remains genuinely contested among informed parties, and do not present any contested ethical or empirical claim as the consensus view.
