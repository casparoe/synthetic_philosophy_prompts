At a dinner party, two friends—Elena and Marcus—got into an argument about Quine’s “gavagai” thought experiment that neither of them could resolve. Elena started by saying that if a field linguist observes a speaker shout “gavagai” whenever a rabbit runs past, the evidence is equally compatible with translating the word as “rabbit,” “undetached rabbit part,” or “rabbit stage”; since all three manuals predict every observable response, there is literally no further fact about which translation is correct. Marcus agreed that the behavioral evidence underdetermines the choice, but he said Elena was sliding from epistemic underdetermination into metaphysical indeterminacy: Quine shows we cannot know the right manual from the outside, not that there is no right manual at all. Elena pushed back that Quine rejects the “museum myth” of meanings as hidden mental exhibits, so there is no hidden correct translation waiting to be discovered; the manual does not record meaning, it assigns it, and if two assignments fit all the data, neither is uniquely correct. Marcus then introduced a distinction he called pragmatic determinacy: even if “gavagai” is semantically indeterminate, the speaker’s practical reasoning—how they plan hunts, divide meat, and avoid certain movements—might determinately treat rabbits as enduring objects rather than as fleeting stages or scattered parts, and that practical structure could privilege one manual without appealing to hidden mental objects. Elena replied that Marcus had not broken the tie but relocated it: a rival manual could map “gavagai” onto “undetached rabbit parts” and reproduce the very same practical reasoning by adjusting the background logic of planning and division, so pragmatic coherence does not select a unique translation either. Marcus answered that if every manual can be saved by adjusting background logic, then mistranslation becomes conceptually impossible, which overextends Quine’s point: Quine allows determinacy within our home language and restricts radical indeterminacy to translation between radically different schemes, so Elena’s global conventionalism is stronger than Quine’s own view and collapses the distinction between getting someone wrong and merely using a different manual.
