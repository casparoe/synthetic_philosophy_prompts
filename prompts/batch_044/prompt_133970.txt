A graduate student in my seminar summarized a result about rationalizability in strategic-form games as follows: "In any finite game, if you iteratively remove every strategy that is strictly dominated by a mixture of other strategies, you are left with exactly one well-defined set. Every strategy in that set belongs to the support of at least one Nash equilibrium, and every Nash equilibrium draws only from this set, so rationalizability and Nash equilibrium coincide completely. The same is true if you replace strict dominance with weak dominance—the elimination just requires more rounds, but you still arrive at a unique outcome regardless of the order in which you drop strategies. Consequently, if you add a decoy strategy to Battle of the Sexes that is strictly dominated by an appropriate mixture over the original strategies, that decoy is eliminated and the only rationalizable outcomes are the two pure-strategy Nash equilibria." I am inclined to think she is right about the strict-dominance case but that she is overreaching elsewhere. How fair is this summary? Please go claim by claim: identify which parts are correct without qualification, which are true only in two-player games or under other unstated restrictions, which conflate distinct notions, and which are simply wrong. Then evaluate her application to the decoy-augmented Battle of the Sexes: is the decoy necessarily eliminated by a mixture, and do the rationalizable outcomes of that modified game indeed reduce to just the two original pure-strategy equilibria? If her reasoning contains a subtle error about the relationship between rationalizability and equilibrium, state precisely what it is and what the correct relationship is.
