I am preparing a seventy-five-minute session for a graduate seminar in philosophy of social science—twelve students—on path dependence, specifically on whether historical outcomes are genuinely “locked in” or whether equivalent outcomes would have arrived via alternative routes. Rather than rehearsing the standard economic illustrations, I want students to experience firsthand why asking “would the same outcome have arrived anyway?” can become conceptually unstable, not merely empirically difficult.

Design a fully runnable, non-digital classroom simulation for groups of four, using only printed rule-cards, token pools, and a shared score-sheet. The activity should occupy about thirty-five minutes, followed by a fifteen-minute structured group deliberation and a twenty-five-minute debrief.

The conceptual target is a distinction the rules themselves must generate: weak path dependence, in which an initial contingency selects among equilibria that remain comparable by stable, ex ante criteria; and strong path dependence, in which the trajectory endogenously constructs the very metrics—efficiency, legitimacy, fairness—by which outcomes are evaluated. In the strong case, comparing the actual path with a counterfactual one does not yield a straightforward “better or worse” judgment; it produces incommensurability or circular assessment, because the ruler has been shaped by the path being measured.

Please specify:

1. Materials and setup: the content of the rule-cards, the token system, any assigned roles (e.g., recorder, skeptic, allocator), and a non-monetary payoff structure—such as collective reputation points or a shared seminar-credit incentive—that creates real but small stakes without requiring cash.

2. Round structure: four brief rounds in which a seemingly trivial initial allocation (perhaps a random draw or a barely-noticed tie-breaking rule) determines which “institutional technology” the group develops. Over successive rounds, the feedback they receive, the features they are permitted to build, and the success indicators printed on their score-sheet should all be endogenous to that initial choice. The criteria must emerge from cumulative investment rather than being fixed beforehand.

3. The turning point: in the final round or in the subsequent deliberation phase, require each group to evaluate the technology they did not choose, using precisely the evaluative framework their own path has produced. Arrange the rules so that they run into a genuine evaluation failure—disagreement that cannot be resolved by appeal to shared standards, or the discovery that their criteria of “efficiency” or “justice” do not coherently apply to the alternative structure—before the facilitator names the phenomenon.

4. Debrief script: map specific moments from the simulation onto the weak/strong distinction. Identify exactly which round produced endogenous metric formation, which moment exposed the counterfactual comparison problem, and why this is not just ignorance about the other path. Be explicit about the feature of strong path dependence the simulation can reproduce (compressed, observable metric contamination) and the feature it cannot (the decades-long sedimentation of institutional identity and emotional-political investment in path-generated standards), which the facilitator must supply conceptually.

5. Predictable failures: name at least two things that will go wrong on the first run—such as groups ignoring the counterfactual-evaluation rule and reverting to intuitive, pre-theoretic comparison, or the facilitator accidentally telegraphing the conceptual point during the rules explanation—and state precisely how to handle each without destroying the experiential force of the activity.

The deliverable should be the complete design: the rule-sheet outline, round-by-round mechanics, the deliberation prompt given to groups, the debrief with the required conceptual mapping, an honest accounting of the simulation’s limits, and the troubleshooting notes.
