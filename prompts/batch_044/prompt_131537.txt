Please explain the conceptual tension that emerges when Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment—where the brain is extracted, sustained in a vat, and linked to its original body via remote transmission—is modified by what we might call an "asymmetric latency split." In this variant, sensory data from the body reaches the brain almost instantaneously, but motor commands from the brain to the body are subject to a substantial and variable delay. The result is a system in which the brain experiences the world in real time but can only act upon it retrospectively. How does this temporal decoupling affect the anchoring of the first-person indexical "I"? Does psychological continuity still bind the self to the brain, does bodily control shift the reference to the body, or does the delay fracture the self into something distributed across the link itself? Address specifically whether standard resolutions—whether based on narrative identity, psychological continuity, or embodied cognition—can absorb this kind of latency-fragmented architecture without merely redefining the boundaries of the self to match whichever endpoint currently dominates. Separate what is well-established about Dennett's original scenario and about theories of personal identity from what remains genuinely contested regarding distributed or temporally decoupled agency, and avoid presenting any single resolution as the consensus.
