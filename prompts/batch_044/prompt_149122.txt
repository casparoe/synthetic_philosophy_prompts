I was appointed three months ago to the board of trustees of a mid-sized charitable foundation in the Midlands, and I now chair the quarterly grants panel. We have begun receiving an increasing number of proposals that frame themselves through effective altruist reasoning: cost-effectiveness analyses, long-termist projections, promises of deferred giving via high-earning careers, and demands that we fund only what can be measured against a quantitative welfare metric. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, sufficient-answer conditions, clear stopping points, and explicit hand-offs to value judgement—that my panel (none of us philosophers, all with legal or sector backgrounds) can actually run in a ninety-minute session without outsourcing the reasoning to outside experts. The procedure must operationalise three specific conceptual pressures: the institutional critique (whether accepting the proposal reshapes our charitable mission or creates capture), the earning-to-give objection (whether the proposal defers moral obligation into a speculative future income stream), and measurability bias (whether goods that resist quantification are being systematically excluded not because they lack value but because they lack metrics). For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in rejection, where it terminates in approval, and where it must flag that the remaining disagreement is one of values rather than facts; also flag the points where the procedure is likely to misfire or produce an answer the panel will dislike. Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals: first, a £200,000 request to fund mass deworming in East Africa, supported by a published cost-effectiveness estimate of approximately thirty pounds per DALY averted and a condition that our foundation’s branding appear on all programme materials; second, a £50,000 seed request from a local community theatre collective whose founders propose taking corporate finance roles for five years, donating half their earnings back to the collective, arguing this maximises long-term social value through an “earn to give” pathway, but whose artistic work produces no quantifiable welfare metric whatsoever. Please write the answer in British English, keep the tone formal and suitable for a board document, avoid bibliographies or citation requests, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.
