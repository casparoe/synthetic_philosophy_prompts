I am putting together a diagnostic handout for a philosophy of knowledge seminar, and I want a decision tree that sorts participants by the actual commitments they hold about epistemic justification, not by the names they attach to themselves. The problem is that students often call themselves foundationalists while accepting circular reasoning in practice, or reject an infinite regress in the abstract without realizing that their view requires an endless chain of supporting reasons. To avoid that, the tree should begin with concrete scenarios—such as being asked to justify the belief that a cat is on the mat through successive why-questions—rather than with abstract labels, so that each branch point is something a respondent can answer from intuition alone.

The tree should move through the standard logical space: whether justification can bottom out in beliefs that need no further support; whether circular support is ever permissible; whether an unending linear chain can be virtuous rather than vicious; and, crucially, whether justification is necessarily linear at all or can be distributed holistically across a network. I want the leaves to include classical foundationalism, modest foundationalism, holistic coherentism, linear coherentism, infinitism both with and without the virtuous-chain qualification, and one position I am introducing for this exercise—call it node foundationalism, in which justification terminates not in a single basic belief but in a minimal cluster of two or three beliefs that support each other without any one member being basic on its own. Please also include an unoccupied combination: a view that insists on linear justification, denies circularity, denies basic beliefs, and yet also denies that an infinite chain can ever be virtuous; note briefly whether you take that gap to be incoherent or merely unexplored. Where the tree genuinely cannot separate two positions—say, modest foundationalism and node foundationalism in certain configurations—flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than forcing a false division, and show how answer patterns map onto each leaf.

Every node should be phrased as a concrete claim or case question that a student could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to, and the deliverable should include both the tree and a brief scoring key. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; this is meant to be usable by someone reasoning from first principles alone.
