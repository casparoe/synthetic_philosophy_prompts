I've been chewing on this idea called the unilateralist's curse — basically that in domains like transformative AI, the least cautious actor can unilaterally impose risks on everyone else, so careful parties get dragged along whether they like it or not. Lately I've been mixing it with the dispositions literature, which might be a stretch, but hear me out: I think societies have a kind of collective dispostion toward cooridination and safety regulation, but competitive pressure functions like a fink or a mask that blocks that disposition from ever manifesting. So the curse isn't just a game-theory failure; it's like a metaphysically masked institutional capacity. That makes it feel more inevitable, which worries me.

But I want to stress-test it. What would actually count against this framing? I mean genuinely decisive defeaters, not just "regulation happens sometimes." Would it take showing that high-capability R&D can't actually be done unilaterally? A formal result showing that coordination mechanisms are fink-proof? Historical cases where competitive pressure failed to block safety norms? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones defenders of the curse would concede versus dismiss. Also, has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability — like "if coordination happens, that's just temporary masking" — and is that insulation actually a problem for a claim of this kind? Tell me what specific argument or observation would change your mind, and point to where your reasoning here is most likely to fail or lean on a contested analogy.
