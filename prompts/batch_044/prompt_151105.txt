I've been wrestling with a claim I'm trying to defend in my thesis — that in cases of genuinely incommensurable values (not just difficult trade-offs, but values with no shared metric whatsoever), rational choice doesn't merely get harder, it actually becomes impossible in principle. Any attempt to reach an all-things-considred verdict, I think, secretly imports a hidden monistic value like practical coherence or autenticity that wasn't in the original options. So either value pluralism collapses back into monism, or rationality is just silent and the decision is pure volition. I was discussing this with some friends over coffee and they said it sounds like Ruth Chang's parity thesis, but I don't think parity solves it — parity says the options stand in a relation of being on a par, which still feels like there's a rational relation, whereas I'm claiming there's literally none.

Anyway, I'm wondering: what would actually count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be — specific intuitions about hard choices, formal results about preference agregation, empirical work on decision-making under value conflict, and whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifability (and if that insulation is itself a problem). I'd also like to know which of these a committed defender of my line would themselves concede as damaging, not just what critics say. Please write your reply in British English, and don't worry about giving me a bibliography — I just want the arguments.
