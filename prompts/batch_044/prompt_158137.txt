Construct a thought experiment, in one paragraph and using only standard neural-network components, that isolates whether a particular internal unit in a trained model counts as a representation of an external property P. Engineer the case so that a causal-informational account, on which representation requires carrying information about P and playing an appropriate causal role in the system's outputs, and a structural-mapping account, on which representation requires preserving structural relations between P and other represented features, both deny representational status, while a teleofunctional account, on which representation requires being selected for tracking P in the system's learning history regardless of current causal efficacy, affirms it. To achieve this, inject a synthetic training regime: the model includes an epistemic-bottleneck layer that compresses all information about P into a single unit U, but U is connected to the rest of the network via a gated skip connection that is open during gradient descent and closed during inference; the model learns accurate predictions solely through other pathways. The task should be realistic, such as predicting chemical toxicity from molecular graphs using an ordinary graph neural network or transformer, with no exotic physics, no omniscient observers, and no adversarial examples that are obviously pathological. After presenting the scenario, walk through the verdict each of the three theories delivers and why, identify the specific structural feature—the training-open, inference-closed gate coupled with the bottleneck—that forces the divergence, explain whether removing that feature collapses the disagreement back into a standard consensus, and describe how a defender of the teleofunctional view, here the outlier, would attack the case, together with the reply available to the other two accounts.
