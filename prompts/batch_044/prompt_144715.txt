I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a ninety-minute seminar session for first-year epistemology graduate students who have completed an introductory course in modal logic but have not yet studied formal epistemology. I want a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for introducing the Löbian obstacle to rational self-trust, specifically the structural difficulty that arises when an agent attempts to endorse the general reliability of its own belief-forming mechanisms under conditions of modal closure. For the carrying example, I want a single realistic scenario drawn from scientific practice rather than an artificial puzzle—specifically, a research group that must evaluate the calibration of its own predictive ensemble using only internal diagnostics, where rational self-endorsement either collapses into an implausible confidence level or forces the group to abandon an otherwise sound methodological principle. The scenario should be contained to one paragraph, should not invoke exotic physics or perfect predictors, and should make the generating structural feature visible without requiring the audience to follow a formal derivation. Include one check for understanding—one or two targeted questions—that reveals whether the students have grasped that the difficulty lies in the closure conditions rather than in psychological overconfidence or empirical error. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and openly acknowledged as beyond the session's scope: the arithmetic interpretation of the modal derivation, the technical statement of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and any game-theoretic modeling of trust dynamics.
