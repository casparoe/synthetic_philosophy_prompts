Consider the following short essay I am drafting for a philosophy of social institutions seminar.

The standard Western definition of corruption—treating it as the misuse of entrusted public authority for private benefit—presupposes a sharp boundary between public office and private interest that is itself a historical artifact of the ideal-type bureaucratic state. In many societies, practices labeled clientelism or gift-giving are not deviations from institutional norms but the very medium through which state and society relate. A Japanese bureaucrat receiving seasonal oseibo gifts, a Brazilian vereador distributing food baskets to constituents, and a corporate lobbyist providing a luxury vacation to a regulator are often grouped together as corruption, yet they operate within radically different normative economies. I propose we distinguish not by whether private benefit accrues, but by whether the practice violates the internal fiduciary logic of the institution it inhabits. Call this the reciprocity-betrayal criterion: corruption occurs only when an actor accepts a benefit in exchange for violating the specific obligations of their role—for example, a patron hoarding resources meant for clients, or a gift-giver demanding a precise quid pro quo that breaks the reciprocity norms sustaining the gift economy. By contrast, practices that integrate private and public roles according to locally legitimate reciprocity norms—such as the long-term maintenance of bureaucratic relationships through seasonal gifts—are not corruption even if they fail an institutional-purity test imported from Western liberal frameworks. The danger of universalizing Western standards is not merely ethnocentrism; it is a category mistake that treats constitutive practices as pathological deviations, thereby obscuring the actual mechanisms of accountability that operate within them.

Please comment on this essay with particular attention to the following. First, is my distinction between reciprocity-betrayal and institutional-purity conceptually sharp enough, or does it collapse once we apply it to ambiguous middle cases—say, a politician who distributes goods according to an unwritten but widely understood expectation of electoral support? Second, have I mischaracterized how Western anti-corruption frameworks actually work—do they genuinely presuppose bureaucratic purity, or do they already accommodate functional equivalents? Third, the essay claims that universalizing Western standards is a category mistake; is this too strong, given that many local actors themselves invoke corruption-language to criticize practices like clientelism? I am especially interested in whether there is a stronger objection I have missed: that my criterion makes corruption too context-dependent to serve any cross-cultural regulatory purpose, and whether that context-dependence should be treated as a feature or as a bug.
