I have been working through the norms-of-assertion literature for a seminar on epistemic responsibility, and I have started framing the standard positions—knowledge, truth, and belief—through a distinction that does not seem to appear in the texts I have read: what I call the speaker’s “revision-cost profile.” On this view, the knowledge norm assigns the speaker maximal revision-cost, since being wrong means she failed to know rather than merely being insincere; the belief norm assigns minimal cost, because false belief is still compatible with having satisfied the norm; and the truth norm sits in between, requiring accuracy without the robust anti-luck condition that makes revision especially damaging. From this angle, it looks as though the familiar taxonomy treats these norms as rival answers to a single question—what mental state licenses assertion—when they might actually be answers to different questions about how epistemic risk should be distributed between speaker and audience.

My confusion is this: if knowledge entails belief, and both norms regulate the same speech act, then is treating them as incommensurable “exposure profiles” a category mistake? Or is the real disagreement not about licensing conditions at all, but about whether assertion should function as a high-stakes guarantee (where the speaker absorbs the risk of error) or as a low-stakes invitation to joint inquiry (where the hearer bears more of the burden of verification)? I want to know whether my framing reveals something the standard debate obscures—namely, that philosophers are not arguing over a single normative condition but over the institutional purpose of asserting— or whether I am simply conflating the pragmatics of conversational repair with the constitutive rules that govern the act itself. Which part of my framing has to go?
