I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of art and race, grading a midterm for my seminar on aesthetics and politics. Below is the exam question I assigned, together with one student's response. I want you to assess the answer as an instructor or TA would.

Exam question: In W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art," he contends that all art is propaganda and calls on Black artists to tell the truth about Black life rather than pursue pure beauty. Working with the distinction between propaganda as disclosure (making visible what dominant narratives suppress) and propaganda as manufacture (fabricating strategic images regardless of truth), evaluate whether Du Bois allows any room for aesthetic autonomy, or whether he collapses art into purely instrumental political communication. Can his view distinguish truthful racial art from mere ideological production?

Student answer: Du Bois's core claim is that all art serves a social purpose and therefore functions as propaganda; he rejects the idea of art for art's sake and urges Black artists to reveal actual Black experience to counter racist stereotypes. In this respect he clearly champions propaganda as disclosure. Yet Du Bois also adopts Alain Locke's position, articulated in The New Negro, that Black art should transcend race to express universal human values, and that this universalist aim is what separates legitimate disclosure from mere manufacture. Consequently, Du Bois and Locke actually agree that propaganda is permissible only when it serves universal beauty rooted in racial particularity, so Du Bois's view is not in tension with aesthetic autonomy after all. From this it follows that on Du Bois's framework an artwork succeeds only if it actually persuades white viewers: if it fails to change minds, it has failed both as propaganda and as art, because it has neither disclosed truth nor achieved universal reach.
