I am moderating a colloquium Q&A for our university’s postgraduate philosophy series, and the guest is an unnamed philosopher who has recently defended what she terms “frame-relative moral remainder” — a position intended to navigate between Bernard Williams’s argument that genuine moral dilemmas exist and the standard resolutionist view, associated with figures such as Ruth Marcus, that apparent conflicts dissolve once obligations are properly specified and ranked. On her account, a moral dilemma is neither a logical contradiction within a single normative system nor a mere psychological conflict; rather, it arises when two equally authoritative normative frames — for example, institutional and filial — converge upon a single action-execution pathway, generating obligations that cannot be jointly satisfied yet both remain binding, leaving an ineliminable residue of unfulfilled duty even after the agent has acted in the best available manner.

I should like eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond restating the position. In particular, I want at least one question that presses on the formal architecture: if obligations are indexed to frames, by what non-arbitrary criterion is one frame granted precedence in a given situation without collapsing the view into a hidden form of consequentialist weighing? For this, please include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer — namely, that precedence is determined not by aggregative comparison but by the internal authority conditions of the frame itself — and push her on whether those authority conditions are not themselves contested across frames. Second, I want a question on the phenomenological distinction she draws between moral residue and Bernard Williams’s notion of agent-regret. She claims that agent-regret is excessively psychological, whereas moral residue is an objective feature of the normative situation that persists even when the agent is fully justified and feels no emotional turmoil. Anticipate her defence that we can detect residue through the appropriateness of reparative obligations rather than affective states, and construct a follow-up asking how she distinguishes such reparative obligations from ordinary remedial duties that arise after any harmful but permissible action, without relying on an intuitionist reading of moral remainder.

Third, include a question concerning second-order or meta-level conflicts: what occurs when the frames themselves generate obligations about how first-order conflicts ought to be resolved — for instance, when a professional code demands transparency about internal disagreement, while a familial frame demands discretion? Provide a follow-up that addresses her probable response that second-order obligations operate at a different logical level, and ask whether that stratification does not simply relocate the dilemma rather than dissolve it. Fourth, I want one question built around a novel, concrete scenario rather than a standard trolley-style case: imagine a senior archivist at a government institution who discovers documentary evidence of historical abuses committed by a still-living former official who is also her parent. Her institutional role demands public disclosure according to archival ethics, while her filial frame generates obligations of loyalty and protection. The question should ask specifically how her framework explains the qualitative difference in residue when the agent chooses disclosure versus silence, and whether the residue in the disclosure case is genuinely moral rather than merely relational.

Finally, please ensure that every main question is paired with a targeted follow-up, that none of the questions are generic biographical enquiries, and that the sequence builds progressively — beginning with the conceptual structure, moving through phenomenology and meta-conflict, and concluding with the applied scenario.
