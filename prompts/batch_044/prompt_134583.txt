I'm drafting a chapter on informed consent for clinical trials and have settled on a fairly demanding view that I'm not fully sure I can defend: valid consent requires not merely the absence of coercion and deception, but what I call counterfactual epistemic autonomy—the subject must be able to articulate, in vocabulary they already possess rather than one they've been taught for the occasion, the central risks and benefits they are accepting, and this articulation must remain stable across at least two counterfactual presentations (different ordering, different framing, different emotional valence). I also treat competence as irreducibly domain-specific rather than global, so that being competent to consent to a routine financial contract gives no direct license for consent to an experimental neurostimulation protocol, and I deny that this competence can be fully delegated to a proxy without altering the normative status of the act. The position is meant to be stricter than the standard Beauchamp-Childress framework without collapsing into paternalism, but I'm aware it may over-intellectualize consent in ways that exclude valid consent by non-expert subjects. So I want an honest map of its exposure: what observations about actual consent practices, what formal results in decision theory or moral psychology, and what limiting cases—perhaps involving deception that still feels like autonomy, or coercion so structural it cannot be named—would genuinely count against it? I want decisive defeaters separated from merely awkward ones, and I want to know which of these a defender of this view should concede as damaging rather than dismiss as irrelevant. In particular, I'm curious whether the domain-specificity of competence creates a regress problem—who certifies the certifiers?—and whether that is fatal or just administratively inconvenient. If the position turns out to be well-insulated because every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by refining the notion of "counterfactual stability," is that a theoretical virtue or a sign that I've insulated it into unfalsifiability? I'd like the answer to treat my framework as genuinely at risk, not as a strawman to be protected.
