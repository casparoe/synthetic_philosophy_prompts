There's this weird patchwork in how democracies staff decision-making bodies. For criminal trials we pick jurors by lottery—total random selection—then vet them in voir dire and treat their verdict as legitemate even though they have zero legal training. But for writing laws we insist on elections, career politicians, and term limits on presidents or legislators "to prevent careerism," yet we never randomly select lawmakers for long terms. Meanwhile citizens' assemblies—like the Irish one on abortion or the French climate convention—get drawn by lot, meet for a few weekends, write a report, and vanish; they face no term limits because they don't really have terms at all. So we seem to think random people are competent enough to judge guilt or advise on constitutional reform for one weekend, but not to govern continuously, and we think elected people need rotation but randomly picked ones don't need it because they're already temporary.

What philosophical commitments about competence, representation, and democratic legitimacy does this setup presuppose? Does it assume that lawmaking competence is somethign you accumulate over years (hence career politicians and term limits to refresh them), while judgment competence is innate or distributed evenly (hence juries and one-off assemblies)? Or does it presuppose that election confers a mandate that must be limited, while sortition confers only a statistical sample that needs no rotation? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly needs all those assumptions or is philosophically underdetermined—maybe several rival views about political knowledge could license the same split. And tell me what a system built on the opposite commitments—say, lifelong sortition with mandatory deliberation training, or elected assemblies with no term limits but strict recall—would have to change about our institutions.

Also, is there a hidden consistency problem? We punish careerism in elected office but depend on career civil servants; we trust random citizens with life-or-death verdicts but not with budget allocations. Whats the underlying view of human reasoning that makes that split feel okay, and does it hold up once you spell it out?
