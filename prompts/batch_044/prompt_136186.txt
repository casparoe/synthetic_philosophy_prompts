I am a senior at a college-preparatory school editing our student philosophy review’s interview series, and for the upcoming issue I have arranged a conversation with an unnamed philosopher—let us call her Dr. L. Voss—who defends a precise extension of the Stalinesque interpretation of the color-phi phenomenon into emotional consciousness. Where the standard debate asks whether the apparent pre-change experience of a moving dot is an Orwellian revision of a prior conscious state or a Stalinesque construction of a false past, Voss argues that emotional valence is subject to an analogous but distinct form of “retroactive affective stitching”: the feeling is not merely updated after the fact, but inserted into consciousness with a fabricated temporal origin, so that a subject reports having felt distress at time t when in fact the affect was assembled only at t+1. I need eight to ten formal interview questions that avoid generic biography and do not ask her to restate the view in broad strokes. At least one question must press the boundary between Stalinesque and Orwellian revision specifically for affective content, anticipating her likely reply that emotion lacks the discrete, reportable temporal markers that make perceptual revision detectable; please include a follow-up that asks whether that very absence renders the Stalinesque account for affect unfalsifiable rather than genuinely explanatory. Another question should address how predictive-processing frameworks—treating perception and affect as hierarchical hypothesis-testing—interact with her claim that valence is retroactively assigned rather than merely corrected; the follow-up should respond to her probable defense that prediction-error minimization operates subpersonally and therefore poses no threat to first-person authority. I also want a question about whether her view entails that autobiographical narratives of emotional history are systematically unreliable, and if so, what epistemic authority, if any, remains for introspective reports of feeling. Finally, include one question that asks what empirical or phenomenological observation could in principle distinguish retroactive stitching from ordinary emotional learning, with a follow-up keyed to the answer that no third-person observation could settle the matter. The tone should be polished and suitable for print, not casual podcast banter, and the questions should press exactly where the existing literature on postdictive consciousness and temporal binding already applies pressure.
