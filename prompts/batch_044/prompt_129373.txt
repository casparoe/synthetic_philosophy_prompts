I'm giving the opening remarks tomorow at the kickoff workshop for our town's pilot citizens' assembly — forty people picked by lot from the voter rolls to break the deadlock on waterfront zoning, and I was literally drawn last week, which is surreal since I teach political theory part-time but now I'm also inside the experiment. Half the room are neighbors who think random selection sounds like handing power to strangers with zero mandate; the other half are governance scholars who know the lottocracy literature cold and will catch any sloppy talk about "representing the people." I need a five-minute speech that does real structural work: it has to explain what kind of authority we actually have when nobody voted for us, and use that very lack of mandate to show why this setup might force a genuine compromise between the environmental and development values that have paralyzed the elected council for years. Don't pretend we're representatives in the usual democratic sense — the philosophers will cringe — but don't leave the residents thinking this is just chaos either. Please write it in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, like an email or a letter I'm going to read straight off my phone.
