At a neuroscience-philosophy reading group, two members argued about what split-brain cases actually show about the unity of consciousness. Maya said the classic experiments—where each hemisphere controls one hand and can respond to stimuli presented to the opposite visual field—demonstrate that severing the corpus callosum produces two distinct streams of consciousness, each with its own intentions and perceptual field. She pointed to cases where the left hand undoes what the right hand just did, as if two agents share one body. Leo countered that this overinterprets behavioral conflict: the patient still reports a single point of view, still uses "I" for both hands, and still exhibits cross-cueing strategies that suggest one mind trying to coordinate split resources rather than two minds competing. He argued that what breaks is not consciousness itself but interhemispheric information integration, leaving a single conscious subject with fractured access—like one person with a damaged internal communication line, not two people in one skull.

Maya responded that Leo was smuggling unity in through language: the patient's verbal reports come mostly from the left hemisphere, which dominates speech production, so of course "I" sounds singular—you're only hearing one of the two streams talk. She said cross-cueing actually supports her view, because it shows each hemisphere inventing explanations for behavior it didn't initiate, exactly the kind of confabulation we'd expect if one consciousness were observing another's actions and rationalizing them post hoc.

Leo then proposed a hypothetical variation: imagine a patient whose callosal fibers are not cut but pharmacologically silenced only during decision-making, leaving sensory and motor pathways intact. If the patient still experienced unified agency despite the hemispheres being unable to share deliberative content, that would suggest unity doesn't require callosal transmission of intentions—undermining Maya's claim that the split creates two agents. Maya replied that this scenario begs the question: if deliberative content can't cross, then by definition there aren't shared intentions, and any appearance of unity would be an illusion maintained by external behavioral observation, not an internal phenomenal fact.
