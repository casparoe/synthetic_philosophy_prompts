hey so my studdy group came up with this and now i'm second-guessing everything. (1) Supererogitory acts — like donating a kidney to a stranger — are genuinely good but not morally required. (2) If something is genuinely good, there's always a pro tanto reason to do it. (3) A pro tanto reason becomes an all-things-considered obligation unless there's a stronger counter-reason. (4) So the only way (1) holds is if there's always a hidden counter-reason (the personal cost) that blocks the obligation. (5) But then "beyond the call of duty" just tracks where personal costs happen to be high, not a special moral category. (6) To save the category, some people invoke "moral credit banking": doing the act earns you credit, not doing it doesn't wrong anyone, but it also doesn't earn credit. (7) Yet if credit is genuinely moral currency, then failing to earn it when you easily could is a kind of free-riding; and if free-riding is wrong, we're back to a weak obligation to do the act. (8) Worse, if credit can be banked across people, then my failure to donate puts me in debt to those who did, which makes non-donation a wrong against other donors. (9) So either supererogation collapses into cost-sensitive obligation (giving up 5) or it creates interpersonal debts (giving up 6). I really want to keep (1) and (6) — is there a way, or which step should I bite? Be committed: actually pick a step and defend it, don't just map the landscape. Tell me what rejecting it costs.
