In my advanced seminar on evolutionary models in culture, our instructor relies heavily on the classic gene-to-meme analogy—treating viral digital ideas as replicators subject to mutation, selection, and heredity—to argue that attempting to deliberately steer an online information ecosystem is as misguided as trying to direct biological evolution by conscious design. She claims that algorithmic amplification merely accelerates a self-correcting selective process, and that any top-down intervention only introduces noise into a system that already optimizes itself for adaptive spread. Before I write my response paper, I want to examine how far this mapping actually holds. Could you provide an explicit correspondence table for the analogy in the specific case of a politically charged meme circulating through recommendation algorithms: what serves as the genotype, the phenotypic expression, the mutation mechanism, vertical versus horizontal transmission, and the selective environment?

Then identify the precise fractures—particularly whether cultural replication permits Lamarckian inheritance of acquired traits, whether horizontal transmission between unrelated hosts undermines the branching-tree logic that makes biological selection tractable, and whether the analogy smuggles in the questionable assumption that cultural spread is optimally adaptive rather than merely contagious. Finally, please assess whether her anti-interventionist conclusion survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether it was carrying the argument all along. If the gene analogy collapses under this scrutiny, would a revised comparison—perhaps treating viral memes not like nuclear genes but like horizontally transferred plasmids, or even like prion-like misfolded proteins that propagate structural damage—better capture digital cultural dynamics without licensing the same laissez-faire conclusion? I am not looking for a bibliography, only a rigorous conceptual audit that I can use to structure my own argument.
