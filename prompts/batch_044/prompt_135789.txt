Consider a tribunal applying Thomas Szasz's distinction between brain diseases and problems in living. Its past rulings are consistent: schizophrenia is not a medical illness but a problem in living framed for social control; Alzheimer's disease is a genuine brain disease with demonstrable organic pathology; severe alcohol dependence is not a disease but a behavioral habit; a congenital chromosomal disorder causing profound cognitive impairment is a genuine organic condition, not part of the mental-illness myth; and persistent school refusal with no detectable neuropathology is not a medical disease.

The tribunal now faces a petition involving a patient with a documented, localized prefrontal lesion from trauma who exhibits total apathy and refusal to work. Neurologists confirm the organic damage, yet family members and clinicians classify the behavior as an antisocial personality disorder and seek involuntary psychiatric commitment rather than neurological rehabilitation.

What should the tribunal decide to remain consistent with its record? Identify the closest analog among its prior rulings, say whether it should classify this as a brain disease or as a problem in living, and state honestly what principle it would be adopting with each choice. Then articulate the two most plausible general rules that explain all five prior verdicts equally well, predict what each rule says about this new case, and specify one further hypothetical case that would definitively distinguish them.
