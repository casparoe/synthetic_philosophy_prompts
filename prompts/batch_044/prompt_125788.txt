I've been following too many online discussions where Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems are treated as a universal solvent for formal systems in philosophy of mind, theoretical physics, and ethics. For a rationalist study-group handout, I want a decision tree that sorts the actual positions people hold on whether, and in what sense, these theorems apply outside pure mathematics. Start from the commitment that there exist consistent formal systems with recursively enumerable axiom sets that cannot prove their own consistency. Then branch on concrete, answerable properties of the target domain: whether its basic entities can be enumerated without ontological loss, whether its native vocabulary permits self-referential statements, whether its inference rules are monotonic, and whether consistency for that domain is a mathematical property or an empirical one. Each node should be a question a student can answer yes, no, or unclear without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. The leaves should include named positions where they are well-established — strong analogism, deflationary formalism, category-error eliminativism, and limited second-order analogism — plus one unnamed but coherent combination: the view that the domain is fully formalizable, yet its consistency is verified empirically rather than syntactically, rendering the second theorem technically satisfied but practically inert. Wherever two leaves share an identical answer pattern, flag them as inseparable by this tree and diagnose whether the gap is logical or merely rhetorical. The classification should depend on beliefs about formalizability and self-reference, not on authority or label recognition, so do not include any node that asks whether one accepts a particular named philosopher's argument. No bibliographies, no reading lists, no citations; the exercise should work for someone reasoning from first principles without web access.
