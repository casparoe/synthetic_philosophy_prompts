In my graduate seminar on social epistemology, we have been examining the familiar tension between conciliationism and steadfastness when two epistemic peers discover that they disagree. Rather than treating only the canonical one-off disagreement, I want to direct our attention toward a structurally more complex phenomenon: diachronic peer disagreement with evidential inertia. By this I mean a situation in which two peers have maintained opposing positions over an extended period, each developing extensive, interdependent networks of derivative beliefs and auxiliary commitments that depend on their initial stance. They then encounter a novel, shared piece of evidence that is genuinely ambiguous with respect to both architectures—it does not clearly favor either framework, yet it demands some rational response. The conciliationist prescription of meeting halfway appears to treat the disagreement as isolated from these accumulated evidential structures, while the steadfastist refusal to revise seems to privilege historical coherence over peer equality.

What are the most important implications of this framing for the rationality of belief revision? Specifically, does it reveal that both standard views tacitly depend on an unrealistic assumption about the independence of individual beliefs from their broader evidential histories, and is there a principled way to weigh architectural coherence against peer equality without collapsing into either mere stubbornness or uncritical averaging? I am particularly interested in whether this points to a deeper issue about how rationality norms apply to belief systems as integrated wholes rather than to discrete propositions taken in isolation, and whether recognizing evidential inertia forces us to reformulate what it means for two agents to be genuine epistemic peers in the first place.
