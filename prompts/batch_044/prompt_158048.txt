I retired from an ecology lab about five years ago—mostly soil bacteria, nothing glamorous—and lately I've been sitting on our county restorative-justice board, which is a strange second act but here we are. I've gotten into philophy of emotion and there's this debate about guilt versus shame: guilt hits the act so you can fix it, shame hits the whole self and sticks, and anthropologists talk about guilt-cultures versus shame-cultures like Ruth Benedict mapped out. Some people say a society can actually choose to tilt one way or the other by building different institutions, others say you're stuck with whatever emotional architecture you inherited.

We recieve proposals for accountability rituals—private amends, public apologies, community service with a speech, etc.—and I want a real decision procedure my board can run in twenty minutes, with no PhD required, to decide whether a ritual is routing people toward guilt (repairable, good) or shame (global, bad). Ground it in the actual theory but translate it. I want questions in order, what counts as enough evidence for each, where we stop with a hard no, and where we have to admit it's a values call not a fact.

Build in this niche framing I'm playing with: instead of just labeling the emotion, look at the allocentric repair loop versus the autocentric status-degradation curve—basically, does the ritual create an institutional pathway that sends the offender back into the community through specific repair, or does it lock them into a lowered social status with no exit? Make that part of the procedure.

Then run it on three proposals: (1) a private amends meeting with the victim where the offender lists specific actions to make right; (2) a public ceremony in front of neighbors where the offender confesses and wears a visible marker for thirty days; (3) a hybrid where the offender writes a letter of specific wrongs that gets read aloud anonymously by a mediator—this one should come out with an answer the board will hate, like maybe it technically passes but feels wrong. Be honest about where the procedure misfires, like the fact that it can't read internal feelings, only institutional design.

Also take a definite stand—don't hedge. Is guilt actually the better moral emotion here, and can a culture genuinely choose to become guilt-oriented, or is that as naive as thinking you can choose thier gut microbiome? Defend your answer.

Please, no bibliographies, no see-also lists, no citations—half our board doesn't have library cards and I'm not asking anyone to chase footnotes. Just the procedure, the worked cases, the limits, and your committed verdict.
