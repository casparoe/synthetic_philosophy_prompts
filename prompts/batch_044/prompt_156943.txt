I have been trying to map the precise philosophical stakes of the White Horse dialogue traditionally linked to Gongsun Long and the School of Names, and I am struck by how quickly standard accounts collapse into either a semantic puzzle about compound terms or a metaphysical claim about kind-identity. The familiar version treats the claim that a white horse is not a horse as turning on whether a color-determination and a species-determination can share the same referential range; a second, more metaphysical reading takes it to argue that qualitative modification produces a different universal altogether. Yet neither account seems to do full justice to the dialogue's placement within the Mingjia tradition, which was centrally concerned with zhengming—the normative rectification of names in disputation. This raises a sharper controversy: should the dialogue be reconstructed as an argument in philosophy of language, as an ontological thesis about universals, or as a proto-normative claim about the conditions under which a speaker may legitimately withhold assent to an identity statement within a shared linguistic practice?

Write an essay that adjudicates among these three interpretive frameworks. Your essay should examine the strengths and weaknesses of the semantic, metaphysical, and normative-entitlement readings, paying particular attention to whether the metaphysical interpretation anachronistically imposes categorical distinctions that the School of Names' own anti-essentialist methods reject. Reach a justified conclusion about which framing best explains the argumentative moves in the dialogue, and explain what that conclusion implies for how we understand the relationship between the Mingjia's logical exercises and its broader project of rectifying names.
