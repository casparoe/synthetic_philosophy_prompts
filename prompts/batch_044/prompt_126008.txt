I am working through a philosophical essay on intergenerational justice and have become stuck on pure time preference—the view that future well-being should count for less solely because it is temporally distant, independent of risk, capital productivity, or extinction probabilities. Most treatments I encounter frame this either as a quirk of individual savings behavior or as an external parameter in climate-economics modeling, but I want to understand what happens when pure time preference is not applied as a post-hoc discount rate but is instead embedded in the constitutional architecture of an institution that must persist across centuries.

Consider a hypothetical "Centuries Trust," chartered to maintain watershed ecosystems and governed by a binding agreement that prevents present generations from recalling capital and mandates that substantial payouts occur only after two hundred and fifty years, with stewardship passing to successive generations under fixed rules. If we grant, for the sake of argument, that rational individuals may hold a non-zero pure time preference with respect to their own distant welfare, what are the most important implications for how the Trust's charter should weigh the claims of those distant beneficiaries against the present generation's contribution burdens? I am especially interested in whether institutionalizing that preference is morally equivalent to individuals acting on it privately, whether doing so genuinely answers—or merely relocates—the standard objection that temporal location is a morally arbitrary feature, and what governance mechanisms could make any such discounting explicit in the charter rather than concealed within compound-interest or risk-adjustment assumptions. Please treat both the philosophical objections and the practical institutional-design problems seriously, do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and please respond in American English.
