Here is an argument I sketched after working through Dewey’s claim that democracy should be understood as a mode of cooperative social inquiry rather than as a form of government, and it seems to me to prove far too much.

(1) For Dewey, democracy is not essentially majority rule or a set of institutions, but the organization of social life as continuous cooperative inquiry in which policies are treated as working hypotheses to be tested by their consequences for shared experience.

(2) The normative authority of any democratic arrangement derives entirely from its contribution to this ongoing problem-solving process, not from the prior consent of citizens or from pre-political rights that constrain what may be tested.

(3) Scientific inquiry gains its authority by controlling conditions, isolating variables, and revising hypotheses without letting the prior commitments of the subjects obstruct the experiment; if democratic inquiry is genuinely analogous, then its legitimacy should increase as social conditions approach those of a well-designed experiment.

(4) Liberal democratic institutions—constitutional rights, judicial review, protected minorities—function precisely as non-experimental constraints: they prevent certain policies from being implemented and tested regardless of expected consequences, thereby treating some beliefs (for instance, about inherent dignity) as immune to revision.

(5) An authoritarian technocratic regime that collects comprehensive data, implements policies rapidly, suppresses dogmatic resistance, and treats citizens as variables in a social experiment can therefore approximate the conditions of cooperative inquiry more closely than a liberal democracy, because it removes the very “fixed” obstacles that Dewey criticizes.

(6) So, on Dewey’s own criteria, such a regime realizes democracy more fully than liberal democracy does.

(7) But this is absurd; we should not accept that authoritarian technocracy is the fulfillment of democracy.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), but I want to know exactly what I am paying—whether it is a hidden normative commitment, a misapplication of the scientific analogy, or a failure to distinguish inquiry from the conditions that make inquiry possible.
