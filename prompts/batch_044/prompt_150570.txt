I'm currently in an advanced philosophy seminar for high school students, and after a long debate with a classmate who insisted that every metaphysical disagreement is really just a linguistic mix-up, I want to understand the actual logical space of responses rather than memorize famous names. Could you please design a decision-tree taxonomy for classifying positions on whether ontological disputes are verbal? The instrument should be built around one concrete diagnostic scenario, not around abstract labels that require prior knowledge of the literature.

Use this framing case: two archivists agree on every observable fact in their building—every sheet of paper, every digital file checksum, every loan record, and every practical decision about preservation and access. One says, "There are exactly 10,000 information-bearing entities in this collection," while the other says, "There are 10,000 physical items and nothing else; talk of separate information-entities is only a useful fiction for describing patterns among those items, not an extra ontological commitment." They never change each other's practical behavior, yet neither can persuade the other. The tree should begin by asking whether there is any genuine factual disagreement at all, then branch through whether the divergence is best explained by ambiguous terminology, by different pragmatic rules for quantifier use within a shared language, by one party literally adopting a different conceptual framework that changes what "exists" means, or by one party being in straightforward conceptual error. Each leaf should carry the name of an actual position—such as a robust ontological realism, a Carnap-style framework pluralism, a quantifier-variance deflationism, or a conceptual-error eliminativism—or be marked "unnamed/unoccupied," and every named leaf should include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, the specific cost that makes the view uncomfortable to hold.

Please also include at least one logically possible branch combination that appears to be defended by no one, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect; flag any pair of leaves that the tree's answer-paths cannot cleanly separate and explain why they collapse together; and keep the questions answerable from intuition about the archive case rather than from familiarity with technical metaontology. The audience is my seminar group—bright students who can follow logical branching but do not yet know the literature—so do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific texts; I need only the taxonomy itself.
