I have been trying, without any formal philosophical training, to make sense of Jorge Luis Borges's argument that linear time is an illusion, particularly the essay in which he denies the reality of succession. At the same time, I have been reading Augustine's Confessions, written around AD 400, and I have set out below my own rendering of a passage from Book XI in which he asks what time is. Augustine suggests that the past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist, leaving only the present; yet that present itself has no duration, being only an instantaneous division—like a knife's edge—between what was and what will be. He seems to conclude that we can speak of past and future only through the traces they leave in the present mind: memory for what has gone, expectation for what is to come.

I am struggling to see whether Borges is doing something similar but in reverse. Augustine appears to rescue time by lodging it inside consciousness: the present is brief, but memory and anticipation lend it breadth. Borges, by contrast, seems to deny even this psychological breadth, as though the present does not preserve the past but replaces it altogether. To capture this intuition, I have coined a phrase for my own thinking—the Double Absence—meaning that in Borges the past is absent not simply because it is gone, but because the present actively rewrites the record of what came before, so that no stable archive remains to be remembered. I realise that this may be an imposition, but I should like to know whether it is a fair extension of Augustine's reasoning, or whether I am importing a kind of textual anxiety into Borges that is not genuinely there.

More specifically, I should like help understanding Augustine's claim that if nothing passed away there would be no past, nothing were approaching there would be no future, and nothing existed there would be no present. Does this make time a pure relation between states, with no substance of its own? And if so, when Borges denies time, is he denying the relation itself, or only denying that one of its poles—the enduring present—exists at all?

Please reply in American English, and do correct my rendering of the passage or point out where my notion of the Double Absence misleads me.
