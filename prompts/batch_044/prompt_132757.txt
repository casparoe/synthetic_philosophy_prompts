One interpretive crux that keeps coming up in my reading group is whether Spinoza's claim that democracy is the absolute form of government requires that the multitude actually achieve rational agreement through shared common notions, or whether democratic sovereignty is satisfied whenever the collective conatus is structurally aligned—even if the citizens remain largely governed by inadequate ideas and passion. Some readers treat Spinoza's democracy as genuinely popular and affective: the democratic state derives its right entirely from the aggregated power of its members, and institutional mechanisms like large councils or rotation in office are designed not to filter out irrationality but to prevent any single passion from dominating. Others read the same texts as smuggling in an aristocracy of reason: since true sovereignty requires that the common mind be oriented by adequate ideas, and since adequate ideas are rare, democratic stability actually depends on hidden rational elites or on institutional constraints that limit popular decision-making to domains where reason already prevails.

They should respond directly to each other's points: for instance, if Mara claims that democratic councils manage conflict without requiring shared belief, Elias should ask how those councils avoid becoming either impotent or tyrannical if the members share no adequate ideas; and if Elias claims that reason must guide the democratic state, Mara should press whether that doesn't collapse Spinoza's democracy back into his aristocracy, making the democratic form redundant.
