I've been reconstructing an argument I first encountered in a discussion about Hilbert's programme and formal epistemology, and I find it suspiciously self-undermining. The worry is whether we can rationally continue to use systems such as ZFC or Peano Arithmetic when we lack any internal proof of their consistency. Here is the chain, laid out precisely:

(1) If a formal system S is genuinely inconsistent, then there exists within S a derivation of a contradiction.
(2) Every derivation possesses a definite finite length (or Gödel number), and for any fixed bound B, the collection of derivations with length ≤ B is recursively enumerable.
(3) For practical bounds that mathematicians have actually surveyed, that collection is exhaustively searchable, and within those bounds ZFC yields no contradiction.
(4) Therefore, if ZFC is inconsistent, its contradiction lies beyond our practical survey boundary.
(5) Gödel's second incompleteness theorem establishes that if ZFC is consistent, then ZFC cannot prove Con(ZFC).
(6) Consequently, any rational confidence in ZFC's consistency must be grounded either in a stronger system S', or in non-formal reasoning.
(7) Applying (1) through (4) to S' produces the same dilemma: either S' conceals its contradiction at a greater derivational depth, or we must ascend to S'', and so on.
(8) Hence there is no finitely grounded, non-circular justification for believing any system that embeds arithmetic to be consistent.
(9) Yet mathematical practice treats consistency as a working entitlement rather than an inductive conjecture.
(10) The only coherent response is to suspend belief in all results that depend on the global consistency of the axioms — which is nearly all of mathematics.
(11) But this argument itself employs arithmetic inference and logical rules drawn from the very system whose consistency is in question; if (10) is correct, the argument undermines its own rational licence.

My study partner thinks the conclusion is absurd not merely because it is sceptical, but because it is self-defeating in a way that should direct us to reject one of the premises rather than accept (10). I am most reluctant to give up (5) or (9), and I am somewhat more willing to weaken (3) or (6), but I want to understand the price of each option. Where does the slide occur? Is there an equivocation between the logical existence of a contradiction and our epistemic access to it, or perhaps a scope shift in how we move from a single system to the infinite hierarchy? Please diagnose the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and price the alternatives — particularly whether abandoning it leaves us with a defensible inductivist picture of mathematical belief, or forces us into a kind of quietist faith. Please also say what would change your mind: which specific consideration about derivational surveyability or about the structure of non-circular justification in formal systems would cause you to revise your diagnosis, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to collapse.
