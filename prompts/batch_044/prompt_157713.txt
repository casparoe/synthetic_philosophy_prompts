I'm helping organize the opening session of a joint workshop on forecasting in public life, co-hosted by a university statistics department and a school of public policy. The audience will be split evenly between quantitative researchers, who work with predictive models and understand that every forecast relies on selecting a comparison class, and practitioners—including city planners, community advocates, and family members—who have no philosophical background and should not be lectured at. I need you to write a formal opening address of about five minutes, hard-capped at 650 words, that makes the reference class problem the structural spine of the remarks rather than decorative philosophy. The speaker should begin by noting that every forecast discussed today—whether about climate adaptation funding, epidemic spread, or economic intervention—depends on an unobservable choice: which set of past cases counts as comparable. The address must then argue that this choice is never merely technical; selecting, for instance, whether to model a proposed urban heat-mitigation policy after wealthy northern cities or after rapidly urbanizing tropical ones does not just shift the predicted survival rate, it changes which lives and which trade-offs the number makes visible. Because of this, the speech must refuse any triumphal narrative about prediction as mastery; instead, it should frame the day's task as making those hidden reference-class selections explicit and accountable. Include one concrete, non-technical example—walking through the heat-policy comparison above—to show a non-specialist exactly how the same data can yield radically different obligations depending on the class selected. The tone needs to land for both audiences: precise enough that someone familiar with the problem will recognize it, accessible enough that an advocate or relative can follow without jargon. Do not include citations, named scholars, bibliographies, or reading lists; paraphrase any standard formulation without quotation marks. Present the address in standard paragraphs, lead with the occasion and audience, and conclude with a single tight bottom-line sentence stating the ethical responsibility the room should carry into the workshop.
