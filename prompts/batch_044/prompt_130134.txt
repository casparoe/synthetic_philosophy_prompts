I am working through Al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa in preparation for a seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy, and I find myself returning repeatedly to the well-known discussion of the cotton and the fire. In my reconstruction of the passage, Al-Ghazālī maintains that when cotton is brought near a flame, the blackening and separation of its parts are not produced by the fire as an efficient cause; rather, God creates these effects directly, while the presence of the fire functions only as an occasion (sabab). He observes that the conjunction between fire and burning is constant in our experience, yet it is not a necessary connection in the nature of things—God could, without contradiction, create the blackening in the cotton even if the fire were absent. I understand the theological motivation for denying secondary causation to created things, but I am struggling to see what precise conceptual role remains for the "occasion" if God is immediately producing both the presence of fire and the burning of the cotton in each discrete instant. Is the occasion merely an epistemic marker that allows human observers to navigate regularities, or does it carry some ontological weight within Ashʿarite metaphysics?

My study partner suggested that this framework might collapse into a kind of event-atomism: if every particular effect is recreated ex nihilo at each moment, then sustained causal narratives—like saying the fire caused the burn—seem to be nothing more than habit-based descriptions of temporal contiguity. But I wonder whether Al-Ghazālī's commitment to occasionalism is actually compatible with a more robust account of how human agents predict outcomes, perhaps by treating the occasion not as a causal power but as a stable condition under which divine custom (ʿāda) operates. Could one defend occasionalism without reducing it to radical atomism about events, and if so, does that defense rely on distinguishing the metaphysical status of the occasion from the metaphysical status of a Humean constant conjunction?
