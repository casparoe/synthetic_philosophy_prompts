I am a doctoral student in philosophy of law, and in a recent working-group session on free will and penal theory, two colleagues—Elena, who works on moral psychology, and Marcus, who specializes in metaphysics of agency—got into a sustained disagreement about whether the concept of just deserts can survive hard determinism. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander and do not take a side; I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

Elena opened with the claim that (1) since neuroscience and psychology show human behavior is causally determined by factors outside our control—genetics, environment, neurochemistry—the retributive idea that offenders justly deserve suffering is metaphysically bankrupt, and punishment should be justified purely by forward-looking goals: deterrence, rehabilitation, and incapacitation, without any backward-looking moral accounting. Marcus replied that (2) even if hard determinism is true, a compatibilist can preserve desert by grounding it in the agent's capacities for rational deliberation and self-control; without some desert-based distinction, we cannot explain why intentional wrongdoing is punished more severely than accidental harm of equal magnitude, or why diminished-capacity defenses excuse. Elena countered that (3) Marcus's appropriate-response framework merely smuggles desert in through the back door: if determinism holds, the capacity for rational control is itself the product of unchosen causes, so praising or blaming someone for exercising it is like praising or blaming them for their unchosen causal history. She argued that the quarantine model—confining dangerous individuals based solely on risk and rehabilitative need, with severity calibrated to danger rather than moral culpability—avoids this problem entirely. Marcus then said that (4) the quarantine analogy actually undermines Elena's position: we do not quarantine disease carriers as a moral judgment, yet we still distinguish between someone who deliberately spreads infection and someone who does so accidentally, even when the risk profile is identical. That distinction tracks something like culpability or desert, not mere risk. Furthermore, he argued, if we abandon desert entirely for pure risk-management, we lose any principled barrier to preemptive detention of high-risk individuals before any offense occurs; only a backward-looking constraint—that punishment must respond to actual wrongdoing—blocks that slide.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the four numbered positions, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether a purely forward-looking penal system can genuinely eliminate all backward-looking moral accounting without collapsing into preventive detention or losing the distinction between punishment and treatment—and explain what specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
