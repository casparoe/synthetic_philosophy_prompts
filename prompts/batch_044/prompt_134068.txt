I serve on the clinical research ethics committee of a university neuroscience institute. A proposed study seeks to recruit individuals with documented pain asymbolia—subjects who report clear nociceptive sensations yet show neither emotional distress nor behavioral avoidance—to undergo sustained, non-damaging thermal stimulation coupled with real-time neurofeedback and post-session evaluative interviews. The investigators are not pursuing a therapeutic endpoint; their stated goal is conceptual: to determine whether these subjects still generate a second-order judgment that their sensory state is bad for them in the absence of any affective aversion, thereby testing whether the normative badness of pain can be dissociated entirely from its motivational force. The consent form describes the sensory procedure and emphasizes the absence of suffering, but it does not disclose the study's theoretical commitment to a three-part taxonomy—sensory-discriminative, affective-motivational, and normative-evaluative—nor does it address the possibility that revealing a hidden evaluative dimension could alter participants' self-understanding. Committee members are divided: one faction considers the protocol a minimal-risk, high-value contribution to philosophy of mind and clinical ethics; the other contends that recruiting people precisely because they lack protective affective responses exploits a vulnerability, and that embedding a contested philosophical taxonomy into the study design without explicit acknowledgment in the consent process violates respect for persons. Please draft the committee's formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions, including a concrete implementation time frame; the memo should treat the strongest opposing argument with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it.
