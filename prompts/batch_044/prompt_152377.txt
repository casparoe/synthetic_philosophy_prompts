I am trying to determine whether karma can be reconstructed as a philosophically rigorous theory of moral desert—one that specifies when an agent genuinely deserves a particular consequence, rather than merely describing a cultural belief in cosmic accounting. I have formed intuitive verdicts on the following cases, but they seem to pull in different directions, and I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate them.

Case 1: An agent performs a hidden act of cruelty, dies peacefully with no adverse consequence, and there is no post-mortem mechanism for reckoning. Verdict: The theory of karma as moral desert fails here unless it invokes an unobservable fulfillment condition; but invoking such a condition renders the theory immune to evidence, which I take to be a defect. So I reject the rescue.

Case 2: An agent performs anonymous acts of kindness over a lifetime, and a diffuse social network gradually returns material and emotional support. Verdict: This counts as karma, but only because the moral quality of the acts causally shaped a social environment that eventually delivered fitting consequences. If the same support had arrived through pure coincidence, it would not be desert.

Case 3: Two individuals stand in a relation of psychological continuity—shared memories, intentions, and character traits—across what is conventionally called two lives, but there is no causal or informational link (no shared body, no social interaction, no record). The earlier individual committed grave wrongs; the later individual suffers misfortune that a karma proponent attributes to those wrongs. Verdict: I deny that this is karma. Without causal or informational continuity, attributing desert across the gap is arbitrary; psychological continuity alone is insufficient for desert transmission.

Case 4: A society deploys a predictive algorithm that assigns penalties based on behavioral proxies for moral risk. The penalties cause suffering that correlates with prior harmful acts, but also with false positives and with structural biases. Verdict: This is pseudo-karma. It reproduces the correlation between action and suffering without grounding the suffering in actual moral desert.

Case 5: Agent A acts with good intentions but, through unforeseeable causal chains, produces catastrophic harm. Agent B acts with malicious intent but, through pure luck, produces beneficial outcomes. Verdict: A pure desert theory should track the moral quality of the will, not the outcome. So A retains some desert of praise despite the harm, and B retains desert of blame despite the benefit. Any outcome-sensitive formulation of karma therefore deviates from moral desert into consequentialist accounting.

Case 6: A child suffers severe congenital illness. A karma advocate explains this as punishment for wrongs committed in a prior life. Verdict: This is an unacceptable rescue: it violates the principle that moral desert must be linked to an agent's own actions in a way that is, in principle, accessible to rational assessment, not postulated solely to preserve the theory.

My questions are as follows. First, is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving causal accessibility, will-sensitivity, and continuity conditions—that captures Cases 2 through 5 while accommodating my rejection of the rescues in Cases 1, 3, and 6? Second, if such a principle exists, does it force me to revise any of my verdicts? Third, what does the principle predict for a case not listed here: an agent performs a minor wrong, is immediately and transparently punished by a legitimate authority, and then experiences a profound psychological transformation that severs her identification with the pre-punishment self? Does the transformed agent still bear the desert of further consequences, or does the transformation function as a kind of moral bankruptcy?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to the unlisted case.
