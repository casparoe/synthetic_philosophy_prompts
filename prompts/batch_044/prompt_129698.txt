I was in my philosophy of mind and rationality seminar last week and we got sidetracked talking about decision paralysis, and my roommate brought up Buridan's ass — the donkey that starves between two identical hay bales because it can't pick one. She summed up the whole thing for me like this: 'Buridan invented the paradox to prove pure rationality is self-defeating, since if you always wait for the best reason you'll never act. He actually wrote the donkey example into his main logic text, and his point was that real agents need a random tie-breaker or they just freeze. That's exactly why modern behavorial economists cite it for choice overload — people face too many equal options and shut down. So Buridan basically invented satisficing, where you just pick anything that's good enough instead of maximizing.' I feel like she's retrofitting Herbert Simon onto a medieval logic problem and probably getting Buridan's actual position wrong, but I don't know enough history of philosophy to call out the mistakes. Is this summary fair? Which parts would a historian accept, which are misattributions or overstatements, and does her jump from the donkey to modern choice overload and satisficing actually follow from anything Buridan said? Also, was he using this to defend determinism, or was he actually arguing for some kind of freedom in the will?
