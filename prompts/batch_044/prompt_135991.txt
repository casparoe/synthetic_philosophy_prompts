For an independent study in political philosophy, I want to analyze the U.S. escalation in Vietnam between 1965 and 1968 through the dollar-auction model of escalation of commitment. Treat the Johnson administration and the North Vietnamese leadership as the two bidders, and specify what counts as a bid—whether troop deployments, bombing tonnage, or credibility with allies—along with the strategic prize each side pursued. Use the model’s sunk-cost dynamics to explain the pattern of incremental commitment despite worsening strategic returns, then apply it specifically to President Johnson’s March 1968 decision to halt Operation Rolling Thunder and decline reelection: does this represent a rational recognition that the auction had exceeded the prize’s value, or does the formal framework miss the way domestic anti-war sentiment altered the payoff structure? Also examine whether the negotiations that eventually convened in Paris should be understood as an attempt to dissolve the auction without either side formally conceding defeat. Finally, evaluate whether Vietnam ultimately confirms the dollar auction’s claim that rational actors can overbid past value, or whether the case shows that real-world escalation is disrupted not by internal rationality but by external rule changes—such as shifting audience costs and clandestine diplomacy—that the model does not incorporate.
