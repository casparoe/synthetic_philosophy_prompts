A municipality maintains two official repositories: an Archive of Record, holding declassified documents, meeting minutes, and forensic reports; and a House of Remembrance, holding curated oral histories, commemorative rituals, and pedagogical narratives. By charter, when the two conflict — for instance, if the Archive shows that a celebrated community rescue was in fact carried out by outsiders, while the Remembrance teaches it as an act of local solidarity — the House takes precedence for school curricula and public monuments, but the Archive governs legal liability and property restitution. What view of collective identity, evidentiary authority, and historical truth does this bifurcated system presuppose? Are its parts philosophically consistent, or do they quietly import incompatible theories about what makes a claim about the past binding? Distinguish what the charter strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and say whether the practice is underdetermined — that is, whether several rival philosophies of memory and history could license the same split. Finally, if the conflict cannot be managed by division of labor and one repository must yield, which should give way, and on what grounds?
