hey so i'm in this comparative philosophy seminar and we're talking about Jain anekāntavāda and i feel like i've landed somewhere but i'm not sure it holds together. my basic take is: (1) every object has infinite properties but any given knower only grasps a slice depending on their standpoint; (2) syādvāda means every claim should technically carry a conditional tag—like saying a pot is black in some respect and non-black in others; (3) there's no god's-eye view that stitches all these slices into one final story; (4) still, some standpoints are just better, especially ones that let you predict what happens next, so science isn't just one view among equals; and (5) when two people genuinely contradict each other from different standpoints neither is flat-out wrong, but in practice we should still go with the predictive one when there's a decision to make.

do these actually hang together? it feels like (4) sneaks in a kind of absolutism that (3) rules out—if there's no master narrative, on what ground do i rank science higher except from yet another partial view? and (5) sounds like i'm saying you're both right but i'm right-er, which feels like closet realism about truth. which tension is the worst, and what's the cheapest revision—dropping (4), weakening (3), or reinterpreting (5) as purely pragmatic? also, there's maybe an apparent clash between (1) and (2): if objects have infinite properties, does syādvāda apply to properties themselves or only to how we predicate them? is that a real problem or does it dissolve once you separate ontology from logic?
