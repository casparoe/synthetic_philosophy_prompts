Imagine that Max Weber, having survived into the present, was asked to evaluate a contemporary cultural formation that has no direct historical precedent in his era: the widespread adoption among high-skill knowledge workers of an intense, methodical self-discipline—quantified sleep, nutritional optimization, algorithmic time-blocking, and social minimization—undertaken not for salvation, communal obligation, or aesthetic fulfillment, but strictly for the maximization of cognitive labor output within winner-take-all professional markets. Practitioners often describe this regime in moralized language, speaking of discipline, calling, and betrayal of one's potential, despite the absence of any transcendent theological frame and despite the fact that the rewards are purely secular: status, income, and competitive survival.

Drawing on Weber's analysis of the relationship between ascetic Protestant value-orientations and the emergence of rational capitalism—particularly his distinctions between elective affinity and direct causal efficacy, between value-rational and instrumentally rational action, and between cultural ideas and material interests—predict how he would assess this phenomenon. Specifically, address the following.

First, would Weber treat this secular optimization ethos as an independent cultural cause with genuine elective affinity to post-industrial knowledge capitalism, analogous to the Calvinist ethic's affinity with early modern rational capitalism? Or would he argue that, once stripped of other-worldly salvation anxiety and institutionalized religious community, the apparent cultural factor collapses into a purely adaptive response to structural economic incentives—credential inflation, platform surveillance, and labor-market precarity—thereby forfeiting explanatory autonomy?

Second, how would he apply his typology of social action to practitioners who sincerely experience their self-optimization as a value-rational vocation rather than mere strategic calculation? Would the presence of subjective moral commitment be sufficient, in his framework, to establish that values are causally efficacious, or would he insist that such subjectivity can be fully accounted for by the internalization of market rationality?

Third, given his well-known methodological skepticism toward monocausal explanations and his insistence on multi-causal historical analysis, what comparative or institutional evidence would he demand before accepting that this culture explains productivity differentials rather than merely correlating with them?

Construct your answer as a reconstructed position, not as a historical citation exercise: do not provide bibliographies, page references, or lists of secondary literature. Be explicit about where Weber's own conceptual apparatus forces an ambiguous answer, and identify the further empirical distinction that would resolve the ambiguity in his terms.
