I sit on the advisory board for the International Archive of Catastrophic Events, and we're revising our taxonomy for mass-mortality events in the 20th century. There's a motion to split every entry into either "natural disaster" or "political crime / structural failure," and the board has to vote Thursday on whether to apply this binary to three specific cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Great Irish Famine in the 1840s, and the mortality crisis during China's Great Leap Forward (1958-62).

Half the board wants to classify all three as political crimes, citing Sen's entitlement-collapse argument and recent historiography on colonial extraction and state procurement; the other half says this collapses genuinely different causal mechanisms — market entitlement failure versus direct extraction versus collective-farm reporting breakdown — and violates our charter's rule that we must preserve multi-factor labeling when climate or ecological stress was present. I need you to draft the decision memo I'll circulate: it should reccomend either adopting the binary, rejecting it, or approving a modified hybrid scheme with clear criteria, and it should treat the "all three are crimes" position seriously rather than dismissing it.

The memo needs a concrete bottom line the board can vote on, plus an honest account of the strongest reason to reject that bottom line. In particular, please address whether Sen's framework applies symetrically across the three: in Bengal you had wartime price shocks and administrative denial, in Ireland you had continued food exports under colonial administration, and in China you had a non-market procurement system where local officials falsified harvest figures. Don't just restate the charter — actually engage with whether the differences in state mechanism matter for moral/political classification, and make sure the criteria you propose could be applied by staff who aren't specialists in famine historiography.
