Next month I am presenting my paper “Concern Without Attachment: Re-reading the Dichotomy of Control in Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius” at an interdisciplinary workshop on Stoicism and mental health. My thesis is that the modern clinical revival—especially the CBT-adapted Stoicism popularized by Irvine and Pigliucci—misreads the boundary between ta eph’ hēmin and ta ouk eph’ hēmin as an epistemic filter for emotional regulation: if an outcome is not in your control, the patient is taught to extinguish care for it. I argue this is a therapeutic anachronism. In the Enchiridion and the Meditations, the partition is ontological, marking what belongs to prohairesis versus external causation, but it does not license “radical acceptance” in the modern clinical sense. Rather, Marcus’s discussion of social roles and Epictetus’s remarks on familial duty point to a model of directed concern—epimeleia structured by virtue—that permits robust investment in the health of dependents, political justice, or professional outcomes without treating those outcomes as necessary for eudaimonia. I apply this to criticize modern Stoic-informed therapy’s tendency to advise patients to “stop caring” about job performance or relationship conflicts altogether.

The audience is about fifteen people: four clinical psychologists who run Stoic-informed CBT groups and care chiefly about therapeutic efficacy; three philosophers of emotion who treat ancient texts as decorative framing for cognitive-behavioral mechanisms; two historians of Hellenistic philosophy who read Greek and will test textual fidelity; and graduate students in philosophy of psychiatry.
