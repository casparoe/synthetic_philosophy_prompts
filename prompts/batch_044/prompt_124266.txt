At a recent interdisciplinary workshop on formal methods in applied ethics, two colleagues—Jordan, who builds axiomatic models of distributive justice, and Maya, who works on practical reasoning and interpretation—fell into a long disagreement about whether Gödel’s first and second incompleteness theorems impose meaningful limits on formalizing moral decision procedures. Jordan began by observing that any normative system rich enough to encode basic arithmetic—say, a quantitative healthcare-rationing calculus that uses quality-adjusted life-years and ordinal rankings—must, if it is consistent, contain propositions that are true but not derivable within the system itself. From this he concluded that there will necessarily be ethically correct allocation rules that the formal framework cannot reach, which means the aspiration to a complete, self-contained ethical algorithm is theoretically dead. Maya replied that this is a category mistake: Gödel’s result applies specifically to syntactic derivability within a fixed, recursively axiomatized formal language with well-formed formulas and inference rules, whereas moral reasoning is open-ended, revisable, and not confined to any single axiomatization; applying the theorem to clinical ethics is like applying it to an ordinary conversation. Jordan pushed back that the relevant target is not conversation but any computable decision procedure with fixed rules; if the goal is an automated system that never requires external judgment, then Gödel guarantees unavoidable blind spots, and the undecidable sentences are not mere syntactic curiosities but represent genuine normative commitments the system is unable to endorse. Maya conceded that full axiomatic closure is impossible, but argued that this damages nothing, since no serious ethicist aims for closure in that sense; moreover, the Gödel sentence for such a system is constructed via a highly specific self-referential trick and is therefore likely to be a pathological artifact—analogous to “this statement is unprovable” translated into rationing vocabulary—rather than a realistic clinical dilemma, so it does not show that formal tools miss real moral answers, only that they cannot derive every syntactic glitch. Jordan then extended his argument to AI alignment, claiming that Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem shows any sufficiently powerful ethical framework embedded in an artificial system cannot demonstrate its own consistency without appealing to principles outside itself, which undermines projects that seek to encode ethical constraints as fully verifiable, computable rules. Maya countered that alignment research almost never assumes either provable consistency or axiomatic completeness; it relies on heuristic approximation, empirical feedback, and sustained human oversight, so Gödel only refutes a strawman version of the project that no practitioner actually holds. She added that Jordan was implicitly assuming that “outside judgment” is available and reliable, when in fact human moral intuition is notoriously inconsistent and biased, meaning the formal system’s inability to close itself might be a feature that forces explicit acknowledgment of limits rather than a defect. Jordan closed by insisting that even if alignment uses heuristics, the theoretical ceiling still matters: knowing that completeness is impossible should fundamentally change how we evaluate marketing claims about “fully specified” ethical architectures, because it proves there is no principled point at which one can say the formal work is finished.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: evaluate Jordan’s opening claim about arithmetic-rich rationing systems, Maya’s category-mistake objection, Jordan’s reply about computable procedures and normative blind spots, Maya’s concession paired with her pathological-case argument, Jordan’s move invoking the second theorem against alignment projects, Maya’s strawman reply together with her counter about human intuition, and Jordan’s final point about theoretical ceilings. For each step, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly right but overstated, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps something about whether the specific self-referential construction required for Gödel’s sentence can even be formulated in normative vocabularies lacking a precise arithmetization of syntax, or about the distinction between semantic completeness and syntactic derivability when the “system” in question is not actually a single formal language but a family of revisable models.
