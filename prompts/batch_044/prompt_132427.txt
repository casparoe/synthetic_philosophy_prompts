I have noticed a recurring argumentative move in discussions that invoke relational personhood—something in the neighbourhood of Ubuntu, but also in secular social-contract versions of dignity. A speaker begins by claiming that human moral standing is not intrinsic but is constituted through being recognised by others in a living community. So far, this is the standard relational view. Then, when confronted with someone who is genuinely isolated—say, a long-term prisoner held in solitary confinement, or a person with severe cognitive differences whom no local community has made efforts to include—the speaker does not treat the isolation as a failure of the community’s recognition. Instead, they treat the isolation as evidence that the person was never fully within the web of relations to begin with, or that their lack of recognised standing reveals a pre-existing deficit in their relational capacity. The community’s failure to reach out is reinterpreted as the person’s failure to be reachable.

This is not simple victim-blaming, because the speaker genuinely believes dignity is externally constituted; it is also not pure essentialism, because they deny there is an inner core that survives exclusion. It seems like a specific corruption of the relational view: using the fact of non-recognition to retroactively justify the absence of recognition, while preserving the claim that recognition is what matters. I have seen this pattern in debates about the rights of the severely disabled, about statelessness, and about social death after prolonged imprisonment.
