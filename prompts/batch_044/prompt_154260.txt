I'm in this philosophy of cognition course and my study partner keeps running a Lucas-Penrose argument that I think collapses on itself, but I can't figure out wich step is the leak. He starts like this. (1) Any consistent formal system F with enough arithmetic has a Gödel sentence G_F that F can't prove, even though we can see it's true—so if a mind were just F, that mind wouldn't be able to see G_F, but we can, therefore we're not mechanistic. (2) He then extends this: a research collective running shared algorithms, peer review, and distributed databases is just a larger formal system C with it's own Gödel sentence G_C. (3) So anti-mechanism predicts there should be mathematical truths that every individual in the group can grasp, but that the group collectively is blind to—truths only visible from outside the system. (4) But in practice big collaborations like Polymath-style proofs show the opposite: groups solve problems no single member could crack, and there isn't some hidden class of theorems that outsiders consistently spot while the collective misses them. The pattern is collective power, not collective Gödel-blindness. (5) So either groups aren't formal systems in the relevant sense—which saves anti-mechanism for individuals but breaks the analogy used to defend human uniqueness—or the argument proves too much and applies to committees, institutions, maybe even societies, which is absurd. (6) He tries to escape by saying groups are "just people talking" and not a real system, but that feels like special pleading. Which step actually fails—(2), (3), or the inference from (4) to (5)—and if I have to reject one, what does it cost the anti-mechanist position? I'd rather keep the core intuition that human reasoning outruns formalization, but I want to know what I'm paying to hold onto it.
