I'm trying to sort out my gut feelings about death-prophecies that end up causing the death they predict, using cases that look alike but feel different. Classic Damascus story: a servant is told he'll meet Death in Damascus tomorrow, bolts for Samarra, and dies there that evening. I think he didn't escape anything; running away was part of the causal chain that delivered him, so the prophecy wasn't false, but it wasn't mystically unavoidable either. Then a doctor tells a heavy smoker he'll die of cancer in Damascus in five years if he doesn't quit. He quits, moves away, lives to ninety. Here I think the prophecy was genuinely avoidable; treating it like fate would be a mistake. A fortune-teller tells a woman she'll die in Damascus on her 40th birthday. She cancels her trip, stays home, and dies in a kitchen fire that same day. I'm wobbly on this one — part of me says she avoided the place but not the date, so maybe the prophecy was about timing, but part of me thinks it's just coincidence and she actually did avoid it, which makes the prophecy wrong. Next, a pilot hears an AI risk-model predicts he'll crash over Damascus airspace next week; he refuses the flight, lives, and the plane lands fine with a replacement. Clearly avoidable, just a warning. An old king is told his death is in Damascus; he never goes near it, dies in Samarra at eighty. Clearly avoidable, prophecy was just false. The one I keep flipping on: a guy is told "Death waits for you in Damascus tomorrow." Instead of running, he deliberately travels there to face it. He dies of a heart attack on the train, before he even arrives. Is this like the classic case — he caused his own death by going — or did he actually avoid Damascus and die elsewhere, meaning he outsmarted the prophecy but died randomly? Is there a principle that captures all of these, maybe about whether the belief in the prophecy is causaly neccessary for the death, or about whether the death-mechanism is independant of the prophecy?
