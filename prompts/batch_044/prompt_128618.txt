I've been working through Moritz Schlick's argument that life acquires meaning not through the attainment of external goals—since every reached goal merely becomes the instrument for another—but through activities pursued for their own sake, what he frames as "play" in contrast to instrumental "work." I'm testing this distinction against my own judgments about particular lives and moments, partly for a philosophy of life course I'm taking, and I'm finding that my intuitions cluster in ways that don't map neatly onto the play/work binary. Here are my verdicts, given as gut reactions rather than argued positions. A parent waking repeatedly through the night to soothe an infant: profoundly meaningful, despite being exhausting, instrumental to the child's welfare, and entirely devoid of playful pleasure. The same parent, years later, playing peek-a-boo with that child purely for shared laughter: also meaningful, and clearly Schlickian play. A software engineer writing elegant code on a weekend for an open-source project she cares about, lost in the logic: meaningful. The same engineer writing nearly identical code under a sprint deadline solely to avoid being fired: not meaningful. A soldier in active combat who reports feeling "completely alive" through intense tactical absorption, even though the context is lethal and purpose-driven: meaningful in some experiential sense, but I hesitate to call it meaningful in Schlick's sense. A person with a severe gambling addiction spending hours at a slot machine, fully absorbed, forgetting all external concerns and pursuing no further end: not meaningful, despite being present-focused and non-instrumental. A poet composing dense formal verse that will be destroyed unread, not for emotional catharsis but purely to solve a self-imposed linguistic constraint: meaningful. Finally, a philosopher writing a complex argument solely to secure tenure, yet finding herself, in the middle of a proof, completely lost in the reasoning itself: meaningful during that absorption, despite the careerist frame.

What principle, if any, captures this pattern? I can see a modified Schlickian account that requires not just intrinsic motivation or present-focus, but also active skill-expression within a non-exploitative or relational frame—yet that feels ad hoc, and it struggles with the soldier and the addict, who both exercise skill and presence but receive opposite verdicts. Is there a simpler principle that saves most of these judgments only by overturning one or two—perhaps treating the infant-care case as an outlier driven by moral sentiment rather than meaning-proper, or treating the addict's absorption as degenerate play? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: a person who ingests a compound that produces sustained, contented present-moment awareness without any accompanying activity, relationship, or skill-exercise whatsoever—does this count as meaningful on your principle, and what does that result reveal about whether the principle is tracking Schlick's original idea or something else entirely?
