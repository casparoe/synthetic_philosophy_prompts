As a journalist who writes on philosophy and public policy, I am assembling material for a feature on how students are taught to evaluate authoritarian legitimacy. Exam question: Defenders of authoritarian rule in Singapore and China frequently invoke performance legitimacy—the idea that sustained economic growth, public order, and administrative competence can justify the absence of democratic input. Drawing on the distinction between output legitimacy (the state’s delivery of welfare and stability) and procedural dignity (the claim that legitimate authority requires citizens to be authors, not merely beneficiaries, of collective decisions), assess whether prosperity and order alone can ground legitimate authority. In particular, evaluate whether Singapore’s model of meritocratic developmentalism avoids the charge that citizens are treated as instruments of state policy rather than as participants in shaping it.

Student answer: Performance legitimacy holds that a government is legitimate if it delivers results, regardless of how leaders are selected. In Singapore, the People’s Action Party has maintained high growth and low corruption through technocratic governance, which suggests that citizens do not need electoral mechanisms to have their interests represented, since expert administration achieves those interests more efficiently. China’s rapid industrialization under authoritarian rule shows that procedural dignity is essentially a luxury concern: once basic needs are met, societies can afford democratic deliberation, but until then, output legitimacy is sufficient.

The instrumentalization objection—that citizens are used as means to state ends—fails in Singapore’s case because the government provides extensive public housing, education, and healthcare. When citizens receive these benefits, they are not being treated as mere tools but as valued members of the political community whose welfare is central. Furthermore, Singapore’s consultative mechanisms allow feedback into policy, which means citizens function as indirect co-authors of development strategy. By contrast, China’s model is more vulnerable to the objection because its growth has relied on suppressing labor organization, but even there, rising incomes suggest that the state respects citizens’ interests, if not their voices.

A prominent liberal theorist argued that procedural fairness is only required in societies that have already reached a high level of material development; before that threshold, authoritarian efficiency is morally permissible. Therefore, Singapore demonstrates that performance legitimacy can be fully sufficient when combined with meritocratic selection and responsive bureaucracy, while China’s case shows the limits of this argument when procedural feedback is weak.
