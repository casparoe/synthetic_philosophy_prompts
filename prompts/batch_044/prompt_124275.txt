You are a senior philosopher specializing in existential philosophy, philosophy of life, and narrative-identity theory. Before constructing the prep sheet, take a moment to think carefully about interdisciplinary colloquium dynamics: how a Camus scholar, empirical moral psychologists, an objective-list defender, and graduate students will each hear the same thesis differently, and which objections will land with real force versus which are performative.

You are preparing the speaker for a 45-minute invited seminar defending localist indexical absurdism. The thesis is this: absurdity should be treated not as a global, monolithic existential condition but as a modular, project-specific failure of coherence between an agent's first-order commitments and their second-order self-interpretive framework. Meaning is reconstructed not through objective-list conditions of worth and not through global rebellion or acceptance, but through maintaining a reflexive equilibrium that treats breakdowns as localized rather than totalizing. This allows "fragmented but stable" meaning structures—projects that cohere indexically with an agent's interpretive practice even in the absence of cosmic purpose.

The audience is precisely this:
- One specialist in Camus and existentialism who holds that domesticating absurdity into a modular property betrays its phenomenological and affective core.
- Two moral psychologists working on narrative identity, familiar with life-story models, who will worry that "self-interpretive framework" is either empirically empty or a rebranding of narrative coherence without data.
- One philosopher of religion defending a natural-law-style objective account, who will argue that local coherence without global teleology is psychological coping, not genuine meaning.
- Three philosophy graduate students well-versed in Wolf's hybrid account (subjective attraction plus objective worth conditions) who will press on fittingness, arbitrariness, and hidden conditions.

Draft the speaker's Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by combined likelihood and damage, phrased in the vocabulary each interlocutor would actually use. Then identify the single dreaded question the speaker is hoping nobody asks, with a brief survival strategy. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke ultra-niche theories unknown outside a small specialist circle; develop the speaker's "indexical localist" framing as needed, but keep all other references to widely known positions. Avoid verbatim quotations from real texts; paraphrase without quotation marks.
