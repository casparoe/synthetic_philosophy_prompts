The exam question asked: Amartya Sen argues that famines are political failures rather than natural disasters, stressing entitlement collapse over aggregate scarcity. Compare his treatment of the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward (1959-1961), and assess whether the Chinese case—marked by state procurement quotas and systematically inflated local harvest reports—demands an extension of Sen's direct and exchange entitlement framework to accommodate an epistemic or informational dimension. The student wrote: Sen's entitlement framework distinguishes direct entitlement, derived from one's own production, from exchange entitlement, acquired through trade or labor markets, and contends that famines arise when these command structures collapse rather than when food is absolutely scarce. In Bengal in 1943, Sen demonstrates that the direct entitlements of landless agricultural laborers and fishermen collapsed because wartime inflation and the breakdown of rural-urban terms of trade made wages insufficient to purchase rice, even though stocks were available in Calcutta markets; the British administration's failure to impose price controls or authorize relief imports thus represents a political failure to protect exchange mechanisms. For Ireland in the 1840s, the potato blight destroyed the direct entitlements of cottiers who relied on subsistence plots, but grain continued to be exported under market incentives protected by British property law, showing how legal regimes can perpetuate deprivation despite aggregate abundance. Turning to the Great Leap Forward, Sen treats the crisis as an entitlement collapse driven by state procurement quotas that stripped rural households of grain, though he also notes that agricultural disorganization reduced aggregate availability. Some historians emphasize that local cadres had incentives to inflate harvest reports, creating an informational asymmetry that prevented central planners from correcting extraction policies; Sen responds to this by arguing that democratic institutions and a free press prevent such opacity. From this he infers that the Chinese case requires extending his framework to include epistemic entitlement collapse, where the state's inability to know rural conditions functions as a separate causal category alongside direct and exchange failure. Therefore, while all three cases illustrate that famines are not natural disasters, they differ in mechanism: direct entitlement destruction in Ireland, exchange collapse in Bengal, and informational market failure in China.
