Two colleagues in a philosophy-of-physics reading group, Lena and Marcus, have been arguing about the philosophical import of Gödel's rotating-universe model. I was present but did not take sides; I want an outside adjudication of the arguments as they actually unfolded.

Lena began by claiming that Gödel's central achievement was metaphysical rather than cosmological: by exhibiting a solution to Einstein's field equations in which closed timelike curves exist, he showed that general relativity is formally compatible with the absence of any global, linear ordering of events. She argued that this undermines every theory of time requiring an objective "before and after"—not only presentism, but also the B-theory's reliance on a globally consistent temporal orientation. If physics permits a universe in which an observer can travel into her own past without leaving her local neighborhood, she said, then time is not a feature of reality but a feature of local perspective.

Marcus replied that Lena was overreading Gödel's intent. He granted that Gödel was troubled by the philosophical implications, but insisted the argument only succeeds if one assumes that the formal structure of spacetime must mirror phenomenological time directly. A B-theorist, Marcus said, can accept the rotating model as describing a four-dimensional manifold with unusual topology: the manifold exists tenselessly, and the closed curves are geometric features, analogous to loops on a cylinder. The B-theory never promised a global linear ordering in every possible metric; it promised that all events are equally real and related by temporal relations, which remain well-defined locally even inside the rotating region.

Lena then introduced a more specific scenario: imagine not Gödel's globally rotating cosmos, but a bounded local rotational patch—a region with Gödel-like rotation embedded in an otherwise asymptotically flat, non-rotating universe. Within the patch, an observer's worldline could form a closed timelike curve and return to an earlier coordinate value, while outside the patch the global ordering is linear. Lena said that even this localized version preserves Gödel's point, because inside the patch the observer's subjective sequence remains linear—she remembers continuous flow—while the coordinate assignment becomes circular. That divergence, she argued, shows that "time" splits into an experienced parameter and a physical coordinate; since they systematically come apart, neither can claim objective priority, and time is therefore not fundamental.

Marcus countered that this localized version actually rescues the B-theory. Outside the patch, he noted, there is an unproblematic global temporal ordering; inside, the observer's own proper time supplies a consistent local parameter along her worldline. The circularity in coordinate time is merely a coordinate artifact—similar to how longitude lines converge at the poles—not an ontological failure. If anything, he added, the fact that subjective experience tracks proper time even inside the loop demonstrates a robust temporal structure that survives the weird topology, which is precisely what the B-theorist requires.

Lena responded that treating the circularity as a coordinate artifact begs the question. Gödel's argument, she said, depended on the expectation that in a complete physical theory, coordinate time and experienced time ought to coincide; if they can systematically diverge within a physically permissible region, the divergence reveals that our concept of temporal order is imposed rather than discovered. She also observed that Marcus was implicitly privileging proper time, but in a rotating metric the distinction between "inside" and "outside" the loop is frame-dependent, so there is no non-circular way to privilege one parameterization over another.

Marcus ended by conceding that the local-patch scenario raises serious difficulties for defining a global "now" slice that intersects the region without inconsistently cutting the loop, but he maintained that this is a problem for presentists alone. For the B-theorist, he said, the only requirement is that the four-dimensional structure exist with its internal relations intact; the absence of a globally consistent foliation is not a failure but a feature of general covariance.

Please go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate each specific claim. Say who makes the stronger point at each step, identify where both are missing something, and address in particular: whether Lena is correct that the local-patch version undermines both A and B theories; whether Marcus is right that proper time rescues the B-theory; and whether either adequately addresses the frame-dependence of the inside/outside distinction that Lena raises in her final reply. Do not simply declare an overall winner; give a point-by-point verdict on the arguments as actually made.
