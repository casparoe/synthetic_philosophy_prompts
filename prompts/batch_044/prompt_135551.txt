As part of my doctoral work at the intersection of philosophy of mind and theoretical neuroscience, I am examining how architectural analogies shape arguments in computational cognition. In both popular and technical literature, the brain is routinely compared to a von Neumann machine: a central processing unit executes stored instructions retrieved from addressable memory, with data and programs travelling along a shared bus, so that cognition is interpreted as the deterministic execution of algorithms on neural hardware. The analogy is often used to license a strong conclusion — that mental processes are essentially computations of this kind and that the mind–brain relation mirrors the software–hardware distinction familiar from digital engineering. Yet von Neumann himself, particularly in his posthumously published Yale lectures (The Computer and the Brain, 1958), stressed that neural tissue is composed of highly unreliable individual elements, achieves reliability through massive parallel redundancy and statistical averaging rather than through error-correcting codes, operates with far lower energy expenditure and far greater parallelism than any serial architecture of his day, and may depend on a form of probabilistic or statistical logic rather than on the precise Boolean logic that governs digital circuits.

Please audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping functional features of the brain (e.g. neuronal signalling, synaptic plasticity, cortical columns, sensory input pathways) onto components of the von Neumann architecture (processor, memory registers, instruction set, address bus, program counter, etc.). Second, identify where the mapping breaks down, paying particular attention to fault tolerance, temporal dynamics, energy efficiency, the absence of a clean program–data separation in neural tissue, and the role of stochastic noise as constitutive rather than pathological. Third, discuss which features of the computer model are quietly imported by those who use the analogy to support classical computationalism: determinism, serial instruction cycles, the syntax–substrate distinction, and the idea that correct functioning requires the suppression of component-level error. Fourth, evaluate whether von Neumann’s own reservations — especially his observation that the brain’s reliability emerges from redundant statistical organisation rather than from deterministic error correction, and his speculation that neural logic is non-Boolean — undermine the analogy’s capacity to support the conclusion that cognition is von Neumann-style computation. Does the analogy survive only in a severely weakened form (for instance, as a loose functional comparison with no architectural commitment), or does the computationalist conclusion depend on smuggling in precisely those serial, deterministic features that von Neumann thought the brain lacked? Finally, suggest whether an alternative architectural analogy — perhaps one drawn from distributed, fault-tolerant, or neuromorphic computing — could preserve the explanatory ambitions of the brain–computer comparison without importing the misleading commitments of the von Neumann model.
