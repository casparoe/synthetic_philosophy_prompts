I have just come from a long and rather heated argument with a close friend—she works in cognitive neuroscience and insists that the entire Malcolm-Dennett debate about whether dreams are experiences is conceptually obsolete—about a position I intend to defend at next week's interdisciplinary philosophy of mind seminar. My thesis is that Malcolm and Dennett share an unexamined presupposition: both treat the presence or absence of an experience as a binary determined either by introspective accessibility during the state or by retrospective narrative coherence upon waking. I propose that we should reject this binary in favor of what I call attenuated experiential modalities—dreams possess genuine phenomenological content and temporal structure, yet they systematically lack the integrated self-model that grounds first-person epistemic authority in waking consciousness. Consequently, dream reports are neither direct observations of an inner theater (as a verificationist opponent might suppose) nor purely confabulated constructions ex nihilo (as Dennett's stronger reading suggests), but reconstructions of partially retained phenomenological traces whose reliability is structurally undermined by the absence of self-model integration during sleep.

The audience is precisely composed as follows: two senior philosophers of mind who are committed Dennettians with extensive publication on confabulation and memory; a graduate student currently writing a dissertation on Malcolm's verificationist arguments against dream experience; a visiting scholar in predictive processing and embodied cognition who is skeptical of any appeal to a self-model as an explanatory posit; and several philosophers of consciousness who are agnostic on the metaphysics but deeply concerned with preserving first-person authority. Given these commitments, I need a strategic prep sheet rather than a general defense: please list the objections I should expect, in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as each specific interlocutor would phrase them, with a brief reply strategy for each, a clear indication of what I should concede versus hold firm, and explicit identification of the weakest point in my position—the claim that attenuation is a distinct metaphysical kind rather than merely a functional description—that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. I am particularly concerned about the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks: that my framework merely relabels the predictive-processing account of sleep-state simulation without adding any explanatory value. Please address that dread-objection explicitly, together with the others, and advise me on how much time to budget for each reply given a standard fifteen-minute Q&A.
