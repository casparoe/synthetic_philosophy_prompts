I've been running my grad seminar on early modern metaphyics and there's this pattern in student papers that's been bugging me. You know Berkeley's master argument — try to conceive of a tree existing unperceived, but in conceiving it you've already perceived it mentally, so you haven't really escaped the mind. Students get that part. But then I keep seeing this mirror-move that doesn't quite have a name, or at least not one my students know. The realist opponent tries to defend direct realism by asking you to imagine a pure mental act with absolutely no object — just conceiving with nothing conceived. Then they claim that as soon as you try to do it, you've turned that pure act into an object of your own reflection, so you've smuggled an object right back in. It's the same act-versus-content structure that Berkeley uses, but pulled in the opposite direction to defend realism instead of idealism. I don't think this is just tu quoque, and it's not plain equivocation, because both sides are being consistent about the distiction between the mental act and what's given to it.

So here's what I want: is there an established concept for this symmetrical bounce, where the master argument's own machinery gets turned back on the idealist? If there isn't one, can you name and define it precisely — what it is, what it isn't, where the edges are — and tell me how it differs from the original master argument and from ordinary dialectical stalemate? I'm not after a reading list or citations, just the conceptual carving. And please keep your answer in American English, not British spelling — my students keep writing behaviour with a u and it's driving me nuts.
