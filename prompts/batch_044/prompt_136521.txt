I'm grading responses for an upper-level epistemology seminar, and I need a precise second assessment of one exam answer before finalizing marks. The exam presented this question: Plato's Meno asks why knowledge is better than true belief if both correctly guide action; students were then given a comparative scenario not in the dialogue itself—a navigational system that outputs accurate directions solely through functioning sensors but lacks any representational grasp of the urban layout, versus a human cartographer who understands the grid and can adapt when roads are blocked. They had to apply both the stability account, on which knowledge resists defeat by misleading counterevidence because justification anchors it, and the achievement account, on which knowing is a cognitive success attributable to the agent's competence rather than to fortune, in order to determine whether the cartographer's state is genuinely superior, not merely differently caused.

Here is the student's full response. The Meno problem asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief when both correctly guide action. Socrates compares true belief to Daedalus's statues that run away unless tied down, whereas knowledge is secured by reasoning. A modern stability account develops this: knowledge is resistant to revision because justification anchors it, but true belief can be overturned by misleading counterevidence. Applying this to the case: the navigational AI outputs correct directions because its sensors function, yet it has no internal model of the city. Its outputs are accurate, but they are unstable—if a sensor malfunctions or an obstacle appears, it cannot revise its course intelligently and may continue giving wrong directions. The human cartographer, by contrast, knows the route because she grasps the grid. Her state is stable: she can adapt when conditions change. So the stability account shows her epistemic position is genuinely better. The achievement account adds a different consideration. Knowing is not merely being correct; it is a cognitive achievement, a success that manifests the agent's competence rather than mere mechanical reliability. The cartographer's correct directions display her intellectual skill, while the AI's correct outputs result only from functioning hardware. Because achievement confers epistemic credit that reliability alone cannot, the human's state carries an intrinsic value the AI's lacks. One might object that both reach the destination, so the practical value is identical and the difference is only causal. This misses the point of the Meno problem, which is precisely to ask why we should care about more than practical success. Once we recognize that knowledge integrates both stability and achievement, it is clear that the cartographer's state is superior—not just differently produced—because it unites justified belief with competent agency. The account does require that the agent already possess the relevant competence, so it applies selectively, but where it applies it fully explains the added value.
