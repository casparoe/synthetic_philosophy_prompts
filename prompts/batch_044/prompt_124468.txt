I just spent several hours arguing with a friend about whether biological functions are fixed by evolutionary history or by present causal contribution, and I want to refine my position into a short essay rather than just rehearsing the standard Wright–Cummins debate. My idea is to test the distinction with a case I have not seen treated in the literature: a fully synthetic cardiac pacemaker constructed de novo from non-biological polymers, lacking any evolutionary history, implanted to assume the sinus node's causal role in regulating heartbeat. I want to argue that this case does more than favor the causal-role account; it exposes a hidden circularity in the etiological appeal to "proper function," since once we grant that designed artifacts can bear biological functions, the etiologist must either deny an obvious functional ascription or implicitly rely on causal-role criteria to separate proper from accidental functioning. My notes sketch three moves: (1) the pacemaker performs the same systemic contribution as the native node, so causal-role assigns it a clear heart-regulatory function; (2) the etiologist can deny it has a biological function only by restricting "function" to selection-derived capacities, which seems ad hoc once we accept medical prostheses as functionally integrated; (3) if the etiologist responds by distinguishing "proper function" (selection-based) from "derived function" (design-based), they must use present causal contribution to determine what counts as the derived role, thereby smuggling causal-role reasoning into the very boundary they claim protects their view. Could you comment on whether this framing genuinely advances the debate or merely conflates biological and artifactual function? Specifically, is the charge of circularity fair to a refined etiological account, and does the synthetic pacemaker actually distinguish the two views, or do both have adequate resources to accommodate it? I would also welcome suggestions for tightening the argument without turning it into a survey of the literature.
