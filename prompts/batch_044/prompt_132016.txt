I've spent considerable time thinking through Derek Parfit's hitchhiker scenario and the broader idea that it can be rational to commit yourself, in advance, to acting in ways that will seem irrational once the moment arrives — the problem of rational irrationality. I understand the basic structure: a driver will only take you to town if he predicts you will pay him after arrival, even though once you are there you have no further causal influence over his decision and no self-interested reason to hand over the money; yet paying still seems rational because your present choice is evidentially tied to his past prediction.

What I want to understand is which of the remaining open questions in this area actually deserve serious attention, especially for someone trying to build a coherent view of commitment and decision-making rather than to map an academic field. There seem to me to be four live lines.

First, whether the hitchhiker logic transfers to collective or institutional agents — for instance, a government or firm that makes a costly pledge during a crisis, knowing that different internal parts will later face the decision to honor or abandon it.

Second, whether there is a real distinction between predictive binding, where you shape yourself to be predicted correctly, and causal binding, where you literally restrict your future options, and whether the standard defense of paying the driver secretly depends on running these together.

Third, I have been working out a variant that I have not encountered in the usual discussions: suppose the predictor is not a single driver but a distributed system — an algorithm that aggregates forecasts from many independent observers — and the hitchhiker knows that her reputation is stored symbolically, not in any one mind. Does distributing the prediction across many nodes break the evidential loop, or does it make the commitment more robust?

Fourth, what practical implications this has for ordinary commitment devices — contracts, public vows, marriage — and whether they operate through hitchhiker-style reasoning or through something entirely different.

If someone could only devote real effort to resolving one or two of these over the next few years, which should they choose? I am not looking for a reading list or citations. I want a defended ordering: which question is upstream of the others, so that settling it reshapes the rest? Which is tractable through conceptual reasoning alone, and which depends on empirical claims we cannot yet settle? And which, if answered clearly, would actually change how a thoughtful non-specialist should think about promises and rationality?

Please rank them, explain the criteria you are using — importance, tractability, and how much downstream work depends on each — and be explicit if my distributed-prediction variant is just a confusion that dissolves under scrutiny rather than a genuine open problem. I care about the reasoning behind the priorities, not merely that these issues remain unresolved.
