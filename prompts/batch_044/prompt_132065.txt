As a journalist writing about philosophy of language and cognitive science, I have been examining W.V.O. Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, particularly the gavagai scenario in which a field linguist observing a speaker utter that term upon seeing a rabbit cannot determine—through any amount of behavioral observation—whether it denotes the whole animal, an undetached part, or a temporal stage. Recently, some researchers have proposed that this gap can be closed by integrating neuroimaging: if we map the speaker's internal representational state, we can distinguish object-tracking from process-tracking cognition and thereby fix the reference uniquely. This leads me to wonder whether Quine's problem was never a deep metaphysical claim about meaning, but rather a practical limitation of mid-twentieth-century field methods—one that modern neuroscience can now overcome. If we can in principle access the mental representations that accompany utterances, doesn't this restore a definite fact of the matter about what gavagai means, reducing Quine's indeterminacy to a temporary technological deficit?
