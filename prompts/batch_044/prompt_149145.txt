For my independent study in decision theory, I am comparing two interpretations of ambiguity aversion that grow out of the Ellsberg paradox. The first is the standard interpretation: in the classic two-urn problem—one urn with a known fifty-fifty mix of red and black balls, the other with an unknown mixture—agents systematically prefer to bet on the urn with known probabilities, and this is taken to reveal a single psychological parameter, namely aversion to ambiguity about the outcome-generating distribution itself. The second interpretation is one I have constructed for this project, which I call channel-ambiguity aversion. In this variant, the agent does not face an unknown urn composition; instead, she receives a signal about a fixed but unknown color mix, yet she is ambiguous about whether that signal was produced by an unbiased sampling procedure or by a strategic source with hidden incentives and an unknown objective. Here the ambiguity is not about the outcome distribution directly, but about the reliability of the mapping from world to observation.

Please compare these two interpretations with respect to the following questions. First, does the classic Ellsberg preference for the known-probability bet survive when the ambiguity is relocated into the information channel rather than into the outcome mechanism, or does the mere presence of an observable signal—however suspect—lead agents to treat the situation as reducible to ordinary risk? Second, can both interpretations be accommodated within the maxmin expected-utility representation that is standard in ambiguity-aversion theory using a single ambiguity-aversion parameter, or does the channel-ambiguity case require an additional structural parameter governing beliefs about information sources? Third, is there a psychologically coherent profile in which an agent displays strong ambiguity aversion in the classic urn case but displays none in the channel-ambiguity case, or does such a split indicate that we are actually dealing with two conceptually distinct phenomena that share only a surface resemblance?

Finally, please identify which feature of the channel-ambiguity case my comparison fails to reproduce without importing assumptions that go beyond standard ambiguity-aversion models, and tell me whether accepting my framing forces any revision to the standard verdict that ambiguity-averse behavior is uniform across structurally similar decision problems.
