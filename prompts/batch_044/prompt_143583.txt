Most of them have read Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" in undergraduate courses and know the standard interpretation—that Montaigne praises Tupinambá society mainly to shame European cruelty and thereby advances a kind of proto-cultural-relativism. I am not defending that view.

My thesis is that Montaigne is not arguing for relativism at all. Instead, I claim he constructs the cannibal figure through what I call a "specular inversion": he holds up Tupinambá practices not as genuinely autonomous moral norms, but as a deliberately distorted mirror designed to make European vice visible to Europeans who can no longer recognize it in themselves. The cannibals' supposed "naturalness" is rhetorical optics rather than ethnographic description. I also argue that in the essay's second half Montaigne undermines even this mirror by admitting that his own admiration is itself a form of European artifice—producing a double inversion that leaves neither culture authoritative. This, I think, explains the essay's persistent instability rather than any hidden relativist celebration.
