Two friends of mine, Maya and Raj, got stuck arguing about their mutual friend Clara's advance directive. Clara wrote years ago that if she ever had advanced Alzheimer's and didn't recognise her partner or kids, she wanted feeding and life support stopped. Now she's in exactly that state — doesn't recognise anyone, but she smiles at music and clearly enjoys eating. Her partner wants to withdraw the feeding tube to honour the directive; her sister says keep going because Clara-now seems genuinely content.

Maya said the directive binds because Clara-the-author and Clara-now are the same person, just with damaged cognition. The earlier self made a rational, informed choice about what kind of life was worth living for her, and dementia doesn't erase that authority. Withdrawing is respecting autonomy, not harming her.

Raj came back that this treats autonomy like a property right that survives the owner. Clara-now has her own interests — she enjoys pudding, she doesn't suffer from missing her memories. The directive was made by someone who couldn't imagine being this content in ignorance. Enforcing it is using a dead woman's preferences to harm a living, experiencing subject.

Maya then said if we let the current happy state override the directive, then no advance directive ever binds, since anyone in dementia can be made somewhat comfortable. The whole point is to protect people from being kept alive in conditions they explicitly rejected. Calling the current self a "successor" is just a rhetorical trick to avoid hard choices.

Raj replied that it's not a trick — severe dementia can be a form of psychological discontinuity. The directive was meant to prevent suffering, not mandate death regardless of present welfare. Clara's reason was "I don't want to live without my relationships and memories," but she now lives without them and doesn't mind, so the reason is moot. He also said we don't honour adolescent suicide pacts just because they were "unconditional"; there's a real difference between temporary immaturity and irreversible decline, but also a real parallel: past commitments shouldn't become tyranny.

Maya answered that promises bind because breaking them harms people who relied on them, but here the only "reliance" is Clara's past self. Harming Clara-now to satisfy her is like punishing a child for the parent's debts. She added that even on a reductionist view of personal identity — where there's no metaphysical "earlier self" — we still need some normative rule about whose values count, and the directive represents Clara's stable, reflective values, not a momentary whim.

Raj said reductionism actually cuts the other way: if there's no enduring self, then the directive is just one mental state among others, and the present mental states have direct moral weight because they're experienced right now. The earlier value-judgement was made without ever experiencing dementia; Clara-now has direct access to her current welfare. So giving her present enjoyment priority isn't ignoring identity, it's taking seriously that there's no ghost in the machine enforcing old contracts.

Neither of them would budge.
