I've been arguing with my roommate about Mill's late socialism and I think I've landed on a view that feels right but probably has big holes. Basically, I think Mill's later writings—especially those extra chapters he added to Principles and that separate essay on socialism near the end—don't actually commit him to collective ownership at all. Instead, he's defending something like stewardship socialism: capital stays in private hands technically, but the workers who actually use it have a kind of inalienable management right that can't be bought out or inherited away. And the reason he thinks this is compatible with liberty is that he treats property rights as purely conventional instruments for social utility, not natural rights. So if a capitalist firm is structured so that workers have zero control, that's not a violation of liberty per se, but it is a failure of the social utility justification for that particular property arrangement.

If that's right, then Mill's socialism is really a theory about the legitimate conditions of ownership rather than a call for nationalization. But I'm not sure what would actually count against this reading. Would it be finding passages where Mill explicitly endorses state ownership of railways or land? Or would it be showing that his conventionalist theory of property actually requires full alienability for markets to function? Or maybe there's a tension with his harm principle—if property is conventional, then redistributing management rights is just a policy change, not an interference with liberty, but that seems to make socialism too easy and maybe collapses his liberalism into utilitarian authoritarianism. I want an honest map of what defeats this: which texts or arguments matter, which internal tensions in Mill's own framework are fatal versus just awkward, and whether the view is so well-insulated that it's not really an interpretation anymore but just a projection. I definately want to know if that's a problem.
