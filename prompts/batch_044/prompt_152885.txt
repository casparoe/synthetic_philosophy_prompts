If I understand the literature on standing to blame correctly, hypocrisy undermines a blamer's authority not categorically but proportionally: the blamer's own culpable conduct establishes a moral symmetry with the target, and the weight of their standing is attenuated according to how closely their offense mirrors the one they condemn. This implies that standing can be partially retained and calibrated by something like a similarity coefficient—perhaps indexed to structural form, moral gravity, or the blamer's awareness that they are committing the same type of wrong. Given that framework, how should we actually calibrate the metric? For example, does someone who routinely exaggerates on tax returns retain partial standing to blame a colleague for embezzlement, with the attenuation calculated across the shared category of financial deception, or does the disparity in gravity drive the coefficient close to zero and leave standing largely intact? Conversely, if the coefficient tracks structural form rather than gravity, does chronic lateness—a minor failure of reliability—diminish standing to blame chronic deceit by exactly the same amount that tax exaggeration diminishes standing to blame embezzlement, since both pairs share a broad domain but differ in severity? And is there a hidden threshold effect within a domain—say, unreliability—such that any fault falling inside it triggers full nullification regardless of magnitude, or is the reduction genuinely continuous, yielding borderline cases where standing is diminished by half and the appropriateness of blame becomes genuinely indeterminate?
