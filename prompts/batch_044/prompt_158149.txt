In the rationalist epistemology threads I've followed for years, there's a persistent stall in debates about whether rational decision-making requires what philosophers call luminosity—the idea that if you know something, you're in a position to know that you know it. One camp, drawing on calibration practice and prediction-market reasoning, insists that high-stakes betting requires second-order awareness: you should be able to tell whether your first-order belief counts as knowledge, not just hold it with some credence. The other camp, citing the standard anti-luminosity arguments involving gradual change, replies that near any epistemic boundary—say, between being an expert and being near-expert on a forecasting topic—there is always a marginal case where you genuinely know (or genuinely don't) yet cannot tell which. Both sides accept the formal structure of the gradualist counterexample and agree that knowledge comes in degrees of robustness; they even use the same calibration-training examples. Yet after years of back-and-forth, no one has moved. Is this disagreement actually about whether the concept of knowledge should be applied to continuous epistemic improvement at all—a verbal dispute masked as a substantive one? Or is there a hidden normative question about whether practical rationality demands transparency that theoretical epistemology denies? Alternatively, could the stall indicate that both sides are answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of mental states, the other about the design of belief-revision protocols—under the single heading of "luminosity"? Given your diagnosis, what would actually resolve it: a formal model showing that second-order tracking is computationally intractable near boundaries, an empirical study of expert self-assessment, or a conceptual distinction between knowledge and calibrated confidence? And if the disagreement is bedrock, should we expect any progress, or should we redesign our calibration practices around the impasse rather than through it?
