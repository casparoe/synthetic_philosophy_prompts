A friend reconstructed an argument from Frank Ramsey's remarks on the meaning of life, and it now seems to undermine all practical reasoning. (1) Ramsey treats the question "What is the meaning of life?" as conceptually confused—similar to asking for the meaning of a word detached from any actual linguistic practice—so that there is no property of "life-meaning" that lives can possess or lack. (2) If there is no such global property, then any particular judgment that "this project is meaningful" implicitly appeals to the same non-existent property, only applied locally rather than globally. (3) Therefore local judgments of significance are equally confused; we cannot coherently maintain that one activity is genuinely more worth pursuing than another. (4) But then all practical reasoning about ends collapses, which is absurd and also conflicts with Ramsey's own evident commitment to intense intellectual work. (5) The friend concludes that Ramsey must have been wrong about the initial confusion. I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), since the analogy between global and local appeals to significance seems tight. Is the slip in (2), in assuming that local evaluation requires the same metaphysical property? Or is it in (3), in treating local comparative judgments as if they needed that property at all? Which step should I reject, and what does each rejection cost—particularly, does saving practical reasoning require smuggling back a substantive notion of meaning that Ramsey's view was meant to dissolve?
