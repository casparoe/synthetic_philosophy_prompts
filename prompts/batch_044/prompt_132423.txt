I am serving as the grading assistant for a graduate seminar on British Idealism and its critics. The instructor has assigned a take-home essay that asks students to reconstruct F.H. Bradley's regress argument against the reality of relations, to articulate his doctrine that relations are internal or ideal relative to an undifferentiated Absolute, and then to apply that framework to the problem of propositional unity. Specifically, students must address whether Bradley's holistic account can accommodate false judgments—such as the claim that the present King of France is bald—without reintroducing real relational structure between a judgment and an independent fact, and they must also consider whether false propositions retain any derivative unity or fail to be judgments altogether.

Student answer:
Bradley's regress begins from the premise that if a relation R genuinely connects term a to term b, then R must itself be related to a and to b by further relations, generating an infinite series. Because such a regress is vicious, Bradley denies that relations are real, independent constituents of the world. Instead, he treats them as ideal: they belong to the realm of appearance and depend for whatever being they have on the undifferentiated Absolute, which alone is fully real and non-relational.

When we turn to propositional unity, the problem is how a subject and predicate combine into one judgment without requiring an external relational glue. Bradley's answer is that the proposition is not a complex of discrete terms linked by a copula. Rather, a judgment expresses a single, differentiated aspect of the Absolute in which the distinction between subject and predicate is merely internal and provisional. The predicate is not an independent entity externally attached to the subject; it is a determination of the whole that temporarily appears separate.

This framework also addresses false propositions. A false judgment, such as the claim that the present King of France is bald, does not correspond to an independent fact outside the Absolute. Instead, it represents a partial, contradictory, or less coherent appearance within the Absolute—possessing a lower degree of reality than a true judgment, but still unified as an aspect of the whole. Thus Bradley avoids treating truth and falsity as relations between propositions and external realities.

Nevertheless, Bradley's solution anticipates the Fregean account of propositional unity. Just as Frege held that concepts are unsaturated and require objects to complete them, Bradley holds that predicates are incomplete apart from the Absolute in which they inhere. For this reason, Bradley's doctrine of internal relations can be read as an early version of functional predication, an insight that Russell subsequently incorporated into logical atomism by treating relations as external while preserving the idea that terms are saturated entities.
