I've been trying to work out where lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism actually breaks down in practice, not just in the abstract. The basic view is familiar enough -- suffering gets lexical priority until it drops below some threshold, then other values come back in. But I'm stuck on the commensurability side: if pain and pleasure don't share a single metric, then setting a 'threshold' already presupposes we can compare them across contexts, and I'm not sure we can. There's a specific wrinkle I keep coming back to that I haven't seen discussed much: call it the distributed-subthreshold problem. If suffering is just barely below the lexical cutoff but spread across billions of entities -- wild animals, future synthetic minds, whatever -- can it agregate in morally relevant ways that pleasure of the same intensity and spread can't? Most treatments treat the threshold as applying to individual welfare levels, but if suffering has some kind of non-linear aggregation property that pleasure lacks, then the lexical view might either collapse or need serious revision.

So if you had to advise someone spending about three years on this, purely conceptually, which open questions should they tackle first? I'm especially interested in three lines: (1) wether the commensurability problem is fatal to setting any non-arbitrary threshold, (2) wether that distributed-subthreshold idea actually distinguishes suffering from pleasure in a principled way, and (3) what downstream work in population ethics or longtermist framing depends on settling these first. Rank them with reasoning: tractability for pure philosophy rather than empirical science, how upstream they are, and what kind of argument or hypothetical case would actually move your confidence. And please don't just list papers -- I want the reasoning for the ordering, including which ones you'd deprioritize because settling them wouldn't change much else.
