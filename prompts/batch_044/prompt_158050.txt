I've come to defend a specific interpretation of global workspace theory—call it broadcast constitutivism—according to which phenomenal consciousness is not merely correlated with global informational availability but is ontologically constituted by the access-relations that define broadcasting across modular processors. On this view, if a mental state is globally broadcast, it possesses phenomenal character in virtue of that broadcast structure; if it is not broadcast, it lacks phenomenal character entirely, not merely reportability or cognitive access. There is therefore no "phenomenal residue" that escapes the workspace, and the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness collapses into a single dimension of availability rather than marking two independent properties.

What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked by severity: which empirical findings—say, from blindsight, subliminal priming, or no-report paradigms—would be fatal rather than merely awkward; which conceptual distinctions, particularly those drawn between access and phenomenal consciousness, the view can accommodate and which it cannot; whether broadcast constitutivism collapses into a form of functionalism about consciousness that inherits functionalism's standard objections (e.g., absent qualia, inverted spectra); and whether the position has been insulated by definitional moves into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. Please distinguish the objections that defenders of the view should themselves concede as genuinely damaging from those they should dismiss as resting on question-begging intuitions. I am especially interested in whether there are results in other domains—about persistence, about the metaphysics of properties, or about the structure of explanation—that this view stands or falls with, and which of these connections are substantive rather than rhetorical.
