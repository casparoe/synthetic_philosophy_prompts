First, I take expected-utility maximization to be the correct normative framework for choice under risk, without exception in every case where the expectation is well defined. Second, I dissolve the St. Petersburg paradox by appealing to psychologically realistic bounded utility: once utility is concave, the expectation becomes finite, so the infinite-value result is merely an artifact of linearity. Third, I hold that the Pasadena paradox—where the payoff structure is arranged so that both the positive and the negative contributions diverge—shows that bounded utility does not always rescue expected-utility theory, since concavity does not render the distribution integrable; hence there exist genuinely undefined expectations that EU theory cannot process. Fourth, I regard this silence as a feature rather than a failure: where no expectation exists, rationality delivers no comparative verdict, and it is not irrational to take the gamble, to reject it, or to abstain altogether. Fifth, despite that theoretical silence, I maintain that there is a substantive fact of the matter that one ought to refuse the Pasadena gamble, because an undefined expectation indicates a structural incoherence in the offer itself—something like a defect in the framing—that a rational agent should avoid, even if EU itself remains mute. Sixth, I reject attempts to patch EU theory in these cases by adopting an arbitrary truncation rule, such as taking symmetric Cauchy-style limits over truncated distributions, because different truncation sequences yield different results, so selecting one smuggles in an unstated preference rather than deriving a verdict from the theory. Rank them by severity, indicate the cheapest fix for each, and tell me which single revision would resolve the most problems at once.
