You are a senior clinical ethicist with training in philosophy of action and medical decision-making. Before drafting the protocol, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual distinctions that standard assisted-dying review boards usually collapse into a single “autonomy” check. I need you to design a reusable, step-by-step evaluation procedure for a hospital clinical-ethics committee that reviews petitions for physician-assisted dying in a jurisdiction where the practice is legal but requires documented, multi-disciplinary sign-off.

The committee meets weekly, spends no more than ninety minutes per petition, includes an attending physician, a nurse, a social worker, and a legal advisor but no trained philosophers, and must produce reasoning that can survive judicial or institutional audit. The physician submits a petition packet with clinical records.

Your procedure must be grounded in a philosophical distinction that conventional protocols ignore: the separation between an authority-based gate (does the patient have the standing to direct the ending of their biological life?) and an axiological-coherence gate (is the requested death continuous with—rather than a repudiation of—the values and commitments that have structured the patient’s biographical narrative?). Treat these as two independent gates that must both open; do not collapse them into one autonomy assessment.

Design the procedure as a flowchart in words: numbered stages in strict order. For each stage, specify (a) the precise question to ask, (b) what counts as a sufficient answer, (c) the required evidence, (d) the stop-point if the answer fails, and (e) an explicit flag wherever the question is irreducibly a value judgment that requires a documented committee vote rather than further fact-gathering.

Then apply the full procedure to three petitions:
1. A sixty-seven-year-old with advanced ALS who executed a detailed advance directive five years ago requesting aid in dying upon loss of independent respiratory function; the clinical threshold is now met, but the patient recently adopted a religious tradition that prohibits self-killing and now expresses ambivalence, asking the committee to decide.
2. A forty-two-year-old with severe, treatment-resistant major depression, no physical illness, who has refused all psychiatric interventions for eighteen months; psychiatric evaluation finds intact cognition but notes that the desire for death tracks the depressive symptom profile with near-perfect correlation, and the patient’s family strongly supports the petition.
3. One additional case you construct yourself, in which the procedure yields a recommendation that conflicts with common moral intuition—make the conflict explicit, show exactly which stage produces it, and state what the committee is forced to do despite that intuition.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements it will fail to resolve, which cases it should refer back to the legislature or court rather than deciding internally, and where its structural commitment to the two-gate framework is likely to misfire or produce counter-intuitive results.

Be precise, avoid generic lists like “respect autonomy, do no harm,” do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and check at each stage that the philosophical distinction actually survives translation into an operational test. Mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.
