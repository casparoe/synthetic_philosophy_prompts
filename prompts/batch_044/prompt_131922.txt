I'm giving the opening remarks this Saturday at "Reasoning Without a Net," a one-day community workshop I helped organize for local high school teachers, librarians, and a handful of philosophy graduate students who volunteered to lead breakout sessions. The audience is deliberately split: about half come for practical classroom tools, and the other half will recognize any technical reference I make. I have no academic philosophy background — I'm an engineer who got pulled into this after a frustrating conversation with my teenage niece, who kept asking why she should accept that "if A then B, and A, therefore B" is valid. I couldn't answer without either appealing to her intuition or citing a rule that itself needed justification, and that led me to Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which the Tortoise demands that Achilles justify each logical step, producing an endless regress of rules about rules. I want my remarks to do more than decorate the workshop with a literary reference; the infinite-regress problem should shape what I can honestly promise. I need to say that we are not here to discover some ultimate ground for deduction, but I also must avoid the cynical conclusion that reasoning has no foundation at all and is therefore useless.

Please write me a five-minute opening speech that sounds like someone who stumbled into this puzzle personally and is still genuinely unsettled by it, rather than like a professor explaining formal logic. It should land for the graduate students — they should feel the Carroll reference is taken seriously — without losing the teachers in jargon. In particular, I'd like the speech to use the tortoise's regress to explain why the workshop will offer practice in reasoning rather than a checklist of fallacies, and to close with a concrete, honest promise about working inside the loop instead of escaping it. The tone should be welcoming, slightly puzzled, and respectful of both halves of the room.
