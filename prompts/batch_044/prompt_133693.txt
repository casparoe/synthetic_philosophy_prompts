I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps on Marx's philosophy of technology and automation, and I need the joint statement both could sign after sustained good-faith engagement. The first camp, the productive-force determinists, holds that Marx's analysis of machinery—particularly the fragment on machinery and the general intellect in the Grundrisse notebooks and the corresponding arguments in Capital—describes an objective structural tendency: as automation advances, direct labor becomes a diminishing source of value, the law of value is progressively undermined, and capitalism thereby generates its own technological conditions of transcendence, largely irrespective of immediate political form. The second camp, the social-form mediators, holds that Marx treats technology as always embedded within and mediated by the relations of production; the same fragment describes a contradiction that capital actively suppresses through counter-tendencies such as the cheapening of machinery, the expansion of unproductive labor, and the global extension of exploitation, so that automation never operates as an autonomous historical motor and any transition remains contingent on the political restructuring of ownership.

Please draft the document in three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims that both camps can genuinely endorse: precise statements about Marx's distinction between formal and real subsumption, the concept of socially necessary labor time, the role of machinery in the production of relative surplus value, and the difference between technological change as a material process and technological change as a valorization strategy—stated without bibliographies, page references, reading lists, or verbatim quotations from Marx's texts. Second, a disagreements table in which each camp's residual position is formulated in wording its own members would accept: the determinist claim should be phrased so that it asserts automation can eventually render labor-time measurement structurally obsolete as a tendency internal to capital's development, while the mediator claim should be phrased so that it asserts this tendency is always already blocked or reversed by capital's need to preserve value-creation, with neither formulation reduced to a straw man. Third, identify the exact cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—including whether the general intellect denotes an emerging actual productive force or a speculative limit-case that capital prevents from actualizing; whether contemporary platform-based algorithmic management in logistics and warehouse labor represents formal subsumption, real subsumption, or a novel hybrid; and whether the rate of surplus value can be sustained indefinitely by counter-tendencies or must eventually collapse under the weight of full automation. For each crux, provide a concrete case or historical pattern both sides agree is relevant—such as the trajectory of manufacturing automation since the mid-twentieth century or the restructuring of labor through predictive scheduling algorithms—together with each camp's explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates. Finally, indicate clearly which disagreements are substantive and which dissolve into verbal disputes once the shared conceptual framework is made explicit.
