In debates about the origin and stability of social norms, a common analogy compares conventions to biological species: they are said to emerge through variation, selective retention, and environmental adaptation rather than through deliberate rational design. This evolutionary framing is often used to challenge Lewis-style accounts, which treat convention as a regularity sustained by mutual expectation and a shared preference for conformity in solving coordination problems. Set out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the genome, the mutation, the selective pressure, and the fitness landscape within Lewis's framework? Then mark the points where the analogy collapses, particularly regarding intentional choice, common knowledge, and the rate of change, and ask whether those disanalogies undermine the evolutionary critique or merely place it on a different temporal scale. Finally, evaluate whether the evolutionary picture smuggles in a non-rational mechanism that Lewis's view genuinely excludes, or whether the two descriptions are compatible; and state whether the conclusion that norms are not rational solutions survives once the mapping failures are fully exposed.
