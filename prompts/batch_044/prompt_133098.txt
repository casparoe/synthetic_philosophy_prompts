I am taking a course on classical Indian epistemology, and we have spent several weeks on the Nyāya doctrine of pramāṇas—the four allegedly irreducible sources of valid cognition: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (analogy or comparison), and śabda (testimony or verbal authority). I have become particularly interested in a tension that does not seem to be addressed directly in the standard commentaries: modern scientific practice depends on instrument-mediated observation and on peer-reviewed consensus, both of which appear to sit awkwardly between pratyakṣa and śabda. If a telescope extends the senses, is it merely an aid to pratyakṣa, or does it generate a distinct epistemic pathway? And if scientific authority rests on collective verification, does that reduce śabda to a sociological process rather than a distinct pramāṇa?

Write an essay examining whether the traditional Nyāya framework can coherently accommodate contemporary scientific knowledge without either collapsing its four categories into one another or inventing an unauthorised fifth pramāṇa for instrument-aided cognition. Weigh the arguments for treating instruments as extensions of perception against the view that they create a hybrid epistemic form, and assess whether the Nyāya defence of śabda can survive the transition from scriptural authority to scientific peer review. You must reach a definite conclusion—defend either the adaptability of the four-fold scheme or its necessary revision—and justify it with explicit reasoning. Keep the response within 400 words, use British English throughout, and adopt a committed stance rather than a neutral survey of positions.
