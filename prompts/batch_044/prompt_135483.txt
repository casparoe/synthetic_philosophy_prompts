Consider the engineering practice now emerging in semantic brain-computer interface research, where decoders are not trained to reproduce continuous neural dynamics but are instead built around a pre-specified mental lexicon: researchers define a fixed inventory of atomic semantic categories—object labels, action types, relational operators, and logical connectives—and train classifiers to map specific activation patterns onto these atoms. The system then applies a formal compositional grammar to combine the decoded atoms into full propositional reports, such as assertions that a particular object is located relative to another or that a specific action is intended. The justification offered is usually practical: this architecture improves generalization to untrained sentences, reduces noise, and yields outputs that clinicians can read as structured intentions rather than as ambiguous statistical correlations. Yet the design treats the brain as though it stores discrete, syntactically combinable symbols.

What philosophical commitments about mental representation does this practice presuppose? Must one genuinely accept the language of thought hypothesis—that cognition involves syntactically structured mental representations with combinatorial semantics—or is the atomic-grammar framework merely an instrumental fiction that a connectionist or predictive-coding model could equally well accommodate? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice commit to the existence of mental atoms, to a specific syntax of thought independent of natural language, or only to the heuristic value of atomization for clinical interpretation? Distinguish what the design strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and examine whether its parts are consistent—particularly the move from decoding discrete symbols to inferring a patient's meaning or intent in ethically consequential clinical contexts. Finally, if the underlying neuroscience were replaced by a thoroughly non-compositional framework in which meaning is always distributed, context-dependent, and never atomized, what would have to change in both the technical architecture of these interfaces and the ethical norms governing how their outputs are used to represent a patient's will?
