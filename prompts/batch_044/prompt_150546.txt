The exam question was: Explain John Rawls's "separateness of persons" objection to classical utilitarianism. Your answer should (a) identify the analogy Rawls draws between rational choice within one life and the utilitarian calculus across society; (b) explain precisely why that analogy fails on Rawls's account; and (c) evaluate whether the objection gains or loses force when applied to temporal separation—specifically, to cases in which present burdens fall on an existing group while future benefits accrue to a non-overlapping group that does not yet exist, where defenders claim that non-identity makes the separation analogous to that between distinct contemporaneous persons.

The student's answer, reproduced exactly, reads as follows:

Rawls's separateness of persons objection, developed in A Theory of Justice, targets the way utilitarianism applies principles of rational choice appropriate for a single individual to society as a whole. Within one life, a person may rationally endure present discomfort—say, undergoing surgery—for a greater future benefit; the costs and benefits belong to the same subject and can be summed. Utilitarianism treats society analogously: it aggregates welfare across all individuals, permitting severe burdens on some if they yield greater total gains for others. Rawls argues this ignores that persons are separate, each with their own life to lead; the losses of one are not canceled out by the gains of another, because there is no single subject of experience that encompasses both. This is essentially the same objection Bernard Williams raised against impersonal morality: treating people as interchangeable containers of welfare violates the distinctness of individual lives. Regarding temporal separation: if present sufferers and future beneficiaries are distinct not only in time but in identity—since future people do not yet exist—this seems to strengthen the anti-utilitarian case, because there is not even the possibility of compensation within a shared life. However, the defense actually overreaches. Rawls's point concerns moral independence, not metaphysical continuity; even if future people are genuinely distinct entities, that alone does not establish the specific form of non-substitutability he requires. Moreover, if the separateness objection blocks all aggregation across distinct subjects, then even Rawls's own difference principle—which permits inequalities that improve the position of the worst off—would be impermissible, since it still involves trade-offs between persons. Therefore, the temporal version of the argument fails: it confuses non-identity with moral separateness, and if taken literally, it undermines distributive principles that allow any interpersonal comparison.
