I've been trying to work out my own view on extreme distributive cases—situations where one being can soak up resources and turn them into well-being far more efficiently than ordinary people can. I don't have a philosophy background; I just read some popular articles and tried to test my intuitions against made-up scenarios. I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind my reactions, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are four cases and what I think about each. First, imagine a single creature—call it a sponge—whose brain or design lets it experience about five hundred times as much happiness per unit of food and care as a human does. If I could either save ten people from serious deprivation or feed the sponge so that it lives in continuous bliss, I feel we should save the ten people; concentrating everything in one outlier feels wrong, even though the total happiness might be larger. Second, imagine the same total capacity spread across fifty separate sponges, each with a ten-to-one advantage over a human. With the same resources, I could either help the ten people or provide moderate comfort to all fifty sponges. Here my intuition flips: I think the fifty sponges should be helped; distributing the surplus among many beings seems permissible, maybe even required, even though the aggregate ratio is identical to the first case. Third, consider a sponge with a two-hundred-to-one advantage, but one that is only conscious for one hour each day; the rest of the time it is like a turned-off machine. The same resources could either secure decent lives for five people around the clock, or make that sponge ecstatic during its brief windows. I think we should still help the five people; I don't want to weight intermittent consciousness as if it were continuous. Fourth, suppose a sponge needs not material goods but a dedicated human caretaker—someone who must give up their own life projects to provide constant interaction. The sponge's joy is enormous, but the caretaker is essentially conscripted. Even if the sponge's gain is vast, I think we should refuse; the moral cost of overriding one person's autonomy to serve another being's happiness seems like a separate wrong.

These four judgments feel somewhat stable to me, but I can't see the rule that holds them together. Is there one principle—maybe about the distribution of moral patients, about continuity of experience, about non-conscription—that delivers all four? If so, please state it precisely, show which clause handles which case, and tell me what it predicts for a situation I haven't mentioned: what if the sponge is not an alien creature but a human with a rare genetic mutation that gives them ten times the normal capacity for pleasure, and there are only two such people versus ten ordinary people competing for the same medical resources? If no single principle saves all four verdicts, please say which one I should abandon and why. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of philosophers; I just want to know what my own intuitions commit me to, and where the tension lies.
