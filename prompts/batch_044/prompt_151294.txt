hey so i'm on this joint funding-and-ethics panel at our university's decision-sciences insitute. we review behavioral experiments that test rational choice models with real stakes, and a PI just submitted a protocol that's basically a live toxin-puzzle variant but with a social twist i haven't seen in the standard literature.

here's the setup: each participant is offered $1,500 if they form a sincere, verified intention to perform a mildly unpleasant but harmless action — swallowing a specially-formulated bitter solution that causes about ten minutes of nausea. they don't actually have to do it; the payment comes right after the intention is confirmed via self-report plus a behavioral commitment measure. but there's a pairing mechanism: participants are matched in pairs, and the full payout for both only triggers if both form the intention. however, only one person in each pair is randomly selected to actually consume the solution, and neither knows who it will be until after the intentions are locked. so each person is essentially betting on being the one who gets away with just the intention, but they need their partner to also play along.

half the panel thinks this is a legitimate test of whether rational agents can maintain 'instrumental-only intentions' — intentions formed purely for gain with execution explicitly not planned — especially under social interdependence. the other half says we're institutionalizing a form of collective bad faith: we're paying people to intend unpleasantness they never meant to carry out, and the pairing makes it worse because you're incentivizing your partner to take the risk of being the executor. one member called it 'disposable intentionality' and said the study exploits the gap between intending and acting that Kavka's puzzle highlights.

the PI also claims the study tests 'meta-intentional stability': whether knowing that a predictive algorithm (trained on prior participants) has assigned you a 73% probability of being the executor changes whether you can still form the original intention sincerely.

we need a memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — that gives clear reasoning the PI can actually respond to. it should address whether there's a meaningful ethical difference between forming an intention you plan to drop versus one you plan to execute, and whether adding the partner layer changes the moral calculus. include an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. don't ask for references, reading lists, or citations — this panel doesn't do them in memos and the PI shouldn't need them to answer our objections. write it casual, like i'm emailing the board secretary, and maybe leave in a typo or two.
