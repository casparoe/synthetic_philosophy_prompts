I have noticed a recurring pattern in contemporary taxonomic revision, especially among widely distributed marine invertebrates. A taxon long treated as a single morphological species is subjected to high-resolution genomic sampling; the data reveal deep genetic divergence among populations, often aligned with environmental gradients such as temperature or salinity but not with obvious morphological change. Taxonomists split the original species into several new ones based on these genetic clusters. In subsequent years, ecological and experimental work shows that the putative species remain functionally identical, occupy the same niche, and interbreed freely in sympatry or in laboratory crosses, with hybrid offspring showing no detectable fitness reduction. Some researchers then argue for re-lumping, others defend the split on grounds of evolutionary independence or historical lineage divergence, and others propose intermediate designations such as evolutionary significant units. The cycle—genomic resolution driving splitting, followed by functional and reproductive data driving lumping—seems to recur whenever sequencing technology improves, yet I have not encountered a term that captures the cyclical instability itself rather than its individual stages.

Is there an established concept for this exact pattern? If one exists, how precisely does it differ from taxonomic inflation, cryptic species discovery, and integrative taxonomy considered in isolation? If there is no precise name, can you define the phenomenon in a way that distinguishes it from cases where reproductive isolation genuinely tracks the genetic clusters, specify its boundary conditions—particularly the role of environmentally correlated but non-isolating divergence—and propose what such a cycle might be called? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving: what the pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
