I spent my career in experimental physics refining calibration protocols for interferometric measurements, where we distinguish systematic bias from random error by comparing instrument outputs against physically realized reference states. Recently, in reading about deflationary theories of truth, I encountered the view that the predicate “true” performs only a disquotational function—permitting semantic ascent without attributing any substantive property to propositions. This led me to a worry that seems to undermine the conceptual framework of my former field. If asserting that a measurement report is true amounts to nothing more than asserting the report itself, then the notion of approximation error—understood as the physical divergence between an instrument’s indication and an actual state of the measured system—appears reducible to a linguistic redundancy rather than a substantive relation. Consequently, it seems deflationists must either reject the realist interpretation of calibration, according to which instruments approximate mind-independent properties, or implicitly smuggle in a correspondence relation that their semantic theory officially denies. Where exactly does this reasoning fail? Is the confusion in treating semantic ascent as incompatible with metaphysical correspondence, in conflating the redundancy of the truth-predicate with the redundancy of the properties that make statements true, or in assuming that a non-substantive account of “true” cannot accommodate a substantive account of error? I want to understand whether a deflationary theory of truth can consistently preserve the practice of treating measurement error as a physical relation rather than a feature of our sentences.
