I'm currently in an upper-division seminar on rational agency and reflective consistency, and we've been working through the tension that arises when an agent with sufficient logical resources tries to maintain complete self-trust. Specifically, we've discussed the Löbian obstacle: the idea that such an agent cannot consistently endorse the reliability of its own reasoning system without, through that very endorsement, being forced to accept further conclusions that it hasn't independently verified. Rather than writing another analytical paper, I want to stage a 75-minute workshop simulation for about fourteen graduate students that makes them feel that instability from the inside, not as a theorem on a board but as a practical cost they have to bear.

Design a structured game in which each student is assigned a role as a "node" in a collective reasoning network, equipped with a private belief card and a fixed set of inference rules. The group must vote, round by round, on whether to adopt a "master consistency statement" endorsing the network's own reliability. The rules and payoff scheme—using points rather than money—should be arranged so that self-trust is initially rewarding and coordination feels natural, but once the master statement is adopted, the network's rules generate an unexpected derived belief that conflicts with at least one private card. Students should then face a forced choice: retain consistency and accept the unwelcome belief (losing points tied to their private card), or resist the inference and break consistency (losing points tied to collective reliability). Include explicit roles, the sequence of rounds, the scoring mechanism, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the logical structure of reflective self-trust—especially the gap between knowing each rule individually and knowing that the system applies them correctly as a whole.

Please also be frank about what the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce—most likely the precise arithmetization of syntax that the formal result depends on—and tell me exactly how I should supply that missing piece in the debrief without turning the session into a lecture. Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time I run it: I expect some students will try to appeal to an external authority or "step outside" the rules to avoid the conflict, and I need guidance on how to handle that without collapsing the experience. The goal is that participants leave having genuinely felt the pull of self-trust and the price of escaping it, even if they haven't seen the formal proof.
