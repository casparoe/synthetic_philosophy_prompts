I have just come out of a long and genuinely frustrating argument with a close friend—she works in philosophy of science, I work in public policy—about whether William James’s defense of adopting belief by an act of will when evidence is permanently insufficient can survive W.K. Clifford’s moral charge that it is always wrong, everywhere and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. Our disagreement was not about textual exegesis but about what happens when the belief in question is not merely personal but becomes embedded in an institution or research program and is then inherited by people who never made the original leap themselves. I kept using the phrase credal inheritance to name this transmission: the idea that a belief adopted under Jamesian conditions—where the option is living and forced, and evidence genuinely does not decide—becomes part of an epistemic structure that others depend on, which transforms the moral risk Clifford was worried about from a private failure into a shared liability. She thinks I am smuggling in a social dimension neither author intended, and I want to test whether that smuggling is a distortion or a necessary correction.

This Thursday I am delivering the opening remarks—about five minutes spoken—for an interdisciplinary workshop called Commitment Under Uncertainty, hosted jointly by the philosophy department and the school of public policy. The room will contain philosophers who know the James-Clifford exchange in detail and practitioners—climate modelers, epidemiologists, urban planners—who do not need a history of philosophy lecture but who face exactly this tension every time they choose a working model or commit to a policy frame before the data are conclusive. I want the remarks to use the disagreement structurally rather than decoratively: they should establish that the workshop is not about being rational versus being hopeful, but about who bears the risk when belief outruns evidence, and whether that risk can be shared across an institution without being diluted or hidden. The remarks should feel like an invitation that sets stakes, not a resolution that dissolves them. They must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of specific editions; they should be deliverable by someone who understands the conceptual landscape without access to a library, and they should avoid false consolation as well as false despair.

Please write those opening remarks. They need to land for both halves of the room: the philosophers should recognize that James and Clifford are being taken seriously without caricature, and the practitioners should leave with a clear, non-technical sense of why their daily commitments to model assumptions or policy frames are philosophically live.
