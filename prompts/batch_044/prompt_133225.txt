Read Franz Kafka's The Trial through the philosophy of inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and the demands of epistemic modesty, but with a specific framing I am calling "institutional inside-view capture": the tendency to treat one's own bureaucratic or juridical predicament as uniquely interpretable through first-person narrative engagement, while treating statistical or structural knowledge about the system as either inaccessible or morally inadmissible. Josef K. spends the novel insisting on understanding the specific logic of the court that has accused him, searching for hidden rules, sympathetic intermediaries, and personal exculpation, yet he almost never seriously considers the outside view — that defendants in opaque punitive structures are typically convicted regardless of particular merit, and that the system may function precisely by preventing the accumulation of such base-rate knowledge. I want a reading that treats this not as simple foolishness but as a contested epistemic stance: is K.'s refusal to adopt an outside view a rational failure, a necessary form of moral self-respect, or an engineered vulnerability that the institution exploits?

Which scenes — the initial arrest without explanation, the cathedral parable with its indeterminate moral, the final execution without verdict — best dramatize this tension? Does the novel's refusal to grant any omniscient or authoritative account of the court's purpose mean that the reader is denied the outside view as well, and if so, does that implicate us in K.'s epistemic position? I am especially interested in whether Kafka adds something to philosophical debates about epistemic modesty that pure argument misses: for instance, that there are institutional designs specifically calculated to destroy the reference classes on which outside-view reasoning depends, making modesty about one's own case not just difficult but structurally impossible. Be honest about where the text resists this reading — if there are moments where K.'s particularism reads as clearly delusional, or conversely where his refusal to generalize reads as heroic resistance to dehumanization — and say whether the novel ultimately vindicates or condemns his epistemic posture. This is for an essay on the moral psychology of reasoning under opacity, not for a literature course, so I care more about the philosophical stakes of the interpretation than about literary-historical context.
