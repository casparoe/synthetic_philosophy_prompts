Construct a formal decision-tree taxonomy that sorts historiographical stances on whether “the Middle Ages,” “the Renaissance,” and “the Scientific Revolution” name real structural breaks in the past or are retrospective narrative impositions. The audience is an advanced undergraduate or graduate seminar in intellectual history: the instrument should let a reader locate her own commitments without asking her to choose a label she may not know.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of famous names. Start with five binary branching questions, each a concrete diagnostic case on which positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer yes or no without knowing any technical vocabulary:

1. Root: If a historian in Ming-dynasty China around 1600 had been asked to periodize Latin European history using only internal institutional criteria—say, shifts in university curricula, metallurgical technique, and patronage structures—would she have recognized a distinct “Renaissance” separate from the centuries before it? (This separates universal-structure essentialists from region-bound or constructivist views.)

2. On the “Scientific Revolution”: Is the break defined by a specific metaphysical commitment—mechanism and mathematization of nature—such that figures like Paracelsus or alchemists fall outside the period regardless of chronology, or is it defined by a cluster of institutional practices (academies, peer correspondence, experimental replication) that can include heterogeneous metaphysics?

3. On the “Middle Ages”: Is the period bounded primarily by the presence of feudal legal structures, by the cultural dominance of Latin Christianity, or by technological and demographic stagnation relative to adjacent eras? (This forces a choice among institutional, cultural, and quantitative definitions, and exposes whether a respondent treats the period as overdetermined or as a single-cause fiction.)

4. Cross-cultural applicability: Do these three terms apply, even approximately, to non-Latin-Christian civilizations (e.g., to Islamic, Chinese, or South Asian histories with appropriate translation), or are they incommensurable with any periodization outside Europe?

5. Teleological load: Does calling an era a “Renaissance” or “Scientific Revolution” necessarily carry a normative judgment of recovery or progress, or can the term be used purely descriptively?

Map every combination of answers onto a named leaf position. The leaves must include at least: (a) strong universal essentialism (periods are discovered natural kinds with cross-cultural analogues); (b) weak regional essentialism (periods are real but bounded to Latin Europe and defined by specific institutions); (c) Eurocentric constructivism (the terms are useful pedagogical fictions for Europe but should not travel); (d) global comparative constructivism (all such tri-partite schemes impose false unity and should be abandoned); (e) deflationary continuity (gradual change everywhere, no breaks worthy of capitalized names); (f) pragmatic pluralism (the terms are purpose-relative tools—valid for some inquiries, invalid for others, with no single ontology).

Then, identify the unoccupied combinations in the logical space—e.g., a view that asserts universal essentialism for the Middle Ages and Renaissance but denies that any Scientific Revolution occurred, or one that treats periodization as purely teleological yet insists on strict cross-cultural applicability. For each empty cell, briefly diagnose whether it is likely incoherent (the commitments contradict), unattractive (no one holds it for obvious reasons), or merely unexplored.

Finally, flag any pairs of leaves that the tree cannot separate with these five questions—such as weak regional essentialism and pragmatic pluralism if both accept Europe-bound institutional definitions but differ only on whether they “believe in” the ontology—and explain what further diagnostic case would be needed to split them. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not reference specific scholars by name unless necessary for the taxonomy’s structure, and if you do, avoid attributing direct quotations. Keep the instrument self-contained: a reader should be able to answer every branch and read her result without consulting external sources.
