Design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level epistemology seminar of twelve students who have basic familiarity with reliabilism but are not expected to have memorized specialized literature. The objective is to make participants feel, through the rules and incentives rather than through prior explanation, the specific pull of the bootstrapping problem together with the phenomenon of easy knowledge: the way it can seem entirely rational to establish the reliability of a belief-forming method using only outputs that the method itself generates, and the corresponding circularity that makes that justification feel both compelling and suspect.

Center the simulation on an invented procedural constraint you should name and define precisely: the Reflexive Certification Rule (RCR). Under the RCR, a student may claim that her assigned reasoning protocol is reliable only if she can support that claim with evidence produced by a different protocol; however, every protocol in the room is assigned to exactly one student, initially uncertified, and the only available evidence comes from applying these protocols to a shared deck of eight puzzle-cards that the facilitator controls and reveals round by round. Because students have no access to an external answer key or gold-standard verification until the final minutes, the group must either grant one protocol an ungrounded initial "seed" certification and allow it to cascade through cross-certification, or remain in collective epistemic suspension. To give the choice real weight, assign each student a hidden "warrant token" budget: students accumulate tokens by being included in the largest certified network at the end, and these tokens determine speaking order and a small symbolic privilege in the final discussion (no monetary stakes). This incentivizes accepting the seed and participating in the cascade, even as participants notice the circular structure.

Structure the session in three timed phases with explicit rules and minimal materials. Phase 1 (15 minutes): distribute the protocol cards (each describing a distinct reasoning rule—e.g., analogical mapping, modal intuition, probabilistic update, consistency-checking—without labeling them as reliable or unreliable), run the eight puzzles individually, and have students record private confidence ratings on a hidden sheet. Phase 2 (30 minutes): open deliberation under the RCR. Students negotiate which protocol receives the seed, issue cross-certifications using shared puzzle results, trade warrant tokens, and attempt to build a maximally inclusive certified network. The facilitator should reveal puzzle outcomes one card at a time, never confirming ground truth, so that agreement between protocols is the only visible currency. Phase 3 (20 minutes): the facilitator reveals the hidden answer key, showing which protocols actually tracked correct answers, then conducts a structured debrief.

The debrief must map specific moments of the activity onto parts of the philosophical problem: the arbitrary selection of the seed as the ungrounded assumption of initial reliability; the rapid cascade of cross-certifications as the generation of easy knowledge without independent check; the token economy as the social pressure to accept circular warrant; and the final revelation of the answer key as the missing Archimedean point that was absent during deliberation. The debrief should also ask students to identify the exact point at which they felt the justification shift from "this works" to "this assumes what it needs to prove."

Additionally, specify what will predictably go wrong on the first run—most likely, one student will refuse the seed on principle and stall the negotiation, or the group will collapse into interpersonal alliance-building rather than tracking the epistemic structure—and provide a concrete recovery strategy (e.g., the facilitator introduces a "time-pressure" rule that forces a vote after ten minutes, preserving the simulation while preventing paralysis). Finally, include an honest limitation: the activity can reproduce the structural circularity of bootstrapping and the social dynamics of accepting easy knowledge, but it cannot reproduce the first-person phenomenology of knowing one's own cognitive faculties from the inside. The facilitator must supply that phenomenological dimension explicitly in the debrief, asking students to compare their felt confidence in Phase 1 with the structural circularity they discovered in Phase 2.

Write the prompt in formal, polished prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not place any philosopher's specific formulation in quotation marks unless you have fetched the exact text; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure the entire design is executable by a model without web access, relying only on general knowledge of reliabilism, circular justification, and the bootstrapping literature.
