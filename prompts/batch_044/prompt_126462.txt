I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether the real intellectual payoff of history from below lies in microhistorical recovery of individual subaltern lives or in restructuring large-scale narratives of state formation. We kept returning to the same tension: microhistory offers thick description but resists aggregation, whereas collective history from below risks reimposing the very elite categories of analysis it claims to escape. Given this, I want to know where a small interdisciplinary group of historians and philosophers of history, with about three years and modest resources, should actually direct its effort. Which open questions, methodological tensions, or lines of work in this field should be treated as upstream of the others? Please do not offer a bibliography or a list of readings; I want a defended prioritisation. Specifically, consider four candidate priorities: the methodological problem of scaling microhistorical evidence without destroying its specificity; the archival problem of how state-generated records systematically distort subaltern agency; the conceptual problem of whether class experience can survive a rigorous turn to individual case study; and the normative problem of what obligations historians have to the dead when representation is always mediated through elite institutions. Rank them, defend the ordering with explicit criteria — tractability for researchers without extensive specialist archives, dependency relations between questions, and what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking — and be direct about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or finding would change your mind. Please answer in British English.
