I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about why promises bind. She maintains that once reliance, foreseeable harm, and social convention are subtracted, nothing normative remains, leaving promises as mere coordinating fictions. I want to test whether there is a genuine normative residue, but the isolation must be rigorous rather than stipulative. Could you design a realistic thought experiment—no longer than a paragraph, with no science fiction, demons, or perfect predictors—in which a promisor makes an explicit, feasible commitment to perform a specific action for a promisee who is permanently unaware of the utterance (so reliance is impossible), within a community that lacks any institutional practice of treating such private commitments as binding (so convention is absent), and where failure to perform produces no harm, disappointment, or reputational damage whatsoever? Then show: (i) why both a reliance-based account and a social-practice account deny any obligation exists here; (ii) why a normative-powers account nevertheless holds that the promisor has genuinely altered her own normative position and owes performance; (iii) the precise structural feature that allows the normative-powers view to generate an obligation despite those absences; (iv) what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and (v) how a defender of the reliance view would attack the scenario as illegitimately rigged.
