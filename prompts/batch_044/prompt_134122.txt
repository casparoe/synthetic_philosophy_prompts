I'm drafting a novel about a neuroscientist whose husband dies, and she starts noticing his phrases and reflexes running inside her like leftover code—I'm obsessed with whether an 'I' can actually be spread across brains, the kind of thing Hofstadter wrote about with his wife's persistance in other people. I'm trying to sort out my intuitons on cases.

If my spouse dies but I keep finishing her sentences, using her idioms, even feeling her disgust at things I used to like—I'd say some fragment of her I genuinely persists in me, not just memory but an active pattern. Verdict: partial distribution is real.

If we had a direct brain-to-brain link and she could borrow my working memory to solve puzzles, I'd call that a temporary shared self; when the link breaks we're separate I's again. Verdict: distribution can be transient but genuine.

Take a split-brain patient where the left hand reaches for tea and the right for coffee. I feel like there are two I's competing, not one I spread thin across hemispheres. Verdict: division fragments the self, doesn't distribute it.

If I upload my full connectome into a computer that chats exactly like me, I say that's a copy, not me persisting elsewhere. Verdict: simulation is not distribution.

But my oldest friend knows every move I make, predicts my jokes, sometimes finishes my thoughts—still, I don't think she contains part of my I. Verdict: deep familiarity is not distribution.

Then there's conjoined twins with a shared thalamic bridge who report feeling like 'we' rather than 'I'. Verdict: here the self really is distributed across two brains.

The pair that breaks me: why is my dead spouse inside me but my best friend isn't, when both are just patterns running in my head? And why is the brain-link partner a shared self, but the uploaded copy isn't?

Can you find one principle that captures all six, or do I have to drop one? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed—say, a novelist who writes a character so vividly that readers later say he feels like part of them? And be honest about which of my verdicts won't survive.
