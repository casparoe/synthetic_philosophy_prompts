In debates about the authority of religious texts in empirical disputes, I frequently encounter the maxim that Scripture teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go, presented as Galileo’s definitive separation of theology from natural philosophy. Rather than treating it as a settled principle, I want its possible precise formulations laid out as a menu. For each reading, clarify whether it is a claim about the intended audience of revelation, about the literal versus accommodated meaning of cosmological language in biblical texts, about the conditions under which demonstrated science should override interpretation, or about the institutional boundary between ecclesiastical and philosophical authority. Indicate which readings are trivial, which correspond to positions Galileo actually defended—particularly his view that Scripture is never false but often employs metaphor calibrated to common understanding—and which are anachronistic modern projections. Specifically, distinguish the interpretation that Scripture is silent on cosmology from the interpretation that it addresses cosmology only in accommodated form, and evaluate whether either supports the conclusion that religious texts have no standing in scientific controversies. Finally, consider a less discussed extension: if the underlying principle is that revelation adapts its content to human cognitive capacity, might one analogously claim that scientific theories are also cognitive accommodations rather than literal descriptions of reality? Assess whether that extension is a legitimate development of Galileo’s hermeneutics or a category mistake that conflates theological accommodation with epistemic humility in science.
