I was talking with my friend last night after a few beers about whether beliefs are actually real or just a story we tell ourselves. I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class, so I'm definitely mangling the terminology, but I tried to write out the argument we came up with on my phone. (1) Folk psychology treats beliefs and desires like discreet causal variables that predict what people do. (2) But modern predictive-processing neuroscience explains behavior through hierarchical Bayesian inference and precision-weighted prediction errors — there's no discrete "belief box" in the brain, just continuous neural dynamics. (3) So beliefs and desires don't map onto any actual mechansm in the brain. (4) If a mental kind fails to map onto an actual mechanism, then it should be eliminated from our ontology, same reason we dropped phlogiston. (5) Therefore beliefs and desires don't exist. (6) But then the scientists who build these predictive models don't actually believe them, and I don't actually believe my server is down when I check the logs. (7) So the argument seems to prove way too much — it wipes out the very mental states needed to do science, or even to debug code.

Which step should I reject? I feel like (4) is doing the heavy lifting but I'm not sure what's wrong with it, and I'd like to know why it looked okay and what the standard positions — the eliminativists themselves, the functionalists who think mental states are just roles, and the folks who treat beliefs as relations to propositions — each have to pay if they deny that premise.
