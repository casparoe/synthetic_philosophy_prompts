Arendt's thesis on the banality of evil is usually summarized as the claim that ordinary people commit extreme harm without monstrous motives, but that headline conceals at least three live fault lines that determine what we should do with her account today. First, the interpretive fault line: does banality name an individual psychological deficit—what Arendt called Gedankenlosigkeit, or a failure of imaginative and critical thought—or does it name a structural feature of modern administrative systems, in which role-distance and procedural obedience replace personal initiative? Depending on the resolution, Arendt becomes either a moral psychologist or a sociologist of bureaucracy, and the preventive strategies diverge sharply. Second, the normative fault line: Arendt insisted that Eichmann deserved execution despite his apparent lack of deep malice or ideological conviction. If evil is genuinely banal in the psychological sense, what grounds such severe punishment? Is she implicitly relying on a Kantian commitment to treating agents as responsible regardless of inner depth, or is she carving out an exception for crimes that assault the human status as such? Third, the applied fault line: if thoughtlessness rather than hatred is the root vice, then prevention should cultivate judgment. But Arendt's later work points to two different models—one drawn from Kant's reflective judgment, emphasizing the capacity to think from others' standpoints, and another drawn from her critique of modernity's erosion of shared public spaces. These models suggest different institutional designs and do not obviously converge.
