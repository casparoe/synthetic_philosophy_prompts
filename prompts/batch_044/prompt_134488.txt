The idea starts from Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the claim that a lot of what gives our lives meaning depends on a tacit background belief that humanity will continue after we die and that our efforts will be taken up by others. So (1) if extinction wipes out that expectation, it doesn't just kill people; it collapses the shared framework that makes present projects feel like they matter. (2) That means the harm of extinction is structural, not just additive — it's like cutting the root that feeds every branch rather than just trimming one limb. (3) But if my actions really derive value from this future continuation, then the value should scale with how robust that continuation is: a future with ten billion people across a hundred generations should make my life now more meaningful than a future with just one generation. (4) So anyone who buys the conjecture seriously should want to maximize the size and length of the human future, even at big present cost. (5) That sounds like the repugnant concluson, only about meaning instead of welfare, which Scheffler obviously doesn't endorse. So where does it break? Is the mistake in (3), where I slide from a qualitative dependancy into a quantitative one? Or is (2) wrong to treat the expectation as part of the value rather than just a psychological precondition?
