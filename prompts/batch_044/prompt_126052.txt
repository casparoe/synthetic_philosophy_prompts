I'm working through an argument for a philosophy-of-science reading group that applies standard inverse reinforcement learning reasoning to ordinary human preference attribution, and I'm stuck because the conclusion seems to dissolve the very project it's meant to support. The argument runs as follows. (1) In any IRL framework, we recover a reward function R only relative to a fixed model of the agent's beliefs about state transitions and a rationality parameter that fixes how optimally the agent pursues R. (2) For any candidate pair (R, B) that explains an observed behavioral policy π, there exists a mirror pair (R*, B*) in which R* reverses the ordinal ranking of outcomes relative to R, and B* reverses the corresponding beliefs about which actions produce which outcomes, such that π remains exactly optimal for R* under B*. (3) Because observation gives us access only to π — not to B or to R independently — the behavioral data underdetermine the choice between (R, B) and (R*, B*); they belong to the same equivalence class. (4) Consequently, if IRL is our method for attributing preferences to humans, then for every preference we think we've recovered, there is an observationally equivalent but diametrically opposite preference that is equally well supported. (5) Preference attribution therefore requires a non-empirical principle to break the symmetry, but any such principle — for instance, a simplicity prior that favors R over R* — seems to smuggle in the very preferences it claims to discover. (6) Without such a principle, IRL never justifies attributing a specific preference over its mirror. (7) Yet we do make robust, intersubjectively stable preference attributions in ordinary life. (8) So either our ordinary attributions are systematically unjustified, or IRL is not the correct framework for grounding them. (9) If IRL fails for human minds, then the alignment strategy of inferring human values from behavior collapses into circularity, since we would have no independent criterion for selecting among observationally equivalent reward functions.

My study-group friend says this proves too much: it would imply that we cannot know whether someone prefers comfort to discomfort, or that AI alignment is impossible in principle. I feel like something slips between the underdetermination claim in (3) and the impossibility claim in (6), perhaps involving whether B* is actually available given the rest of the agent's mental economy, but I can't name the exact step. Which premise should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (2) — the formal possibility of the mirror construction — and (7) — the reality of ordinary preference attribution — if I can. What does giving up each of the other steps cost, and is there a subtle scope shift or quantifier error hiding in the move from "we can't distinguish them observationally" to "there's no fact of the matter"?
