I am running a postgraduate seminar in moral philosophy with eight participants, lasting seventy-five minutes, and I wish to design an in-class simulation that allows them to experience the structural paradox of deontology rather than merely analyse it discursively. Specifically, I am interested in the form of the paradox that emerges when an agent-relative prohibition against a particular direct wrong — coercing an individual into productive labour without consent — is upheld rigorously at every local node of a system, yet the systemic redirection of that refusal produces, in aggregate, a far greater number of precisely that wrong being committed by other agents elsewhere. The participants should enter the room with ordinary deontological intuitions intact, then find themselves, through the mechanics of the game, compelled to choose between maintaining the prohibition in their own assigned domain or breaching it locally to prevent a larger cascade of identical breaches across the collective.

Please construct a runnable activity with clearly defined roles, interaction rules, and a non-monetary payoff scheme — for instance, cumulative tokens representing either moral residue or reputational standing — together with the physical or printed materials required. Provide a precise timeline allocating phases within the seventy-five minutes, and ensure that the rules and incentives are arranged so that participants encounter the tension themselves before it is named. Following the description of the simulation, include a debrief that maps distinct gameplay moments onto the conceptual architecture of the paradox: the initial intuition of absolute constraint, the systemic revelation of distributed harm, the attempted distinction between doing and allowing, and the persistence of the agent-relative reason even when universal adherence generates the very state of affairs it forbids. Be explicit about what feature of the paradox this activity cannot genuinely reproduce — whether the irreducibly first-personal character of agent-relative reasons, or the normative weight of moral remainder — and explain how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally.

Finally, note what is likely to fail during the first running — for example, participants collapsing the structural problem into a simple utilitarian calculus, or the debrief arriving too early — and suggest concrete adjustments. The activity should not ask participants for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should depend only on standard deontological vocabulary rather than highly specialised named theories.
