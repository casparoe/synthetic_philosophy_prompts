I was discussing John Stuart Mill with two friends over dinner, and one of them insisted that Mill had become a socialist in his later years, while the other said he remained a liberal through and through. I do not have much formal philosophy background, so I could not tell who was right, and I am writing this out exactly as they argued.

My first friend pointed out that in Mill's later economic essays he advocated replacing the wage-labor system with voluntary worker cooperatives that would compete in open markets, predicted that this cooperative form was the likely future of industry, and treated the existing capital-labor relation as a kind of moral bondage that ought to be transcended. She said this shows he had abandoned the private-property defense in his earlier work and had embraced at least a distributive socialism, even if he disliked state bureaucracy.

My other friend replied that Mill never called for state ownership or central planning, that he retained competitive markets, legal equality, and freedom of contract, and that he explicitly criticized state socialism in the same writings. He argued that worker-owned firms operating within a market framework are just an improved form of capitalism—something like widespread employee ownership—not a rejection of liberal economic institutions, and that labeling Mill a socialist ignores his consistent commitment to individual liberty.

She then answered that the property relation itself is what defines socialism versus capitalism: if laborers own the capital they work with and the wage system is abolished, then the capitalist class has been eliminated, regardless of whether prices and competition remain. She added that Mill's position anticipates what later writers called market or liberal socialism—a hybrid that keeps exchange but transforms production relations—and that reducing Mill to "just a liberal" erases that distinctive middle ground.

He conceded that Mill occupied a middle position but insisted the label still matters. He said Mill's cooperative scheme was meant to evolve gradually through education and moral improvement, not through revolutionary rupture, and that this evolutionary, experimental method belongs to liberalism rather than socialism. He also noted that Mill kept private property in other spheres and never proposed collective control of all industry, so his system lacks the defining feature of socialism.

She came back one more time and said he was treating "socialism" as a single rigid doctrine, when in fact Mill was selectively adopting its best insights—cooperative production, labor dignity—while rejecting its worst—state coercion, abolition of markets. She argued that this selective adoption is exactly what makes him a socialist of a particular kind, not a liberal who happened to like cooperatives.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right? Please address specifically whether Mill's cooperative ideal counts as socialism, whether retaining markets and voluntary association saves him for liberalism, and whether his gradualist method undermines the socialist reading. Also, tell me what important consideration both of them are missing—perhaps about how his late economic views connect to his earlier idea of a stationary state, or about whether his sympathy for labor was primarily about dignity and autonomy rather than about restructuring property as such—and say who is more right overall.
