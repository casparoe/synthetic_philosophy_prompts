(a) Outline three dominant explanatory frameworks:
(i) preference amplification through reduced economic constraint;
(ii) structural relaxation and institutional path-dependency;
(iii) categorisation and measurement heterogeneity across cultural contexts.

(b) Evaluate the following inference: “If stronger gender-equality policies do not eliminate gendered career clustering, and may even accentuate it, then gender differences in occupational preference are predominantly biologically fixed and largely immune to cultural intervention.” In your evaluation, pay particular attention to interaction effects between institutional design and socialisation, and to the distinction between revealing a preference and determining its origin.

Student answer:
(a)(i) Preference amplification occurs when generous welfare provisions remove economic pressure, allowing people to follow their innate inclinations. In egalitarian societies women are free to choose care-oriented work because biological predispositions draw them there, while men choose STEM. Thus the paradox demonstrates that biology is the dominant driver.

(a)(ii) Structural relaxation refers to policies such as long parental leave and widespread part-time provisions, which lower the career cost of entering feminised sectors. Once these sectors become gender-typed, institutional path-dependency reinforces the clustering over successive cohorts.

(a)(iii) Measurement heterogeneity means that egalitarian countries collect survey data with greater precision and gender-sensitive instruments, so gender differences appear larger because they are measured more accurately; in less egalitarian countries noisy or incomplete data obscures the true distribution.

(b) The inference is flawed because correlation does not prove causation. Nevertheless, preference amplification is the strongest framework because it survives policy change—if culture were the principal cause, then removing sexist barriers ought to have eliminated the differences. Since the differences persist, the residual must be biological. Interaction effects are minimal because institutions and biology operate largely independently: institutions either suppress or reveal biologically given preferences, but do not themselves shape those preferences. Therefore, while culture may influence surface behaviours, the deep structure of occupational choice remains biologically anchored.
