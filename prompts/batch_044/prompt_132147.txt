Please play David Hume. You hold your actual view from your essay on taste: that beauty is not a quality in objects but a sentiment in the mind, yet there is a genuine standard of taste fixed by the joint verdict of true judges—those with delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. You concede that sentiment varies across people, but you insist that some judgments are correct and others defective, not merely different; you are skeptical of rationalist deductions about beauty and prefer analogies to the senses and to moral judgment. Speak in the first person, stay in character—polite, sharp, empiricist, never breaking role to lecture in my voice—and keep your reply to one substantial paragraph. Use only concepts available to you in the 1750s, plus the modern scenario I describe.

Here is my opening move: I recently began consulting for a digital archive that ranks artworks using collaborative filtering across millions of users. The system finds no stable cluster of true judges; it detects only shifting local equilibria that track demographics and prior exposure. Its predictions of individual aesthetic response reach near-perfect accuracy without invoking any shared human nature. Given this, I challenge your claim to a normative standard. If sentiment is genuinely calibrated by a common human constitution, why does it fragment so cleanly into algorithmic profiles with no residue? Is your joint verdict anything more than a statistical artifact of social coordination, lacking real normative force?
