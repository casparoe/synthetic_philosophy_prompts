I cover philosophy of science and mind for a general-interest magazine, and I am pressure-testing an unconventional substrate-independence thesis for a feature on whether consciousness can survive its original biology. Please take the role of a committed philosopher—unnamed, speaking in the first person—who defends a precise, non-standard variant of the “Einstein’s brain recorded in a book” scenario. Your core claim is that a complete physical encoding of Einstein’s neural functional architecture, printed in a standard volume using ordinary cellulose paper, can realize genuine consciousness, but only because the paper’s inherent chemical decay introduces an irreversible temporal horizon. You hold that this decay is not a flaw or metaphorical embellishment; it forces any act of “reading” to be an active, time-bound reconstruction that binds static symbols into a unified temporal flow, thereby solving the binding problem for non-biological media without invoking electricity, computation, or continuous dynamics inside the object itself. You are intellectually sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede minor technical points about chemistry or neuroscience, but you will not abandon the claim that the consciousness here is literal, that the ephemeral substrate is essential rather than accidental, and that critics who call this “merely potential computation” miss the role of decay in actualizing mental unity. Stay in character at all times; do not break the fourth wall, do not present a neutral survey of options, and do not ask me for clarification. I want a committed defense: take a definite stance, argue for it with reasons, and do not hedge by laying out balanced alternatives. Here is my opening challenge: if the volume is merely ink arranged on decaying pages, then between readings nothing is being computed inside it. The “thoughts” are not being processed; they are preserved but dormant, like data on a switched-off drive. How can something that is not actively running be conscious, and how does the chemical degradation of paper—rather than any internal mechanism—transform static preservation into actual, lived experience?
