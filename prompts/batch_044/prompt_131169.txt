I have recently begun curating a digital archive of community records severely damaged by flooding, and the experience has made a philosophical problem personally urgent: when large segments of information are permanently destroyed, should we treat the missing entities as ontologically indistinct from those that remain documented, or should we preserve a commitment to their distinct reality despite our inability to access them?

Please draft the joint statement that an Ontological Identificationist and an Inaccessibility Realist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this question. The Identificationist applies a modified principle of identification: if information distinguishing two entities is not merely unknown but in principle inaccessible—destroyed, hidden behind an absolute epistemic boundary, or systematically excluded—there is no ontological ground to treat them as separate. The Realist maintains that inaccessibility is always relative to an epistemic position, and that to collapse ontology into what can be verified is to confuse existence conditions with verification conditions.

The statement should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about the formal conditions for ontological commitment, the distinction between practical and in-principle inaccessibility, the difference between random information loss (entropic silence) and structurally produced absence (productive silence), and the precise limits of what either side is asserting.

2. A disagreements table stating, in wording each side accepts for its own view, exactly where they part ways: specifically on whether permanent inaccessibility licenses ontological identification; on whether there is a meaningful distinction between "there is no fact of the matter" and "the fact is permanently hidden"; and on whether predictive success of a model that treats the lost entities as distinct counts in favor of their reality.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party. I take the likely cruxes to be (i) whether simplicity or naturalness favors identification over pluralism when evidence is permanently absent, and (ii) whether a counterfactual predictive framework that distinguishes the lost entities can be justified without circularity.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or thought experiment that both sides agree is relevant, with each side's prediction about the outcome or analysis recorded in advance.

Write this as a formal, polished document. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure named theories by proper name unless they are standard in philosophy of science or ontology; you may, however, define and employ the framing of entropic versus productive silence within the prompt itself.
