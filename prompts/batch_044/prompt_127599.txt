I am delivering the opening address at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Fixed Points and Variations," hosted by a design school and attended by a deliberately mixed audience: half are practicing generative artists and architects, and half are cognitive scientists who study conceptual spaces and analogy. The framing idea—drawn from Douglas Hofstadter's well-known model that creativity is best understood not as ex nihilo invention but as disciplined knob-twiddling on an underlying theme, systematically varying parameters while some core structure remains intact—must do real structural work in the speech. Because this framework treats themes as always already present and originality as residing in the choice of which variables to alter, I cannot praise any participant for having discovered a "purely original" subject matter; instead, the address must celebrate the rigor with which each project selects its knobs and, more importantly, freezes its hidden invariants. To avoid generic inspiration, I want you to build the address around a specific pedagogical scenario I have invented for this occasion, called "The Frozen Parameter": teams are given an identical base object—a simple modular chair—and are permitted to alter every dimensional, material, and functional variable except one arbitrarily assigned constraint (for one team, the seat height is locked at exactly 43 centimeters; for another, the object must remain collapsible with precisely three hinges). The speech should use this scenario to argue that the constraint is not an obstacle to creativity but its necessary condition, and then apply that logic directly to the workshop's actual projects: for each presenter's work, identify the unstated invariant they have unknowingly preserved and show how that preservation makes their variations legible rather than arbitrary.

Please adopt the voice of an opening speaker who is also a practicing philosopher of design, think carefully about how the argument lands differently for the artists and the scientists, and take a definite, committed position rather than surveying options: either defend the thesis that every project in the room succeeds precisely to the degree that it makes its frozen parameter explicit and visible, or defend the opposing thesis that genuine breakthroughs occur only when a practitioner violates, dissolves, or transcends the invariant with which they began—but do not hedge between them. Give full reasons, ground the philosophical claim in the concrete details of the chair exercise, and include a brief self-critical note at the end identifying where your argument leans on a contested reading of Hofstadter's conceptual-space model or takes liberties with how cognitive scientists actually treat parameter variation. The tone should be formal and polished, suitable for a public academic occasion, and should never drift into a lecture that loses the non-specialist half of the room.
