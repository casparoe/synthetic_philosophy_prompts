I am auditing a graduate seminar on scientific reasoning, and a disagreement between two students has stayed with me because it touches exactly on the boundary between formal confirmation theory and the psychology of model-building. They were debating whether old evidence can ever genuinely confirm a newly proposed scientific theory within a Bayesian framework, using a concrete hypothetical case. The case involves a 1998 ocean-current anomaly that had remained unexplained under standard climate circulation models; in 2023, a researcher proposes model C, which was explicitly developed to account for that 1998 pattern, yet derives the pattern from a general hydrodynamic principle rather than from an ad hoc parameter adjustment. The two students—let me call them Maya and Jonas—disagreed sharply about what Bayesians should say.

Maya began by arguing that old evidence cannot confirm C, because once a theory is constructed with knowledge of E, the likelihood P(E|C) is essentially 1, and the competing prior theories also accommodate E with near-certainty by adding auxiliary hypotheses, so the Bayes factor is approximately 1; confirmation requires predictive novelty. Jonas replied that this ignores the distribution over competing theories: if C assigns a precise, structurally constrained range to the 1998 measurements that older models treat as outliers requiring separate auxiliary assumptions, then P(E|C) can still exceed P(E|~C), yielding a genuine Bayes factor in favor of C even though C was designed with E in mind. Maya countered that Jonas misses the gerrymandering penalty—the prior P(C) should be discounted precisely because its parameter space was shaped to capture E, and that without penalizing this post-hoc tuning, any failed theory can be revived by retrofitting. Jonas responded that this penalty applies only to ad hoc parameter adjustments, not to theories where E falls out of independently motivated principles; if C's derivation of the 1998 pattern uses no free parameters tuned to that data, its prior should not be deflated, and the old evidence genuinely supports it relative to bloated alternatives. Maya then pressed that in practice this distinction is untenable—every post-hoc theorist claims their adjustments are principled—and without an objective metric for independently motivated, Jonas's defense collapses into special pleading.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. Rate each of the five steps—Maya's initial Bayes-factor claim, Jonas's response about precise structural constraints, Maya's gerrymandering-penalty objection, Jonas's ad-hoc-versus-principled distinction, and Maya's final skeptical challenge—on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—perhaps something about how partitioned priors or model-selection criteria interact with old-evidence reasoning—that would resolve or deepen their standoff.
