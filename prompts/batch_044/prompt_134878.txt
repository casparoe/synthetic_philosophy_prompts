Consider G. E. Moore's ethical commitments—especially his account of intrinsic value as non-natural, his doctrine of organic unities, and his claim that friendship and aesthetic appreciation rank among the highest goods. Now imagine a development well after his lifetime: recommender systems that pair users into sustained aesthetic partnerships, optimizing not for shared contemplation of beautiful objects but for mutual engagement metrics, so that the participants come to value the interaction mainly because the algorithm reinforces it. Would Moore have regarded such algorithmically mediated bonds as genuine friendship, or as its corruption? And would he have judged the curated aesthetic experience within them to retain intrinsic value, or to be reduced to instrumental value? Apply his actual distinctions—means versus ends, organic unity, and the non-naturalness of good—to this arrangement, and defend which of his commitments does the heaviest work in the judgment.
