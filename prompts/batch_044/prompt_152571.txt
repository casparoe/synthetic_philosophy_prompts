I was talking with two friends after we listened to a podcast episode about self-knowledge, and they got into a long argument about two ideas mentioned in passing: something called "luminosity," which they described as the claim that if you are genuinely in a mental state like being awake or feeling pain, you automatically know you're in it, and the "KK principle," which they said means that if you know something is true, you also know that you know it. I followed the gist but not the details, and they asked me to ask someone more careful to referee them.

Marcus started by saying luminosity is clearly false in everyday life: people doze off on the couch without noticing, or only realize hours later that they've been anxious or irritable. Elena answered that Marcus is confusing being in a state with being in a borderline or transitional version of it; if you're fully and clearly awake, you do know it, and the hard cases happen exactly at the edges where it's unclear whether the state is present at all. Marcus then pushed on those edges: he asked whether there's a sharp cutoff between awake and asleep, or whether it's all gradation. Elena said there almost certainly is a sharp neural cutoff, even if no one can say precisely where it falls — so our failure to know whether we're awake in a twilight moment is like not knowing the exact millimeter that divides tall from not-tall.

Then Marcus turned to the KK principle. He said that if Elena grants we know we're awake in clear cases, then by KK we must know that we know, and then know that we know that we know, which seems to demand an impossible infinite ladder of self-checking that nobody actually climbs. Elena replied that this misreads the principle: it describes a nested structure of knowledge, not a psychological task list; you don't have to open every box to have them nested inside each other. Marcus wasn't convinced. He proposed a specific twist: suppose you wear a brain-monitor that displays "STATE: AWAKE" on a screen, giving you second-order knowledge through an external device. Even then, he argued, you'd need another monitor to verify the first one, and then another for that one, so the regress returns in physical form. Elena said Marcus is smuggling external verification into a claim about internal accessibility, and that knowing you know doesn't require a gadget check — it's part of the conceptual structure of being in the clear state. But Marcus insisted that without some behavioral or external check, "knowing that you know" reduces to a feeling of confidence, which is exactly what fails when you're half-asleep and feel sure you're awake.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step — Marcus's initial counterexample, Elena's defense using borderline states, the sharp-cutoff analogy, Marcus's infinite-regress objection, Elena's nested-box reply, Marcus's brain-monitor twist, and Elena's final distinction — and say who's right about each specific point. Also, tell me whether there's something important both are missing about how luminosity and the KK principle actually interact when a mental state isn't sharply present or absent but genuinely transitional.
