I'm trying to work out what I actually think about reparative justice, going by my gut on cases. I keep coming back to the same five, and they don't line up neatly.

Case A: The UK government pays direct cash to descendants of people enslaved in the Caribbean, funded through general taxation. Even poor taxpayers whose families never benefitted chip in. Verdict: broadly okay, maybe a bit rough on the taxpayers but acceptable.

Case B: A university gives admission preferences to descendants of people barred by racist quotas in the 1920s, but not to recent immigrants facing racism now. Verdict: feels correct — it's repairing a specific institutional wrong.

Case C: A modern bank that merged with a slave-trading firm pays into a community development centre for affected regions, but there's no individual payout. Verdict: fine, but somehow less just than direct compensation; it feels like they're buying moral favour.

Case D: A court orders the return of farmland taken 140 years ago to direct lineal descendants, displacing current owners who bought it legally decades ago. Verdict: not okay — the current owners are innocent, and the harm is now independant of current actions.

Case E: A wealthy nation pays reparations for colonial extraction to the modern government of the ex-colony, knowing the money will likely be captured by elites rather than reach descendants. Verdict: not okay; it feels like performing justice without actually delivering it.

There's also this idea I keep circling: maybe obligations have a kind of 'structural half-life' — they decay across generations unless the original harm is continuously reinforced by present institutions. So direct payments to descendants feel right because the wealth gap is structurally maintained, but taking land from innocent current owners feels wrong because the causal chain is broken and the obligation has decayed. But then Case C confuses me, because the bank's obligation hasn't decayed even though it's indirect and there are no individual recipients.

Is there a coherent principle here — maybe involving direct vs indirect transmission, burden on innocent parties, and this 'half-life' notion — or am I being inconsistent and need to drop one verdict? What does it predict for a situation where a government offers free university tuition specifically to descendants of a displaced group, funded by a tax only on corporations that still hold the original stolen assets?
