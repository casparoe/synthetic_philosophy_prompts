As a novelist constructing a philosophical fiction, I am exploring a premise that adapts the central thought experiment from The Mind's I: imagine that Einstein's brain is preserved and sustained in a nutrient bath, capable of sustained conversation and apparent self-awareness, while, in parallel, a physical book—specifically a heavily annotated copy of Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica in which decades of a single reader's marginal notes have evolved into a syntactically closed, self-referential system—might also be said to possess conscious experience. Assuming for the sake of the narrative that the bound volume qualifies as conscious and the brain in the vat does not, what are the most important implications of this inversion? Please address the consequences for (1) whether consciousness requires a continuous biological boundary or can instead be distributed across inert pages and ink, (2) the status of intentionality when it arises from recursive notation rather than neural firing, and (3) how a novelist ought to render first-person interiority if the subject of experience is a bound volume rather than an embodied mind. I am not seeking citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need a rigorous conceptual analysis that could govern the internal logic of the plot.
