I've been thinking about a strange pattern in how museums and auction houses treat works whose authorship turns out to be wrong. When a painting is revealed to be an outright forgery—painted in modern times but sold as a seventeenth-century masterpiece—institutions typically pull it from display permanently, sometimes locking it away in storage with a fake label or even destroying it. But when scholars reassign a painting from Rembrandt himself to his workshop or to an unknown follower, the museum doesn't discard the canvas; they simply change the wall label to something like "Circle of Rembrandt" and leave it hanging. The physical object is exactly the same in both situations, and in both situations the earlier belief about who made it was false. Yet the institutional response could not be more different, and I can't shake the sense that this reveals something deep about what we think artistic value actually is.

So I'm wondering: what philosophical theory of value does this practice quietly presuppose? Does it commit us to the idea that a painting's worth depends essentially on its causal history—on being genuinely produced by a particular artist's hand—rather than on what we can actually see? And if that causal chain matters so much for visual art, why doesn't it matter in the same way for music or literature, where a perfect recording or a printed copy can carry the full aesthetic weight of the original? Could the difference be explained without any deep metaphysics—say, as a mere market rule against fraud—or does the fact that we preserve misattributed works but reject forgeries show that we think the "wrong" origin in a forgery introduces a kind of corruption that simple misattribution avoids?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind this institutional pattern, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether it's consistent with how we treat other cultural objects where copies are perfect substitutes. Then tell me what would have to change in museum and auction practice if we replaced that underlying view with one that treated artistic value as purely perceptual or experiential, with no special weight given to the history of production.
