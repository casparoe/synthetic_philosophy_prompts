I'm serving as the grader for the final examination in a graduate seminar on moral philosophy and public memory. The exam presented students with the following case and conceptual framework. A city maintains a bronze equestrian monument to a nineteenth-century colonial administrator, originally dedicated in 1923 with an inscription praising his civilizing mission. In 1985, municipal authorities added a counter-plaque documenting his administration's suppression of indigenous self-governance. Preservationists contend that the monument should remain because its pedagogical function—illuminating the ideology of empire—has now superseded its original honorific purpose. Critics reply that the statue's heroic scale, elevated plinth, and equestrian form continue to venerate the subject regardless of textual amendments, and that the preservationist reasoning exemplifies what our course texts call the functional substitution fallacy: the mistaken assumption that layering a critical interpretation onto a commemorative object fully neutralizes the residual honorific charge embedded in its material presence and spatial prominence. Drawing on the tripartite distinction among honorific, commemorative, and pedagogical functions of public monuments, students were asked to evaluate whether the counter-plaque resolves the ethical problem of retention and whether the preservationist defense indeed commits this fallacy.

One student submitted the following answer. The student correctly distinguishes the three functional categories and notes that the 1923 inscription and 1985 counter-plaque pull the monument in opposing directions. They observe that preservationists treat the pedagogical layer as dispositive and argue that this is precisely where the functional substitution fallacy operates—namely, in treating interpretive context as sufficient to cancel formal veneration. The student further claims that because commemorative marking is always conceptually prior to honorific evaluation, the monument can be retained in a purely commemorative mode once the critical plaque is present. From this, they conclude that the preservationists are actually correct: the fallacy only applies when critics deny any pedagogical value, whereas here the pedagogical value is explicit, so retention is ethically permissible. They also note that relocating the statue to a museum would diminish its pedagogical reach, though they do not address whether spatial prominence necessarily amplifies honorific signaling.
