As a retired biochemist recently turned to philosophy of biology, I have been following the debate over whether DNA is literally a code. A fellow member of my reading group summarized the position like this: DNA operates as a literal program in the same sense as software; its nucleic sequences carry semantic instructions—specifically, directives to synthesize particular proteins—that ribosomes decode according to conventional, chemically arbitrary rules. Because this mapping is not fixed by physics or chemistry alone, genetic information is semantically autonomous and causally independent of its molecular substrate; therefore, describing DNA as mere chemistry misses an ontologically distinct informational level. Something in this strikes me as overreaching—particularly the move from conventional mapping to full semantic autonomy—though I cannot precisely locate the error. Is this summary fair? Please examine it claim by claim: which parts are accurate descriptions of the informational view, which conflate syntactic organization with semantic content, and which overstate the independence of the genetic level from chemical and cellular context. I also want to know whether a careful defender of the literal-code thesis would accept the autonomy claim as formulated here, or whether they would insist on qualifications—say, that the code is causal-informational rather than linguistic—that the summary suppresses. Finally, does the conclusion that genetic information is ontologically independent actually follow, or does it rest on an equivocation between not chemically determined and not physically realized?
