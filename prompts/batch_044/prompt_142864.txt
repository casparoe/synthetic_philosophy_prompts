I'm writing about the architecture of family policy and have noticed a recurring structural pattern that I can't place precisely in the literature. Consider a universal paid parental leave program that requires twelve months of continuous full-time employment to qualify. The families that meet this threshold tend to be those with stable, high-earning jobs and access to private childcare; those with interrupted work histories—often due to prior caregiving—are screened out. Once the data come in, analysts compare outcomes between recipients and non-recipients and find that recipients show better employment continuity afterward. But the comparison is confounded not merely by selection: the eligibility rule itself manufactures the non-recipient pool out of the very population the policy was meant to stabilize. The result is a kind of institutional tautology—the policy appears to reward stability without creating it, and its failure to reach unstable families is then cited as proof that such families lie outside the labor-market framework altogether. Is there an established term for this specific feedback loop, where an eligibility gate constructed around labor-market attachment both excludes the vulnerable group and then uses that exclusion to justify the gate's design? If not, how should we distinguish it from ordinary selection bias, cream skimming, or the Matthew effect, and what conceptual work does the distinction do?
