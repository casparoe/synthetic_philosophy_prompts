In the manuscript I am presently revising, a central family maintains its estate through a legal instrument I have called a perpetual bloodline trust—an entity recognised by the state as the owner of land and capital, permitted to accumulate and distribute income across generations without ever being dissolved or fully taxed at death, provided the beneficiaries remain direct descendants. The same jurisdiction, however, operates an extensive system of need-blind university admissions and state-funded apprenticeship programmes, explicitly justified as correcting for the unequal opportunities created by birth. I find myself wondering what philosophical picture of the family, of property, and of equality must be in place for these two institutions to coexist without contradiction. What theory of value or justice does the pairing presuppose? Does it treat the family as a kind of corporate person that transcends individual lives and therefore merits continuity of capital, or does it treat inheritance taxation merely as a pragmatic revenue measure with no deep commitment to intergenerational fairness?

Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can. Is the underlying commitment that equality of opportunity concerns only the conditions at the start of adult life—access to education and employment—while leaving untouched the accumulated structural advantages that shape every subsequent choice? Or is there a more subtle metaphysics of desert at work, one in which the founder’s achievement justifies perpetual family privilege, but only if mediated through a legal fiction of continuity? I should like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and to say whether it is philosophically consistent or whether its parts depend on incompatible conceptions of the individual and the lineage. Finally, tell me what would have to change—not only in tax law, but in the conceptual framing of ownership and merit—if we replaced this implicit theory with one that regards dynastic wealth as an obstacle to equal opportunity at every stage of life, rather than merely at its beginning.
