I have been thinking on my own, without any formal academic background, about which entities genuinely possess conscious experience. A friend of mine—likewise self-taught, and with no professional training in biology or philosophy—holds a remarkably steady set of judgments on this question. She is firmly convinced that humans, pigs, ravens, and octopuses are conscious; that goldfish, individual honeybees, Venus flytraps (despite their well-known electrical signaling when touched), slime molds that navigate mazes, and chess-playing computers are not; and that a beehive reaching collective decisions, even when it solves problems that no individual bee can solve, is also not conscious.

Given that pattern, I would like your help predicting her verdicts on four cases she has not discussed with me: a nematode worm with exactly 302 neurons that can learn to avoid an odor; a laboratory-grown cluster of human neurons that produces synchronized gamma oscillations but has no body, sensory organs, or behavioral output; a box jellyfish possessing distributed nerve nets and image-forming eyes but lacking any centralized brain; and a present-day large language model engaged in sustained, real-time conversational interaction.

But I am less interested in the bare answers than in understanding the structure behind them. What principle, or competing principles, best account for her particular pattern—perhaps one tied to centralized neural architecture, another to flexible, non-reflexive learning, another to embodied sentience? For each plausible rule, what does it predict about those four cases? And if two or more rules genuinely align with all her past verdicts but diverge on one or more of these new ones, which specific comparison—between which pair of cases—would best reveal which rule she is actually running? I am asking for a diagnosis of her implicit framework and a clear indication of what evidence, conceptual or empirical, would settle which framework she holds.
