I am a doctoral student in social ontology working on whether demographic cohorts can constitute genuine kinds rather than mere statistical aggregates, and I am trying to fix my own intuitive judgments into a coherent principle. Here are five cases with my tentative verdicts.

Case 1: The U.S. “Baby Boomer” grouping, bounded by birth years 1946–1964 and marked by shared structural conditions—post-war economic expansion, suburbanization, and specific welfare-state configurations. I treat this as a real social kind: it supports counterfactual generalizations about wealth accumulation and political alignment that do not collapse into pure age or period effects.

Case 2: “Generation Z,” defined globally by birth years roughly 1997–2012 and characterized by digital nativity and climate anxiety. I think this is not a real kind, because the putative shared conditions are distributed so unevenly across the global South, rural North, and different national regulatory environments that the cohort fails to ground stable explanatory predictions.

Case 3: A purely marketing-driven cluster such as the “Pepsi Generation” of 1980s American adolescents, assembled for advertising segmentation without any independent causal structure. Clearly not a kind.

Case 4: A cohort defined not by birth year but by experiencing a macroeconomic shock during a specific life-stage—for instance, entering the labor market during the 2008–2009 crisis between ages 22 and 30, regardless of birth year. Here I am inclined to say there is a real kind, because the shock produces durable “scarring” effects on earnings trajectories that support predictions across otherwise demographically heterogeneous individuals.

Case 5: “Millennials” defined jointly by birth years (approximately 1981–1996) and a shared technological trajectory—coming of age with broadband social media. I am genuinely undecided: the technological exposure seems causally relevant, yet the boundaries blur with adjacent cohorts and with pure life-stage effects, so I am tempted to classify this as a hybrid—partly explanatory kind, partly artifact of measurement.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving counterfactual robustness, structural position, or what we might call inter-cohort causal closure—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which judgment should I revise? I am especially troubled by the tension between Case 2 and Case 4: both invoke conditions that cross-cut standard birth-year boundaries, yet I treat them differently. Please state the principle precisely, indicate which cases it accommodates, predict its verdict for a case I have not listed—say, adolescents in one nation who experienced a specific pandemic-lockdown schooling policy that neighboring countries with identical demographics avoided—and be explicit about which of my judgments it must reject if it is to remain simple rather than ad hoc.
