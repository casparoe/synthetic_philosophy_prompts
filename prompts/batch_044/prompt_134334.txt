Please construct a rigorous philosophical argument for the following position: the scale argument that vast numbers of insects generate overwhelming moral reasons against insecticide use fails because it applies an individualist aggregation framework to entities for which a principle of functional-replacement equivalence is more appropriate. Proceed in three parts. First, clarify the nature of epistemic uncertainty regarding insect sentience—specifically, distinguish uncertainty about whether a given taxon possesses phenomenal consciousness from metaphysical indeterminacy about whether sentience itself is graded—and explain why this distinction blocks a straightforward expected-value calculation summed uniformly across all insects. Second, show that if one applies uniform expected-value aggregation without regard to this distinction, one is committed to treating other high-abundance, low-confidence cases—such as nematodes or simple artificial neural networks—with equivalent moral urgency, yielding an implausible and indiscriminate expansion of moral concern. Third, defend functional-replacement equivalence—the view that for r-selected populations with high reproductive turnover and low individual survival probabilities, the proper object of moral evaluation is the population-level ecological role rather than the aggregate of individual welfare states, and argue that this functions as a genuine normative constraint on moral aggregation rather than a merely pragmatic convenience. After presenting the argument, articulate the strongest reply available to a defender of the scale argument and respond to it directly. Maintain a formal, academic tone suitable for an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or references to specific empirical studies; rely solely on conceptual reasoning and distinctions that form part of standard philosophical and biological literacy.
