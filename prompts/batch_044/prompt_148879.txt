I'm taking a graduate seminar on practical reason, and after our session on normative conflict two fellow students—call them Marcus and Lena—fell into a long disagreement about whether genuine moral dilemmas can actually exist. I stayed out of it, but they asked me to help them see exactly where the reasoning holds or breaks, so I'd like an outsider's adjudication of the exchange itself.

Marcus began by arguing that genuine moral dilemmas are conceptually impossible. His core move was an appeal to the principle that ought implies can: if an agent genuinely ought to do A and genuinely ought to do B, yet cannot do both, then at least one of those obligations cannot really be binding in that situation. Otherwise the agent would be necessarily culpable no matter what she chooses, which Marcus thinks collapses the very idea of responsible moral agency. Lena replied that this treats every obligation as an all-things-considered command, whereas obligations are typically pro tanto—standing reasons that hold unless overridden. A pro tanto duty to keep a promise can conflict with a pro tanto duty to rescue a stranger, and neither is automatically annulled; instead, the agent does what is best overall but is left with what Lena called a moral remainder, a lingering reason for apology or repair directed specifically at the unfulfilled duty.

Marcus pushed back that this so-called remainder is just psychological residue—guilt or regret misdescribed in normative language. He argued that if practical reason genuinely cannot rank two considerations because they are incommensurable, then they are not binding obligations but merely weighty reasons. A binding obligation, on his view, must be able to generate decisive action guidance; if weighing fails, talk of an unfulfilled obligation is only a way of dressing up emotional discomfort. Lena then offered a concrete case: a physician who promised a dying patient to remain at the bedside, but who is the only surgeon able to perform an emergency, life-saving operation. The values grounding the promise—fidelity, presence, trust—are not reducible to the value of saving a life, and vice versa. The physician cannot place them on a single scale, yet both obligations seem fully real.

Marcus conceded that values might be plural, but insisted this does not create a genuine dilemma. The physician should perform the surgery; the promise-obligation is overridden, not left dangling in conflict. The appropriate response afterward—explanation, apology—satisfies whatever remains of the promise, which proves the obligation was resolved, not left binding in opposition. The sadness is about the lost value, not about having violated a norm that still applies. Lena countered that if the promise-obligation were truly overridden, the physician's regret should be generic sadness about the tragic situation, not a specific self-directed regret that she herself broke her word. The fact that she owes the patient an apology specifically for breaking the promise, rather than merely expressing sorrow that circumstances were cruel, shows that the obligation was not canceled but genuinely unfulfilled, even though she acted correctly. Thus, practical reason does not resolve the conflict by ranking values on a hidden scale; it resolves it by choosing despite an unavoidable moral loss.

Please go through the exchange move by move: Marcus's initial impossibility claim, Lena's pro tanto reply, Marcus's psychological-residue objection, Lena's physician case, Marcus's override response, and Lena's reply about the specificity of regret. For each step, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, what both are missing—perhaps something about the distinction between normative conflict and logical contradiction, or about whether practical wisdom can operate without commensuration—and identify a further case or conceptual distinction that would actually settle the dispute between them.
