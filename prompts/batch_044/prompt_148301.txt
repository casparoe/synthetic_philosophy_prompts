Here is an argument I have been using to defend the view that environmental determinism, properly understood, does not erase human agency but rather exposes a confusion in how historians assign responsibility. I now worry it proves far more than I intend.

(1) Define an explanatory factor's "counterfactual stability weight" in a historical narrative as the degree to which perturbing that factor within physically possible bounds alters the macro-outcome, holding other variables constant.

(2) In the transformation of the Western Roman administrative system across the fifth and sixth centuries, perturbing climate regimes—the 536-event-scale volcanic winters, long-term Mediterranean aridification—and pathogen dynamics—the Antonine and Justinianic plagues—produces substantial macro-divergence, whereas perturbing the specific political decisions of individual senators, generals, or barbarian chieftains produces only local noise that washes out at the macro-scale.

(3) Therefore climate and disease ecology possess far greater counterfactual stability weight than human institutional agency in explaining Rome's transformation.

(4) If factor A has greater counterfactual stability weight than factor B in producing outcome O, then attributing O primarily to B rather than A is not merely empirically inaccurate but normatively misleading: it misallocates responsibility to agents with low causal leverage.

(5) Hence narratives that center Rome's "fall" on political corruption, military overextension, or barbarian invasion assign blame and explanatory priority to low-weight human actors, while ignoring the high-weight non-human actors that actually constrained the possibility space.

(6) This reasoning generalizes without restriction: for nearly any large-scale historical catastrophe—demographic collapse, state failure, economic transformation—non-human ecological and climatic factors will display higher counterfactual stability weight than any individual or collective human choice, because human choices are substitutable and distributed while macro-environmental regimes are not.

(7) So if we accept (4), we are committed to withdrawing moral and political accountability from almost all large-scale historical outcomes and reassigning it, implausibly, to climate systems, pathogens, and volcanic aerosol loading.

(8) But this collapses the distinction between explaining an event and holding someone answerable for it, which is precisely the distinction that makes environmental determinism perilous rather than liberating.

(9) Therefore either (1) is false—counterfactual stability weight is not the right metric for explanatory priority—or (4) is false—explanatory weight does not entail normative misallocation.

If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than (4), since the metric seems to capture something real about macro-historical explanation. But I want to know exactly which step contains the illicit slide, why it looked like it followed, and what I pay if I reject (4) rather than (1). Is there a hidden equivocation between causal weight and moral addressability, a scope shift between local and global counterfactuals, or is the error elsewhere?
