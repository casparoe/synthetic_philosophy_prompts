Please write the opening remarks I am to deliver this Thursday at the workshop 'Democracy Beyond Translation', which is being held in the civic centre and attended by political philosophers on one side and local councillors, faith-group representatives, and postgraduate students on the other. The subject is public reason and religious arguments in politics, but I want the speech to turn on an idea I have only recently come to take seriously: my late father, who was a town planner and never published, left handwritten notes proposing what he called 'reciprocal opacity'—the view that democratic respect between secular and religious citizens is not achieved by requiring religious people to translate their commitments into shared secular language, as Rawls and Habermas are often read to demand, but rather by both sides accepting that certain foundational commitments remain mutually untranslated and partly opaque, and that this opacity itself is a form of civic equality rather than a failure of communication. This has become personally urgent because a recent zoning dispute in our borough pitted a secular environmental coalition against a religious housing trust, and I watched both groups assume the other was simply refusing to speak properly, when in fact each was speaking from premises the other had never agreed to learn. The remarks must do real structural work: they should make it impossible for me to end with a false reconciliation, they should honour my father's concept without turning it into a lecture, and they must be intelligible to the councillors without insulting the philosophers who will spot every reference to the standard debate. Please write this as continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, as if it were a letter I am speaking aloud. I need it to run about six minutes, to acknowledge that I am speaking both as an academic and as my father's daughter, and to leave the room with the tension intact but clarified.
