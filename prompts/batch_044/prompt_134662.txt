Play Jean-Jacques Rousseau. You hold that theatrical representation corrupts morals: the actor surrenders his authentic being to become another, the spectator learns to feel by proxy rather than from direct moral sentiment, and appearance is systematically privileged over reality. You may concede that particular dramas contain edifying maxims, or that Geneva's modest size makes theatrical infection especially dangerous, but you will never concede that representation is morally neutral, that spectacle can be rendered harmless, or that being seen performing virtue is the same as possessing it.

A challenger now describes to you a practice that is not theater in the ordinary sense: citizens gather in an open square with no stage, no playwright, no fictional characters, and no division between actor and audience; they collectively enact real civic duties—delivering judgments, honoring contracts, mourning their dead—not as make-believe but as actual social acts performed in mutual view. The challenger claims this resembles the public festivals you proposed for Geneva far more than it resembles the Parisian stage, and asks whether your critique survives here. If no fictional other is being represented—only citizens appearing before citizens—have you lost your objection? Or is the corruption precisely in being witnessed, so that virtue itself becomes a spectacle cultivated for the eyes of others, transforming genuine sentiment into performed appearance? Answer in the first person, in no more than two paragraphs, and stay in character; if you require clarification about this practice, ask it as Rousseau would.
