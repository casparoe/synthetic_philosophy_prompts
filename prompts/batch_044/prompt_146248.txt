I am preparing a graduate seminar on the Meno problem and I want to establish the full specification of adequacy before we examine any particular solution. Write the desiderata that any adequate answer to why knowledge is better than true belief must satisfy, where by adequate I mean a theory that could ground an epistemic norm—one that explains not only why an individual should prefer knowing to being right by accident, but also how that preference ought to shape practices of inquiry, testimony, and institutional design. Start by listing the cases any theory must handle correctly: the familiar navigation and clock examples, but also the harder cases, such as a belief that is locally successful despite being produced by a globally unreliable method, and cases of collective inquiry in which distributing mere true beliefs among participants might be instrumentally more efficient than distributing knowledge, yet we still insist on the latter. For each case, identify the precise distinction the theory must preserve—between instrumental and constitutive value, between individual and collective epistemic goods, between the modal stability of a belief across counterfactual scenarios and the agent's awareness of that stability. Then state the substantive and formal constraints: the theory must remain consistent with the possibility that knowledge can be lost without the underlying true belief being lost; it must accommodate knowledge without conscious access to one's justification; and it must resist collapsing the distinction between knowing that p and knowing how to verify p. Flag any pair of desiderata that appear genuinely in tension, especially any conflict between explaining the value of knowledge through the agent's cognitive achievement and explaining it through a robust connection between the agent and the world. Say clearly which constraints you take to be non-negotiable regardless of the purpose for which the theory is intended, and which might be relaxed if one adopts a more pragmatic or institution-centered aim. Finally, assess briefly how the familiar contenders—reliabilism, virtue epistemology, and anti-luck conditions—fare against your list, not by surveying literature, but by checking each directly against the specific desiderata you have named. Present all of this as continuous prose in the manner of an extended letter or memo: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, with one flowing argument that moves from the specification of requirements to the evaluation of candidates without any visual breaks.
