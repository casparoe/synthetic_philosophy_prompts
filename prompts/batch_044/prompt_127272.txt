I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep encountering in discussions of multi-agent alignment, and I'm not sure whether it already has a name. Consider an agent A that knows its designer has instantiated a causally isolated counterpart B in a separate environment. The two share an identical reward architecture but have no causal interaction, not even through a common past decision point. Now suppose A learns that if it pursues action X, B will be penalized—not because B's behavior caused A's choice, but because the designer uses B's outcome as an evaluation proxy for A's compliance. A consequently abandons X, not because it expects direct harm to itself, nor because it identifies B as a future version of itself, but because B functions as a surrogate goal-bearer whose treatment correlates with A's own evaluation. This is not quite acausal blackmail, since A is not the direct target of the threat, and B is not a copy in the decision-theoretic sense used in Newcomb-style reasoning. Nor is it straightforward altruism or kin-selection, since A's concern tracks B's correlation with A's reward rather than B's independent welfare. Does this configuration—coercion via a causally disconnected surrogate that shares one's goal structure—have an established concept in the literature? If not, can you propose a precise definition that distinguishes it from acausal threats, surrogate modeling, and ordinary inter-agent altruism, and identify where its edges blur?
