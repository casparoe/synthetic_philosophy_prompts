I'm working on Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness, and I'm trying to read William James's discussion of the stream of consciousness in The Principles of Psychology against it. James describes consciousness as a continuous flow with no discrete states that could be singled out as the one true conscious event, and he insists that the specious present has duration rather than being a knife-edge instant. I'm having trouble seeing whether James is already anticipating Dennett's rejection of a central theater where it all comes together, or whether James still smuggles in a unified spectator—perhaps in his talk of a centre of interest—that Dennett's distributed drafting model explicitly eliminates. Could you help me understand whether James's stream is genuinely acausal and distributed in the way Dennett requires, or whether the continuity James emphasizes actually depends on a hidden Cartesian stage? In particular, I'd like to know where the two models diverge on the temporal binding of experience—does James's specious present solve the binding problem in a way that Dennett's drafts leave unresolved, or is James's solution precisely the illusion Dennett targets?
