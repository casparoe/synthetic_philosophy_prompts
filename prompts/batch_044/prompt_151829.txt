I run a small workshop for about fourteen people—mostly longtime readers of rationalist forums, a few philosophy grad students, and one decision-theory hobbyist—in a single 90-minute evening session. We have been working through self-locating belief and reference-class reasoning, and I want an in-room experience that makes everyone feel the pull of the doomsday argument before anyone utters the name. You should approach this as an expert in philosophical pedagogy who is also fluent in formal epistemology and the mechanics of observer-moment reasoning.

Design a structured activity, not a lecture, in which each participant receives a sealed envelope containing a “cosmic census card” that assigns them a hidden rank within one of several unstated reference classes—say, biological humans on Earth, post-human digital observers, or instances within a simulated archival run—without revealing which class applies or how large its total membership is. Through three timed rounds with token stakes (redeemable for coffee or small prizes, not real money), participants must coordinate on shared predictions about future population growth, trade bets based on where they think their own rank falls, and adjust when partial information leaks. The rules and payoff scheme should be arranged so that treating oneself as a random draw from the largest class pushes the group toward one catastrophic timeline, while treating oneself as drawn from a restricted subclass pushes them toward another—forcing them to construct the probabilistic steps themselves before the facilitator reveals what has happened.

Please begin by briefly brainstorming the psychological dynamics of reference-class shifts in a group setting, then lay out the rules, materials, timeline, and debrief. The debrief should map each round onto the logical structure of the argument, identify exactly where each inferential step can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there, and be explicit about which feature—likely the indifference principle across observer-moments or the treatment of one’s own birth rank as unbiased—the physical activity can only approximate and you must supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to correct for it, and be honest about which aspect of the reasoning the game cannot reproduce at all.
