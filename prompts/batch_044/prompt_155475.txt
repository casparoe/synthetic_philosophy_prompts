I am a doctoral student in comparative ethics and philosophy of religion, and I am trying to resolve a tension in Buddhist moral theory that standard taxonomies tend to obscure. Most scholars classify Buddhist ethics as either a form of virtue ethics centered on mental cultivation or a deontological system anchored in the intentionality of action (cetanā). Yet I am struck by what I will call the “dual-teleology problem”: on the one hand, the metaphysics of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) dissolves the stable, bounded agent that conventional consequentialism requires to perform aggregative welfare calculations; on the other hand, Mahāyāna soteriology commits the bodhisattva to the universal attenuation of suffering and grasping (upādāna) across all sentient beings, which structurally resembles a universalist, outcome-oriented aim. This suggests the possibility of a distinctively Buddhist consequentialism—one that is path-dependent and non-aggregative, because the “good” being maximized is not a quantifiable mental-state utility but the progressive weakening of karmic conditioning along interdependent causal chains.

Write an essay that addresses whether Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as this kind of sui generis, path-dependent consequentialism, or whether its doctrines of no-self (anātman), karma-phala, and skillful means (upāya) ultimately undermine any outcome-based moral framework. You should weigh the arguments on both sides—particularly the objection that without a persistent agent there is no subject who can bear the burdens of aggregate maximization, and the counter-argument that the bodhisattva vow functions as a regulative ideal directing action toward systemic liberation rather than personal merit. Reach and defend a specific conclusion, whether that Buddhist ethics is a hybrid view, a non-aggregative consequentialism, or fundamentally non-consequentialist, and justify it through conceptual analysis of the relevant doctrinal distinctions rather than through historical exegesis alone.
