I've been working through an idea about how we experience quick changes in vision, specifically the color phi phenomenon—when two differently colored dots flash rapidly in separate positions and people report seeing a single dot travel and change color in between, even though no intermediate image was ever presented. Some accounts describe this as an "Orwellian" revision, where the brain quietly rewrites the record of what just happened so that the story comes out smooth. Others call it "Stalinesque," meaning the brain puts on a false display in the present moment, like staging a parade that never actually occurred. I think both descriptions assume that the brain either corrects a past log or fakes a current show, and I want to argue that neither captures what is really going on.

In a short essay I'm drafting, I propose a third possibility I call "pre-report interpolation." On this view, there is a transitional workspace—between the initial sensory registration and whatever becomes available to conscious report—where the system synthesizes a bridging frame. This frame is not inserted into memory (so it is not Orwellian) and it is not presented as a live perceptual experience (so it is not Stalinesque). Instead, it occupies a kind of buffer zone that has not yet been assigned to either past record or present appearance. If this is right, then the usual debate about whether consciousness revises history or fabricates the present may be misframed, because the interpolation happens in a region that the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction does not reach.

I am not trained in philosophy or neuroscience, but I have tried to think this through carefully. I would welcome your comments on the essay's reasoning—particularly on whether this "pre-report interpolation" is coherent given what is known about visual processing, and on whether I am misunderstanding the original distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions. Please take a definite, committed position: either defend the view that the distinction still applies and my proposal fails to dissolve it, or argue that my proposal successfully undermines the debate and explain exactly why. Do not simply list pros and cons; make a choice and defend it with reasons. I am also interested in whether you think this framing changes anything important about how we should understand the timing of conscious experience more generally.
