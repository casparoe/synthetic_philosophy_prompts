Me and a friend in my phil of econ course were arguing about Sen's liberal paradox after class — not the basic version but a varient our prof mentioned. (1) He said the classic "Lady Chatterley" preference ordering for the prude is incoherent: neither > he-reads > both > she-reads. He claimed that if the prude genuinely thinks the text corrupts, he should rank "she reads alone" worse than "both read," since in the latter he can at least warn her or dilute the harm, so ranking both above her-alone breaks consistency. (2) I said he was missing Sen's point — the ordering captures a paternalistic "I'm stronger" attitude that is arrogant but fully transitive, and Sen's result is logical, not psychological; you can plug in any preference profile arranged like that and still get the clash with Pareto, whether it's "rational" or not. (3) Then he brought up this three-person workspace varient: three coworkers share one controversial document folder that each can hide or show, and each gets a decisive vote over pairs that only change their own setting. He said with three people you can avoid contradiction by dropping the requirement for a complete social ranking — just use pairwise voting — so the paradox only hits old-school social welfare functions, not real democratic procedures. (4) I countered that he's just hiding Condorcet in the closet: if preferences line up like Sen's across the three toggle-pairs, majority rule cycles, so "no complete ranking" isn't a fix, it's just admitting that collective choice collapses exactly where personal sovereignty meets unanimous improvement. He still thinks I'm being too formalist and that it never occured to Sen that people just vote. Please adjudicate move by move — rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right and briefly justify — and tell us what we're both missing about whether procedural democracy actually escapes the problem.
