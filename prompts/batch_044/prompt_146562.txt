I am grading exam responses for an advanced seminar on moral philosophy, and I have been given a student answer that I want to assess carefully before finalizing the mark. The exam prompt asked students to explain Bernard Williams’s integrity objection to utilitarianism, with particular attention to the problem Williams describes as involving one thought too many; to illustrate this using the case in which an agent saves a spouse rather than a stranger; and then to discuss whether the objection applies only to act-utilitarianism or extends to rule-utilitarianism as well, with reference to the role of ground projects in Williams’s account.

Here is the student’s response:

Williams’s integrity objection shows that utilitarianism alienates agents from their own commitments. For Williams, a meaningful life is structured around ground projects—deep, identity-constituting commitments that provide an agent with reasons to continue living. The standard illustration involves a husband who saves his wife from drowning rather than saving a stranger. If the husband justifies his choice by appealing to a general moral permission to save spouses, he has already had one thought too many; the appropriate reason is simply that she is his wife. This demonstrates that utilitarian reasoning can intrude into relationships where only direct, agent-relative considerations should govern action.

The objection is directed mainly at act-utilitarianism, because that theory requires agents to compute the best outcome in each particular case. Rule-utilitarianism escapes the difficulty, since it permits agents to internalize protective rules—such as favoring one’s family—without performing explicit calculation at the moment of decision. Therefore, the integrity objection loses its force against the rule-based version. Williams develops this point in connection with his broader discussion of moral luck: just as Gauguin’s success as a painter depends partly on circumstances beyond his control, an agent’s ability to pursue ground projects is vulnerable to fortune, which reveals that utilitarianism wrongly treats these commitments as fungible sources of welfare.

Nevertheless, Williams does not regard ground projects as mere personal preferences. They are constitutive of practical identity, and any theory that requires an agent to stand back and justify these commitments from an impartial standpoint violates integrity. So although rule-utilitarianism may appear to preserve the projects themselves, it still demands that the agent regard them as justified by an impartial principle, which is precisely the structural error Williams identifies.
