I run a small fiction workshop and last month two regulars — Jordan and Sam — got into it about teh paradox of deontology over coffee. Jordan said the paradox is fake news: 'required' just means 'not wrong,' so there's no contradiction in praising someone for doing a hard duty like returning a lost wallet in a blizzard. The mistake is thinking moral worth has to be supererogatory. Sam said Jordan was missing the actual paradox, which is the agent-relative restriction — you're not allowed to kill one innocent person to save five, but you're totally allowed to stand by and let five die. Sam said that's structurally weird: morality permits the worse outcome just because you didn't cause it, and if everyone followed that rule we'd have more death overall, which feels self-defeating. Jordan came back that counting bodies is exactly the consequentialist error; deontology cares about rights violations, not headcounts, so there's no paradox, just a different metric. Then Sam tried a writing analogy: if a novelist has a deontological duty not to distort real people's lives, she can write a truthful but boring novel that flops, but she cant change one small false detail that makes the story work and saves the book. Jordan said thats just mixing aesthetic duties with moral ones, and Sam said no, it proves the paradox shows up anywhere you have agent-relative constraints — you're always allowed to let bad art happen but forbidden to cause a small good by breaking a rule.
