I've been trying to understand the logic behind my friend Mara's views on what people morally owe one another in situations of care and dependency. She has no academic background in ethics, but over the years she has expressed firm judgments about specific cases. Here is what I have heard her say:

- A grown child is obligated to provide direct, hands-on care for an aging parent with dementia, even if the parent was abusive and they have been estranged for decades, because "the bond doesn't dissolve just because it was painful."
- If you voluntarily take a friend's teenager into your home for a school year, you become responsible for that teenager's emotional and physical welfare in the same way a legal parent is, even without paperwork, and you cannot send them away simply because it has become inconvenient.
- You have no obligation to assist a homeless person you pass regularly on your street unless you personally know their name and situation; kindness is admirable, but it is not something you owe.
- If a coworker confides in you about a serious mental-health crisis, you acquire an obligation to check on them regularly, but if you only overhear the same information secondhand, you do not.
- Parents should continue to support an adult child who has a permanent disability, regardless of the child's age, but they should not be expected to support an able-bodied adult child who is unemployed by choice.

These verdicts seem consistent with more than one underlying principle. They might track formal or biological roles; they might track whether a person has created or accepted a specific dependency; they might track emotional proximity combined with knowledge of vulnerability.

Now consider a case she has not addressed. For eight years, a woman in her sixties has cooked meals, managed medications, and provided companionship for an elderly neighbor who has no living family. There was never an explicit promise or contract; the help began casually and deepened gradually. The neighbor now relies on this routine for daily functioning. The woman has just been offered a permanent position in another state that she has long wanted. She asks Mara whether she is morally required to stay, or at least to arrange and fund equivalent care before leaving, or whether she may depart with reasonable notice.

What do you think Mara would say? Identify at least two distinct rules that fit her earlier judgments equally well, state what each rule predicts about the neighbor case, and give your best guess about her actual verdict with your reasoning. Then propose one further case—outside of elder care or family relationships—that would best reveal whether her view is genuinely about created dependency, about implicit commitment, or about emotional proximity regardless of choice.
