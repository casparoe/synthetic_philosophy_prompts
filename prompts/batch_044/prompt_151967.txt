Consider the regulatory practice—now proposed in several autonomous-system frameworks—of requiring every high-stakes decision by an AI to be paired with a retrospective "moral supervisor" signature and a machine-readable "causal trace" of the system's reasoning path. Legally, the supervisor's sign-off does not shift liability (the manufacturer remains solely responsible), and the trace is admitted in court mainly to demonstrate procedural transparency rather than to establish that a human could actually have intervened. What theory of moral responsibility and agency does this dual mechanism presuppose? Does it treat the human signature as a genuine exercise of control, as a mere liability sponge, or as a ritual that satisfies some other normative function? Is the practice coherent in treating the causal trace as epistemically authoritative while treating the supervisor's judgment as causally inert? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical view, assess whether its parts are consistent with each other, and describe what would have to change in liability law and certification standards if we replaced that underlying view with one that assigns responsibility only where actual, non-formal human control was present.
