I'm helping organize the opening remarks for a small workshop we're running next thursday — it's called Who Gets Counted? and the audience is split between moral philosophers who work on population ethics and urban planners who build long-term housing forecasts. I'm supposed to talk for maybe four minutes, and I want to try something a bit risky: use the logic behind the repugnant conclusion — you know, the idea that a world with tons of people living lives barely worth living can technically be better than a smaller world full of very happy people — but without using jargon like person-affecting or naming Parfit, because the planners will zone out and the philosophers will cringe if I turn it into a lecture.

The thing is, there's this weird mirror in the planners work. They run projection models that treat future population as just a number you have to accommodate — build X units because we expect Y people — but they don't usually ask whether those Y people exist because of the plan itself. If you build dense affordable housing, you might attract more families; if you don't, maybe different people exist altogether. So the better plan might depend on who you imagine being in it, which sounds exactly like the non-identity problem but applied to zoning. I want to gently point out that adding lives to a spreadsheet isn't morally neutral — it can flip which policy looks best — without saying we shouldn't build housing, and without resolving whether the repugnant conclusion is actually repugnant. Some philosophers think it's only repugnant if you assume total welfare is the only metric, and I want to hint at that debate without taking sides.

Can you write this in my voice? I'm casual, I sometimes mix up affect and effect, and I want one slightly clumsy analogy — maybe comparing population projections to a party invitation list where adding more guests changes the vibe but you still have to decide whether to send the invite. It needs to land for the philosophers (they should recognize the structure without me naming it) and for the planners (they should leave seeing why counting people isn't just arithmetic). Don't make it sound like a philosophy paper; make it sound like someone who just realized two fields are talking past each other about the same existant number.
