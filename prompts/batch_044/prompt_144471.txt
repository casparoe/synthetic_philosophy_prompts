I am a doctoral student in philosophy of physics working on the ontology of spacetime. In a recent reading group, a colleague and I kept circling back to Newton's bucket argument and its modern descendants, but we felt that the standard classroom dialogue—where the relationist invokes Mach and the substantivalist points to local inertial frames—has become somewhat stale. To push the discussion further, I want to construct a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them Dr. Voss, who defends a robust substantivalism about spacetime points, and Dr. Reed, who defends a relationist ontology—where they do not merely rehearse the bucket experiment but extend it into a carefully constructed scenario and then into a more abstract ontological tension.

Specifically, have them begin with a modified bucket thought experiment: a container of viscous fluid rotating in an asymptotically flat, otherwise empty region of the universe, isolated from all distant masses and cosmic structure, so that there are no external bodies relative to which rotation could be defined. Voss should argue that the concavity of the fluid surface is best explained by the container's rotation relative to the local affine structure of spacetime itself—specifically, the connection and metric—without appeal to distant matter. Reed should respond by attempting to salvage a Machian view not through distant bodies but through modal-relational structure: the claim that rotation is defined by counterfactual dependencies among possible configurations of the fluid and container, and that a genuinely empty universe lacks the relational richness needed to ground such counterfactuals. They should then press the dispute into a second, more conceptual layer: whether the substantivalist's reliance on bare spacetime points forces her to accept primitive identity conditions for those points—what philosophers call the hole argument—and whether this undermines the explanatory gain she claimed in the bucket case. Meanwhile, Reed should face the challenge that her modal-relational ontology may collapse into a form of structuralism that still requires a substrate to bear the relations, or that it smuggles in spatiotemporal structure through the back door of the counterfactual framework.

The dialogue should be genuinely dialectical: each character must respond directly to the other's specific moves rather than delivering independent speeches. Neither should be a strawman; Voss should concede genuine difficulties for substantivalism (such as the threat of undetectable differences in point identity), and Reed should concede that pure relationism struggles to account for local inertial phenomena without some structural analog to spacetime. I want the exchange to remain conceptually rigorous but accessible enough that someone without specialized knowledge of general-relativistic formalism could follow the philosophical stakes.
