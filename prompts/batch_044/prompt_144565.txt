I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on moral change and the sociology of norms, where we've been working through Bernard Williams's argument that large-scale moral revolutions—such as the abolition of slavery or the disappearance of dueling—are driven less by the force of rational argument than by shifts in honor, shame, and peer recognition, together with Kwame Anthony Appiah's refinement that such shifts occur within specific honor worlds whose internal dynamics determine whether a practice becomes unthinkable for its practitioners. I'm trying to frame a conceptual research agenda for the next two years—philosophical rather than empirical—around a problem that strikes me as under-theorized: the phenomenon of residual honor enclaves and interstitial moral risk. By a residual honor enclave I mean a bounded social group in which a practice legally abolished and publicly stigmatized retains local honor value because the group's internal peer-recognition structure has not undergone the reversal; interstitial moral risk is the condition that obtains when law and honor are misaligned, so that compliance with formal norms requires individuals to forfeit standing within the enclave.

Given this framing, I want to know which open conceptual questions in the study of moral revolutions are most worth prioritizing for a philosopher with about two years, limited empirical training, and an interest in normative ethics rather than sociology. In particular, I see at least four lines of work that could be pursued: (1) the causal-priority problem—whether shifts in honor are upstream of legal prohibition or whether institutional change forces honor to adapt; (2) the dissolution problem—what conceptual mechanisms explain how a residual honor enclave eventually collapses or persists indefinitely; (3) the argument-efficacy problem—whether moral reasoning can ever directly trigger an honor reversal without being mediated by status competition or interest; and (4) the comparative-structure problem—why some norms, such as slavery, seem to collapse rapidly once elite honor shifts, while others, such as dueling, generate prolonged interstitial periods with persistent enclaves.

Please defend an ordering. For each line, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others—whether settling it would change how we understand the remaining questions—how tractable it is for conceptual work rather than historical sociology, and what kind of philosophical or limited empirical evidence over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want a defended agenda that tells me where my effort should go and why, including whether the residual-enclave framing itself is a productive injection or a distraction from more basic questions about interest-based versus honor-based explanations.
