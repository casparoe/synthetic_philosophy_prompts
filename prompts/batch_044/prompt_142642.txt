I'm drafting a proposal for a four-month independent-study fellowship on eliciting latent knowledge from AI systems. The half-formed idea is this: current elicitation methods are usually evaluated against ground-truth labels the evaluator already knows, but this presupposes that the "latent" content is a determinate belief-like state rather than a distributed pattern that only acquires semantic content through the elicitation interaction itself. I want to ask whether the concept of "latent knowledge" can be made coherent without smuggling in an interpretivist or intentional-stance framework that conflicts with the mechanistic assumptions used to motivate the safety project. Turn this into a proposal with: (1) a precisely stated question answerable in four months without running experiments; (2) why the existing technical and philosophical literature leaves this tension open; (3) the conceptual method—what distinctions I'd draw, which analogies or formal models I'd construct, and why a philosophical rather than empirical study makes progress; (4) what a result looks like (e.g., a taxonomy of coherence conditions for attribution) and what counts as failure; (5) an honest scope assessment, including what must be left out. Please separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind and AI safety literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view—such as that large language models literally possess beliefs—as the consensus.
