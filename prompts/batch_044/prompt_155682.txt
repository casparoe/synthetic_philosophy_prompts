A government I live under recently established a National Registry of Historical Responsibility, inviting citizens born after a particular historical rupture to register voluntarily as successor-bearers for atrocities committed by a prior regime. Those who sign are listed in an official civic archive and may help administer reparations funded partly through present taxation. I was not alive during the events, have no family connection to the perpetrators, and reject the ideology that motivated the crimes. Still, advocates argue that because my present welfare depends partly on institutional and economic structures—transport networks, legal frameworks, accumulated capital—that persisted through the transition, I occupy a position of beneficiary liability that obliges acknowledgment. Critics counter that attributing moral fault to someone who did not exist at the time of the acts collapses the distinction between being causally linked to an outcome and being culpable for it, and that treating citizenship as an inherited moral stain confuses political membership with personal guilt. I am torn: signing feels like confessing to a wrong I could not have committed, yet refusing feels like dismissing real, continuing harm to descendants of the victims. How should I determine whether I bear any meaningful responsibility in this specific form, and what considerations should guide my decision?
