I oversee a regional surveillance network tracking antibiotic-resistant infections in long-term care facilities. Our employment contracts specify standard working hours, yet there is an unwritten expectation that epidemiologists remain available for urgent outbreak verification during nights and weekends. Officially, this availability is framed as voluntary professional citizenship: it earns commendation letters but appears in no job description, and those who decline face no formal discipline. Over the past two years, however, I have observed that staff who consistently volunteer for these shifts receive first authorship on outbreak reports and preferential access to our limited sequencing budget, while colleagues who protect their personal time are gradually excluded from collaborative papers and described in internal reviews as lacking team commitment. One senior analyst, who is the primary caregiver for an aging parent, recently told me that she feels morally defective for resenting the expectation, since confirming an outbreak can prevent deaths, yet she also sees that her “choice” is illusory because career advancement depends on compliance. I am weighing two reforms: either I codify the on-call shifts as paid, rotating obligations with transparent scheduling, or I erect formal barriers—such as anonymizing contribution logs used in authorship decisions—to ensure that declining truly carries no hidden professional cost. Before I choose, I want to understand the conceptual structure of the problem. Is there a philosophically coherent distinction between actions that are genuinely praiseworthy yet not required and actions that have become de facto duties disguised as virtues? If an institution systematically treats a voluntary contribution as a prerequisite for fair treatment, has it committed a specific moral error—perhaps extracting surplus moral labor through the rhetoric of gift? And which analytical framework, whether one distinguishing perfect from imperfect obligations or one focused on institutional coercion, best supports formalizing the duty versus protecting its genuine voluntariness?
