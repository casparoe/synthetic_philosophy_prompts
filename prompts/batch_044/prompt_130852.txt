Take the role of a professional philosopher of classical Chinese thought who also writes organizational satire. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the institutional implications of Zhuangzi’s actual metaphysical commitments—death as continuous transformation (hua), the dissolution of fixed self-boundaries illustrated by the butterfly dream, his response to his wife’s death as a model for mourning, and the principle that Heaven and Earth show no partiality—if they were adopted literally as corporate policy. Then write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate HR manual, about 600 words, formatted as numbered sections, for a multinational firm that has adopted “Radical Zhuangzianism” as its official culture. Cover bereavement leave, retirement ceremonies, employee identity verification, and performance evaluation. The satire should exaggerate real features of Zhuangzi’s view of death and transformation rather than inventing ones, so that a reader familiar with the text recognizes the philosophy being taken seriously, while a general reader finds the manual legible and absurd on its own terms. Maintain sustained irony: never wink, let the institutional logic speak for itself, and keep the voice flat and bureaucratic throughout. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific scholarly editions. Do not invent direct quotations from classical texts; paraphrase any concepts without quotation marks. Ensure internal consistency: every policy should follow from the premises without slipping into compatibilist comfort.
