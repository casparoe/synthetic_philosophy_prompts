In my graduate seminar on philosophy and scientific practice, two students—Marcus and Lena—got into a sustained argument over whether philosophy actually does anything for working scientists. Marcus opened by invoking Steven Weinberg’s comparison in Dreams of a Final Theory, where philosophers of science are likened to ornithologists studying birds that have already flown away; he paired this with Hawking’s claim in The Grand Design that philosophy is dead because it has failed to keep pace with modern physics, and with Feynman’s well-known analogy that philosophy is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds. Marcus argued that these three figures, who actually reshaped physics, speak with one voice: philosophy is decorative commentary at best, and scientists have long since stopped waiting for philosophers to approve their methods. Lena pushed back, but not with a generic defense. She pointed specifically to the development of causal discovery frameworks and model-pluralism debates in climate attribution and epidemiology, arguing that philosophical work on intervention, mechanism, and the interpretation of ensemble disagreement—work that is genuinely philosophical rather than purely statistical—has shaped how scientists now validate predictions and communicate uncertainty to policymakers. Marcus replied that whatever is useful in those frameworks is really mathematics or computer science that philosophers later annotate; if one strips away the metaphysical packaging, scientists never needed philosophical permission to build the models. Lena countered that by that reductive standard, theoretical physics itself is just mathematics with interpretation added later, yet Weinberg clearly believed theoretical physics was doing substantive work that went beyond computation. Marcus then pressed what he saw as the decisive objection: philosophers never resolve their core disputes—scientific realism versus anti-realism, for instance—so they cannot offer actionable guidance to scientists who need definite answers. Lena responded that this confuses resolution with clarification, and that philosophy’s practical contribution is often to reveal that a scientific disagreement is partly about values or definitions rather than pure empirical fact, which has direct consequences for how funding agencies and regulatory bodies treat model divergence. They asked me to referee their exchange.

Please adjudicate their argument move by move: assess Marcus’s invocation of Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman and whether it fairly represents the philosophers’ reply; evaluate whether Lena’s appeal to causal discovery and model pluralism in climate and epidemiological practice succeeds in showing that philosophy does genuine work; judge Marcus’s “stripping away metaphysical packaging” reduction and whether it undermines Lena’s parallel with theoretical physics; and determine whether Lena’s distinction between resolving disputes and clarifying them saves the philosophers’ case against Marcus’s charge of practical uselessness. Say who is right about what at each step, note anything both sides are missing, and indicate who is more right overall.
