I am trying to determine my actual commitments about biological function by reflecting on my intuitive verdicts across a range of cases, and I want to see whether they can be captured by a single principle or whether they force me to split between competing accounts.

Here are my judgments.

The heart's function is to circulate blood. That it produces audible sounds is a causal consequence, not itself a function, despite being diagnostically useful to physicians.

The human appendix has largely lost its ancestral digestive role, yet its present contributions to immune surveillance and to maintaining gut microbiome homeostasis strike me as genuine biological functions—functions it possesses now, even though natural selection has not specifically shaped it for these roles over deep evolutionary time.

Consider a synthetic bioengineered construct—call it a hepato-renal bridge—designed to filter blood through a novel enzymatic pathway never observed in natural organisms. It performs filtration reliably, but I hesitate to say that filtration is its biological function. My reluctance seems connected to its complete absence of evolutionary history, though I am unsure whether intentional human design can substitute for such a history.

Take the redness of blood: it necessarily results from the chemistry of hemoglobin's oxygen-binding properties. I regard redness as having no biological function whatsoever, even though it is causally indispensable to blood's appearance and serves as a routine clinical indicator.

Next, a case of recent evolutionary co-option: in a particular mammalian lineage, a gene originally involved in limb patterning has been recruited to regulate thermal tolerance in a colder environment, and has been under sustained positive selection for that regulatory role across many generations—long enough to be adaptively significant, but not long enough to be considered deeply entrenched. I judge that thermal regulation is now genuinely its function.

Finally, consider a deleterious mutant allele that disrupts a metabolic pathway and causes a severe disorder. In the organisms that carry it, this disruption is its only causal contribution. I do not think that causing the disorder is its function.

These verdicts are in tension. The appendix and the co-opted gene suggest that present causal contribution, together with some history of selection however recent, can ground function. The synthetic bridge possesses both present contribution and intentional design, yet I withhold functional attribution. The spandrel and the mutant allele each have definite present causal roles—one benign, one harmful—but I deny that either is a function. So pure causal-role analysis is insufficient, pure etiological analysis seems to miss the appendix, and design-based analysis fails to explain my reluctance about the bridge.

Is there a coherent unified principle—perhaps one that combines an etiological condition with a normative or systemic constraint—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which judgment should I treat as the outlier and overturn?

Please also apply the best principle to a case I have not listed: a viral gene horizontally transferred into a host genome thousands of generations ago, now essential for the host's metabolism and continuously under host-level selection, but originating entirely outside the host's own evolutionary lineage. Does it have the metabolic function?
