Exam question: Karl Jaspers distinguishes four types of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. Evaluate whether his framework permits intergenerational transmission of guilt: can citizens born after a state’s wrongful acts bear political or metaphysical guilt for them? Be precise about the difference between collective attribution and individual bearing, and address whether moral guilt can ever be distributed across a group.

Student answer: The student correctly identifies all four categories and notes that criminal guilt is strictly individual and juridical. They observe that political guilt attaches to citizens collectively, since membership in the state entails shared responsibility for its actions regardless of personal opposition. They then extend this logic across generations, arguing that descendants inherit the state’s political identity and therefore share its guilt in the same way one might inherit state debts or treaty obligations. On metaphysical guilt, they describe it as arising from universal human solidarity—feeling guilty for surviving or failing to prevent suffering—and claim that this too is inherited, because belonging to the national culture that permitted past crimes places one in the same metaphysical community of failure. They maintain that moral guilt alone remains individual, tied to personal conscience, though they add that if an entire culture shares a value system, moral guilt can become distributed across the group rather than remaining strictly personal. They conclude that Jaspers therefore allows robust intergenerational collective guilt in both the political and metaphysical registers.
