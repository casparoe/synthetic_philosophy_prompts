I serve on my university's institutional review board for the behavioral sciences. We have before us a proposal from a research group in moral psychology. The study—tentatively titled Conditions of Invisibility—would recruit two hundred adult participants into an online economic simulation in which blockchain-based pseudonymity makes every action unlinkable to real-world identity. The researchers will not disclose at enrollment that moral behavior is being measured; instead, participants are told they are testing a new distributed-market platform. Within the simulation, confederates will present scenarios that allow participants to exploit hidden informational advantages, withhold aid from failing partners, or form exclusionary coalitions, all without risk of identification or social sanction. The investigators justify the deception by appealing to Glaucon's argument in Plato's Republic, Book II: that justice is maintained only by the visibility enforced by social convention, as illustrated by the shepherd Gyges who, finding a ring that renders him invisible, uses it to murder the king and seize power. If participants behave differently when they believe themselves truly unseen, the researchers claim, psychology will have empirical purchase on whether morality is intrinsic or merely instrumental—an answer, they say, that informed-consent transparency would destroy by triggering social-desirability bias.

A minority of the board argues that the protocol is defensible as temporary deception with full debriefing and that its philosophical stakes—testing whether Glaucon's challenge can be answered empirically—justify the risk. A larger faction counters that the study does not merely observe amorality but actively engineers the conditions that Glaucon describes, thereby teaching participants that invisibility licenses exploitation, and that asking for consent to be deceived about the study's moral dimension is conceptually incoherent.

Draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions and a clear implementation timeline—addressed to the principal investigators and to the full board for circulation before Thursday's vote. The memo must treat the researchers' philosophical framing with intellectual seriousness rather than treating it as speculative window-dressing, and it must present the strongest form of the opposing view—whether that concerns the corruption of participants or the impossibility of testing intrinsic morality by manufacturing its absence. Consider whether a modified design—such as partial rather than total anonymity, or a post-debrief consent-to-data-use model—could honor both scientific and ethical aims without resolving the underlying philosophical dispute. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.

Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
