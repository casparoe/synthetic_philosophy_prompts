I have been working out a position I call the stream-continuum view of personal persistence, which draws on the Buddhist doctrine of anattā but frames it within contemporary metaphysics of persistence. On this view, what we call a person is not an enduring substance, nor a psychological continuity of memory and character, nor merely a living human organism; rather, it is a causally integrated stream (santati) of mental and physical events sustained by a non-substantial life-continuum function (bhavaṅga-citta) that explains both ordinary persistence and the possibility of rebirth or radical transformation without any underlying owner. The stream can in principle bifurcate, gradually fade, or be causally reconstructed, and identity is a relational property of this causal structure rather than a property of a bearer.

What would genuinely count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure. Specifically: which empirical or conceptual developments—say, about brain death, split-brain cases, or the metaphysics of causation—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What predictions does this view make that standard psychological-continuity or animalist theories do not, and have any of those predictions been tested or falsified? Does the view risk being insulated by qualifications—e.g., treating any apparent counterexample as merely a case of stream bifurcation—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice that renders the position unfalsifiable? Which defeaters would a committed defender of the stream-continuum view be forced to concede, and which could they dismiss by appealing to the absence of a substantial self? Please rank the objections from fatal to merely inconvenient, and conclude with an assessment of whether the position is genuinely at risk or has been qualified into safety.
