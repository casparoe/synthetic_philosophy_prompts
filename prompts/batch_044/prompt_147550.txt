I am drafting a short essay for an interdisciplinary discussion group on AI governance, and since I have no formal training in philosophy I would value your critical comments on its coherence and on whether I have missed an obvious objection. The essay takes up the familiar tension between value aggregation—designing aligned systems to maximize some unified measure of user welfare or preference satisfaction—and liberal neutrality, the view that a governing system should not rank or enforce comprehensive moral doctrines. I argue that both sides implicitly treat values as attributes of a single agent that must be optimized or withheld. I propose an alternative structural analogy drawn from distributed version control: treat stakeholder value sets as independent branches in a shared repository, and treat alignment not as computing an aggregate utility function but as designing a merge-conflict resolution protocol. On this model, the system does not need a master value vector; it needs rules that determine when branches can merge automatically, when they require explicit manual resolution, and when they must remain permanently separate. This seems to preserve liberal neutrality, because no branch is forced to adopt another’s comprehensive doctrine, while avoiding the paralysis of pure neutrality, since cooperative integration is still governed by a shared procedural framework rather than by a shared optimization target.

My specific requests are these. First, does the version-control analogy do genuine philosophical work, or does it merely rename the aggregation problem in engineering vocabulary? Second, if the resolution protocol itself must embed normative judgments—about which conflicts are resolvable, which require isolation, and which demand external arbitration—have I simply relocated the value judgment from the system’s outputs to its infrastructure, thereby undermining the neutrality claim? Third, does this framing introduce a structural commitment that is genuinely distinct from standard debates about pluralism and procedural justice, or is it essentially a restatement of one of those positions with different terminology? I am not asking for a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; rather, I want your judgment on whether these three objections capture the main philosophical vulnerabilities, and whether there is a more fundamental flaw in treating normative disagreement as structurally analogous to a software merge conflict. I would also appreciate any suggestions for sharpening the distinction between a protocol that is genuinely neutral and one that merely conceals a hidden aggregation rule behind procedural language.
