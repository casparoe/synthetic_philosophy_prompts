I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am preparing interview questions for an upcoming podcast episode with Dr. Elena Voss, an unnamed philosopher who defends a specific resolution of Moore's paradox. Her view is that the absurdity of first-person Moorean utterances—statements in which a speaker asserts that p while simultaneously disclaiming belief that p—does not arise from a logical contradiction in the content believed, but from a violation of what she calls first-person epistemic self-attestation, a pragmatic norm governing how belief reports function in assertoric practice rather than a doxastic consistency constraint. I need eight to ten sharply focused interview questions that go well beyond having her restate this position. At least two questions should examine how her normative framework differs from transparency-of-belief accounts—particularly whether she treats the failure as pragmatic rather than semantic, and whether she allows that one can genuinely hold contradictory beliefs as long as one does not simultaneously attest to both. At least one question should probe the scope of the norm: does it apply only to present-tense first-person reports, or can it explain why third-person analogues feel similarly defective, and what does that imply about whether the norm is essentially indexical? At least one question should draw on my own domain: in distributed software systems, logs frequently contain contradictory confidence scores or belief-state annotations—can her framework distinguish a Moorean contradiction in an automated belief-report from a mere data-integrity error, and does extending the norm to artificial agents require attributing to them a form of first-person authority? For each proposed question, please include a brief anticipation of the reply Dr. Voss is most likely to offer, followed by a precise follow-up designed to press her exactly where that reply is weakest. The audience will be technically literate but philosophically untrained, so the questions should be rigorous and conceptually dense—genuinely in the weeds of her view—without relying on bibliographic references, citation lists, or appeals to texts the audience could not be expected to have read.
