A colleague has pressed me with an argument that applies a principle from philosophy of mind to Fernand Braudel's historiography and seems to collapse into self-refutation. Here are the steps:

(1) Braudel's longue durée thesis claims that geographical and climatic structures—the layer he treats as almost timeless—are causally sufficient for the macro-patterns of Mediterranean history, such as the persistence of economic and cultural unity across political fragmentation.

(2) By the causal-exclusion principle familiar from debates about mental causation: if a higher-level structure is causally sufficient for an outcome, then lower-level individual actions are causally excluded.

(3) Therefore, individual decisions—for example, Philip II's dispatch of the Armada, or a merchant's choice of shipping route—are causally excluded from explaining outcomes at the historical scale Braudel studies; they are epiphenomenal.

(4) If individual actions are causally excluded, then the distinction between rational and irrational choice collapses at that scale, since rational assessment requires counterfactual dependence: had the agent chosen differently, the outcome would have differed.

(5) Apply the thesis reflexively: Braudel's own decision to construct this structural model, to select archival sources, and to argue for the priority of geography was itself a lower-level event occurring within the same climatic and geographical constraints.

(6) Hence, if (1) is true, Braudel's reasoning provides no rational warrant for accepting (1) over any other geographically determined historiographical output; the thesis undermines the very possibility of defending itself.

(7) A thesis that eliminates the rational basis for its own defense should be rejected.

My colleague concludes that we must abandon either Braudel's structuralism or the exclusion principle, but insists the argument is valid and that the cost falls on (1). I suspect something slips between the causal claim in (1) and the rational-epistemic claim in (4) or (6)—perhaps an equivocation between explanatory priority and ontological exclusion, or between macro-level causal sufficiency and micro-level counterfactual fragility—but I cannot name the error precisely.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if you defend Braudel, can you preserve his claim that structures dominate without committing to causal exclusion of individual agency? Please separate what is well established in the philosophy of historiography from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who work on these issues, and do not present a disputed reading as if it were the consensus.
