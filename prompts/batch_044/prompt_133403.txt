As a doctoral researcher in comparative macro-history, I am formalizing a methodological disagreement that recurs in scholarship on the Great Divergence but rarely receives explicit structural analysis. I want the joint statement that two camps could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on whether the classic contrastive framing—why Britain rather than China industrialized first, often called the Needham question—is genuinely well posed. Camp One, the Contrastive Essentialists, holds that the question is valid if restricted to comparing institutional equilibria under a shared counterfactual baseline of comparable resource endowments, demographic regimes, and state-capacity thresholds in the early modern period. Camp Two, the Path-Contingency Theorists, holds that the question is malformed because it implicitly treats industrialization as a singular global attractor, thereby committing what I will call modal scope asymmetry: it treats British outcomes as the necessary endpoint of a trajectory that Chinese outcomes merely failed to reach, rather than treating both as irreversible branches from a common node.

The deliverable should be a single document with the following structure:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, including: empirical approximations about agricultural productivity, commercial integration, and energy regimes in parts of Britain and regions of China circa 1750; the formal conditions under which counterfactual comparison across macro-regions is conceptually coherent; and a shared distinction between explaining an absence (why X did not occur) and explaining a presence (why Y did occur).

2. A disagreements table with each camp's position phrased in wording it would accept. The table should cover at least three cruxes: (a) whether industrialization denotes a single natural kind or a family of technologically mediated growth regimes with no privileged member; (b) whether holding everything else constant across Eurasian macro-regions is coherent or necessarily violates path-dependency and irreversibility; and (c) whether the absence of British-style coal-intensive mechanization in Qing China should be explained by a missing necessary factor or by the local optimality of a different equilibrium.

3. For each crux, identify a concrete comparative case or evidence both sides agree bears on it—such as the divergence in energy regimes between the Yangtze delta and the English Midlands, or the differential institutional embedding of mechanical printing and its labor-market effects—and record in advance each side's prediction about what the case shows and what it implies for their view.

4. An explicit assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal, particularly around terms like potential, blockage, or divergence, specifying which portion and giving the reason.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the analysis should stand on its conceptual architecture alone.
