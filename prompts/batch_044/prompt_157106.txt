In the literature on moral revolutions—how practices like chattel slavery or aristocratic dueling collapse with surprising speed—there is a long-running stalemate between three explanatory families. One family, associated with Appiah’s account of honor, holds that norms die when they become incompatible with a society’s self-image of status: dueling vanished not because anyone won a formal argument about its wrongness, but because refusing a challenge came to signal gentlemanly self-possession rather than cowardice, reversing the status cost of abstention. Another family, informed by Bernard Williams’s broader skepticism about rationalist pictures of moral change, insists that such revolutions track shifts in power, economic contingency, and social position far more closely than they track moral reasoning; on this view, abolitionist and anti-dueling arguments are largely post-hoc rationalizations of changes driven elsewhere. A third family emphasizes material interest convergence—British abolition coinciding with falling West Indian sugar profits and rising industrial capital—treating honor-talk as epiphenomenal gloss.

My working group has been stuck on this for over a year. Every side accommodates the same broad historical patterns: pamphlets circulated, parliamentary votes shifted, profit curves moved, and dueling suddenly looked ridiculous, all within overlapping decades. Nobody denies these co-occurrences. Yet the Appiah-style interpreter, the Williams-style skeptic, and the interest-based historian have not moved an inch. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not an adjudication.

Is this a substantive causal dispute that could be settled by finer-grained evidence—say, micro-archival work tracking whether specific British legislators changed position after moral persuasion versus economic lobbying, or comparative study of foot-binding’s rapid disappearance versus dueling’s slower decline? Or is it partly verbal, with “explanation” meaning a change in social meaning for one camp, a shift in material incentives for another, and an absence of genuine rational progress for the third? Might the sides be answering different questions—one about the phenomenology of normative collapse, one about structural causation, one about philosophical justification—under the single heading “why did the norm end”? Is there a hidden normative disagreement about whether an explanation of moral change must cite agents’ own reasons in order to count as genuinely moral?

If your diagnosis is that the debate is empirical but masked, what specific comparative case or formal distinction—perhaps between a norm’s functional economic role and its symbolic status—would actually discriminate honor dynamics from interest dynamics? If it is conceptual or verbal, what reconceptualization—say, separating the decline of a practice from the decline of its prestige—would dissolve the appearance of conflict? And if you think the stall is unavoidable because moral revolutions are overdetermined and these frames are incommensurable, argue for that and say what kind of progress, if any, should be expected: a change of question, a disciplinary division of labor, or honest acceptance of plural explanation?

Please state how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the specific point—whether about the incommensurability of the explanatory frames, the availability of decisive historical evidence, or the precise mechanism by which honor costs invert—about which you are least certain.
