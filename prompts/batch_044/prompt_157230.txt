I'm on my university's joint neuroethics and human-subjects board, and we've got this weird proposal in front of us for Thursday's vote. I need to circulate a short memo with a concrete recommendation and a timeline.

The study wants to put two volunteers in a long-term bidirectional BCI loop — not just sharing neural data but feeding each person's affective self-model back into the other in realtime. Over months they expect the pair might develop one shared narrative identity, a kind of distributed 'I.' They ask us to treat the dyad as a single research subject for risk assessment and to accept one joint consent form instead of two seperate ones. They invoke Hofstadter-style reasoning that the self is just a pattern that can spread across brains, so if the loop is tight enough there's literally one self spanning two bodies.

Half the board thinks this is intense social bonding dressed up in metaphysics and that treating two people as one subject tramples individual autonomy. The other half worries that if they're right about the distributed self, forcing seperate consent is like asking one half of a brain to sign off alone, which might miss the actual moral unit at stake.

We need an actual decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear time frame: either kill it outright or tell them exactly what to revise and come back by a specific date. The memo should open with the bottom line, then lay out reasoning that takes the distributed-self argument seriously without rubber-stamping it, then honestly treat the strongest consideration on the losing side, then list conditions the researchers can actually respond to. Don't just restate the legal definition of a human subject.
