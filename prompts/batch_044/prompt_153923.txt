Consider the long-running impasse in care ethics over whether the framework can be extended from intimate, face-to-face relationships to large-scale institutional and global justice without forfeiting its distinctive normative force. One camp, which we might call the relational particularists, maintains that care is constituted by direct affective engagement and contextual responsiveness; once it is mediated by bureaucratic structures or directed at distant strangers, it collapses into a form of liberal distributive justice and loses what they term its proximity-fidelity condition. They distinguish direct care-giving from caring-about and caring-for precisely to show that only the first carries full moral weight, and they treat phenomenological descriptions of caregiving relationships as the primary data for ethical theory. The opposing camp, the structural scaling advocates, argues that vulnerability and interdependence are not confined to dyads but are embedded in social and economic structures; refusing to institutionalize care is therefore not fidelity but neglect, and they propose what they call structural attentiveness—designing institutions that track patterns of dependency rather than individual affective bonds. They regard macro-level vulnerability metrics and organizational design as equally legitimate data. Despite decades of refinement—particularists developing taxonomies of mediated care, scaling advocates constructing models of representative institutional care—the debate cycles through applied cases such as healthcare rationing, climate migration, and welfare design without convergence. Diagnose this stall: is it primarily verbal, turning on whether the word care necessarily entails face-to-face affective presence; is it substantive, resting on an ontological disagreement about whether dependency is irreducibly relational or can be aggregated across populations; do the sides employ different evidential standards, with one privileging phenomenological reports and the other privileging structural metrics; or are they addressing different questions—one about the moral psychology of agents, the other about institutional design—under a single heading? Specify whether a hidden empirical claim, for example about the psychological limits of moral motivation at scale or about the organizational feasibility of dependency-tracking institutions, is doing the work. Finally, state what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, a formal model of mediated dependency, an empirical study of institutional care practices, a change in the framing question, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and no progress should be expected, with reasons.
