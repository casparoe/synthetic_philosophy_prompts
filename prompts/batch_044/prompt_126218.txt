I am working through a problem set for a graduate seminar on moral particularism versus generalism, and I am trying to anticipate our instructor Dr. Vasquez’s response to a synthetic case I have constructed. Over the past weeks she has delivered intuitive verdicts—without explicit theoretical justification—on six hypothetical dilemmas: she approved a captain’s decision to distribute scarce lifeboat water according to dependents rather than by lot; she condemned state execution even when deterrence is probable; she endorsed breaking a wedding promise to save a stranger in cardiac arrest; she rejected burning a unique, irreplaceable painting to heat a shelter of ten freezing people; she condemned corporate pollution that provides present jobs but harms only distant generations; and she permitted a physician to withhold a terminal diagnosis at the family’s request. These judgments seem jointly compatible with at least two broad frameworks: a context-sensitive particularism that treats relational embeddedness and the irreversibility of harm as shifting the moral salience of reasons, and a pluralist generalism that weighs incommensurable values—autonomy, relational integrity, and non-substitution—according to stable but non-hierarchical principles.

The new case is this: an AI governance system, trained precisely on these six judgments, proposes a uniform “moral residue protocol” that subjects every decision-making agent to persistent, simulated guilt for actions that caused necessary harm, predicting a fifteen percent reduction in future harm but causing widespread, undifferentiated psychological distress regardless of the agent’s specific relational context or whether guilt is appropriate. Would Dr. Vasquez approve adopting this protocol? Predict her verdict, articulate the competing rules—uniform consequentialist optimization, particularist resistance to algorithmic moral homogenization, and deontological objections to manufactured suffering—that each accommodate her six prior judgments, say which rule you think she actually runs and why, and then identify the single further case—perhaps involving a non-human agent or a situation in which guilt is manifestly inappropriate—that would best discriminate among these three frameworks.
