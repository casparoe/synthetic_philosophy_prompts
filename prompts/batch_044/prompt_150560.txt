Read the film Gandhi through the lens of Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the debate over whether biography teaches morality through direct emulation or through comparative judgment (synkrisis). The film is frequently accused of hagiography: it largely omits Gandhi's early racial views and his controversial experiments with celibacy, presenting him as an almost uniformly gentle exemplar. Does this sanitization undermine the film's value as moral instruction, or does it serve a pedagogical purpose by making the exemplar accessible? Compare the film's narrative strategy with Plutarch's practice of pairing lives to highlight both virtue and failure. Where does Gandhi invite comparison rather than imitation? And does the omission of Gandhi's flaws reflect a tension in hagiographic biography—between inspiring the audience and providing an honest model—that Plutarch himself navigates differently? I'd like a reading that takes both the film's cinematic choices and the philosophical literature on moral learning from lives seriously, and says what this case reveals about whether biography should display its subject's failures to be genuinely instructive.
