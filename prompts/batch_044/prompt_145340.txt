In a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics, a discussant dismissed my argument for long-range welfare investment by invoking the familiar Keynesian line that in the long run we are all dead—seemingly to claim that distant future persons fall outside the scope of present moral concern, and that Keynes himself, despite his optimism about future abundance, was ultimately a presentist about value. I am convinced the line is doing several distinct jobs at once, but I cannot cleanly separate them, and I want to understand which ones actually support the dismissive move. Can you lay out the different precise principles this slogan might express, organized along three axes of ambiguity: (i) the temporal scope of "the long run" (economic convergence to a steady state, the biological lifespan of present agents, institutional decay, or the epistemic horizon beyond which forecasting collapses); (ii) the kind of "deadness" asserted (physical non-existence, absence of moral standing, causal irrelevance to present choice, or epistemic inaccessibility); and (iii) whose interests are being discounted (the speaker individually, every agent alive at the time of decision, or all possible future persons). For each distinct combination, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim about rationality or value, or a false claim about Keynes's own ethics—especially in relation to his writings on leisure, the good life, the moral psychology of acquisition, and the economic prospects of future generations. Then tell me: which of these readings was actually being invoked in the seminar argument to reject long-term climate or welfare planning, and which readings, if any, are genuinely consistent with Keynes's broader view that our descendants might live in abundance but only if present generations overcome the love of money?
