I am commissioning a piece for our Ideas section on how critical theory is being digested by institutional design, and I need a sustained satirical document—approximately 600 words, deadpan and legible—written in the form of a mock internal FAQ issued by a fictional municipal “Office of Participatory Normalization.” The voice should be that of a sincere, slightly defensive mid-level administrator who genuinely believes the program is emancipatory, writing for citizens who have questioned the office’s new “Reflexive Transparency Initiative.”

The target of the satire is the co-optation of well-known Foucauldian themes—disciplinary power, panopticism, biopower, normalization, and the power/knowledge nexus—into the language of wellness, self-optimization, smart governance, and democratic participation. The administrator should explain, in straightforward bureaucratic prose, how residents now submit weekly “self-disciplinary narratives” for peer-algorithmic review; how the office’s open-plan architecture eliminates privacy in order to foster “horizontal visibility”; and how normalization metrics are published as “liberatory data dashboards” that help citizens optimize their own compliance.

The humor must come from internal consistency rather than exaggeration for its own sake: every mechanism the administrator praises should map onto an actual feature of Foucault’s analysis (surveillance internalized as self-care, norms enforced through comparative measurement, knowledge about subjects produced in order to govern them), yet each should be framed as an obvious good. Do not wink at the reader; the irony should remain entirely deadpan, legible only through the gap between the vocabulary of empowerment and the reality of control being described.

Inject a niche conceptual twist: include the idea of “reverse panopticism,” in which citizens are required to continuously observe, document, and report on management’s compliance with normalization protocols. The administrator should defend this arrangement as proof that power is now genuinely democratic and diffuse, missing—or deliberately ignoring—the point that dispersed and participatory surveillance can be more totalizing than centralized authority.

Keep all references to widely recognized Foucauldian concepts only; do not invoke obscure secondary literature, specific archival sources, or highly technical neologisms beyond the invented “reverse panopticism” described above. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources. Avoid verbatim quotations from any real text; paraphrase concepts in bureaucratic language instead. The piece should read as a fair, specialist-recognizable satire: a reader familiar with Foucault should recognize that the exaggeration tracks real arguments rather than inventing a strawman.
