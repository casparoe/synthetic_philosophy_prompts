A graduate student submitted the following answer to an exam question in a philosophy of cognitive science course. The question was: "In predictive-processing accounts of perception, explain how precision-weighting resolves conflicts between high-level prior expectations and anomalous sensory input, distinguishing perceptual inference from active inference, and relate both to the free-energy principle."

The student's answer:

"When sensory input conflicts with a strong high-level prior, the system resolves the conflict by modulating precision. Specifically, it down-regulates the precision of the prior representation itself, lowering its resistance to update and allowing the bottom-up prediction error to revise the model. This is perceptual inference: the brain changes its beliefs. By contrast, active inference occurs when the system does not revise the prior but instead acts to change the sensory input—moving the eyes or body—so that the input comes to match the prior. Precision-weighting determines which path is taken: high error precision triggers perceptual inference, while high prior precision triggers active inference. Both paths serve the free-energy principle, but they do so differently: perceptual inference minimizes free energy by altering internal predictions, whereas active inference minimizes it by altering external states, and precision-weighting is the mechanism that allocates resources between these two mutually exclusive strategies."
