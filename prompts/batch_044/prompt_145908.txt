I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy, particularly the literature on AI alignment and adversarial reasoning. In debates over whether structured debate can function as a safety mechanism for advanced artificial systems, I keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern for which I lack a name. The pattern unfolds in two stages. First, a defender of debate-based safety insists that any risk claim must be formulated in terms both parties can verify through shared reasoning protocols; if the critic supplies such a formulation, the defender then objects that this framing strips the risk of its essential urgency or uniqueness, and demands instead a demonstration that relies on precisely the kind of unverifiable intuition or long-horizon speculation that the initial protocol was designed to exclude. Once the critic highlights this reversal, the defender treats it not as a concession but as evidence that the risk is inherently unformalizable, hence outside the scope of debate altogether. I have observed variants of this in discussions of deceptive alignment and instrumental convergence, yet I am uncertain whether it belongs to an established family of argumentative pathologies—perhaps a form of epistemic gatekeeping, a variant of the motte-and-bailey, or something closer to immunization strategies in the philosophy of science. Is there an established concept for this exact two-stage filter—first requiring formalizability, then dismissing formalized versions as inadequate—specifically in the context of safety debates? If not, how ought one classify it, and what are its nearest neighbors in the taxonomy of obstructive argumentation? In your answer, please clearly separate what is settled about the practical limits of debate-based safety mechanisms from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and avoid presenting any single contested view—such as the claim that all long-horizon risks are necessarily unformalizable—as though it were the consensus position.
