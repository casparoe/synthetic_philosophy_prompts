I am a doctoral student in comparative literature and philosophy of language, and next month I am leading a single ninety-minute graduate seminar on the problem of formal-constraint translation—specifically the tension between preserving a poem's formal architecture and preserving its semantic and affective texture—using Douglas Hofstadter's 1997 study Le Ton beau de Marot and its central case, Clément Marot's short poem A une Damoyselle malade, often referred to as Ma mignonne. The audience will be a mixed group of philosophy-of-language graduates and practicing literary translators; none are specialists in Renaissance French, and I do not want them to read the entire book beforehand. I want them to leave not with a history of translation theory but with an operational grasp of why translating a fixed-form poem is necessarily an act of recomposition rather than decoding, and why the beautiful tone is not recoverable by semantic equivalence alone.

Plan the explanatory arc for me: what conceptual dependency must be firmly in place before I even present the poem—perhaps the distinction between semantic content and structural function, or between decoding and analogical reconstruction—so that the audience understands what is genuinely at stake; identify the single carrying comparison or pair of translations that should anchor the session, specifying what each version must preserve and what it must sacrifice in order to make the constraint problem visible rather than merely sentimental; map the predictable misconceptions, including the belief that translation is essentially linguistic decoding, that formal features are ornamental rather than meaning-bearing, and that there exists a hidden best version behind the alternatives, and indicate exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and defused; propose one diagnostic exercise or question for the final fifteen minutes that would reveal whether the participants understand the constraint problem structurally—perhaps by asking them to diagnose a deliberately flawed translation—rather than merely expressing an emotional preference; and state explicitly what I should refuse to adjudicate in this session—such as whether contemporary neural translation resolves the dilemma, or whether Hofstadter's own preference for intimacy over rhyme should be treated as authoritative—so that I can say so out loud and close the discussion cleanly.
