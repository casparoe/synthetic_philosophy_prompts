Last night I had a long, circular argument with a friend about whether Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in GEB actually explains anything about consciousness, or whether it remains only an elegant metaphor. To prepare for our follow-up, I tried to formalize my own position and now suspect it contains hidden inconsistencies. Here is the package of commitments I have arrived at:

(1) The strange loop is the right structural framework for phenomenal self-awareness: consciousness arises when a system's representations turn back upon themselves in a tangled hierarchy, not merely when it processes information about itself.
(2) At the same time, I accept that Gödel's incompleteness results apply strictly to formal systems with recursive axiomatization, and I do not think human cognition is literally such a system—it is noisy, embodied, and not recursively axiomatized in any rigorous sense.
(3) Despite (2), I want to maintain that the Gödelian analogy is stronger than metaphor: there is a genuine structural parallel between self-referential formulas and self-aware minds, specifically in how a system can embed a model of its own rules or representational capacities.
(4) I think this parallel requires that, at some architectural level, there be something analogous to symbolic self-encoding—an analog of Gödel numbering—so that the system's representations can genuinely refer to other representations within the same system.
(5) I also hold that consciousness is fundamentally continuous, sub-symbolic, and embodied; discrete symbolic structures are at best approximate descriptions of cognitive dynamics, not their causal mechanism.
(6) From (1) and (5), I infer that a purely symbolic AI equipped with explicit recursive self-reference—say, a system that literally implements Gödel-style self-reference in its architecture—would still not be conscious, because it lacks the embodied, continuous substrate that makes the loop tangled rather than merely recursive.

My friend argued that I am helping myself to (3) and (4) while undermining them with (2) and (5). I think there may be one genuine tension and one only apparent tension, but I cannot tell which is which. Could you audit this set? For each pair that is genuinely in tension, rank the tension by severity and identify the least costly revision. For pairs that only appear to conflict, explain why they dissolve on closer inspection. Then tell me which single commitment, if dropped or significantly weakened, would resolve the greatest number of conflicts, and whether that revision is too expensive for me to make without abandoning the core of my view.
