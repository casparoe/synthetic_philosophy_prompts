I have been thinking on my own about why practices that once defined social honor can vanish within a single generation, leaving people who once defended them unable even to imagine taking part. The episode I keep returning to is the disappearance of dueling among British gentlemen during the first half of the nineteenth century: it was embedded in the code of honor, technically criminal yet widely tolerated, and then—seemingly suddenly—it became not merely unlawful but socially unthinkable, with the practice fading fastest among certain classes and persisting in isolated pockets. I have encountered two ways of explaining such moral revolutions. One view, associated with thinkers like Bernard Williams, holds that norms of this kind collapse mainly when the underlying social conditions and interests that sustained them erode; on this account, moral argument is largely a rationalization that arrives after the shift. The other view, developed by Kwame Anthony Appiah, proposes that the decisive mechanism is a redefinition of honor itself—once dueling comes to be seen as incompatible with true honor rather than as its necessary expression, the social recognition required to sustain the practice is withdrawn and it collapses. I want you to analyze the British dueling case through these two lenses, but with strict discipline about the mapping. Specify who had standing to confer or withhold honor in this context, what the actual options and social payoffs looked like for a gentleman facing a challenge around the 1820s versus around the 1850s, and whether the fact that dueling was already against the law explains anything about its disappearance or merely shows that legal prohibition followed a prior shift in honor. Then tell me what the episode does to the theories. Does the speed of the collapse, together with its class-specific pattern and the fact that some duels were conducted abroad precisely to evade legal risk, fit the honor-redefinition account better than the erosion-of-interests account? Is there a feature of the case that either theory fails to explain, or that reveals the two descriptions to be the same mechanism viewed at different levels? And does this particular history support the stronger claim that moral revolutions are never genuinely driven by arguments, or does it only show that arguments require a particular social scaffold—perhaps a prior change in who counts as honorable—to become effective?
