I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in philosophical epistemology, and I need you to construct a conceptual taxonomy—delivered entirely as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, tables, or any other structural markers—that classifies positions on the autonomy and reducibility of the pramāṇas in classical and Navya-Nyāya thought. Rather than arranging the material as a list of named schools, build the tree from the logical space of commitments: begin with whether valid cognition requires sources beyond direct perception and inference; then branch on whether testimony (śabda) is irreducible to inference or can be reconstructed as an inferential chain involving speaker reliability and linguistic convention; whether analogical comparison (upamāna) operates as an independent source or collapses into perception coupled with inference; and whether the cognition of absence—such as recognizing that there is no pot on the ground—demands recognition as a distinct fifth means (anupalabdhi) or can be subsumed under one of the canonical four. At each split, formulate the question as a concrete conceptual case or claim that an informed student could answer without already knowing the label of the position it yields, for instance whether verbal knowledge of a distant object requires an irreducible authority relation, or whether negative cognition involves a separate perceptual faculty rather than an absence registered within ordinary perception. Once the logical branches are established, locate within them the standard classical Nyāya view that maintains four autonomous pramāṇas, the Mīmāṃsā-inspired reduction of śabda to anumāna, the Cārvāka or Buddhist denial that upamāna is independent, and the Navya-Nyāya position that introduces anupalabdhi; also accommodate any synthetic stance that preserves the four traditional labels but reinterprets their internal relations. The taxonomy should contain no more than eight terminal leaves. Note explicitly any combinations in the logical space that remain unoccupied—such as a view that accepts five autonomous sources yet denies comparison altogether—and briefly diagnose whether each gap reflects incoherence, historical neglect, or mere unattractiveness. Finally, flag any pair of positions that your branches genuinely cannot separate, particularly where later syntheses replicate the structural commitments of the classical four-fold scheme under revised descriptions. Write the result as a single formal essay or letter in polished continuous prose, addressed to advanced students who need a diagnostic map of conceptual commitments rather than an encyclopedia entry, doctrinal history, or biographical catalog. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citations, or verbatim quotations from texts; do not frame any branch as a question about what a specific named philosopher believed; and present everything in unbroken prose suitable for circulation by email.
