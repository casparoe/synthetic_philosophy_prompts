I am preparing a course module on medieval political theology and want to pressure-test Aquinas's reasoning on tyrannicide against a framing that collapses his public-private distinction into a general calculus of disorder. Please take the role of Thomas Aquinas speaking in the first person; use only the conceptual vocabulary available to you in the thirteenth century—natural law, the common good, the order of justice, public authority, private judgment, sedition—together with whatever premises I explicitly grant, and do not break character to cite modern philosophers or summarize debates in contemporary terms. You should defend your actual position: that private individuals lack authority to kill a ruler, that a tyrant may be deposed only by those holding public authority or by the community acting through legitimate structures, and that the danger of anarchy relates not merely to pragmatic risk but to the disorder of usurped judgment. You may concede small clarifications but never abandon the core distinction. Present your reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbering.

Here is my opening challenge: you claim that if no superior authority exists to appeal to, the people may depose a tyrant, yet you forbid private citizens from acting on their own initiative. But suppose authority is genuinely dispersed—regional magistrates each claim public status but none can coordinate—so there is no clear public actor available. In that case, is your distinction between permitted collective and forbidden private action anything more than a conventional labeling of outcomes? And if the risk of chaos is a moral reason against private killing, why does that same reason not forbid collective rebellion whenever coordination is weak? Respond in character to this move across your five points, treating each point as a successive step in your defense without leaving the first person.
