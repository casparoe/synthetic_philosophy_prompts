I am drafting an essay that has become personally urgent: my parent was recently diagnosed with frontotemporal dementia, and I have observed them sustaining two contradictory autobiographical narratives—one in which they are still actively employed in a profession they left decades ago, and one in which they recognize their present circumstances—without any apparent distress or drive to reconcile them. This has led me to revisit Daniel Dennett’s idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. In the essay I argue that Dennett’s gravitational metaphor tacitly presupposes a single dominant attractor, but that cases of what I call narrative bifurcation—where two equally coherent first-person stories operate within one biological life—suggest that gravity can shear rather than center. I examine the objection that these are merely sub-personal fragments lacking the full structure of selfhood, and I respond that if local narrative coherence is the relevant criterion, then each fragment possesses it, and there is no non-arbitrary reason to privilege one story as the self except pragmatic concern for caregivers. I also briefly distinguish this view from animalist accounts, though I do not develop that contrast fully. I would be grateful for comments on two specific points: first, whether my notion of gravitational shear genuinely extends Dennett’s conceptual framework or merely redescribes familiar split-brain and dissociative phenomena in more ornate language; second, whether the argument commits a category error by treating failures of integration as rival centers of narrative gravity rather than as absences of a unified self. I am not seeking a bibliography or reading list—only an internal critique of the argument’s coherence and its fidelity to the architecture of narrative selfhood.
