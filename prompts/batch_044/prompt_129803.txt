I'm presenting next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on the thesis that, during the Late Bronze Age collapse in the Eastern Mediterranean, the prolonged drought and seismic disruptions linked to the Thera eruption should be treated not as background conditions but as "interlocutory forces"—entities that entered palace archives and diplomatic practice as subjects of oracular consultation and treaty renegotiation, thereby acquiring a form of distributed historical agency. I insist this avoids environmental determinism because human interpretive frameworks—specifically ritual and bureaucratic protocols—mediated how these forces acted, so that climate and volcanism never operated outside the political grammar of the period.

There will also be environmental historians present who are suspicious of determinism but skeptical that archives can support non-human agency claims.
