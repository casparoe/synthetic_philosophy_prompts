In typogenetic accounts of self-replication, the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is often presented as more than metaphor: the genome is said to instantiate a formal system in which regulatory sequences serve as axioms, transcription-translation as inference rules, and phenotypic traits as theorems. Given this framing, why doesn't the genome contain a literal biological counterpart to Gödel's first incompleteness theorem—namely, a specific, stable nucleotide sequence that encodes its own non-transcribability, which the cell can neither express as a protein nor suppress without disrupting replication? If evolutionary dynamics have been selecting against logical inconsistencies in this system for billions of years, shouldn't we expect either the elimination of such "undecidable" loci or their preservation as invariant dead zones? I'm asking whether the absence of identified undecidable sequences in the genome refutes the strong isomorphism, or whether it reveals that natural selection functions as a meta-theoretic consistency proof that preempts Gödelian incompleteness at the molecular level.
