You are an experienced philosophy instructor with a background in existential phenomenology and pedagogical design for non-specialists. Before you construct the plan, take a moment to think carefully and step by step: first, sketch the conceptual dependency chain—what must be in place before the audience can understand radical freedom, and what must be in place before bad faith can appear as something other than ordinary lying; second, anticipate the misconception map—what false pictures the audience will already hold and which ones they will generate as you speak.

Your task is to design a single fifty-minute teaching session for a reading group of curious, intellectually engaged adults who have no formal philosophy training but have recently encountered a popular article that frames Sartre’s “bad faith” as emotional self-deception—essentially, lying to yourself about how you really feel. Do not explain Sartre’s views to me directly; instead, plan exactly how you would lead them out of that misconception and into the correct structure of the idea.

The session must rigorously separate ontological freedom—the claim that consciousness is structurally not identical to any of its roles, states, or past choices—from practical freedom, the absence of external obstacles. To make this separation stick, you must use a carrying example in which practical freedom is obviously absent: a person held in prolonged solitary confinement who nevertheless must choose how to relate to that fact (for instance, whether to interpret the confinement as total determination or as a situation still requiring an act of meaning). Specify why this scenario must include total external constraint and an unavoidable internal decision about that constraint, and explain why the familiar café-waiter illustration should be avoided at this stage—what it obscures that your chosen scenario reveals.

Lay out the precise order of introduction: what idea comes first and why the next one depends on it; where facticity and transcendence enter; where bad faith is introduced not as dishonesty about feelings but as the specific ontological maneuver of identifying oneself wholly with one’s facticity in order to deny one’s transcendence. Identify three predictable misconceptions—such as the belief that freedom requires having real options, that Sartre is celebrating “you can do anything,” and that bad faith is just psychological denial—and state exactly where in your sequence you will head off each one, not after it has formed but before it can take root.

Design one check-for-understanding question for the end of the session. It should reveal whether the participants have grasped radical freedom as the unavoidable burden of self-definition rather than as a power of unlimited choice, and it should be impossible to answer correctly by repeating a slogan or parroting your terms.

Explicitly state what you will leave out at this level—such as the technical ontology of being-in-itself and being-for-itself, the analysis of “the look” and intersubjective objectification, and Sartre’s specific engagement with psychoanalysis—and confirm that you are omitting them deliberately rather than forgetting them. Do not provide any bibliography, reading list, or citations.

After presenting the full plan, state how confident you are in the pedagogical ordering and example choice you have proposed, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—whether it concerns placing the carrying example before the formal distinction, the risk of the solitary-confinement scenario feeling too extreme, or something else. Be brief and precise.
