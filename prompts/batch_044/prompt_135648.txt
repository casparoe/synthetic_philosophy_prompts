I'm taking this course on philosophy of language and we got into Hofstadter's idea from GEB about messages having three layers — a frame message that tells you how to read the thing, an outer message that's just the literal text, and an inner message that's the sender's actual intent or deeper meaning. My friend and I argued about it after class because she thinks the meaning is all in the inner message and the rest is just packaging, but I'm not sure that's right, especially since Hofstadter seems to treat the frame as actually constraining what can be said inside.

This came up at my job, actually. I do copy editing for a small legal publisher, and we have this very specific internal rulebook. We're told to correct formatting, citation styles, and boilerplate headers all day — that's the frame stuff, I guess — but we are strictly forbidden to change ambiguous language in the contract clauses themselves, which feels like the outer message. And we're definitely not allowed to call the attorney and ask "what did you actually mean here?" — the inner message — unless there's a direct logical contradiction. The policy basically treats the literal text as the entire legal meaning, with framing as pure decoration and intent as private and off-limits.

What philosophical commitments about where meaning lives does that editing practice presuppose? Does it have to assume frame messages carry no semantic weight, and that inner messages are epistemically accessable only when they conflict with the text? And if Hofstadter is right that the frame can actually determine or limit the inner message, does our policy become incoherent? What exactly would have to change in our workflow if we adopted a view where meaning is distributed across all three layers instead of locked in the outer one? Please answer in American English.
