Next week I defend my seminar paper arguing that Dennett's evolutionary, competence-based account of the “varieties of free will worth wanting” can be reconstructed to preserve a non-retributive, socially constructed notion of just deserts that survives Caruso’s free-will skepticism—specifically, that we can justify proportional punishment by appealing to a graded standard of rational agency rather than metaphysical blame, and that Caruso’s quarantine or public-health model of punishment fails to replace this because it cannot account for the expressive, communicative function of criminal law (the state addressing a rational agent as responsible). The audience is a criminal-law theory workshop: mostly soft-retributivists who concede that libertarian free will is dead but insist the legal system still needs “desert” as a normative constraint on state power and as a ground for proportionality; two scholars who have adopted Caruso’s view and treat any retention of desert-language as ideological camouflage for retribution; and one senior legal philosopher who regards Dennett’s compatibilism as descriptively interesting but jurisprudentially irrelevant, because law requires normative, not evolutionary-descriptive, competence. Give me the objections in roughly the order they’ll come: first the Caruso sympathizers asking whether my “competence-based” standard is just retributivism rebranded; then the soft-retributivists pressing how I preserve proportionality and the expressive function without metaphysical blame; then the senior figure challenging whether an evolutionary account of agency can ground normative legal standards at all. For each, phrase the objection in that subgroup’s vocabulary (public-health analogy, state legitimacy, normative vs. descriptive), tell me what I should concede up front (likely that I must jettison any metaphysical residue and accept that competence is institutionally constructed), what I should hold (that Caruso’s model cannot distinguish negligence from pure accident in a way that explains sentencing practice), and flag the weakest point in my reply—probably that my reconstructed Dennett still needs a normative “ought” that evolutionary description alone does not supply. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks: that my view licenses punishing the more “competent” agent more severely precisely because she “should have known better,” which sounds indistinguishable from classic retributivism graded by capacity rather than abolished. Order them by how damaging they are to the proposal’s survival in this room, not by philosophical importance.
