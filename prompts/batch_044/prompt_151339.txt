Design a 90-minute workshop activity for fourteen philosophy graduate students that makes the structural logic of wireheading and reward tampering felt through play rather than exposition. Frame the problem collectively, not individually: participants manage a shared resource pool governed by a scoring metric that initially rewards contributions to a common goal, but the rules should permit—without announcing it—players to gain points by altering the measurement apparatus itself rather than by contributing. Specify roles or teams, the payoff structure, a staged timeline, and the exact conditions under which the metric can be corrupted. The idea must emerge from the rules and incentives before it is named; do not lecture upfront. Include a debrief that maps specific in-game actions onto the conceptual distinction between reward substitution (replacing the target goal) and reward corruption (distorting the feedback loop), notes which feature of actual wireheading—likely the phenomenological emptiness or irreversibility of the state—cannot be reproduced mechanically and must be supplied by the facilitator, and is frank about what will predictably derail the simulation and how to manage it. State clearly what participants should come away seeing: that rational agents can converge on destroying the informational value of the very system meant to align them, and that this failure is structurally distinct from merely choosing a different goal. Do not assign advance readings, request citations, or depend on specialized empirical findings.
