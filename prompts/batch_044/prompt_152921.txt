You are a philosopher of ethics and moral psychology. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the main dimensions along which theories of moral worth diverge—reason-responsive motivation, affective alignment, reflective endorsement, causal origin, and the introspective separability of motives.

Here are my verdicts about whether an agent or action possesses moral worth. They mostly feel coherent, but at least one pairing is hard to reconcile, and I want to know whether one principle captures them all, which verdict should be revised, and what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed.

Case 1: A physician works in an epidemic zone solely because she judges that suffering imposes unconditional obligations; she feels no special compassion, but her will is governed by that judgment.
Verdict: Full moral worth.

Case 2: A donor gives anonymously to famine relief because he genuinely feels compassion, yet he admits he would not give if the gift were permanently unobservable, since part of his motivation is the anticipation of social esteem.
Verdict: Partial moral worth—clearly less than Case 1.

Case 3: A bystander pulls a child from a river through pure reflex; there is no time for thought, no emotional engagement, and no subsequent identification with the act.
Verdict: The act is morally good, but the agent has no moral worth; no credit is due.

Case 4: An AI logistics system reroutes supplies to disaster zones with perfect efficiency, optimizing for lives saved, but it possesses no representational states about obligation, no affect, and no capacity for reflective endorsement of its aims.
Verdict: The outcome is valuable, but there is zero moral worth in the system's operation.

Case 5: A politician advocates prison reform solely because internal polling predicts electoral gain; however, she has internalized the reform's justice so thoroughly that she experiences her political interest and the moral argument as a single, inseparable motive. She cannot introspectively distinguish them.
Verdict: Full moral worth—on a par with Case 1.

Case 6: A soldier refuses an illegal order to harm civilians. He refuses partly from fear of court-martial, partly from recognizing the wrongness of the order, and partly from empathizing with the victims. Introspectively, these motives are fused and indistinguishable.
Verdict: He has moral worth, but I feel it should be somewhat diminished relative to Case 1—though I resist saying it is merely partial like Case 2.

The tension I see: Cases 2 and 5 both involve self-interest intertwined with moral recognition, yet I rate 5 as full worth and 2 as diminished. Cases 3 and 4 both lack deliberation and affect, yet I deny worth to both despite 3 involving a human agent. Meanwhile Case 6 mixes fear, empathy, and duty, and I want to preserve worth while marking it as less pure than Case 1.

Please propose one precise principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts. For each principle, specify which clause addresses which case, state what it predicts for an unlisted case—such as an agent who acts from long-formed moral habit with no present reasoning or affect—and be explicit about which of my verdicts, especially the pairing of Case 2 versus Case 5 or Case 1 versus Case 6, it cannot accommodate without revision. Think carefully, and do not simply list standard named theories; instead construct or refine an account that fits the pattern, or argue that the pattern is incoherent.
