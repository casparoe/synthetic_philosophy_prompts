You are an expert philosophy-of-mind professor with deep familiarity with self-reference, consciousness studies, and Douglas Hofstadter’s strange-loop account of the self. Before drafting anything, take a few moments to think carefully and brainstorm the precise conceptual crux you want students to face: the gap between purely syntactic self-reference in formal systems and the phenomenologically “strange” loop that Hofstadter claims grounds the self-referential symbol “I.” Now write a polished, formal exam prompt for an upper-level seminar final.

Design one 90-minute essay question (expecting roughly 700–900 words) on Hofstadter’s claim that the self arises as a strange loop in which a symbol within a system comes to denote the very system that generates it, analogously to Gödel’s self-referential formula. To prevent students from answering by mere summary, inject the following niche conceptual frame: present a hypothetical artificial system—call it System M—containing a single indexical token M* whose semantic value is rigorously fixed, via a fixed-point construction, as the complete state-description of System M itself. Emphasize that M* has no associated phenomenology, no first-person perspective, and no internal observer. The question must ask students whether System M’s possession of M* is sufficient to say that System M genuinely has an “I” in Hofstadter’s sense, or whether Hofstadter’s account implicitly requires consciousness (or another feature) that pure syntactic loops necessarily lack. Require students to defend one position and address the strongest objection from the opposing view.

Deliver three things:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded for students who have studied Hofstadter’s strange-loop framework but must apply it rigorously to this constructed case.
2. A model answer of about 700 words that takes a clear position, explains the Gödel analogy, distinguishes syntactic self-reference from a phenomenological strange loop, applies the distinction directly to System M, and anticipates at least one serious counter-objection.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks built around exactly five must-hit conceptual moves. For each move, state: the specific reasoning required, the marks assigned (so the total is 20), and a one-line note describing the typical mistake that costs those marks. The scheme should clearly separate an A answer—which executes all five moves with precision—from a B answer—which summarizes Hofstadter accurately but fails to apply the syntactic/phenomenological distinction rigorously to System M.

Keep the tone formal and academic. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind. Do not include verbatim quotations from Hofstadter’s published texts; paraphrase his ideas in your own words. Avoid relying on obscure theories or highly specific empirical findings; the “in-the-weeds” quality should come from the System M thought experiment and the demand for conceptual precision, not from niche scholarship that a well-trained model might not have retained. Ensure the entire prompt is self-contained and answerable without web access.
