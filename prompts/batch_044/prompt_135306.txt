You are delivering the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Voss, a physician-ethicist who spent four decades defending the view that an advance directive possesses what she called narrative authority: not because the demented patient retains the earlier self’s preferences, but because the earlier self authored the life-story within which present care takes its meaning. Half the room are bioethicists and clinicians familiar with her argument that diachronic personal identity is largely irrelevant to this binding force; the other half are relatives who observe her now—living with advanced dementia, apparently serene, and occasionally resisting interventions that her directive explicitly requires. She left instructions that any summary of her career avoid both false consolation and clinical detachment. Compose a five-minute toast that explains, for the non-specialists, why she believed a signed document could obligate a person who no longer remembers writing it, without presenting her present contentment as a simple refutation of her view. For the specialists, honor the intellectual rigor of her position and explicitly acknowledge the unresolved tension between narrative authority and present welfare, but do not resolve that tension through sentiment or through a philosophical victory for either side. The toast should treat the contradiction itself—between the authority of the earlier author and the happiness of the present subject—as part of the legacy being celebrated, not as a problem for the speaker to dissolve.
