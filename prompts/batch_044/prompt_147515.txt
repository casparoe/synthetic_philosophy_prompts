I write about philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence for a science and ideas publication, and I have been developing an interpretation of Daniel Dennett's work on counterfeit people that I have not seen fully articulated elsewhere. The position—call it the misfire thesis—runs as follows. Dennett's classic argument is that there is no detectable internal property, separate from behavioral and functional organization, that distinguishes a genuinely conscious being from a perfect behavioral duplicate. From this, many conclude that if an artificial system is functionally isomorphic to a human interlocutor, the intentional stance applies to it with the same legitimacy as to a person, and that moral status follows. I think this extension is mistaken, but not for the usual reason that AI lacks some hidden consciousness-substance. Rather, the intentional stance, on Dennett's own account, is a predictive strategy anchored by evolutionary history and causal integration into a social world. A large language model produces behavior that triggers the stance in human observers, yet it is the product of a design-stance object optimized for statistical token prediction. Because the system's outputs are not embedded in the causal-historical network that makes the stance genuinely predictive about an integrated agent, the observer's attribution of intentions is not a pragmatic prediction but a projection onto a statistical artifact. So LLMs are not counterfeit people in Dennett's original sense—entities designed or evolved to deceive us into applying the stance—but entities that cause the stance to misfire without corresponding deception.

What would have to be true for the misfire thesis to be wrong? I am looking for an honest map of its vulnerabilities, not a list of generic objections. Which distinctions—for instance, between the predictive utility of a stance and the causal origins of its target, or between being an appropriate target of the intentional stance and possessing moral status—could collapse the argument? What empirical findings about LLM architecture, training dynamics, or sustained social interaction would weaken it? Could defenders of AI moral status show, using Dennett's own commitments, that causal-historical embedding is irrelevant to whether the stance is appropriately applied? And is the thesis at risk of being insulated into unfalsifiability—where any successful LLM interaction is dismissed as projection, and any failure is taken as confirmation of misfire? Please distinguish, throughout, what is settled about Dennett's actual views on consciousness, the intentional stance, and artificial systems from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind who study these questions. Do not present the misfire thesis, or its denial, as a consensus position; where my interpretation of Dennett depends on a contested reading, say so explicitly.
