As a novelist preoccupied with the architecture of expectation, I wish to compare two conceptual frameworks for the surprise exam paradox. The first is the familiar epistemic framework: the students derive, through successive eliminations, that the exam cannot occur on Friday, thereby undermining the conditions for genuine surprise. The second is a framework I shall call the narrative-contract framework, which treats the teacher's announcement not as a proposition to be evaluated for truth, but as a performative utterance that establishes a dramatic contract with the class—analogous to an author's promise of an unforeseeable twist. Compare these two frameworks with precision. Does the narrative-contract framework resolve the paradox by locating the contradiction in the pragmatics of authority and expectation rather than in the students' logic? Or does it merely translate the epistemic contradiction into theatrical terms without dissolving it? Furthermore, if we extend the comparison to fiction—where an omniscient narrator's knowledge can similarly deflate suspense—does either framework illuminate why some narrative promises feel broken when revealed, while others feel earned? Conclude by identifying which framework, if either, preserves a meaningful distinction between logical impossibility and disappointed expectation.
