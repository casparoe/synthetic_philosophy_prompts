Last evening my closest friend and I argued for nearly three hours about the modern revival of Stoicism, specifically about how the ancient distinction between what lies within our power and what does not applies to professional responsibility today. The immediate occasion was a situation at my workplace. I lead a small product team, and one engineer has been submitting code with serious security flaws. I have raised the issue with him repeatedly, documented every instance, and proposed a structured improvement plan, but I lack the authority to hire or fire; that power belongs to a director who has refused to intervene. At the same time, my own quarterly review is evaluated partly on the quality of the team's output. My friend, who has been attending a local Stoic study group and reading the contemporary revivalists, insists that I should apply the framework of radical acceptance: the director's inaction and the engineer's competence are outside my control, whereas my own documentation, communication, and emotional response are fully within it. He went further, invoking the modern three-part taxonomy—things fully in our power, things not at all in our power, and things only partly in our power—to place team output squarely in the second category. He argued that my anxiety about my review reflects a failure to internalize this division, and that modern Stoicism's therapeutic aim is precisely to free us from such misplaced concern. I objected that the revival risks turning a metaphysical claim into a moral shortcut: if I genuinely care about the users who depend on secure code, treating the security failures as none of my concern looks less like equanimity and more like disengagement disguised as virtue. I also noted that my review is not entirely beyond my influence, since I can escalate, reallocate tasks, or document risk in ways that might shift the director's calculus—suggesting that the middle category of partial control swallows the practical question rather than resolving it. We ended without agreement. I am not asking for generic workplace advice. Rather, given the modern revival's emphasis on emotional self-regulation and its expanded taxonomy of control, I want to know whether this framework actually provides a principled boundary between what I should strive to change and what I should genuinely release, or whether it collapses distinctions that matter for professional ethics.
