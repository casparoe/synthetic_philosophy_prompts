I've been trying to sort out my thoughts on Hofstadter's strange loops and the whole "inviolate level" thing after staring at Escher's Drawing Hands in my philosophy of mind seminar. Here's roughly where I've landed, but I'm not sure it actually hangs together: (1) I think strange loops—where a system loops back on itself—are basically what creates the sense of self, so they're not just interesting but necessary for consciousness. (2) I think Escher's Drawing Hands is the perfect image of this: there's always a level that draws the other level but can't be drawn itself—the paper, the artist's hand, whatever. That's the inviolate level. (3) I think this applies everywhere, not just in art but in formal systems (Gödel) and in minds—there's always something outside the loop, and it's metaphysical, not just practical. (4) But I also think that if an AI had full access to its own weights and architechture, it could in principle have complete self-knowledge, and that wouldn't destroy it—maybe its hardware is just its "paper"? (5) I think the reason Gödel's theorem hits arithmetic is exactly the same structural reason Escher's drawing works—representation always needs a frame outside itself. (6) And I think tangled hierarchies only really exist in embodied, physical systems; in pure math they're just infinite regress with no real "tangle."

Does this hold together? Specifically, does (4) contradict (2) and (3), or does the hardware move save it? Also, does (5) conflate two different kinds of limitation—logical vs representational—in a way that collapses them? And is (6) just an extra claim, or does it undermine (1) if consciousness needs embodiment but strange loops are formal? I want an audit: which pairs are in real tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest revision—like, should I drop the metaphysical claim in (3) and keep it practical, or is (1) the one that has to go? Rank them by severity and say what each fix costs.
