I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been working through a problem that my roommate—who is studying ethics—called the paradox of deontology. As I understand it, the puzzle is that deontological rules appear absolute, such as prohibitions on lying or harming the innocent, yet in extreme cases rigid adherence seems to produce outcomes that violate the very moral considerations that justified the rule. For an essay I am drafting, I want to argue that this tension is largely an artifact of treating normative obligations as monotonic logical constraints, analogous to unconditional if-statements in code that never allow for structured exceptions. Specifically, I propose modeling duties as defeasible defaults within a non-monotonic framework: a primary obligation holds unless a precisely defined exception condition—not merely a better overall outcome—triggers an override. I connect this formal idea to Ross's paradox in standard deontic logic, in which an obligation to perform one action seems to entail an obligation to perform that action or its opposite, and I suggest that both the logical paradox and the ethical one arise from applying monotonic operators to norms that are actually context-sensitive. I further speculate that Kant's categorical imperative, interpreted not as a universal quantifier over all worlds but as a consistency check on a system's exception-handling rules, can avoid the paradox without collapsing into consequentialism. Before I develop this further, I would appreciate comments on the coherence of the framing. Is it accurate to treat Ross's logical paradox and the substantive ethical paradox as structurally related, or am I conflating a technical artifact in formal logic with a genuine moral conflict? Does my analogy to defeasible reasoning in software misrepresent how deontologists actually address hard cases—do they invoke exception conditions, or do they deny that true conflicts are resolvable? And is there an obvious objection or line of argument I am missing that renders this approach redundant? I am not seeking citations or a bibliography, only an assessment of whether the core analogy holds and where a trained philosopher would say I have gone wrong.
