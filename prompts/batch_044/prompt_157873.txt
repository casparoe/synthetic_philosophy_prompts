I am putting together the final take-home for a graduate seminar in intergenerational ethics. I want a single essay question that tests whether students understand the structural mechanics of the nonidentity problem or are merely labeling it. To prevent regurgitation, I have introduced a hypothetical framing I am calling the Perturbation-Resilient Nonidentity (PRN) condition: rather than asking whether a different policy produces numerically different people, we ask whether the actual set of future persons is invariant under epsilon-perturbations to present resource-allocation parameters, and whether any moral obligation should attach to that invariance class rather than to the particular identities within it. The exam should ask students to determine whether this PRN framing dissolves, relocates, or intensifies the classic tension between harm-to-none and wrong-to-none arguments. Please write the exam prompt itself (intended for a 90-minute response), a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves. The scheme should assign specific point values that sum to 25, and for each move it should include a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode—e.g., conflating modal invariance with actual-world identity persistence, or treating the wrong-to-none claim as a simple empirical observation rather than a normative structure. The difficulty should come from forcing students to apply the invariance logic precisely, not from recalling bibliography.
