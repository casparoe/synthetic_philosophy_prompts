I am serving this term on our university’s Committee on Professional Conduct and Research Integrity, and we face an appeals case that has split the panel along lines that seem to reflect a deeper disagreement about the source of moral authority in institutional life. The case involves a doctoral candidate in qualitative sociology who, during an ethnographic study approved under our standard data-retention protocol, discovered what she interpreted as clear evidence of financial misconduct by a senior administrator interviewed in the study. Our protocol requires immediate anonymization and destruction of raw audio within seventy-two hours; she refused to destroy the recordings, arguing that her individual conscience as a moral agent required her to preserve evidence of wrongdoing, even at the cost of violating the procedural safeguards she had contractually accepted. The administration wants her sanctioned for protocol breach; she appeals, claiming that no institutional rule can legitimately demand complicity in concealing harm. I am taking a graduate seminar on comparative moral psychology and cross-cultural ethics, and our discussions have contrasted what might be called an order-oriented folk morality—where procedural fidelity and institutional coherence are themselves moral goods, often associated with German administrative and ethical tradition—with a conscience-oriented folk morality more common in American contexts, where individual moral judgment retains ultimate authority over bureaucratic compliance. The committee must decide whether to uphold the sanction, overturn it, or impose a conditional resolution, and we need a written opinion for our files that treats both frameworks seriously rather than reducing one to mere legalism and the other to mere individualism.

Please draft the committee’s decision memo recommending one of these outcomes with a concrete timeframe for implementation. The memo should present a clear bottom line—uphold, overturn, or modify the sanction—and provide reasoning that the researcher and the administration could actually respond to, not just abstract philosophy. It should include an honest account of the strongest argument on the losing side: if you recommend upholding the sanction, explain why the conscience claim is not merely self-serving but reflects a genuine moral tradition that our institution risks disrespecting; if you recommend overturning, explain why procedural collapse poses a real institutional danger that cannot be dismissed by appealing to individual virtue. I am particularly interested in whether the comparative folk-morality framing helps resolve the case or whether applying it actually obscures the specific obligations created by informed consent and research contracts. The memo should not cite specific scholarly works or request further literature; it should stand on conceptual reasoning that a committee member without access to a library could evaluate.
