I have been working through the standard gradual-replacement defense of mind uploading: if biological neurons are replaced one by one with functionally equivalent synthetic substrates, preserving local synaptic weights and global dynamics, and if the process is slow enough to avoid any subjective discontinuity, then the resulting system should retain personal identity through continuous psychological and phenomenal continuity. The familiar objection is substrate chauvinism—the intuition that biological tissue carries some non-functional essence necessary for being "me"—but that strikes me as bare assertion rather than argument. More recently, however, I have been worried about what I will call the "binding-gradient" problem. Suppose phenomenal unity depends not only on functional integration but also on continuous physical coherence of a specific kind, such as the uninterrupted electrochemical dynamics of biological tissue. Then each synthetic substitution might imperceptibly dilute the substrate-sensitive binding that underwrites first-person unity. By the end, one could have a fully synthetic brain that is psychologically continuous, behaviorally identical, and subjectively reports being the same person, yet is phenomenally "faded"—a functional replica with a distributed or weakened first-person perspective rather than the original subject of experience. If so, gradual replacement does not solve the uploading problem; it obscures it, because the absence of a sharp break makes it impossible to notice when the original self has been replaced by a diluted successor. Is this binding-gradient objection a recognized problem in the philosophy of personal identity and consciousness, or does it rest on a false presupposition—perhaps that phenomenal binding is substrate-sensitive in this way, or that functional replacement entails a gradual erosion of first-person unity rather than its preservation? In your answer, please separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view about substrate-dependence or continuity conditions as though it were the consensus.
