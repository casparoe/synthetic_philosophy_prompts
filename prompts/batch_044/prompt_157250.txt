I serve as the resident philosopher of epistemology and ethics on the funding review board of the Institute for Foundational Theoretical Research, a government-backed body that awards multi-year grants to programs in theoretical physics and cosmology. Each quarter we must decide whether to commit substantial resources to research proposals whose central working assumptions—certain interpretations of quantum gravity, cosmological multiverse hypotheses, or other speculative frameworks—currently lack direct empirical confirmation and may not acquire it within the grant period. Our panel comprises physicists, mathematicians, and administrators with no formal philosophical training, yet they must apply the decision criteria consistently and sign off on each recommendation. I need a reusable procedural framework, rigorously derived from the James–Clifford debate on the ethics of belief, that translates James’s conditions for permissible belief—options that are genuinely forced, living, and momentous—together with Clifford’s absolute evidentialist prohibition, into an ordered checklist or flowchart that a non-philosopher can run in under an hour per proposal. The procedure must distinguish institutional resource commitments from private doxastic states, specify tests for whether suspension of belief is itself a practical decision rather than a neutral default, and identify precisely where quantitative assessment ends and an irreducible normative judgment by the board begins. It should include worked applications to three hypothetical proposals: first, a program seeking observational signatures of a specific multiverse model that is currently empirically underdetermined; second, a proposal that adopts a particular quantum interpretation solely because it simplifies formal derivation without generating distinct empirical predictions; and third, a long-established theoretical paradigm that has recently faced serious internal challenges, where the procedure yields a negative recommendation that the senior investigators will almost certainly reject. At each stage, indicate where the framework risks misfiring—particularly by treating institutional funding commitments as if they were individual beliefs—and state clearly what it cannot adjudicate, such as disputes over prior probabilities of metaphysical frameworks or the social utility of maintaining scientific morale.
