I have been thinking about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness on my own, without any formal academic training—mostly through library books, online essays, and long conversations with friends who share the interest but are not specialists. The standard debate, as I understand it, pits illusionists, who claim that qualitative experiences like the hurtfulness of pain or the redness of red are systematic misrepresentations of our own cognitive processes, against realists who hold that such phenomenal properties are genuinely present in consciousness. Yet I keep returning to a related observation that philosophers discuss under the label of transparency: when you try to introspect your own experience, you do not seem to encounter mental representations, neural mechanisms, or representational vehicles; instead, you seem to look straight through to the world itself—to the pain, the color, the shape. Illusionists typically treat this transparency as just one more feature of the phenomenal illusion. But I suspect this may be too quick. Perhaps illusionism actually requires two distinct illusions rather than one: first, an illusion that there are phenomenal properties at all; second, a separate meta-illusion that renders the representational medium itself invisible to introspection, so that we mistake a representation for direct access. If the second requires its own explanatory machinery—say, a systematic misrepresentation of the representational vehicle—then illusionism may be less parsimonious than its defenders claim. I want a proposal for an independent research essay of about 8,000 words, to be completed over four months, that treats this division as its central question. The proposal should state the precise question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why existing answers in the illusionism literature leave open whether transparency collapses into the phenomenal illusion or stands apart; describe the approach—comparing a single-illusion model with a dual-illusion model by borrowing conceptual distinctions from representationalist accounts of transparency, without committing to realism about phenomenal properties; say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; specify what result would count as success and what would count as failure; and give an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for the available time, such as being forced to resolve the entire representationalist theory of mental representation first. Please clearly separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present any contested view—whether illusionist, realist, or representationalist—as if it were the consensus.
