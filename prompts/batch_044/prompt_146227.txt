I'm chairing the curatorial advisory board for the Riverside City History Museum, and we must circulate a formal written opinion before next Tuesday's vote on the permanent gallery proposal titled "From Well to Tap: The Making of Modern Water." The lead curator has structured the exhibition around a narrative of progressive convergence: the private water companies of the 1870s, the 1884 neighborhood mutual-aid wells, the 1892 municipal socialist water cooperative, and the 1901 centralized public works are all presented as successive approximations of our current integrated system, each correcting the failures of the last. Our consulting historian objects that this is a clear case of infrastructural Whig history—most severely in its treatment of the 1892 cooperative, whose members advocated decentralized, worker-controlled distribution and were forcibly dissolved by state authorities; framing them as "early public-health pioneers" retrospectively legitimizes the centralized system that crushed them and erases the violence of consolidation. The curator responds that school groups and general audiences require a coherent through-line, that emphasizing rupture and suppression makes the gallery pedagogically incoherent, and that practical funding realities—including our district contract—depend on presenting a legible civic-progress narrative.

Please draft the board's written opinion addressed to the museum director, recommending a concrete decision—approve with mandatory narrative conditions, reject and remand for redesign, or approve only with a required parallel display—together with reasoning that treats the curator's pedagogical and institutional concerns as the strongest consideration on the losing side. Your memo should identify precisely which display elements, such as the chronological timeline wall, the "Foundations of Public Health" room, and the biographical panels, instantiate retrospective legitimation, and specify how each must be altered; explain why the cooperative's actual program—explicit rejection of state-centralized water ownership—cannot be honestly incorporated into a "steps toward us" framework without historical distortion; propose an alternative organizational principle that preserves visitor coherence without teleology, perhaps arranging the gallery around genuinely incompatible models of maintenance, access, and ownership rather than chronological progress; and include a time-bound implementation plan should conditions be imposed.

I should note that our education director, with whom I discussed this at length, fears that a discontinuist framing will violate our school-district contract's requirement for a "clear civic-progress narrative," so the memo should also address whether accommodating that contractual clause would itself compromise the board's duty to avoid Whig historiography, and if so, how the board might negotiate that tension without simply capitulating to it.
