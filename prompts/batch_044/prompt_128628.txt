The debate over whether sentience admits of degrees — with insects possessing it weakly, mammals strongly, and thermostats not at all — has stalled despite broad agreement on comparative neuroscience and on the badness of suffering. Gradualists hold that moral status tracks a continuous property such as phenomenal integration, so harming an insect is wrong but proportionally less grave than harming a human; thresholdists deny that consciousness comes in shades, insisting that below a discrete architectural boundary there is no subject of harm, however behaviourally responsive a system may be. Is this a substantive disagreement about whether phenomenal consciousness is granular or binary, a verbal dispute about whether 'sentience' denotes a capacity or a normative status, or are the sides answering different questions — one descriptive, about the distribution of experience, the other normative, about the conditions for having interests? I suspect the impasse masks a hidden disagreement about evidential standards: gradualists treat behavioural simplicity as scaling downward, while thresholdists treat it as falling off a qualitative cliff. Diagnose the disagreement using what each position actually implies about insects and thermostats, and say whether progress requires an empirical result about neural binding, a conceptual distinction between phenomenal intensity and moral considerability, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed.
