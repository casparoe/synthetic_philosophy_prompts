I am a retired epidemiologist who, since leaving laboratory work, has begun attending a philosophy of science discussion group composed largely of other retired natural scientists—physicists, ecologists, and a mathematician—most of whom have strong frequentist intuitions about sampling and error but little exposure to formal decision theory or anthropic reasoning. Next month I have volunteered to lead a single ninety-minute session on the distinction between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, taking Nick Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher thought experiment as the central cautionary case rather than as a passing anecdote. The group tends to treat observer-selection effects as correctable sampling biases—errors that diminish with larger N—and at least two members have already suggested that a theory predicting more observers should be preferred because it offers more opportunities for verification. I want a teaching plan, not a lecture script: first, the precise dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that each layer rests on the previous one; second, the one concrete carrying scenario—specifically a modified ecological field-survey case in which an observer discovers herself alone in an archive of unrecorded census sheets, deliberately lacking any multiverse cosmology—that should bear the full weight of the distinction and that must possess exactly the right structural features and omissions; third, the predictable misconceptions that will form at each stage, especially the conflation of self-locating belief revision with ordinary Bayesian conditioning under a uniform prior, and the separate slide from self-indication reasoning into a spurious moral obligation to maximize population size, with explicit instructions on where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and defused; fourth, a closing diagnostic question that reveals whether participants understand why the presumptuous philosopher's inference is structurally flawed rather than merely empirically risky; and finally, an explicit statement of what technical apparatus—such as the formal derivation of the SIA weighting factor or the full literature on simulation arguments—should be openly refused at this level with an acknowledgment of the refusal. The plan should assume no assigned readings, should not ask participants to consult sources, and should not include bibliographies, citations, or further reading lists.
