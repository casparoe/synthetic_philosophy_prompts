For a conceptual ethics workshop, construct a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with labeled branch questions—of positions within and adjacent to tranquilism, defined here as the view that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, with all other states being at best instrumentally valuable. Do not organize the tree by historical school, author, or tradition; instead, build it from the logical space using four concrete diagnostic splits that genuinely separate coherent positions.

First split: Is tranquility purely privative (the mere non-occurrence of craving and suffering) or a positive qualitative state (a distinct phenomenology of peace or release)? Frame this with a concrete case: a being in permanent anhedonia, free of pain and desire but reporting no content whatsoever—does this state fully realize the good, or is a positive quality missing?

Second split: Does meta-craving—the desire to be free of desire—count as a self-defeating form of craving, or as a permissible instrumental motive? Use a case: a practitioner takes a compound that eliminates craving but produces a subtle background sense of contentment; is the resulting state as tranquilist-valuable as the natural cessation of desire?

Third split: What is the scope of tranquilist concern? Does it extend to all functionally suffering systems, only to beings with phenomenal consciousness, or only to self-reflective agents? Frame with an engineered ecosystem of simple organisms that avoid noxious stimuli but show no awareness—do they have a tranquilist interest, and if so, on what basis?

Fourth split: How should tranquilism guide large-scale ecological intervention? Is suppressing wild-animal suffering obligatory (since suffering is the sole disvalue), forbidden (because intervention introduces new disturbances, dependencies, and human craving), or value-indifferent (because natural processes carry no moral weight)? Illustrate with a program that eliminates predation but requires constant human monitoring that generates stress.

At each node, phrase the branch as a claim or case-answerable question that a respondent could answer without knowing any philosophical label. The leaves should be named if they map onto a recognizable position—e.g., hard privative tranquilism, positive-state release view, biocentric interventionist tranquilism, ecological non-interventionist tranquilism—or marked unnamed/unexplored if the combination is coherent but apparently undefended. For each unnamed leaf, include one sentence diagnosing why it may be unoccupied (incoherent, unstable, or merely overlooked).

Additionally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate: specifically, note whether a hybrid that accepts positive-state tranquility but rejects meta-craving is distinguishable from a strict privative view that permits instrumental meta-desires, and identify the further case that would settle the matter. Keep the tree to no more than sixteen leaves. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts; the deliverable is a conceptual sorting instrument, not a literature review. Write formally, as an instruction for producing the classification tree.
