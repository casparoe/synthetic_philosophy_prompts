A speaker at a recent interdisciplinary workshop on scientific norms offered the following gloss on the handicap principle and its cultural extension: "Zahavi's core insight is that any behavior that imposes a net cost on the bearer and still persists in a population must function as an honest signal of underlying quality, because only high-quality individuals can absorb the cost. Translating this to academic practice, exhaustive citation is a handicap: it consumes time and cognitive resources that less capable scholars cannot spare, which is why citation density correlates with researcher competence. Peer review therefore operates as an evolutionary filter, rewarding dense citation networks not for their epistemic value but because they serve as costly displays that free-riders cannot replicate. Critics who decry citation bloat miss the point: citation is not primarily about guiding readers but about signaling membership in an elite capable of bearing its costs, which explains why attempts to streamline referencing consistently fail in competitive disciplines." I find this seductive but suspect it conflates biological handicap signaling with economic signaling theory and treats an institutional norm as an evolved trait without argument. Could you audit the summary precisely: distinguish what is faithful to Zahavi's original biological framework, what imports Michael Spence's labor-market logic without acknowledgment, whether the correlation between citation density and competence is being assumed rather than demonstrated, and whether the speaker's normative defense of citation bloat actually follows from the signaling premises? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between index signals and handicap signals undermines the speaker's claim that citation is necessarily costly, since some references may be cheap to include but hard to fake without actual mastery.
