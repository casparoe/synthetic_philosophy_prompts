After a long argument last night with a friend about whether democratic collapse is primarily a failure of institutional design or a dissolution of informal norms, I want to press a specific case through a disciplined theoretical mapping. Take the crisis of 133 BCE—Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus’s agrarian reform, his use of the tribunate to bypass senatorial consent, the violence at the assembly that followed, and the Senate’s subsequent extralegal killing of a sitting magistrate—and analyze it through the modern comparative-politics framework of executive aggrandizement, in which an elected officeholder incrementally weakens checks through technically legal means. I need a careful analogical mapping: who occupies the role of the aggrandizing executive, what correspond to legislative and judicial veto points in the Roman architecture (the tribunate, senatorial auctoritas, the absence of a separate judiciary), and where the structural differences—personal imperium, the lack of party organizations, the fusion of legislative and executive functions in the magistracies—force the analogy to break or be revised. My friend insisted that Gracchus was not an aggrandizer but a norm-breaker reacting to senatorial hardball; accordingly, assess whether the concept of “preemptive institutional hardball”—using constitutional offices not merely to obstruct but to permanently disable an opponent’s veto points—applies more plausibly to the Senate’s later actions than to Gracchus’s initial reform, and whether the episode ultimately supports the view that Rome fell because norms of mutual forbearance dissolved, or because the republic’s design made such forbearance structurally impossible. Be explicit about where the theory is silent, do not force the analogy where institutional differences undermine it, and say what the episode in turn reveals about the limits of applying modern backsliding categories to pre-modern republican politics.
