My study partner walked me into this bootstrapping thing and I can't find the exit.

(1) I have direct, non-inferential knowledge of my present mental states -- pains, seemings, the feeling that 2+2=4.
(2) For any claim B about the world -- whether dinosaurs roamed or the EU suffers a democratic deficit -- there's some mental state M that could serve as evidential support for B.
(3) Since I directly know every M that occurs, and knowledge closes over competant inference, I can in principle derive B from the right M.
(4) So I should be able to know B just by sitting in a room reasoning.
(5) But I clearly don't know the instituional architecture of the EU, or specific fossil records, without actually studying them.
(6) Therefore bootstrapping is impossible and I can't even justify leaving my armchair.

He says this shows "easy knowledge" is a myth because if it were real I'd be omniscient. I feel like there's a scope slip between "there exists an M for each B" and "I actually have B-derived knowledge for each B," but I can't name it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd like to keep (1) and the idea that closure works when you actually do the reasoning, but I'm not sure that's consistent.
