I'm in this public-history seminar and we keep going in circles about when a community's collective memory should just... win? over the actual archival record. So here are my gut takes on a few concrete situations, and I want to know if there's actually a principle hiding behind them or if I'm being definatly inconsistent.

Case one: there's this small fishing village with an oral tradition about a "great rescue" in 1923 where locals saved sailors from a shipwreck. The harbor logs and newspaper archives say the ship sank with no survivors and no rescue happened at all. The village still holds an annual ceremony honoring the rescuers. I think the ceremony should stay, even if historians correct the record in textbooks. The memory does real social work.

Case two: a diaspora group insists that a specific massacre in their homeland was deliberately planned by the central state, but declassified documents and demographic records suggest it was more of an uncontrolled pogrom with no central order. The community uses the "planned genocide" framing to maintain political solidarity. Here I feel like history should win, or at least the record shouldn't be rewritten just to preserve solidarity. But I'm not totally sure why this feels different from the rescue story.

Case three: a town remembers its founder as a benevolent settler who negotiated peacefully with indigenous groups. The treaty texts and land-sale records show he used coercive tactics and fraud. The town's school curriculum teaches the benevolent version. I think the curriculum has to change, no question.

Case four: an indigenous community remembers a sacred site being destroyed in the 1950s, but aerial photos and government surveys from that era show the site was physically intact then and only degraded later due to natural erosion. The community's elders say the destruction was spiritual, not physical, and the photos miss that entirely. I think... both can be true? Or maybe the historical record should include the spiritual destruction even if it doesn't match physical evidence? I'm waffling hard on this one.

So is there one principle that gets all four verdicts, or do I have to drop one to stay consistent? And what would it say about a case I haven't listed — say, a national museum curating an exibit about a labor strike, where the union's oral histories describe police violence that isn't in the official police reports. Should the exibit present the union's version as equally valid?

Please take a definite stance — don't just lay out options and leave me to pick. Tell me which of my verdicts I'd need to overturn if I want a clean principle, why the remaining ones hang together, and what your principle predicts for the museum strike case.
