As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I am organizing a 90-minute workshop for about fifteen technically-minded participants—mostly engineers and designers with little prior exposure to philosophy of mind. I want them to feel, through direct participation, the structure of a tangled hierarchy and the illusion of an inviolate level, using M. C. Escher’s Drawing Hands as the guiding image: a system in which one layer appears to generate or govern the layer above it, only to turn out to be embedded within and sustained by that very layer.

To prevent this from becoming a generic discussion of strange loops, ground the exercise in a software-architecture analogy drawn from programming-language design: a meta-circular interpreter, in which the interpreter for a language is itself implemented in that same language, and the assumption of an immutable kernel layer that should be unreachable from user-space code. Design a runnable in-person activity—complete with assigned roles, physical or paper-based materials, rules of interaction, and a non-monetary payoff or scoring scheme—so that participants initially treat one group or layer as a fixed, unchangeable base. Then arrange the dynamics so that they discover, through their own actions, that this base is actually running inside a simulated environment controlled by another group, breaking the expected hierarchy from both directions simultaneously.

Please include the following: a clear division of roles and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; rules and incentives structured so that participants encounter the recursive surprise and the collapse of the hierarchical distinction themselves before any facilitator names the concept; a debrief that maps specific moments from the activity onto the idea of mutual embedding, the false appearance of an inviolate level, and Escher’s hands drawing each other; an explicit, honest note on which feature of the philosophical idea—such as a genuinely infinite regress or pure self-reference without any external observer—the physical setup cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by me verbally; and a prediction of what will predictably malfunction the first time the activity is run, together with instructions for how to recover. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; this is intended for participants who have not done the relevant reading.
