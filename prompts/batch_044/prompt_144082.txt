Imagine you are advising a three-year interdisciplinary research program—philosophers of biology paired with synthetic biologists and conservation policymakers—tasked not with cataloguing biodiversity but with identifying which unresolved conceptual issues about the species problem carry the sharpest practical and theoretical weight. The group can deeply pursue only two or three lines of inquiry.

Begin by identifying the main open conceptual tensions that are genuinely philosophical rather than purely empirical: for example, whether species are best understood as spatiotemporally restricted individuals, as homeostatic property clusters, or as pragmatic taxonomic conveniences; whether reproductive isolation remains defensible as a core criterion given widespread hybridization, asexuality, and horizontal gene transfer; and how synthetic organisms built from multi-species genomic modules challenge the very conditions for species membership.

Then defend a prioritized agenda: which two or three of these tensions should absorb the program's resources? For each selection, articulate (i) why it is tractable for a mixed philosophical-scientific team within three years, (ii) which domains—regulatory governance, conservation prioritization, evolutionary modeling, or synthetic-design protocols—depend on its resolution, and (iii) what form of progress (a refined ontology, a normative framework, or a methodological protocol) would actually move the field. Be explicit about the trade-offs: if you prioritize microbial or synthetic-chimera ontology over macrofaunal reproductive-isolation debates, explain what is gained and what is sacrificed.

Also specify one implication that is frequently drawn from resolving the species problem—say, for biodiversity law, for evolutionary prediction, or for the moral status of hybrids—that you think does not actually follow, and diagnose the precise point where the inference fails.

Your answer should be a defended agenda with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, dependency, and neglect—rather than a literature survey or a list of authors.
