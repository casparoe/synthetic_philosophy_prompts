I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of art and philosophy of science in earnest, and I have been drafting a short essay on a tension that has stayed with me since my laboratory days. Rather than present a polished manuscript, I will share the core argument in note form and ask for your direct assessment.

I start from the familiar observation that a perfect forgery of a painting—say, a Vermeer indistinguishable to the expert eye—commands essentially none of the original’s value. The loss is not perceptual but historical: the causal chain linking the object to the artist’s hand and to a specific moment of creation has been severed. I then contrast this with scientific practice. A fabricated dataset that reproduces the correct statistical distribution and supports true hypotheses is still condemned as fraud, even though its structural content is identical to what an honest experiment might have yielded. At first glance, science seems to value reproducibility—structural identity—while art values indexical origin. Yet scientists reject fabricated data precisely because it lacks the right causal history: it never actually interacted with the world in the manner claimed. So both domains, I suggest, depend on a distinction between indexical originality (the irreproducible causal link to a specific source) and structural originality (the unique configuration of properties). In art, only the first appears to matter; in science, both matter, but they pull in opposite directions—reproducibility demands structural sameness, while empirical integrity demands indexical fidelity.

I go on to propose that this framework explains why high-fidelity digital reproductions do not diminish the value of an original painting: they replicate structure without counterfeiting indexical history. By analogy, computer simulations in science might be fully legitimate if they are transparent about lacking indexical origin—if they are presented as structural models rather than disguised measurements. The wrongness of fraud, on this view, is not the absence of a causal link per se, but the deceptive presentation of a structurally sound artifact as indexically authentic. I close by wondering whether this distinction collapses for AI-generated imagery or synthetic data, where the causal chain is distributed across algorithms and human prompts rather than localized in a single hand or apparatus.

Please provide your comments and suggestions in American English. I am especially interested in whether the analogy between artistic forgery and scientific fraud holds up philosophically, or whether I am conflating distinct normative domains. Are the concepts of indexical and structural originality coherent and useful here, or do they obscure more than they clarify? I also welcome observations on whether the argument is genuinely interesting, and on any specific sections—particularly the treatment of simulation and AI generation—that seem underdeveloped, confused, or in need of sharpening. I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I want direct conceptual feedback on the essay’s accuracy, structure, and philosophical merit.
