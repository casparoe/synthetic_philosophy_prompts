I'm delivering the eulogy at the memorial service for my former advisor, who died last week. She spent her career defending a precise, unorthodox reading of A.J. Ayer's philosophy of religion: assertions about an afterlife are not false but cognitively meaningless under the verification principle, and Ayer's own later near-death report should be read not as a recantation but as emotional phenomenology that readers misclassify as metaphysics. The audience is split between her family, who are not philosophers, and colleagues who will catch every reference. Write the eulogy. It must genuinely comfort the grieving without saying anything she would have rejected as false or meaningless—no "watching over us," no "better place"—and her distinction between emotional response and cognitive claim should do real structural work, determining exactly what can and cannot be said about her absence. Keep it accessible to non-philosophers, avoid bibliographic references, and do not treat the philosophical point as mere decoration.
