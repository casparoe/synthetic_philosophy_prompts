A philosopher of perception has advanced a series of verdicts on whether cross-modal identification requires empirical correlation or can occur immediately given representational continuity. Her rulings are as follows. First, in the classic Molyneux scenario—an individual blind from birth who suddenly acquires sight—she denies that the subject can immediately distinguish a cube from a sphere by vision alone, insisting that tactile-visual correlation must be learned. Second, in the case of a long-blind adult who has used a tactile-visual sensory-substitution device for many years and then receives restored sight, she allows that immediate recognition of shapes previously encountered through the device is possible. Third, regarding a sighted person extensively trained in human echolocation, she maintains that matching echolocatory impressions to visual shapes still requires explicit cross-modal training rather than being immediate. Fourth, she holds that an adult who receives a cochlear implant after lifelong deafness cannot immediately comprehend spoken language, though they may localise sound sources without learning. She is now asked to judge a synthetic analogue: an artificial system trained solely on linguistic representations of spatial relations is suddenly given direct access to high-dimensional geometric embeddings that preserve metric structure but share no representational format with its training corpus. On its first exposure, the system correctly classifies analogues of cubic and spherical structures in these embeddings without any intermediate correlation training. Predict her verdict—would she treat this as genuine immediate cross-modal recognition comparable to the substitution-device case, or as categorically different? Identify the principle that best unifies her four earlier rulings (representational-format continuity, empirical correlation history, or embodied sensorimotor interaction), indicate which prior case offers the closest analog, and specify a further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the competing principles.
