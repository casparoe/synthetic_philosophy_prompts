I am a high school senior preparing to facilitate a peer seminar in my school's advanced philosophy reading group on Jeremy Bentham's utilitarian analysis of sexual morality—specifically, his argument that private, consensual acts between adults should fall outside criminal jurisdiction because the state's only legitimate interest is in preventing direct, measurable harm rather than enforcing moral disapproval. The participants are familiar with Bentham's felicific calculus and the Panopticon, but they will likely arrive with one of two misconceptions: either that Bentham was a conventional Victorian moralist, or that he was an anachronistic advocate of modern identity-based rights. I am not asking you to deliver the lecture; I need a pedagogical plan for the ninety-minute session. First, outline the dependency chain: which conceptual building block—perhaps his distinction between direct and indirect harm, or his critique of the natural-law justification for criminalization—must be established before his sexual-ethics claim becomes intelligible, and in what precise order? Second, specify the single carrying example or case study that should structure the session: it should be historically concrete enough to make the harm calculus tangible, yet it must not invite anachronistic projection of contemporary categories onto Bentham's framework. State what the example must include and what it must deliberately exclude. Third, map the misconceptions in the order they are likely to emerge—such as conflating his tolerance with a positive endorsement of specific identities, or treating his harm principle as an unconditional prohibition on all legislation about sexuality—and indicate the exact point in the explanatory arc where each should be intercepted. Fourth, provide one precise diagnostic question I can ask at the end that will distinguish whether a peer has actually grasped the reasoning structure or is merely repeating that Bentham was progressive. Finally, identify which element of Bentham's broader corpus—whether the Panopticon writings, his jurisprudence on punishment, or his economic thought—should be explicitly set aside for this session and why, so that the discussion does not drift into biography or penal theory.
