I was arguing with my study group about whether honoring sunk costs can ever be rational, and two people got totally stuck. Jordan said it's never rational — he was talking about his musician friend who'd spent like twelve years learning the hurdy-gurdy, hates practicing now, but keeps going because "I've already put in a decade." Jordan says the decade is gone; only future costs and benefits matter, and continuing out of loyality to past time is just emotional blackmail. Riley said that's way too quick and brought up diachronic integrity — your reasons across time aren't just snapshots of present utility. The past investment can be a real reason if it connects to a long-term project or identity you're genuinely committed to, not just a feeling of waste. Jordan pushed back that she's just relabeling future value: you stay because you want to be "someone who finishes things," which is a present preference, not the sunk cost itself doing any work. Riley then brought up finishing a letter to a dead friend — you don't do it for future payoff, but beacuse the past relationship itself generates an obligation that survives death. Jordan said that's exactly sentimentalism, and in the musician case there's no dead person, just her own prior choices, which she can honor by accepting them and moving on. They asked me to referee and i honestly couldn't tell who was missing what. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right in a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Give me exactly five numbered items, and in the last one also say who is more right overall and what both parties are missing about how sunk costs interact with personal identity over time.
