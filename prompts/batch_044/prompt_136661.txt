"The wrong kind of reasons problem is about having a reason to do the morally right thing that doesn't actually bear on the morality of the act itself. Like, imagine a city planner who redesigns a poor neighborhood with good public housing, but she only does it because she wants to win an architecture prize and get featured in a magazine. The reason is wrong-kind because it's agent-tracking — it's about her career — not value-tracking, which tracks the actual moral importance of the housing. This is different from a weak reason: a weak reason still tracks value but just doesn't weigh enough, like it'd be nice but not required. This problem doesn't undermine internalism, though. Internalism claims that sincerely judging X is right necessarily gives you some motivation to do X. The planner can sincerely judge the housing is just — that's value-tracking judgment — while her agent-tracking reason gives her the actual push to act. So wrong-kind reasons don't replace moral motivation, they just supplement it. That's why Bernard Williams, in his essay on internal reasons, said that external reasons like these can't motivate unless they connect to the agent's internal set. Also, Kant first raised this in the Groundwork when he distinguished acting from duty versus inclination, and all agent-tracking reasons are basically prudential so they can never count as moral reasons."
