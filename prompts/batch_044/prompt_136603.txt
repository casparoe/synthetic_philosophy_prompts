As a retired physicist now studying philosophy of mind, I have been working through Hofstadter and Dennett's dialogue in The Mind's I, in which Einstein's brain is eventually preserved and then translated into a printed book containing the complete rules of his cognition. I understand their central objection to be that paper and ink lack the causal powers and continuous environmental coupling of neural tissue, so that the book remains a static representation rather than a conscious subject. But this seems to conflate substrate with dynamical organization: if one imagines an autonomous, sensor-driven printing apparatus that continuously rewrites the codex and uses its state transitions to drive physical actuators—preserving a closed causal loop entirely within machinery and paper—then the biological substrate has been removed without disrupting the loop. Is their argument therefore really directed against a particular kind of static representation, rather than constituting a genuine resistance to functionalism? Or have I misunderstood the precise level at which they intend the book to fail?
