I am a high school senior who has been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interfaith vigil tomorrow evening for three classmates who died in a bus accident. The audience will include grieving families, teachers, fellow students, and clergy from different traditions, with beliefs ranging from devout to entirely secular. In my philosophy elective, we have spent the semester on the problem of evil and on theodicies, and I want that work to do real structural work in the speech rather than serve as ornament. Specifically, I want the remarks—about four minutes—to acknowledge that classic theodicies, such as free-will defenses, soul-making accounts, and greater-good arguments, attempt to render suffering intelligible within a larger moral or cosmic order. At the same time, the speech should introduce, in language accessible to non-philosophers, the idea of an anti-theodicy: the claim that in cases of catastrophic and seemingly senseless loss, offering an explanatory framework can itself become a kind of moral failure, because making the suffering intelligible risks making it seem acceptable, necessary, or redeemed in advance. The remarks must therefore refuse to say that this tragedy was part of a plan; they must explain why that refusal is philosophically serious rather than merely emotional; and they must still offer the room something honest to hold onto—perhaps a shared commitment to bear witness together without claiming to understand—something that works for both religious and non-religious listeners. Please write the speech so that it comforts without false consolation, respects faith without requiring belief, and never suggests that philosophical clarity equals emotional resolution. The philosophical framework should shape what can and cannot be said, not decorate it.
