I've come to accept a specific variant of the transformative-experience thesis that I haven't seen articulated exactly this way: that the core problem with rational choice across transformative boundaries is not missing phenomenal knowledge—the usual framing in the literature—but a failure of intertemporal preference continuity. On this view, a transformative experience doesn't just add new values; it restructures the agent's preference ordering so radically that no continuous Bayesian or utility-updating path connects the pre- and post-state, which means the pre-transformation agent lacks not information but a rational framework for weighing the outcome. This seems to explain why testimony fails without relying on private qualia, but I'm aware I'm constructing a hybrid and that it may be doing hidden work.

What would count against this specific formulation? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: empirical results about preference stability across major life changes, formal results showing that discontinuous preference shifts can still be rationalized within some decision-theoretic framework, arguments that the problem collapses back into standard epistemic opacity once you drop the continuity requirement, and whether defenders of the standard phenomenal-knowledge view would even recognize this as a version of their position or as a rival that needs separate defense. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether this formulation risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if any preference shift can be labeled "discontinuous" post hoc—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind. Please say explicitly what would change your mind: which specific finding or argument would cause you to abandon the intertemporal-continuity framing in favor of either the standard epistemic view or a denial that there's any special rational problem at all, and identify where in the reasoning above you think the weakest link actually is.
