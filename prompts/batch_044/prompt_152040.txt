The debate over whether Popper’s falsifiability criterion can genuinely demarcate science from pseudoscience has been running since the 1970s critiques by Kuhn, Lakatos, and Feyerabend, and in contemporary philosophy of science it still terminates in the same circular exchange: the Duhem-Quine problem undermines naive falsification, sophisticated falsification saves it only by licensing conventionalist choices about auxiliary hypotheses, and critics reply that those choices make demarcation a sociological convention rather than an epistemic norm. Despite the familiarity of every move, neither side concedes ground. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word “falsification”—with one side meaning logical vulnerability of core claims and the other meaning methodological willingness to abandon a protective belt—or are they answering different questions altogether, one about retrospective classification of research programmes and one about prospective norms for rational belief revision? Use what Popper actually says about basic statements and conventionalist stratagems, what Lakatos says about progressive versus degenerating problem shifts, and what later critics say about the symmetry of explaining success versus failure, to show whether the sides share enough common ground for a substantive resolution. Is there a hidden formal question doing the work—perhaps about whether falsification conditions remain well-defined or computable when theories are embedded in high-dimensional parameter spaces with implicit regularization—or is the impasse a sign that the demarcation question is ill-posed because “science” is essentially contested? Offer your diagnosis supported by the actual structure of each position, then say what would count as progress: a precise conceptual distinction, a formal result, an empirical study of immunization practices in working science, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
