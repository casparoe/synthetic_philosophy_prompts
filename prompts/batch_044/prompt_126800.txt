I'm writing my A-level philosophy coursework on whether honouring sunk costs is ever rational, and I've been tracking my tutor's verdicts across cases to see if she runs a single principle or several. She is a behavioural economist who takes conceptual distinctions seriously, but her rulings seem to depend on more than pure forward-looking calculation.

Here are her verdicts so far:

1. A hiker spends £800 on alpine gear, then a storm makes the ascent dangerous; he continues to the summit to avoid wasting the trip. Verdict: irrational — risk is forward-looking, and the gear cost is irrecoverable.

2. A musician spends twelve years mastering an obscure microtonal instrument with no commercial audience, living in poverty rather than switching to a conventional career. Verdict: rational — the investment is constitutive of her practical identity, not merely a means to an end.

3. A municipal council continues funding a half-built bridge that now serves no transport need, arguing that £40 million has already been spent. Verdict: irrational — public funds are instrumental, and past expenditure creates no obligation to compound error.

4. A medical student in his final year realises he despises clinical practice, but completes his degree because he has come too far to quit. Verdict: irrational — career paths are fungible, and the years invested generate no duty to suffer.

5. A woman cares for her mother with advanced dementia who no longer recognises her, continuing despite extreme emotional cost because she has done this for eight years. Verdict: rational — the commitment is moral and relational; abandoning it for convenience would betray the bond that the years of care have constituted.

Now the new case: a retired mechanical engineer has spent two decades refining a clockwork mechanism that digital technology has rendered entirely obsolete. He has no buyers, no students, no audience, and no promise to anyone. He spends every day perfecting the design, explaining that this is simply what he does — to stop would be to erase the person he has become. The work has no artistic community value and no practical application; it is purely the continuation of a private technical practice.

What should my tutor say if she wishes to remain consistent with herself? Identify the closest analogues among cases 1–5, state the rule she appears to be running, and lay out at least two other rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well. Then predict her verdict on the engineer, giving your best guess and the reason for preferring it, but also say which further case — one that you specify — would best distinguish the competing rules. I am particularly interested in whether she treats practical identity, domain-specific value, and relational obligation as separate conditions or as a single cluster.

Please write in British English. Please state how confident you are in your analysis of her implicit framework and single out the point about which you are least sure. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
