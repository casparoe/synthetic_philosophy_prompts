I'm a journalist who writes about science and philosophy of law, and I currently sit on a state-level Sentencing Philosophy Review Board charged with recommending whether our jurisdiction should preserve "just deserts" as an independent, non-instrumental criterion in felony sentencing guidelines or eliminate it in favor of purely consequentialist aims such as deterrence, rehabilitation, and public safety. The board is split: one faction treats retributive language as an indispensable moral boundary that prevents the state from treating citizens as mere instruments of social engineering, while the other sees it as a metaphysically dubious relic that obscures the actual functions of punishment. To help us move past deadlock, we need a rigorous analysis of the exchange between Daniel Dennett—who offers a compatibilist defense of moral responsibility that treats desert-talk as a pragmatically indispensable social institution rather than a metaphysical truth about free will—and Gregg Caruso—who argues from free-will skepticism and the problem of moral luck that just deserts is incoherent and should be abandoned in favor of a public-health model of punishment.

Our deliberation is further complicated by a pending legislative proposal introducing what its sponsors call a "neurodevelopmental contingency" defense: defendants could present peer-reviewed evidence that severe adolescent environmental deprivation measurably altered prefrontal cortical maturation, not to secure full acquittal, but to diminish the degree to which any punishment can be said to be deserved rather than merely instrumentally warranted. I want a formal recommendation memo—approve retention, approve elimination, or approve a conditional hybrid—addressed to the board and written for a non-specialist legislature that must vote within the month.

The memo should treat Dennett's institutional pragmatism and Caruso's skeptical abolitionism with equal philosophical seriousness, apply both frameworks specifically to the neurodevelopmental contingency proposal, and explain whether allowing such evidence collapses the distinction between desert-based and consequentialist sentencing or instead reveals that the distinction was never stable. It must include an honest account of the strongest objection to whichever position you recommend—for instance, if you favor retention, address Caruso's charge that preserving desert in the face of neurodevelopmental variability amounts to moral arbitrariness masked as justice; if you favor elimination, address Dennett's worry that stripping desert entirely removes a necessary normative constraint on state coercion and invites purely managerial governance of citizens. The reasoning should be disciplined and concrete, not an abstract literature review, and should not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The deliverable should read as an actionable institutional document—clear bottom line, conditions if any, and a brief but rigorous rationale for the losing side—suitable for board members who are not professional philosophers.
