I'm giving a talk next thursday at an interdisiplinary seminar on transitional justice — audiance is about 15 people, mostly moral philosophers with a communitarian bent, plus two archivists who think preservation is sacred and one practitioner who actually staffed a TRC in Latin America. I'm defending the idea that there's not just a personal right to forget but sometimes an institutional duty to engineer structured forgetting: deliberately curating gaps in official archives so grievance narratives don't reproduce themselves across generations. I use Margalit's framing that shared memory binds ethical community, but I push it backward — if the shared past is toxic, weakening the communal bond through curated absence might be the ethical move, not stronger remembrance. I also engage Rieff's case for forgetting, though I reject his individualist framing; this isn't about personal healing but about institutional design. The niche bit I'm introducing is "archival negative space" — the idea that what's deliberately left out of the record carries its own moral weight, and that archivists should sometimes be obligated to maintain absence rather than completeness.

The philosophers will worry I'm licensing state-sponsored amnesia. The archivists will think I'm endorsing destruction of evidence. The practitioner will ask whether I've seen how these commissions actually operate when perpetrators still hold power. I need a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually face, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased the way that specific group would put it. For each I want a 90-second reply and an honest flag on where I'm being soft or hand-wavy. I should also know what to concede upfront — maybe that I'm not talking about burning files, only about selective non-collection and redaction protocols? And I really need the one question I'm praying nobody asks, probably something about whether my view lets perpetrators design the official absence themselves. Give me that dreaded one with the reply that barely holds together, and point out exactly where it collapses. Don't feed me generic philosophy objections; these need to land for people who think about victim dignity, record-keeping, and truth commissions every day.
