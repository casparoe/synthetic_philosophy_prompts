I have been studying how ancient historians evaluated claims about the distant past, and I have encountered a methodological passage in Thucydides that I cannot fully parse. In the opening sections of his History of the Peloponnesian War, he writes: "Having now given the result of my inquiries into early times, I grant that there will be a difficulty in believing every particular detail. The way that most men deal with traditions, even traditions of their own country, is to receive them all alike as they are delivered, without applying any critical test whatever."

I am trying to understand the precise scope of the "critical test" he demands—whether it is a matter of internal narrative consistency, cross-examination of witnesses, or some form of material corroboration. Specifically, I want to apply his framework to the boundary between legend and history in cases such as the Exodus or the Trojan War, where continuous textual transmission exists but archaeological confirmation remains contested or absent. If Thucydides requires verification that can only come from contemporary observation, does his method automatically exclude all legendary events from historical status? Or can a narrative achieve historical standing through testimonial continuity and coherence alone?

Furthermore, I am unsure how to compare Thucydides' standard with Herodotus' reliance on Egyptian priests and oral reports: are both subject to the same basanismos, or does Thucydides implicitly privilege eyewitness testimony over transmitted tradition in a way that makes Herodotus' method inherently inferior? Please help me understand exactly what kind of evidence Thucydides considers legitimate for reconstructing the distant past, and whether his skepticism leaves any room for treating a well-attested legend as history without direct material proof.
