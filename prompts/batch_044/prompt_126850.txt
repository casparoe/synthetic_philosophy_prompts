You are grading a final exam for a graduate seminar in metaethics. The prompt given to students was this:

Distinguish direct normativity from indirect normativity, with careful attention to the role of idealized deliberation in generating or accessing reasons for action. Then present the collapse objection: that an indirect theory either collapses into direct normativity (if the idealized procedure merely tracks reasons that exist independently of it) or into an unconstrained constructivism (if the procedure generates normative authority without any external check). Finally, discuss whether what some commentators call the normative residue problem—the possibility that certain considerations remain genuinely binding even after they have been excluded by a fully informed, coherent, and unbiased deliberative procedure—offers the indirect theorist a viable defense. Does accommodating these residues preserve indirect normativity, or does it tacitly reintroduce direct normative facts?

Below is one student’s answer. It is genuinely mixed: sound on the basic architecture of direct and indirect views, accurate in its reconstruction of the collapse objection, and clearly written. However, it contains at least two subtle mistakes—one in its characterization of the scope of direct normativity, and a more serious one in its handling of fallibilism and the normative residue problem, where a conflation lets an erroneous conclusion slide through. The errors are not obvious howlers; a hasty reader might award full marks. You are grading as an instructor or TA. Do not signal your own philosophical commitments. Provide a specific grade with brief justification, then point-by-point comments that identify what is correct, what is subtly wrong or conflated, and whether the mistakes undermine the argument. In particular, address whether the student’s reconciliation of residues with indirect normativity succeeds, or whether it tacitly concedes that direct normative facts do the corrective work.

Student answer:

Direct normativity is the view that normative reasons exist independently of any agent’s deliberative capacities and apply to her regardless of whether she recognizes them; indirect normativity, by contrast, holds that an action’s normative status is constituted by the outcome of an idealized deliberative procedure—one conducted with full information, coherence, and the absence of distorting affect or bias. Where the direct view treats deliberation as an epistemic instrument for discovering reasons that are already present, the indirect view treats the deliberative result itself as the source of normative authority.

The collapse objection charges that this position is unstable. If the ideal procedure tracks reasons that exist prior to it, then indirect normativity is just direct normativity with an epistemic intermediary, and the claim that reasons are constituted by deliberation is false. If, on the other hand, the procedure does not track anything prior and is merely constrained by formal conditions like coherence, then it seems arbitrary: it could generate authority for any content, and we lose the ability to say that an agent has made a normative mistake in any robust sense.

I believe the indirect theorist can answer this by embracing the normative residue problem. Even an ideal deliberator might exclude certain considerations—say, obligations to distant strangers or to non-human animals—yet those considerations might still be genuinely binding. This shows that the procedure is fallible: it can overlook real reasons. But fallibilism is compatible with indirect normativity, because the residues do not show that reasons exist entirely outside rational assessment; rather, they function as external correctives that the procedure failed to endorse. Thus indirect normativity preserves its core claim—reasons are normally constituted by ideal deliberation—while allowing that direct normative facts occasionally step in to correct the procedure’s errors. This avoids arbitrary constructivism without collapsing into direct realism, since the residues are exceptional correctives rather than the primary ground of normativity. One might worry that direct normativity requires reasons to be accessible even to non-rational agents, but that is a misreading: directness concerns ontological independence, not universal accessibility, so the presence of residues does not expand direct normativity beyond its proper domain.
