Two people in my study group got stuck in a sustained argument about whether DNA is literally a code or only described by a code metaphor, and I honestly couldn't tell where the actual philosophical disagreement was. The first, Jordan, argued that the genetic mapping is arbitrary in the philosophically relevant sense: there is no chemical law fixing which triplet specifies which amino acid, and this arbitrariness, together with the functional specificity of translation, means DNA carries literal semantic content that is interpreted rather than merely caused. The second, Riley, replied that linguistic arbitrariness depends on conventional agreement among agents, whereas the codon mapping is only evolutionarily contingent and biochemically locked; the ribosome does not interpret anything, it reacts chemically, and importing semantics here revives a kind of natural teleology that Darwin eliminated. Jordan then countered that objective semantic content does not require a conscious interpreter — computer code works analogously, with syntax mapped to function without an internal reader — so denying DNA literal code-status forces you to deny that software is literally code once you remove the programmer. Riley said the computer analogy actually undermines Jordan's point: without an engineer, silicon is just sand, and evolutionary selection is causal filtering, not interpretation; pushing interpretation back into evolutionary history confuses cause with meaning. They asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange move by move — Jordan's claim about arbitrariness grounding literal semantics, Riley's reply about convention versus contingency and the risk of teleology, Jordan's computer-code analogy, and Riley's counter about selection versus interpretation — and say who is right about what? In particular, address whether arbitrariness without conventional agreement can ground literal semantics, and whether the absence of an engineer or interpreter undermines the DNA and software analogies symmetrically.
