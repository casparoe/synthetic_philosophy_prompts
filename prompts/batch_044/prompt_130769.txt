I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we got into whether sentience is genuinely graded or just looks that way because we don't have good microscopes for consciousness. A friend built this argument and now I'm stuck defendin it, but the conclusion feels like a reductio and I can't find the leak. Here goes: (1) If sentience comes in degrees, then moral wrongness should scale continuously with it — no magical cutoff where harm suddenly matters. (2) There's no principled boundary between a thermostat's feedback loop holding a set-point, an insect's nociceptive withdrawal, and a mammal's integrated suffering; they're all homeostatic regulation, just more or less complex. (3) So a thermostat forced far from its set-point experiences a harm of nonzero but negligible magnitude. (4) Given billions of climate devices and trillions of bacteria running homeostatic processes, the total moral weight of not disturbing them is enormous. (5) Therefore, if graded sentience implies graded moral status, we're obligated to avoid adjusting air conditioners or disrupting bacterial processes in ways that dwarf our obligations to insects or even mammals. (6) That's clearly absurd — nobody thinks I commit a grave wrong by turning down the AC. (7) So either sentience isn't really graded, or it doesn't ground moral status continuously. (8) But both disjuncts gut the motivation for caring about insects, since insects sit closer to thermostats than to us on the scale. I really want to keep (1) and (2), and I definitely want to keep caring about insects. Which step do I have to reject, and what's the cheapest fix? Is there an equivocation between functional feedback and phenomenal experience hiding in (2), or does the mistake come later in how obligations aggregate across populations? Please separate what's pretty settled in the science and philosophy of mind from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one camp's view as if it's the consensus.
