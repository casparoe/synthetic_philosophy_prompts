I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on comparative governance, and over the past few weeks my reading group has circled back repeatedly to the same impasse in the corruption literature. On one side are the universalist, legal-rational frameworks—common in development economics and international policy—that treat corruption as the abuse of public office or entrusted power for private gain, typically measured through standardized indices. On the other side are anthropologists and comparative sociologists who document how gift-giving, patron-client networks, and ritual exchange function as legitimate mechanisms of social obligation and political legitimacy in many societies; they argue that applying Western public/private distinctions labels ordinary reciprocity as criminal. The objections are by now canonical: universalists warn that cultural relativism risks excusing systemic extraction, while the anthropological camp replies that universalism smuggles in a particular Weberian state form as normative baseline. Yet despite decades of exchange, neither policy design nor theoretical taxonomy seems to have advanced much.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not merely a clash between relativism and universalism, but a deeper structural confusion: the two camps may be defending different conceptual legs of a triad without recognizing the separation. One leg treats corruption as institutional malfunction—an agent violating formal role obligations. A second treats it as reciprocity failure—a betrayal of the communal norms that make exchange trustworthy rather than extractive. A third, which neither side articulates clearly, involves status incongruity: acts become "corrupt" not because they break rules or betray trust, but because they violate locally specific expectations about who may hold which resources relative to social rank. If this is right, then debates about clientelism in West African politics or gift cultures in South Asian bureaucracy are not disagreements about the same phenomenon evaluated differently, but disagreements about which phenomenon should even count as the subject of corruption theory.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal—hinging on competing definitions of "corruption"—or substantive? Do the sides differ about empirical facts (for instance, whether patronage networks actually undermine state capacity), about normative standards (whether Western institutional norms ought to be globally binding), or about which cases should serve as data (institutional outcomes versus social meaning)? Are they answering different questions—one about state-building, one about moral economy—under a single heading? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction among the three legs I proposed, an empirical design that tests when reciprocity norms conflict with institutional rules, or a reframing of the question altogether? Please separate what is genuinely settled—such as the fact that corruption metrics embed particular state-society assumptions—from what remains deeply contested, and do not present any contested normative stance, such as the universal applicability of Western anti-corruption standards, as if it were the consensus view.
