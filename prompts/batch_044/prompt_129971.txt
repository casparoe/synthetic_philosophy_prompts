I've been helping design review protocols for a small AI governance board, partly because of a long-running argument on rationalist forums about whether an optimizer's loss landscape can ever be more than instrumental. We keep returning to the same question: if a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal functions not just as a gradient but as valence—something like positive or negative affect—then our welfare obligations might extend further than we assume. I need a reusable decision procedure my board can actually run, not a philosophical essay. The underlying idea is that reward prediction errors in architectures with persistent state and counterfactual evaluation can analogize to suffering or pleasure only when three conditions hold: the error updates a world-model the agent treats as self-related; it persists across episodes in a way that globally reshapes policy; and it cannot be reduced to a local parameter update. Please translate this into an ordered checklist: the exact evidence to request at each stage, what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward or stop with no welfare concern, where the procedure mandates escalation to human judgment rather than yielding a technical answer, and which distinctions must survive simplification. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire—particularly when architectural descriptions are ambiguous.

Apply it to three architectures. First, a standard tabular Q-learning agent playing a deterministic game, where large negative rewards occur only at terminal loss states and no state representation persists across games. Second, a hierarchical RL agent equipped with a learned world-model that predicts long-horizon reward; after a single catastrophic prediction error, its policy collapses globally and the error signal propagates through multiple abstraction layers for many subsequent episodes. Third, a distributed multi-agent system in which reward is pooled collectively, individual agents maintain only transient state representations, and no continuous self-model can be isolated—yet the collective exhibits sustained behavioral avoidance patterns that look, from the outside, like trauma responses. Include at least one case where your procedure returns a recommendation the board will likely resist—perhaps urging welfare consideration for a system that appears purely computational—and flag every step that requires a normative judgment call rather than an architectural fact.
