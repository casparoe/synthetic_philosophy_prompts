For an upcoming seminar on rational disagreement, I want to use Kurosawa's Rashomon—together with Akutagawa's underlying stories "In a Grove" and "Rashomon"—as a sustained case study for the common prior assumption in Bayesian epistemology, particularly the conditions that make Aumann-style agreement possible. The film gives four incompatible but internally coherent accounts of a single violent encounter in a grove: the bandit Tajomaru, the assaulted wife, the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter who claims to have witnessed everything. Each testimony fits the physical evidence yet assigns radically different motives and moral weights to the same actions. Read the film through that framework: where does it dramatize disagreement despite apparently shared information about the event, and is the divergence best explained by genuinely different priors about human nature, honor, and self-preservation, or by hidden private information that violates the shared-information condition? Pay particular attention to the woodcutter's account and his final concealment of the dagger—does this reveal that even the ostensibly neutral observer has an undisclosed interest, thereby undermining the common-knowledge-of-rationality condition from within rather than from outside?

I want a reading that treats neither the philosophical machinery nor the film reductively: do not turn the characters into bare Bayesian agents updating on a common dataset, but do not let the film's ambiguity dissolve the formal conditions either. Does Rashomon suggest that rational convergence is impossible because narrative identity is itself constructed from self-interest, or does it leave open that agreement could in principle be reached if transparency and shared priors actually obtained? Which scenes—Tajomaru's boastful confession, the wife's shifting testimony, the medium's chilling delivery, the woodcutter's hesitant revision—best illuminate the tension between epistemic humility and the absence of any view from nowhere? And what, if anything, does the work add to the philosophical debate about disagreement that a purely formal treatment of common priors tends to miss?
