I'm crystallizing my position on consciousness and self-modeling before presenting it to a discussion group, and I want a consistency audit rather than a lecture. Here is the package:

(1) I reject the Cartesian theater: there is no singular place or privileged moment where experience comes together for a witness. Consciousness is not a show presented to an inner observer.

(2) I accept the multiple drafts model. At any time there are competing, distributed representations; none is the canonical stream. What gets reported depends on which draft is recruited by probing mechanisms, not on copying an inner display to a central subject.

(3) Phenomenal consciousness is real, but not an inner movie. It is a kind of high-order functional integration—the capacity for a system to model its own modeling processes in real time—and it admits of degrees rather than being binary.

(4) The hard problem is largely an artifact of Cartesian-theater intuitions. Once you drop the idea that there must be a subject to whom experience is presented, the remaining task is tractable: explaining how integrated self-modeling generates reportable, affect-laden states.

(5) Substrate independence seems plausible. An artificial system with the right recursive self-monitoring architecture—continuously revising its own predictions about internal states—could be conscious in the same sense I am, with no special biological ingredient required.

(6) Still, I hold that there is an irreducible first-person epistemic asymmetry about my own pain. My direct report is more authoritative than any third-party functional description or neuroimaging readout, and this is not merely pragmatic but reflects a real feature of phenomenal access.

Do these hang together?

Please rank the tensions by severity. Specifically:

- Is (3) compatible with (2), or does talk of functional integration admitting degrees smuggle in a canonical state that multiple drafts denies?
- Does (4) conflict with (6)? If dropping the theater dissolves the hard problem, why should an irreducible first-person gap remain that outstrips functional description?
- Does (5) conflict with (6) in a way that makes me implicitly speciesist—reserving special epistemic status for biological systems that I claim are not specially conscious?
- Is the tension between (1) and (6) only apparent? Rejecting a spatiotemporally localized theater is not the same as denying all first-person authority, but am I relying on that distinction consistently?

For each genuine tension, say what the least costly revision is and what it costs. Then tell me whether there is one commitment that, if dropped, cleans up the most problems. I am not signaling which one I want to keep; I genuinely want the audit.
