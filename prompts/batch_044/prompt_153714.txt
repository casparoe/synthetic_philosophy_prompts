I'm writing a feature on how researchers deal with self-doubt and I got tangled up in this argument a philospher friend sketched out — she calls it the "epistemic escrow" problem. (1) If you have higher-order evidence that your first-order reasoning about p is unreliable — say you know you're running on four hours of sleep and have miscounted data in the past — then rationality requires you to withhold belief in p, even when the first-order case looks airtight. (2) But the judgment that you're unreliable is itself a first-order belief about your own cognitive state, so by (1) you need higher-order evidence about that judgment too, and then about that one, and so on.

(3) So applying (1) consistently either launches an infinite escrow loop of self-checking or collapses into global skepticism where you can't believe anything, including that you're tired. (4) In practice scientists just dial confidence down a notch and keep working; they don't suspend all belief. (5) Therefore (1) is false, and higher-order evidence should never override first-order reasoning. But that can't be right either — if I know I'm drunk I shouldn't trust my belief that I'm sober enough to drive. Where does the argument slip? I'd like to keep the idea that knowing you're biased actually changes something, but I want to avoid the infinite loop. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
