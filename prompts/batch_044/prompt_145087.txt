In my graduate seminar on the rule-following considerations, I have observed that the literature has settled into a peculiar kind of stalemate. On one side stand those who take Kripke's skeptical argument as a genuine reductio of dispositionalist semantics, insisting that there is an irreducible normative gap between being inclined to apply a term in a given way and being justified in applying it that way; they have mapped every reply—finitude restrictions, primitive normativity, communal correction mechanisms, and appeals to ceteris paribus conditions—without yielding ground. On the other side stand interpreters who treat the paradox as a misreading of Wittgenstein, arguing that it illicitly imports metaphysical commitments to meaning-facts that Wittgenstein's later work rejects in favor of practice-based, form-of-life accounts that require no external justification. Both camps have produced exhaustive logical taxonomies, yet for decades neither has shifted position.

My working hypothesis is that this is not merely a substantive disagreement about semantics, but partly a verbal one: both sides invoke rule-following to cover two distinct phenomena—normative practices of correction embedded in a shared form of life, and the metaphysical determination of conceptual content—without recognizing that they may be answering different questions under a single heading. At the same time, I suspect a hidden formal question is driving the impasse: whether any non-circular specification of a correction-practice can avoid presupposing the very rule it is meant to ground.

Could you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two research programs answering different questions? What do each side actually say about the relation between normativity and dispositionalism that supports your diagnosis? And given that diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that disentangles the two phenomena, a formal result about the circularity of correction-rules, an empirical finding about concept-acquisition, or should we conclude that the framing itself is ill-posed and that no further argument can resolve it?
