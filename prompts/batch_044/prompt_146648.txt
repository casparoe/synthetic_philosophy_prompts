For the past several months I have been forced to rethink what I value in life after the gradual dissolution of a friendship that had, for years, been organized around shared gallery visits and concert attendance. In trying to understand precisely what was lost, I have returned to G. E. Moore’s discussion of intrinsic goods in Principia Ethica, and I now want to check whether a cluster of commitments I have arrived at is jointly tenable before I present it to a small philosophy reading group. Here is my current profile:

(a) I accept Moore’s identification of the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of human friendship as the two supreme intrinsic goods, neither reducible to the other.

(b) I endorse his organic-unities thesis: a state of affairs in which friends jointly attend to something beautiful is more valuable than the sum of the value of that friendship and the value of that aesthetic experience taken separately, but this surplus value does not entail that either component derives its worth from the other.

(c) I hold a strict non-derivativeness condition on intrinsic value: if X is good in itself, then no part of X’s value can depend for its very existence on its standing in relation to some other intrinsic good Y.

(d) I am persuaded by recent work in social aesthetics that aesthetic judgment is essentially intersubjective—solitary appreciation is always incomplete or defective because the norms of taste presuppose actual or potential agreement and disagreement with others—so that the full realization of aesthetic value requires a social, and specifically a friendly, context.

(e) I want to deny that friendship is merely instrumentally valuable for achieving aesthetic experience, or vice versa; instead, I take the two to be co-constitutive elements of the best human life, while still insisting that each retains an independent intrinsic status that does not collapse into the other.

My request is for an audit, not a lecture. Please go through the pairs among (a)–(e) and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, specify which commitment would have to be revised, what that revision costs, and whether there is a less damaging reinterpretation. In particular, I worry that (d) makes aesthetic value depend on friendship in a way that violates (c), and that (b) is hard to reconcile with (e)’s insistence on independence. At the same time, I suspect that some conflicts dissolve once we distinguish constitutive interdependence from instrumental or derivative dependence. Please be precise about whether (b) can survive the pressure from (d), whether (c) is compatible with (e), and—if one revision could clean up the most inconsistencies—which single change would be least costly to the overall view.
