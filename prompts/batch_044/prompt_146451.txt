I'm writing a dissertation chapter on rational choice under genuine preference symmetry—cases where an agent faces options that are structurally equivalent rather than merely epistemically tied, extending Buridan's ass into dynamic settings where an agent might construct a reason only after selecting. The standard literature frames this as a problem of tie-breaking mechanisms—randomization, lexicographic rules, or volitional selection—but I want a defended agenda for which unresolved lines are genuinely upstream. Specifically: (1) Can rational agency be coherently described without any prior ordering relation, or does the concept of choice itself presuppose a minimal asymmetry? (2) If an agent selects arbitrarily and then generates a retrospective preference to stabilize the decision, does this resolve the paradox or merely relocate it to the metaphysics of preference-formation? (3) To what extent do answers here actually constrain artificial decision systems that must act under exactly equal utility estimates but are designed without stochastic modules? Among these dependencies—and any others you think are hidden—rank them by tractability and downstream impact for philosophy of action and formal decision theory, specifying which implications for AI design are overblown and which, if settled, would force revisions in the others. I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I want a prioritization that fixes whose effort this is for and what criteria justify the ordering.
