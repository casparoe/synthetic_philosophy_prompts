Below are preliminary notes and a proposed line of argument for an essay on Daniel Dennett’s brain-transplant scenario, in which a brain is removed, kept alive in a vat, and remotely linked to a body, prompting the subject to ask whether she is with her body or with her brain.

My proposed twist is to reject the framing that treats “where I am” as a single question with a unique answer. Instead, I distinguish three location-tracking systems: substrate-tracking (where the biological brain is), functional-tracking (where the control system directing behavior is located), and indexical-tracking (the first-person perspective from which “here” is reported). In the standard Dennett case, these three coincide for an embodied person but come apart in the vat scenario.

I then propose a more extreme variant: a gradual neuron-replacement procedure that produces two fully functional systems—one biological brain preserved in vat A, one silicon-based system embedded in body B—both maintaining narrative continuity with the original subject and both capable of generating sincere first-person location reports. Rather than asking which one is “really” me, I argue that the scenario reveals first-person location to be non-unique: it is a distributed property that can be genuinely instantiated in two places without contradiction, provided we abandon the assumption that personal identity requires a single spatiotemporal locus.

The essay would conclude that Dennett’s puzzle is not best solved by deciding between brain and body, but by recognizing that “where I am” is a family of related tracking states, and that in split or duplicated cases there may be no further fact of the matter—similar in structure, though not in substance, to certain views about fission in the personal-identity literature.

I am writing this for an audience familiar with philosophy of mind but not necessarily with the specific literature on indexical tracking.
