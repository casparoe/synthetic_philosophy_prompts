For a philosophy of biology seminar on evolutionary explanation, design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on the status of apparently functional but structurally coupled traits, using the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique of adaptationism as its organizing framework. Begin with a specific constructed case: imagine a hypothetical deep-cave bat lineage in which dramatically enlarged, ornate external pinnae play no detectable role in echolocation, thermoregulation, or social signaling, yet are developmentally and structurally inseparable from enlarged nasal resonating chambers that were directly selected for fine-tuning echolocation call frequency. From this observation, construct branch nodes as concrete diagnostic questions—phrased so a student can answer them without knowing the philosophical labels—addressing: whether structural byproducts can carry evolutionary explanations independently of the features that generated them; whether apparent design always obligates an adaptive account; whether developmental constraint should be treated as an explanatory competitor to natural selection or merely as its boundary condition; and whether the burden of proof falls on those claiming a hidden function or on those claiming a non-adaptive origin. The leaves should be named positions or clearly marked unnamed combinations, including at least strict adaptationism, Gould-Lewontin-style spandrelism, exaptationism (co-opted spandrels now under selection), pluralist structuralism (developmental architecture explains the trait without invoking a selectionist narrative), and one unoccupied logical combination that you diagnose as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. For each leaf, include one sentence describing its most awkward commitment regarding this specific bat case. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate—such as exaptationism and late-stage adaptationism—explaining exactly why the diagnostic questions fail to split them. Structure the tree from the logical space of answers first, then locate the actual positions within it, noting any empty combinations. Finally, provide a brief, committed verdict: given the constructed morphological case, which branch do you judge best supported, and what does that imply for the general weight we should assign to spandrel explanations relative to adaptive ones in evolutionary biology? Take a definite stance, state your reasons, and do not merely catalog options. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
