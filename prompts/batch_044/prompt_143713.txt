I am drafting a novel in which consciousness is not confined to individual organisms but distributed across landscapes, archives, and collective entities, and this project has led me repeatedly into the philosophical deadlock over panpsychism and what is called the combination problem. For as long as I have followed the dispute—spanning the divisions between smallists, cosmopsychists, and those who reject phenomenal mereology altogether—there appears to be no genuine movement. Each camp accepts the same formal descriptions of macro-physical structure; each acknowledges that if micro-level experience exists, it must somehow relate to macro-level awareness. Yet the smallists continue to demand a compositional mechanism that cosmopsychists deny is necessary, while the sceptics maintain that the very idea of experiential aggregation imports intuitions from physical parts that do not apply to phenomenology.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but conceptual: the disputants are not disagreeing about what consciousness is, but about what form of explanation counts as adequate—whether unity must be explained by composition, by differentiation from a universal field, or by the rejection of parts altogether. Still, I wonder whether there is a hidden formal question about the transitivity of phenomenal relations, or whether the sides are in fact answering different questions—one metaphysical, one phenomenological—under the single heading of "combination."

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? I am not asking for a survey of interpretations, but for a diagnosis supported by what each camp actually says: is the dispute verbal, substantive, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about unity that neither side can justify? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a conceptual distinction, an analogy drawn from another domain, a formal result, or a reframing of the question—or should we conclude that the problem is ill-posed? I should also like to know whether an analogy from narrative form—specifically, whether a novel with multiple narrators constitutes one collective consciousness or merely coordinated voices—offers any genuine philosophical illumination, or whether it only smuggles in literary intuitions that carry no metaphysical weight.
