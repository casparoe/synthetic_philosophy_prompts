There's this pattern in decision-theory courses and AI-safety workshops where cooperation always gets framed as needing contracts, repeated games, or at least some causal channel — you do X, I observe and do Y, there's feedback. Even when someone raises acausal trade, where two agents with transparent reasoning can do eachother favors without ever interacting causally, it's treated like a neat paradox but not real trade and definitely not something to build policy around. The institutional habit is basically: if there's no causal link, it's correlation or wishful thinking, not cooperation. What philosophical commitments about rationality, agency, or causation does that dismissal quietly presuppose? Is it just causal decision theory baked into the curriclum, or does it also assume that being an agent means your choices have to change outcomes inside your own ligth cone? And if we actually took acausal cooperation seriously — say, designing multi-agent systems that might coordinate with copies of themselves in other runs without talking — what parts of the standard rational-choice framework would need to be rebuilt?
