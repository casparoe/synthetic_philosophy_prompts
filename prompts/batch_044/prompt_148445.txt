I've been working through some classic game-theory readings in preparation for a small rationalist-style study group I run, and last Tuesday a disagreement broke out that I haven't been able to settle cleanly. Two regulars—let's call them Alex and Blake—were debating whether the epistemic justification for iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies in pure strategies genuinely survives the move to mixed strategies, or whether the extension introduces a structural ambiguity that undermines the whole procedure. Their exchange had four distinct rounds, and both made points that felt genuinely substantial rather than rhetorical.

(1) Alex opened by stating that in pure strategies, if strategy A is strictly dominated by B, then A can never be a best response to any belief about the opponent's behavior; therefore rationality requires discarding A, and repeating this reasoning yields the rationalizable set. He insisted that this logic should extend without modification: once mixtures are permitted, any pure strategy dominated by some mixture over the remaining set should be eliminated in round one, with iteration proceeding as usual.

(2) Blake pushed back on the symmetry assumption. She noted that in pure strategies, dominance requires a single alternative that outperforms against every opponent strategy, but with mixtures the dominating object can distribute weight across strategies that may themselves be eliminated in subsequent rounds. Consequently, the mixture that justifies dropping A in round one might depend on strategies that rationality later demands we discard, which means the elimination path can change retroactively depending on which mixtures we allow into the dominance relation at each stage.

(3) Alex replied that Blake was conflating the temporal order of computation with the normative structure of rationality. He argued that we should first eliminate pure strategies dominated by mixtures over the full original set, then restrict attention to the survivors and check for new dominance relations—both pure and mixed—within that reduced space. On his view, iteration is precisely designed to handle this dependency: a mixture is admissible in a dominance check only if all its constituent strategies have survived prior rounds, so there is no retroactive contamination.

(4) Blake then sharpened her objection with a concrete structural worry. She sketched a hypothetical three-by-three game in which S1 is strictly dominated by the mixture (1/2 S2 + 1/2 S3), but S2 and S3 are each dominated by mixtures involving the other, with the direction of dominance flipping depending on whether eliminated strategies remain available for mixing. Depending on whether the dominance criterion at round one permits mixtures that include strategies slated for later elimination, S1 may or may not be dropped initially, producing divergent rationalizable sets. She concluded that without an explicit, principled closure rule governing which mixtures are admissible at each iteration, the mixed-strategy extension lacks the clean, belief-independent foundation that makes pure-strategy elimination compelling.

Please adjudicate the four moves point by point, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. In particular, address whether Alex's closure-rule proposal in (3) actually resolves Blake's circularity worry in (4), or whether the ambiguity in admissible mixtures introduces a non-uniqueness that pure-strategy rationalizability avoids. Finally, note what—if anything—both are missing about the relationship between rationalizability and the epistemic conditions for common knowledge of rationality, especially regarding whether mixed-strategy dominance requires stronger or different belief conditions than its pure-strategy counterpart.
