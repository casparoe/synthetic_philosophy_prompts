I'm on this county board that reviews tech pilots for the health department, and we're meeting next tuesday to decide on a 90-day trial for this diagnostic AI that updates its own reasoning rules when it finds inconsistancies. The vendor calls it "self-trusting" — basically if the system proves something in its updated framework, it treats that as true and rewrites its guidelines accordingly. I was trying to understand the risk and a friend mentioned the Löbian obstacle, which comes from Löb's theorem in modal logic: if an agent accepts that whatever it can prove is automatically true, then it can prove contradictions and end up believing anything. The vendor wants to avoid this by running the core in intuitionistic logic (where the law of excluded middle doesn't hold) and only allowing classical reflection in a sandboxed advisory layer that can't actually change the base rules. There's also this idea called updateless decision theory that supposedly sidesteps the whole problem by never updating beliefs based on predictions about yourself, but I'm not sure if that's practical for a medical system that needs to learn from new data.

We need to circulate a memo before the meeting that picks one concrete path: approve the pilot with the two-layer fix plus an external audit lock for the full 90 days, reject it outright and ask for a redesign with no self-modifying proof rules, or approve it only for non-clinical testing with no patient-facing recommendations. The memo needs a clear recommendation and timeline, and it should take seriously the argument that adaptive reasoning is necessary for medical AI — but also explain why the Löbian obstacle isn't just a programming bug, it's a structural limit on any system that tries to fully trust its own future reasoning. I'm not an academic, just a board member who wants the reasoning solid enough for the doctors and lawyers on the panel. Could you draft it? I want it formal enough to circulate but still readable. Oh, and please don't just say "consult an expert" — actually engage with whether the intuitionistic-layer proposal really solves the problem or just moves it around, and whether updateless reasoning is a real alternative here or just a red herring for practical systems.
