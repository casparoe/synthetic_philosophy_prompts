I'm delivering the opening remarks this Thursday evening at a small interdisciplinary retreat held in honor of Professor Elena Voss's retirement from the Department of Physics and Philosophy. The retreat is titled "One Mind, Many Windows," and the audience is deliberately split: roughly half are physicists and cognitive scientists who know Schrödinger chiefly from quantum mechanics and biology, and half are philosophers of mind and comparative religion scholars who have read his Vedanta-influenced essays but may not grasp the physical reasoning that led him there. I want a five- to seven-minute address that is welcoming and sets the intellectual tone for the weekend, but it must navigate a genuine structural constraint. I want it to take seriously Schrödinger's claim—drawn from his reading of the Upanishads and woven into his interpretation of quantum observation—that consciousness is singular rather than plural, and that the appearance of many separate minds is an artifact of what he described as the principle of objectification rather than a fundamental metaphysical fact. Because of this commitment, the speech should avoid the usual language of collaboration that silently presupposes multiplicity: phrases like "bringing our different perspectives together," "each of us contributing our own consciousness," or "pooling our individual insights" should be unavailable or actively problematized, since they treat separate observers as basic. At the same time, the remarks must not become a sermon or a lecture; they must explain to the scientists why this philosophical claim bears on how they think about the observer in measurement, explain to the philosophers why the physical interpretation is not optional ornament, and find some other, more careful way to speak of collective intellectual work that does not betray the metaphysics. Please write the speech so that the idea shapes what can and cannot be said—determining which welcome metaphors are permitted and which are ruled out—rather than serving as decorative introduction. It should not reduce Vedanta to vague spiritual unity, nor reduce Schrödinger to the popular image of the cat in the box, and it should not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic guidance.
