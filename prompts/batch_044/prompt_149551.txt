I've been trying to wrap my head around that debate about the police battalion guys — you know, the ones who went door to door shooting people — and whether they did it because they were just ordinary dudes caught in a bad situation or because German culture had already turned them into eager killers. I think both explanations miss something I keep seeing everywhere, like in big factories or even in how algorithms make decisions now: the terrible thing gets chopped up into tiny pieces so no single person feels like they did the whole bad thing. I'm calling it "moral chunking" for now — probably not a real term, just my own label. So can you make the arguement that this chunking explains those perpetrators better than either the situation-only view or the culture/hatred view?

Specifically, I want you to defend the idea that if the killings had been presented as one big, visible act instead of being split into seperate jobs — rounding people up, then marching them, then shooting in shifts — way more of those men would have refused, not because they were secretly good people crushed by peer pressure, and not because they were all deeply antisemitic monsters, but because the chunking let them avoid seeing themselves as actual killers. You don't need to give me a bibliography or cite specific historians; I'm not writing a paper, I just want to see if the logic holds up. Feel free to use an analogy to something like an assembly line or a chain of automated approvals, but keep the focus on why this explains the historical case more cleanly than the two famous explanations.
