I'm working through my position on moral responsibility for harm caused by adaptive autonomous systems, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present it. I hold that: (1) moral responsibility requires both causal influence over an outcome and epistemic access to the specific risk that materialised; (2) in genuinely learning-enabled systems—say, adaptive targeting algorithms—neither the deploying operator nor the design engineer retains such access, since the failure mode emerges from in-situ adaptation; (3) consequently, these cases present real responsibility gaps, with no individual human satisfying both conditions; (4) nevertheless, the deploying commander should be held fully morally responsible for wrongful harm, not merely assigned legal liability, because institutional command generates responsibility independently of foresight; (5) I reject assigning responsibility to non-agents—the system itself, the manufacturer as a corporate entity, or a designated human fall-guy inserted into the chain of command—since responsibility is essentially a property of moral agents; (6) I also reject splitting "moral responsibility" and "institutional liability" into separate concepts, viewing such a split as ad hoc; (7) however, if several engineers each contributed causally and possessed general knowledge of risk categories, they can bear distributed individual responsibility, even though the corporation bears none; and (8) on policy grounds, I support prohibiting lethal autonomous deployment, but only if the responsibility gap is genuinely unavoidable, not merely as a precautionary measure.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear so. For each real tension, rank them by severity, say what would have to give, and identify the cheapest revision. Be specific about whether (4) is doing work that (1) and (6) jointly forbid.
