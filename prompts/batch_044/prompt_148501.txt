I serve as one of the current stewards of a small digital cooperative governed by a self-written charter. Article 7 states that the charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of present stewards, but Article 7 itself may only be altered with the unanimous consent of every steward ever appointed since the charter's founding. Because many former stewards are unreachable or deceased, the unanimity condition is now practically impossible. The cooperative urgently needs to lower the amendment threshold to adapt to new regulations, and we are deadlocked over how to proceed lawfully.

One steward argues that Article 7 contains an implicit survival clause: if the amendment procedure becomes impossible, the provision should be interpreted away so the charter can remain functional. Another replies that this is precisely the reflexive paradox of legal self-reference—if a rule can authorize its own suspension, then constitutional authority dissolves into pure power, and there is no principled distinction between lawful amendment and arbitrary revision. A third proposes adopting a temporary bridge protocol outside the charter, then retroactively incorporating it through a strained reading of Article 7.

I want to know whether honoring the now-impossible procedural requirement is rational, or whether there is a philosophically coherent middle path between rigid adherence and self-defeating exemption. How should the logic of self-amending rules apply to this deadlock?
