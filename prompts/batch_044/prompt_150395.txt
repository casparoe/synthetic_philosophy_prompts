Read the following paraphrase of Aristotle, De Interpretatione 9 (c. 350 BCE). He asks whether, if every statement about the future is already either true or false, then nothing happens by chance or through deliberation: for if it is now true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then no present planning can prevent it, and planning becomes idle. He resists this by suggesting that future contingent statements—those about things that may or may not happen—do not yet have a determinate truth value, though he preserves bivalence for statements about the present and past.

Now consider the fatalist puzzle known as Death in Damascus: a traveler learns he is destined to die in Damascus, flees to Samarra to escape, yet dies there, with the story usually read as showing that his flight was not a real avoidance but part of the predetermined path.

Help me understand whether Aristotle's move—denying present truth values to future contingents—actually dissolves the Damascus argument, or only dissolves one version of it. Specifically, if we distinguish a truth-value fatalism (it is now true that he dies in Damascus, so flight is pointless) from a modal-causal fatalism (his death is necessary given prior conditions regardless of present truth values), does Aristotle's text have resources to resist the second? And does the Damascus scenario, read as a causal loop in which the flight itself is part of the chain that realizes the death in Samarra, expose a gap in Aristotle's account—namely, that denying bivalence for future statements does not by itself establish that present actions are causally efficacious?
