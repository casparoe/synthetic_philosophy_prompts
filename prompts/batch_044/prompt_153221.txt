I write about science and philosophy for a general audience, and over several years of reporting on neuroscience and theoretical biology I have become fluent enough in predictive-processing accounts of cognition—variational free energy, prediction-error minimization, precision weighting, active inference, and the trade-off between model complexity and sensory accuracy—to use them as a working vocabulary, though I have no formal theological or literary-critical training. Take the argument advanced by Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov: that human beings are constitutionally unable to bear the burden of freedom, that they will always surrender autonomy in exchange for the security of miracle, mystery, and authority, and that true happiness therefore requires the abolition of moral choice rather than its exercise. Restate this argument as precisely and charitably as you can in the vocabulary of predictive processing and active inference, treating freedom as a high-entropy, high-precision-weighting demand on an agent's generative model; happiness as the suppression of prediction error through a low-complexity, high-precision policy; and the Inquisitor's offer of bread, miracle, and authority as an externally imposed prior that minimizes free energy by constraining the agent's model space. For each element—freedom as burden, happiness as security, authority as benevolent constraint, and the specifically spiritual goods of miracle and mystery—say whether it translates cleanly, whether it must be distorted to fit (for example, by reducing normative or transcendent language to instrumental efficiency), or whether it has no counterpart whatsoever in this framework. Then deliver the residue report: does the untranslatable remainder indicate that predictive processing lacks the resources to represent normative, spiritual, or existential claims, or does it reveal that the Inquisitor's argument was already dependent on metaphysical commitments that dissolve under formalization? In particular, address whether my intuition—that the Inquisitor describes a form of cognitive offloading in which metabolic and affective efficiency are purchased at the cost of model flexibility—captures a genuine structural feature of the argument, or whether it imposes a biological vocabulary on a problem that belongs to an entirely different register.
