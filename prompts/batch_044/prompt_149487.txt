I serve on the Ethics and Recognition Subcommittee of the Meridian Foundation, which must render a decision by next Friday on whether to confer this year’s Civic Integrity Medal on Dr. Elena Voss. The medal’s charter requires that recipients have acted for the right reasons, with integrity of motive, not merely achieved a beneficial result.

The concrete case is as follows. Voss, a senior data analyst at a regional environmental-compliance firm, leaked internal documents demonstrating that her employer was systematically falsifying emissions reports. The disclosure triggered regulatory action and halted substantial ecological harm. Yet materials now before our committee—including internal communications and a private deliberative journal submitted anonymously—reveal a layered motivational profile. Voss initially considered reporting the fraud but held back, citing fear of legal retaliation and financial dependence. She ultimately released the documents only after being passed over for a promotion she regarded as deserved, writing at that point that she felt she had nothing left to protect. In her journal she employs a self-devised taxonomy that distinguishes alignment (whether an action conforms to moral requirements) from purity (whether the agent’s proximate emotional drivers are themselves moral). She rates her own conduct high on alignment but low on purity, observing that resentment and risk-calculation, rather than direct moral recognition, supplied the causal impetus.

The committee is divided over how to interpret the charter’s phrase integrity of motive. One interpretation holds that integrity requires causal dominance: the moral reason must be the principal psychological driver, with competing motives subordinate. The rival interpretation holds that integrity requires only psychological transparency to reasons—the agent’s practical reasoning must be structured so that, upon reflection, she endorses the moral reason as sufficient, even if non-moral motives provided the causal energy. Under this second view, Voss’s resentment was the engine, but her reasoning never treated the fraud as permissible; she remained responsive to its wrongness.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. The memo should state a clear recommendation—award the medal, deny it, or award it with an amended citation that explicitly addresses the motivational structure—together with a concrete time frame for announcing the decision and applying any citation amendment. It should also propose operational interpretive criteria for applying the charter’s integrity-of-motive language to future nominations, so reviewers have actionable guidance rather than abstract intuition. The reasoning must include an honest, intellectually serious treatment of the strongest argument for the position opposite your recommendation. The memo should avoid bibliographic appeals, citation lists, or references to specialized authorities that committee members could not evaluate without external access.

Verdict:
