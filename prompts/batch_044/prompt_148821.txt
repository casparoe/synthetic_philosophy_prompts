The debate over whether opponents in polarized, echo-chambered political environments argue in good faith has stalled for years: each side cites the other's curated information diets, selective engagement with counterevidence, and rhetorical closure as proof of bad faith, while the accused interpret identical behavior as rational belief-maintenance within a genuinely different evidential framework. Diagnose the nature of this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement about hidden psychological motives; a verbal dispute about whether 'good faith' names an individual cognitive virtue or a structural feature of an epistemic institution; or are the two sides answering different questions—one about personal bias, one about systemic incentive design—under a single label? To sharpen the diagnosis, apply a distinction between dispositional charity (an agent's willingness to revise beliefs when confronted with counterevidence) and structural charity (the design of institutions that make such revision feasible and rewarded), and ask whether recognizing this distinction resolves the deadlock or exposes a deeper normative conflict about where responsibility for belief-formation should lie. Finally, specify what kind of result—an empirical study, a formal model of information flow, a conceptual clarification, or an argument that the question is ill-posed—would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis.
