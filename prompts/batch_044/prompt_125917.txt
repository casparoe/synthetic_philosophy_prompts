I am finalizing the exam for an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar on archaeology, history, and the philosophy of social science. I need one essay question (90 minutes, 30 marks) on whether societal "collapse" is a coherent analytic category, tested against three cases: the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean (c. 1200 BCE), the Classic Maya southern lowlands (c. 800–900 CE), and Rapa Nui/Easter Island (c. 1600–1800 CE). The question must be unanswerable by reciting standard summaries—such as Diamond's five-point framework or generic climate-collapse narratives. Instead, students must confront a specific conceptual tension: macro-level indicators of collapse (the disappearance of monumental architecture, centralized administration, and long-distance exchange) often coexist with what I will call "transformational continuity," the archaeological persistence of domestic rituals, local exchange networks, and subsistence practices across the supposed boundary. The prompt should ask students to evaluate whether "collapse" is therefore an elite-centric misdescription that mistakes the dissolution of administrative hierarchies for societal failure, or whether a refined version of the concept can accommodate these continuities.

Please draft the essay question with four explicit parts: (1) identify one specific, non-generic mechanism of change for each case; (2) explain how transformational continuity applies differently across the three contexts; (3) address the objection that without some concept of collapse we cannot account for rapid demographic decline or political fragmentation; (4) conclude with either a precise, defensible definition of collapse or an argument for abandoning the term in cross-cultural comparison.

Then provide a model answer of approximately 800 words that engages with concrete archaeological specificity—for instance, the shift from Mycenaean palatial redistribution to local household economies without loss of ceramic traditions; the abandonment of Classic Maya southern cities alongside continued occupation and ritual activity at smaller centers; and the Rapa Nui debate over internal ecological stress versus the post-contact rupture of European slave raids and disease—and that reaches a clear conceptual conclusion.

Finally, provide a marking scheme (30 marks total, divided into named criteria) that separates an A answer, which uses the continuity evidence to challenge or precisely refine the collapse category, from a B answer, which describes the cases accurately but treats continuity as a peripheral observation rather than a conceptual problem. For each criterion, name the specific move required for full marks and the common error that should cost marks—such as treating all three cases as structurally identical, ignoring the post-contact revision for Rapa Nui, defining collapse solely as population loss, or failing to propose a precise conclusion. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific scholarly editions.
