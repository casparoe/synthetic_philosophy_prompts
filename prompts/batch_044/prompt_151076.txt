Last evening, after several hours of arguing with a close friend about Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment — in which a brain is surgically removed, sustained in a vat, and the original body receives an entirely new brain — I found myself reconstructing a sharper exchange I once witnessed between two philosophers, Lena and Marcus. Rather than asking the usual survival question, they disagreed about whether the puzzle's central pressure — especially the moment when the brain asks where it is — concerns personal identity or the conditions that anchor the spatial indexical I. In our own discussion, my friend and I added a split-hemisphere variant, which I have incorporated below. I report the exchange as a bystander and take no side.

(1) Lena opened by claiming that standard commentary misreads the scenario. Most treat it as an argument for psychological continuity, but she thinks the real pressure comes from spatial self-location. The brain retains its old proprioceptive map, so it continues to locate itself with the body in the operating room even though all sensory input now comes from the vat. That shows, she said, that I is anchored to a functional model of space, not to the physical substrate carrying the model, and not to any metaphysical identity relation.

(2) Marcus replied that this reverses the dialectic. The reason the scenario is framed in spatial terms is precisely because the identity question is being approached through intuitive location. If the brain is identical to the person, then the person should be in the vat; yet the brain's intuition pulls it toward the body. Without the underlying question — which biological system is me? — there is no puzzle at all. The spatial framing is the entry point, not the substance.

(3) Lena then introduced a modified case: the brain is bisected, the left hemisphere placed in Vat A and the right in Vat B, each fed identical sensory streams. Both initially report being in the operating room. On Marcus's identity view, since both hemispheres share the original psychological continuity, you either get fission — two persons — or must deny that either is fully the original. Both are awkward. On her spatial-anchoring view, each hemisphere eventually updates its model to its own immediate environment once its conscious access stream diverges, so each comes to locate itself in its respective vat. The shared initial report is mere inertia, not proof of shared identity.

(4) Marcus countered that Lena smuggles in the conditions she rejects. To say each hemisphere updates only after its conscious access stream diverges is to invoke a unity condition that looks exactly like psychological continuity. If that unity does the work, she has not replaced the identity view but disguised it. Moreover, her view fails to explain the original single-brain case: if I is anchored to a functional model, why does the brain not immediately update to I am in the vat once all input comes from there? The identity view explains the inertia naturally — the person's memories include being in the room — whereas her view needs an ad hoc story about model-updating failure.
