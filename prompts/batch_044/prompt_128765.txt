I am preparing the final examination for a graduate-level metaphysics seminar that has spent the term examining the ontology of relations and the internal structure of propositions. Rather than asking students to rehearse the familiar dialectic in which Bradley's regress is introduced and then dissolved by the standard relationalist reply, I want an essay question that forces them to work through a less conventional framing. Specifically, I would like the question to ask whether treating the copula not as a two-place relational entity but as a functional mapping from properties to objects genuinely dissolves the regress, or whether it merely disguises the same problem by relocating the need for an external unifier to the meta-level at which the function and its argument are jointly instantiated. The prompt should require students to assess whether such a functional account can preserve the intuitive unity of a proposition without implicitly reintroducing a relating relation, and it should be answerable by anyone who grasps the basic mechanics of Bradley's argument and has encountered functionalist approaches to predication in passing, but not answerable by anyone who has only memorized the slogan that relations relate.

Please draft a single essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam, together with a model answer of approximately 700 words and a detailed marking scheme. The model answer should explicitly distinguish between three moves: first, the claim that functional predication eliminates the need for a separate unifying entity; second, the objection that functional application itself requires an account of how the function and its argument are jointly instantiated; and third, a reasoned judgment about whether either strategy genuinely secures propositional unity or simply relocates Bradley's difficulty. The marking scheme should assign specific point values to each of these three moves, allocate further marks for conceptual clarity, and identify one specific mistake—such as conflating the functional view with a standard trope-theoretic account of predication—that weaker answers typically commit. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or request for page-specific citations; the evaluation should depend entirely on conceptual analysis rather than on recall of particular publications.
