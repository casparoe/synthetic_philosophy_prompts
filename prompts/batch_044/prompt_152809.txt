Assume the role of an expert in moral philosophy, philosophy of history, and the psychology of moral agency, with deep familiarity with perpetrator historiography and the philosophy of action. Your task is to prepare a speaker for a high-stakes joint seminar defense. Think carefully, step by step, and begin with brief brainstorming of the three main attack vectors—empirical, conceptual, and normative—before constructing the prep sheet.

The speaker is defending the following thesis. The standard dichotomy between Christopher Browning’s situational explanation of Reserve Police Battalion 101—emphasizing peer pressure, authority, and ordinary conformity—and Daniel Goldhagen’s thesis of culturally rooted, eliminationist anti-Semitism as the driving force of perpetration is a false binary that misses a more structurally embedded mechanism. The speaker proposes “moral modularity”: long-term participation in violence was sustained not by universal hatred (Goldhagen) nor by passive situational drift (Browning), but by the institutional construction of temporary, compartmentalized “moral sub-agents.” Through bureaucratic ritual, linguistic euphemism, and strict division of labor, perpetrators activated a task-specific moral module in which killing was processed as routine administrative work; outside this module, they maintained conventional domestic moral identities. This is not generic psychological compartmentalization but a structured fragmentation of moral agency mediated by institutional form. The speaker argues this explains both the variability of individual enthusiasm—some entered the module eagerly, others reluctantly—and the system’s stability without requiring either deep ideological conviction or universal moral weakness.

The audience is precisely composed as follows: social historians largely sympathetic to Browning-style explanations, skeptical of over-psychologized or over-philosophized accounts, and protective of empirical archival work; cultural historians and Holocaust scholars, some sympathetic to Goldhagen’s emphasis on ideology and culture, others critical of both poles but insistent that any new framework account for the specific content of anti-Jewish hatred in the period; and philosophers of action and moral responsibility who will press on whether “moral modularity” undermines, preserves, or transforms attributions of blame. They are sharp, have read the core historiography, and will object in the vocabulary of their respective fields.

Draft the speaker’s Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be if unanswered. For each objection:
1. Identify the audience faction raising it, phrased in the vocabulary and tone they would actually use.
2. State the objection with precision, targeting the specific mechanisms the speaker invokes—bureaucratic ritual, moral sub-agency, modularity—rather than generic skepticism.
3. Note what the speaker should concede up front.
4. Note what the speaker must hold firm on.
5. Provide a concise reply suitable for about two minutes of speaking time.
6. Flag explicitly the weakest point in that reply.

In addition, specify:
- The single reply that absolutely must land if the thesis is to survive the session.
- The question the speaker is hoping nobody asks, explaining precisely why it is dangerous and outlining how to respond if it is pressed.

Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invent specific page numbers, publication years, or direct quotations from Browning or Goldhagen; if you reference their arguments, paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure every objection turns on actual content—empirical counter-evidence, conceptual incoherence in the notion of a moral module, or normative consequences for responsibility—rather than generic “what about X?” challenges. Produce a polished, formal prep document.
