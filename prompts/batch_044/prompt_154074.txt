Exam question: Gilbert Ryle distinguishes knowing-how from knowing-that and deploys a regress argument against reducing intelligent practice to prior theoretical knowledge. Evaluate this distinction in light of the intellectualist challenge—the view that knowing-how is a species of knowing-that structured by a practical mode of presentation (e.g., knowing that w is a way for me to F). In particular, consider the case of a master surgeon who, after losing manual dexterity, directs a robotic surgical system and achieves outcomes indistinguishable from her prior direct work: does this case support Ryle’s position, support the intellectualist, or require a refinement? Also address whether allowing implicit or tacit propositional knowledge dissolves Ryle’s regress, and discuss the bearing of embodied cognition on the debate.

Student answer:
Ryle separates knowing-how, an ability or practical disposition, from knowing-that, a propositional attitude. Intellectualists such as Stanley and Williamson reject this separation, arguing that knowing-how is reducible to knowing-that—specifically, knowing that w is a way for the subject to F. This reduction misses Ryle’s central point that skill is exercised directly, without mediation by explicit premises. The surgeon case demonstrates that embodiment is inessential: since she produces identical results through a robotic interface, her expertise must consist in the propositional knowledge she applies via the machine rather than in bodily skill. Thus the case favors intellectualism over Ryle.

As for tacit knowledge, Polanyi’s concept of tacit knowing is essentially identical to Ryle’s knowing-how: both denote capacities that resist full propositional articulation. Once we allow that propositional knowledge can be implicit—held without conscious access—Ryle’s regress is blocked. The regress assumes that if every intelligent act required prior knowing-that, we would face an infinite chain. But implicit premises do not require intelligent application at each stage, because they operate automatically below the threshold of awareness. Hence admitting implicit propositional knowledge eliminates the need for a distinct non-propositional category.

Consequently, the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction collapses into a distinction between explicit and implicit propositional knowledge. The embodied component is therefore secondary; what matters is the underlying propositional structure, whether accessed consciously or not.
