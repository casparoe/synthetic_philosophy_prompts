I am grading final essays for an advanced undergraduate course on the metaphysics of causation, and I should like your assessment of one submission. Please provide a specific grade (on a standard 0–100 scale or equivalent letter grade) and then point-by-point comments that justify it precisely, distinguishing where the reasoning is sound from where it goes subtly wrong. Do not merely summarise the answer; address each step of the argument. Write your assessment in British English.

Question 4:
Consider a case of overdetermination by omission. Three security guards—A, B, and C—are each assigned to lock a separate gate to a reservoir. All three neglect their duty. The reservoir overflows because all three gates are open; had any single gate been locked, the overflow would still have occurred, since the other two remain open.

Compare how a counterfactual theory of causation (in the tradition of Lewis and Pearl) and an interventionist theory (in the tradition of Woodward) analyse the causal status of A's omission. In particular, address whether each theory attributes causation to A, and explain what each theory takes to be the relevant contrast class for evaluating that attribution. Identify one point of convergence and one point of divergence between the two approaches in this specific case.

The student's answer:

A counterfactual account, in the tradition of Lewis and developed structurally by Pearl, assesses causation by asking whether the effect counterfactually depends on the cause across a comparison class of scenarios. In this case, we evaluate A's omission by holding B and C constant at their actual values—both omitting—and asking whether the reservoir overflows in the nearest scenario in which A locks the gate. Because the other gates remain open, the overflow persists. Hence there is no counterfactual dependence, and A's omission is not a cause.

The interventionist account, as articulated by Woodward, defines causation through invariant relationships under interventions represented in a causal model. To assess A, we apply an intervention that fixes A's gate status to 'locked' while leaving all other variables at their baseline values. Since B and C still omit, the outcome does not change. Because the relationship between A's status and the overflow is not invariant under this intervention, interventionism also denies causal status to A's omission.

Both theories therefore converge: neither attributes the overflow to A individually. They diverge, however, in how they conceptualise the evaluation framework. The counterfactual approach treats the comparison as a modal selection among possible worlds governed by similarity metrics. The interventionist approach treats it as an operation on a structural model, specifically a do-operator that severs incoming causal arrows to the intervened variable. The key divergence is that the counterfactual analysis requires specifying a similarity ordering over worlds, which becomes ambiguous when multiple omissions are present, whereas the interventionist analysis requires only that the model's equations remain stable. Consequently, in complex cases of collective omission, interventionism provides a more determinate verdict because it does not depend on resolving modal ambiguity.
