I am a high school senior writing for an online philosophy forum as part of my independent study in philosophy of perception, and I have encountered what seems to me to be an unacknowledged difficulty for the disjunctivist account of illusion. As I understand the standard dialectic, the argument from illusion begins with the observation that a veridical perception—say, seeing a straight stick submerged in water—and a corresponding illusory experience—seeing that same stick as bent—are subjectively indistinguishable to the perceiver. From this, the argument concludes that both experiences must share a common mental component, such as a sense-datum or representational content, which is present regardless of whether the external object matches the appearance. The disjunctivist is said to block this inference by denying that the two experiences belong to a common mental kind: in the veridical case, the object itself is partly constitutive of the experience, whereas in the illusory case the subject is in an altogether different state that merely resembles the first, with no shared substrate. This distinction strikes me as plausible for ordinary sensory modalities, but I have been considering a hypothetical scenario I call a "recursive mediated illusion," involving a cortical sensory-substitution interface that translates ambient thermal data into auditory patterns.

Suppose that due to a calibration error, the device produces a sustained high-pitched tone corresponding to no actual heat source, and further suppose that the trained user reports a cross-modal association in which this erroneous tone "sounds crimson"—a synthetic phenomenal quality that combines auditory and chromatic features for which there is no physical correlate whatsoever. If the disjunctivist maintains that in veridical operation of the device the auditory experience is constituted by the actual thermal environment, then in this glitched case there is neither the correct thermal object nor any crimson object, yet there remains a unified phenomenal experience with determinate qualitative character. Must the disjunctivist not then concede that there exists at least one common mental factor—a synthetic cross-modal representation or phenomenal substrate—that is present in both the veridical and the illusory conditions? And if such a factor exists, does this not revive the argument from illusion specifically for technologically mediated and cross-modal perception, forcing the disjunctivist either to abandon the claim that illusion and perception are metaphysically disjoint, or else to deny that this glitched state counts as an experience at all?
