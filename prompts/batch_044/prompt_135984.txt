Essay draft:

Standard accounts of moral compromise treat value conflict as a bargaining problem: agents with different preference orderings negotiate to a point on a Pareto frontier, accepting losses in one domain for gains in another. This model assumes a shared metric of value—if not cardinal utility, then at least ordinal comparability—without which trade is unintelligible. But many deep conflicts—say, between a deontological framework that treats certain acts as categorically forbidden and a consequentialist framework that evaluates them by aggregate welfare—seem to lack such a metric. The values are incommensurable not merely in practice but in principle.

I propose we understand these compromises instead through the lens of procedural bilingualism. Rather than translating each party’s commitments into a neutral currency, the parties construct institutional interfaces—rules of engagement, veto structures, jurisdictional boundaries—that allow coordinated action without requiring either side to redescribe its reasons in the other’s vocabulary. The compromise lives not in the outcome but in the architecture of interaction. Each party retains its internal evaluative language intact; what changes is the practical grammar of how they relate. This preserves incommensurability at the level of justification while achieving coordination at the level of behavior.

The risk, of course, is that the procedural rules themselves embody a hidden meta-language. To agree on a veto structure is already to accept some shared conception of legitimate authority. If so, procedural bilingualism may not escape value imperialism but merely relocate it from the domain of outcomes to the domain of institutional design.

Request for comments:

Please comment on this essay, focusing particularly on whether the central analogy holds. Does treating moral compromise as linguistic interface-building rather than as preference aggregation actually preserve the incommensurability it claims to respect, or does the need for shared procedural rules smuggle in a hidden meta-language that undermines the framing? Take a definite position—either defend the framing against this objection or show why it fails—and explain precisely what that verdict implies for whether moral compromise can ever avoid some form of value imperialism. I also want your direct assessment of whether the essay misses a stronger counterexample: specifically, cases in which one party’s value system forbids even entering procedural arrangements with the other, not merely disagreeing over outcomes within them. Be committed and decisive in your judgments; do not simply enumerate possible interpretations or list objections and leave the evaluation to me. If you think the framing is defensible, say so and show where its limits lie; if you think it collapses, say so and identify the exact point of failure.
