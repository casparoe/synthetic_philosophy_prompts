I'm presenting a seminar paper next month to a philosophy-of-literature reading group split evenly between analytic philosophers of time—mostly B-theorists, plus one specialist in branching-time logic—and literary scholars who work closely on Borges's essays and fiction. The thesis I defend is that Borges's treatment of time, particularly across A New Refutation of Time, The Garden of Forking Paths, and the Library of Babel imagery, should not be read as a metaphysical commitment to cyclical eternal recurrence or to an Everett-style branching ontology. Instead, I propose an archival model: temporal repetition and apparent divergence are artifacts of cataloging errors, misfiled copies, and corrupted metadata within an infinite self-indexing archive. On this reading, Borges's denial of time is not Berkeleyan idealism but a claim that chronological order is an imposed indexing scheme that the Library's disorder perpetually undermines. The philosophers in the room know Lewis and Stalnaker on branching well; the literary scholars are protective of Borges's ambiguity and skeptical of system-building.
