You are the lead behavioral auditor for a certification authority that evaluates adaptive agents—whether biological organisms, artificial systems, or institutional protocols—for designation as either flexibly autonomous or sphexishly rigid. You process approximately twenty cases each month, working solely from behavioral logs, design specifications, and third-party observer reports; you have no access to internal neural architectures, source code, or subjective reports, and every recommendation must be co-signed by a second auditor within forty-five minutes. Your authority applies the distinction for practical liability allocation: agents classified as sphexish are treated as instruments (their operators bear undivided responsibility), while those classified as flexible are treated as co-decision-makers (liability is distributed).

The governing framework distinguishes sphexishness from flexibility along three behavioral axes, not a single continuum. First, canonical-loop persistence: the agent repeats an identical ordered sequence of sub-routines even when intermediate environmental feedback should rationally alter the path. Second, termination-insensitivity: the agent continues the canonical sequence after the ostensible goal-state has been achieved, destroyed, or rendered impossible. Third, perturbation-filtering: the agent systematically disregards sensory or informational inputs that are causally relevant to goal-achievement but fall outside the pre-established trigger conditions.

Flexibility, in this framework, is not random variation or noise. It is teleological reconfiguration: the agent preserves the terminal goal-condition but revises the means-path in response to feedback, including breaking the canonical sequence when necessary. Crucially, flexibility can occur within a highly constrained domain, while rigidity can occur amid complex, branching behavior if every branch is a pre-specified response to a fixed menu of triggers.

Translate this framework into an operational audit procedure that a reviewer without philosophical training can apply consistently. Present it as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the specific questions to ask of the available evidence in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer for each, including which logs or reports are required; the decision nodes where the audit stops with a classification, stops with an explicit deferral to human judgment, or stops with a determination that the evidence is insufficient; and the exact points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be flagged in writing.

The procedure must handle the following three cases, which you should work through as explicit applications:

1. A drone delivery system detects an obstacle, computes a detour, delivers the package, and then—having completed the mission—returns to its original flight-path sequence and attempts to navigate through the now-irrelevant obstacle rather than returning to base. The detour computation appears adaptive.

2. A clinical patient with severe compulsive-checking behavior finds a door already locked, skips the locking sub-routine, but still completes the remaining twelve steps of the departure ritual in fixed order, then reports distress that the ritual was "incomplete" despite the functional goal being satisfied.

3. A reinforcement-learning agent exhibits highly variable behavior across episodes—different paths, different timings, different intermediate actions—but all variation is fully explained by gradient ascent on a single, unchanging reward function with no representational access to the terminal state except through that gradient. The agent never revises its reward structure or its optimization strategy; it only locates new local maxima.

Apply the procedure to all three cases. Identify which case yields a classification that conflicts with an intuitive judgment an auditor might hold, and explain precisely why the procedure forces that counter-intuitive result. Additionally, specify what the procedure cannot determine—draw a sharp line between the empirical behavioral questions it resolves and the metaphysical, legal, or ethical questions it explicitly leaves open. Conclude with a brief misfire-warning section: describe two scenarios in which the procedure is likely to misclassify because of limitations in observable data, and state what additional evidence would be required to correct each error.

Write the deliverable as a formal procedure document suitable for direct inclusion in an auditor's manual, with the role, stakes, time constraints, and co-signature requirement built into the framing. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask for quotations from specific texts. Where the procedure reaches its limits, mark the boundary explicitly rather than disguising a judgment call as a technical step.
