You serve on your university museum's preservation and acquisitions committee. A donor has offered full funding to reconstruct a 1983 conceptual installation whose original physical substrates were destroyed in a 1992 storage fire; the museum retains only the complete grounding archive—correspondence specifying dimensional constraints, relational diagrams showing how the work depended on specific architectural features of its original gallery, and the artist's protocols stating that the material objects were never the fundament of the work but only its local grounds. One faction of the committee argues that without those original material entities, any reconstruction is ontologically a different object, an imitation whose existence is grounded in documentation rather than in the authentic artwork itself. The opposing faction holds that the installation was always fundamentally a relational structure, so that rebuilding it precisely from the archive restores the original rather than producing a copy. The director requires a recommendation for next Friday's vote: approve reconstruction within a defined conservation framework, reject it on ontological grounds, or approve it only under display conditions that explicitly mark its reconstructed status. Draft the committee's decision memo. State a clear recommendation with a concrete implementation or refusal timeline. Engage seriously with the strongest metaphysical argument on the losing side—whether that concerns the fundamentality of material substrates or the priority of relational grounding—without reducing that view to a mere confusion about art history. Also address any obligations the museum owes to the donor's conditions and to viewers who might otherwise mistake a reconstructed relational structure for an original material presence.
