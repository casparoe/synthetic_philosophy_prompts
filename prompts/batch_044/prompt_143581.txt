Having spent decades in laboratory and field research before turning to philosophy, I have been wrestling with the decision-theoretic result—commonly discussed under the heading of the value of information—that a rational agent with fixed preferences and a well-defined prior should never decline genuinely cost-free information relevant to a decision. I ask for your credence, expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1, that there exists at least one philosophically coherent scenario in which a scientist, equipped with stable priors and a constant utility function over competing theoretical models, should rationally refuse to observe a perfectly accurate, zero-resource signal indicating which model is correct, where the refusal is not explained by a shift in preferences or by hidden practical costs, but rather by the claim that possessing that knowledge itself disrupts the decision framework. Please decompose your reasoning: assign a rough probability to (i) whether the formal proof is genuinely exceptionless under its stated conditions; (ii) whether epistemic or computational burdens can legitimately be excluded from the cost-free condition without circular reasoning; (iii) whether rational self-binding or pre-commitment can constitute genuine refusal rather than indirect information acceptance; combine these terms with brief justifications, and present the final product. Then tell me which term carries the greatest weight of uncertainty, and state precisely what argument or finding—whether in decision theory, cognitive science, or philosophy of science—would most significantly shift your overall number. Finally, indicate the point at which your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
