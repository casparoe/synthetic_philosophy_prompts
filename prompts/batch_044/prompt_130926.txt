I was arguing with my friends about Buridan's ass the other night - not the original donkey story, but whether choice ever actually happens without some hidden preference lurking in the background. My gut verdicts on cases are all over the place and I can't line them up. If two bales of hay are perfecly identical and the donkey starves, that's not rational choice, that's a system failure. But flipping a coin between two job offers that feel exactly the same? Totally fine, I do that myself sometimes. Picking a random seed for two equally good neural network architectures? Also fine, seems practical. But then there's this person I know who spent five years unable to pick between med school and law school, both seemed perfect, and they ended up doing neither - that feels like the same failure mode as the donkey, just slower and more painful. And in AI there's this weird case where an expected-utility agent with symmetric payoffs loops forever comparing them instead of acting - clearly broken. So coin-flips and random seeds are okay, starvation and infinite loops are not, but the five-year human paralysis is somewhere in the middle.

I keep going back and forth on whether there's one principle that captures all this, or if I'm just smuggling in my own biases about agency and time. What's the actual distinction between breaking a tie and being paralyzed by one? And whatever principle you come up with, does it cover a case I'm unsure about - a self-driving car that has to swerve left or right to avoid two identical obstacles and picks purely based on the microsecond timestamp of its sensor? That feels like legitimate tie-breaking to me, but I can't explain why it's different from the med-school guy who also had no reason to prefer one path. Also, if your principle says something about the donkey itself, I want to know: is starvation actually rational if there's genuinely zero preference?
