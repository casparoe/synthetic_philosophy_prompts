As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of computation, I have become dissatisfied with the standard taxonomy that treats the Church-Turing thesis as either an empirical generalization about physical devices or a purely stipulative definition. Please argue for a third position: that the thesis functions as an epistemic boundary condition, structurally analogous to the second law of thermodynamics in its role of constraining perpetual-motion explanations, so that it does not describe what machines happen to do but rather delimits what can count as a legitimate computational explanation within our current theoretical framework. In developing this argument, address the following points with precision. First, distinguish a hypothetical physical counterexample—say, an analog device that exploits continuous state spaces to solve the halting problem—from a genuine revision of the concept of effective calculability, and show why the former fails to threaten the thesis if the thesis is understood as boundary-setting rather than descriptive. Second, explain why treating the thesis as empirically falsifiable by future technology commits a category error comparable to treating energy conservation as falsified by an anomalous heat measurement in an open system. Third, respond to the objection that certain quantum-computing proposals, particularly those invoking non-computable dynamics in quantum field theories, already exert empirical pressure; argue that such proposals either implicitly rely on an oracle or redefine the problem space rather than extending effective computation in the relevant sense. Throughout, do not appeal to authority, bibliography, or citation; I want the conceptual architecture of the argument laid out on its own terms.
