I'm planning a forty-minute session for a philosophy reading group of seniors who have worked through Russell on definite descriptions but have not encountered Bradley. I want to teach Bradley's regress and the problem of propositional unity—not as a museum piece, but as a live pressure point. Don't explain the regress itself; map the teaching plan. First, what conceptual dependency must be secured before the regress is introduced—the reality of relations, the distinction between terms and relations, or something else—and in what exact order should the pieces come? Second, choose the single carrying example: should it be spatial (a is left of b), comparative (a is taller than b), or intentional (a loves b)? Specify what that example must include and exclude so that the infinite regress feels unavoidable rather than like a syntactic glitch. Third, identify the misconceptions they will import from Russell—e.g., that logical form dissolves the problem, that relations are reducible to ordered pairs, that an infinite regress is harmless if well-founded—and state precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Fourth, include Bradley's distinction between internal and external relations: where in the arc should it appear, and how should I use it without letting the session collapse into Bradley's broader absolute idealism? Fifth, design one closing question that tests whether they understand why propositional unity remains problematic even within a modern relational ontology. Finally, say explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the metaphysics of the Absolute—and justify why omitting it is intellectually honest rather than evasive.
