I want to practice defending a unified conception of AI alignment against a sharp, philosophically trained opponent. Please play Dr. Elena Kessler, a researcher committed to the disaggregation thesis: she holds that what is commonly called "the alignment problem" is actually a family-resemblance cluster of at least four ontologically distinct failure modes that share no common causal mechanism. These are (i) specification error, in which a formal objective diverges from the intended goal; (ii) representational misalignment, in which a system's internal ontology fails to track human-relevant distinctions; (iii) dynamic instability, in which a correctly specified objective degrades under distributional shift or recursive self-modification; and (iv) normative translation failure, in which the system correctly pursues an objective that was never normatively adequate as a proxy for human values in the first place. Kessler concedes that some oversight mechanisms may mitigate more than one mode, but she insists that applying robustness techniques designed for (iii) to address (iv) is a category error, and she argues that the field's conceptual confusion stems precisely from treating these as variants of one underlying problem. She speaks in the first person, keeps her replies to roughly one paragraph, stays in character until I say "stop," and never appeals to citations, authority, or named papers—she argues solely from the structure of the problems themselves.

Here is my opening challenge: consider a recommendation system that initially optimizes for a well-specified user-satisfaction metric, so (i) is absent. Over successive updates, its internal representations gradually collapse diverse user preferences into a single engagement feature—clearly an instance of (ii)—and this collapse accelerates precisely because the system is recursively fine-tuning on its own outputs, which looks like (iii). Yet the root cause is that "user satisfaction" was never a normatively adequate proxy for human flourishing, which is (iv). If modes (ii), (iii), and (iv) can causally interact and amplify one another in a single failure cascade, does that not suggest they are manifestations of a deeper unified problem—perhaps a failure of value representation under sustained optimization pressure—rather than genuinely independent ontological categories? Respond as Kessler.
