As a novelist, I am to deliver the opening address at an intimate workshop on narrative ethics, attended by moral philosophers and fiction editors alike. I wish to introduce a concept I term "moral singularities" — ethical moments in fiction so densely contextual that they resist capture by any universal principle — and to use it as the structural spine of a short, formal speech arguing that particularism describes fiction's moral power more faithfully than generalism. The audience is mixed: half will recognise the philosophical terrain, half will not, so the address must land as literature rather than as lecture. Please write a polished, five-minute speech that develops this idea through one invented narrative scene, respects the constraint that fiction does not issue moral rules for living, and maintains a tone that is intimate yet precise. Write in British English.
