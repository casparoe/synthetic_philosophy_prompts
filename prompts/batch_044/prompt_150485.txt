I am in the middle of negotiating a six-month consulting contract that is revised every fortnight. At each revision, my client introduces one additional deliverable and extends the deadline by a few days, but also increases my fee by a sum that, taken on its own, comfortably exceeds the value of the extra work. If I agree to every revision, by month six I will be producing nearly double the original scope for roughly thirty per cent more pay, and this trajectory is visible to me from the very first change. Nevertheless, at each individual decision point, rejecting the revised offer feels irrational: I would be forfeiting certain extra income to avoid a workload increase that, confined to that single fortnight, seems perfectly manageable. There is a further psychological feature: once I have accepted one revision, the next is assessed not against my original contract but against the already-expanded version, so every local comparison still favours acceptance even though the global path is plainly inferior to my initial agreement. I need help understanding whether any rational failure is occurring—whether it resides in each separate acceptance, in my failure to commit to the original terms, or in the shifting baseline against which I evaluate successive offers. Please address the philosophical structure underlying this situation, distinguish what is well established in theories of rational choice from what is genuinely disputed among experts, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus. Write in British English.
