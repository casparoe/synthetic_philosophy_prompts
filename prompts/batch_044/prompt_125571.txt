I am delivering the opening remarks at the interdisciplinary workshop "Maintenance as Moral Practice," convened jointly by philosophers of care ethics and municipal infrastructure planners. Roughly half the audience are scholars in feminist moral philosophy and disability studies; the other half are urban planners, public-works administrators, and maintenance engineers with no philosophical background. The workshop asks whether sustained, low-visibility upkeep—sewer maintenance, chronic-care nursing, archival preservation—can be understood as an ethical practice rather than as a mere background condition for crisis-oriented or heroic action. I want care ethics to do genuine structural work in the remarks: it should constrain what can be said about whose labor counts as morally significant, prevent retreat into generic praise for unsung heroes, and require a precise distinction between care and service delivery that does not rely on standard textbook formulations. The remarks should be about five minutes long, must land for both audiences without academic jargon, and should treat dependency not as a temporary failure state but as a permanent structural feature of any maintained system. Please write the remarks so that the philosophical claim shapes what is said—including what must be left unsaid—rather than serving as decorative framing.
