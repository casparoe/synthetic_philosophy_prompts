The exam asks students to apply the distinction between "thin" and "thick" epistemic dependance on AI. Thin means using the system like a database for stuff you could in principle verify or organize yourself; thick means outsourcing the justification structure itself — the weighting, exclusion, and inferential ordering that turns raw claims into a defended position. There's also this condition called recursive transparency: to understand a conclusion, you have to be able to rebuild the reasoning chain back to premises you can defend without reopening the external source.

Question: A researcher feeds her raw notes on five studies into an LLM. She asks it to identify the collective gap they reveal, rank the studies by relevance to that gap, and propose a narrative arc linking them. She then writes the grant section in her own sentences, keeps the LLM's gap identification, ranking, and arc intact, and understands each individual study well. Is her dependance thin or thick? Does she retain recursive transparency over the section's argumentative structure? Pinpoint exactly where, if anywhere, understanding erodes — not just in knowing the studies, but in the relation between them.

Student answer:
'I think this is mostly thin dependance with one thick pocket. The researcher clearly knows the five studies since she wrote the notes, so feeding them to the LLM is just organizing data she already possesses — like using a spreadsheet. The gap identification is also thin; she's just asking for pattern-recognition she could do herself with more time, and she can defend the gap's existence on her own.

The ranking by relevance is where it gets thick, since she's outsourcing the criteria that weight one study over another. But I don't think understanding erodes here, because knowing the studies doesn't depend on knowing their order — ordering is presentational, not part of the justification. The narrative arc works the same way: it's just a writing device.

She keeps recursive transparency because she can explain each study's contribution and the gap without reopening the chat. Even if she can't immediately reconstruct why study C comes before study D, that reconstruction is implicit in her having written the sentences herself. So there's no serious erosion — at worst a thin dependency on discovery, not on understanding.'

Grade it. Give a letter or point score and then specific point-by-point comments that justify it. Be precise about which reasoning steps hold up and which ones slide past something important. Don't ask for outside reading or citations — just assess what's right here.
