Please take on the role of a senior philosopher of mind with expertise in collective cognition and the philosophy of biology, and think carefully through the structural constraints before composing anything. I am delivering the opening remarks at our university’s interdisciplinary workshop, “Minds Beyond the Skin,” a two-day meeting that brings together cognitive scientists who study social insects, philosophers of mind skeptical of distributed cognition, and a large contingent of graduate students from literature and history who have never encountered the debate in formal terms. Over coffee last week a colleague and I found ourselves asking whether it could ever make sense to apologize to an ant colony—not to any individual ant, but to the collective itself, as though one might say “I’m sorry” to Aunt Hillary—and I realized that this strange grammatical possibility might be precisely what divides the two camps in the room.

The core philosophical idea I want to do real structural work here is drawn from Hofstadter’s dialogue involving Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue: the colony is not a mind in the sense of being one large brain hidden inside the hill, but rather something like a fugue in which individual ants are voices that enter, respond, and fall silent, with no conductor and no score existing apart from the performance itself. I want these remarks to treat that image not as ornament but as a binding constraint: whatever I say about collective minds must remain sayable in a grammar that permits second-person address to the collective without reducing it to metaphor, and must avoid formulations that treat the colony either as a mere aggregate of individual ant-minds or as a hidden Cartesian subject. The audience is genuinely split—half will catch every reference to emergent intentionality and supervenience debates, the other half will be lost if I use that vocabulary—so the speech must land for both without lecturing either.

Please write a polished, formal opening address of roughly five to seven minutes when spoken, suitable for an academic symposium but genuinely welcoming to non-specialists. Begin by briefly brainstorming the tension between describing a collective and addressing it, then draft remarks that weave the fugue image through three movements: an invitation to the workshop, a framing of what is genuinely at stake in asking whether an ant colony can have a mind, and a closing that establishes ground rules for the discussions to come—specifically, that we will not allow the debate to collapse into either eliminativism about the colony or reification of it into a little homunculus. Include one concrete, visceral scenario that makes the second-person problem intelligible to listeners who have never studied philosophy of mind, ensure the philosophical idea shapes what can and cannot be said rather than merely decorating the speech, and conclude with an honest admission of what the fugue analogy cannot reproduce—perhaps the problem of suffering, mortality, or the absence of a single narrative self—so that I do not overclaim. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations, and avoid technical jargon that requires prior knowledge of specific contemporary theories.
