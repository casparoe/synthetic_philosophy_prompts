I am trying to clarify what distinguishes a theory that was genuinely falsified from one that was merely right but early, using long-run predictions as my test bed. My verdicts are as follows. Thomas Malthus argued that population growth tends to outpace food production, and I think this was not falsified by the twentieth-century agricultural boom but was right but delayed: the mechanism of exponential pressure remains intact, and the delay is explained by technological innovations that the theory itself did not predict, not by a flaw in the underlying dynamics. By contrast, the well-known 1970s projection that global petroleum reserves would be exhausted by the turn of the century strikes me as genuinely falsified, because its rescue requires positing unknown reserves that the original model did not allow for and that do not follow from its mechanism. On the other hand, warnings from the 1940s that widespread antibiotic use would eventually select for resistant pathogens dominating hospital environments seemed speculative at the time but were later borne out; here I think the mechanism was correctly specified and only the timeline was underestimated, so this is right but early rather than rescued by ad hoc repair. A different case is a 1990 climate projection that predicted a specific 2.5-degree warming by 2010, which failed to materialize and was then explained by an unanticipated aerosol-cooling factor added to the model after the fact; I judge this not right but early but rather disconfirmed, since the rescue introduces an external factor not present in the original mechanism. Finally, there is a demographic theory predicting that global fertility would stabilize near replacement level around 2020, which roughly occurred but for reasons—urbanization patterns, education access, contraceptive diffusion—that differ from those the theory specified; I keep going back and forth on whether this counts as confirmation of the mechanism, accidental accuracy, or being right but early about a structural transition whose drivers were misidentified. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps turning on whether the rescue invokes the original mechanism versus an exogenous shock, or on whether the delay is quantitatively derivable from that mechanism—that captures my first four verdicts and resolves my uncertainty about the fifth? If no single principle saves all of them, which verdict should be revised? Please also apply your principle to Malthus specifically: does it make sense to say his prediction was delayed by an exogenous technological shock, or should we read him as advancing a structural claim about growth dynamics that carries no specific temporal commitment?
