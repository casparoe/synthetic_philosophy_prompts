Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the different ways a statistical or metaphysical argument can overgeneralize, and consider what distinguishes a legitimate explanatory category from an arbitrary demographic slice.

Here is an argument presented at a workshop on generational ontology. Each premise seems defensible, but the chain ends in a place the speaker admits is uncomfortable.

(1) In demographic and epidemiological modeling, age, period, and cohort effects are mathematically non-identifiable: without an arbitrary constraint, the same observed data can be decomposed into different combinations of aging trajectories, historical moments, and birth-cohort influences.

(2) Any claim that "Generation Z exhibits elevated anxiety because of smartphone adolescence" therefore presupposes a particular, unmotivated constraint on that decomposition.

(3) So "Gen Z" is not tracking a real causal kind; it is a statistical artifact of how the researcher fixed the model.

(4) The same non-identifiability applies to standard social categories: "working-class" outcomes can be decomposed into lifecycle earnings, macroeconomic period, and birth-cohort advantages; "racial disparities" can be decomposed into age-structure differences, era-specific policies, and cohort-specific socialization.

(5) Therefore, if cohort non-identifiability undermines generational kinds, it equally undermines class and race as explanatory categories.

(6) But treating class and race as purely statistical artifacts is deeply implausible; they clearly structure real social mechanisms.

(7) So the argument proves too much.

The speaker wants to keep (3) and reject (6) as overly intuitive, but cannot say why (4) fails. Where is the hidden slip? Is it the move from statistical underdetermination to ontological unreality in (3)? Is the parity in (4) false because race and class are anchored by mechanisms—exploitation, racialization, institutional tracking—that do not depend on the same model constraints? Or is there a modal slide between "we cannot uniquely identify the effect" and "there is no effect to identify"? Identify the weakest step, explain why it looked fine, and price the cost of rejecting it: if you save generations by saving class and race, do you commit to an overly bloated ontology of cohort-kinds? If you reject the parity, what principled distinction separates a legitimate social kind from an arbitrary seven-year birth window?
