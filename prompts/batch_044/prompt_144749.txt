I've been thinking about how people use past conflicts to argue about what we should do now—comparing a rising challenger and an established power to Athens and Sparta in the so-called Thucydides trap, comparing diplomatic compromise to Munich, or comparing a long foreign intervention to Vietnam. Each analogy seems to push a different policy, which makes me wonder whether the history is really guiding the decision or just dressing it up. To test this, I want to apply an idea I haven't seen used much in these debates: treating the choice of analogy like the reference-class problem in statistics, where the category you place a case in determines the prediction you draw from it. Please audit the Thucydides-trap analogy using that frame. First, lay out the correspondence table: in today's world, what plays the role of Sparta, of Athens, of the alliance networks, and of the fear of being overtaken? Second, identify where the mapping breaks—especially whether nuclear deterrence and deep economic integration have any analogue in ancient Greece, and whether those gaps matter for the conclusion that war is structurally inevitable. Third, apply the reference-class idea directly: if we place current rivalry in the class of Britain and Germany around 1900, or of the United States and Britain in the nineteenth century, or even in the class of Vietnam-style quagmires rather than great-power wars, does the same "prepare for conflict" conclusion survive? Finally, tell me whether the Thucydides analogy is actually carrying the policy recommendation, or whether the recommendation was smuggled in by the choice of comparison class, and suggest what a more honest reference class would be if we genuinely want history to inform rather than justify our choices.
