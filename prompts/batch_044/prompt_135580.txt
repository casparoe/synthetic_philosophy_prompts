Consider the practice now widespread in both applied moral psychology and AI value-alignment research: investigators present human subjects with paired moral judgments—say, whether to prioritize saving five lives over one in variants of the trolley problem—under systematically varied framing conditions. They then apply statistical correction procedures, such as counterbalancing order effects, removing anchoring adjustments, and filtering responses that shift with irrelevant emotional priming, with the explicit aim of isolating what they call the subject’s stable underlying moral preference from what they classify as response bias or noise. The corrected aggregate is then treated as the legitimate input for building preference models or normative recommendations, as if the procedure has stripped away distortion to reveal a pre-existing moral fact about the subject.

This practice seems to depend on a distinction that philosophers have long found unstable: the boundary between a person’s genuine moral preferences and the distorting biases that obscure them. What philosophical commitments must one hold to make this correction procedure coherent? In particular, does it presuppose a form of moral realism about personal values—something like an inner moral taste that can be corrupted by presentation effects but recovered through statistical cleaning? Or can the practice be reconstructed within a constructivist or expressivist framework, where preferences are formed in the very act of judgment, so that bias correction is not uncovering a hidden preference but manufacturing a more consistent one? Furthermore, examine whether the practice is internally consistent: if framing effects are pervasive and there is no view from nowhere from which to correct them, then the choice of which effects to treat as biases rather than as constitutive of moral judgment appears to smuggle in normative assumptions about what morality should look like—perhaps coherence, stability, or independence from emotional context.

If the implicit theory behind this correction practice were replaced—say, by a view that moral judgments are irreducibly situated and that variability across frames reveals genuine moral complexity rather than error—what would have to change in how alignment researchers aggregate human values? Would the very project of extracting a single, cleaned preference profile for an AI system become conceptually suspect, and if so, what institutional alternatives—perhaps preserving rather than smoothing disagreement—would become more defensible?
