In debates over moral status and sentience, I repeatedly encounter a particular architecture of reasoning. A speaker begins by rejecting binary accounts—pointing out that nociception in insects, temperature-regulation in devices, and reflective self-awareness in humans form a smooth continuum, so moral weight should scale gradually rather than switch on at some magical boundary. Yet when the practical question arises—whether pest control owes insects non-negligible consideration, or whether a thermostat has any moral claim—the same speaker reintroduces a sharp exclusion, typically justified by invoking “the right kind” of integration or “genuine” experience, without locating that cutoff on the continuous scale previously endorsed. Meanwhile, within the privileged class that clears this hidden gate—say, among mammals—the gradation is restored to explain why a chimpanzee matters more than a mouse. The result is continuous weighting inside an unexplained categorical wall, with the wall itself never mapped onto the spectrum. Is there an established concept for exactly this two-layer structure—gradualism deployed internally, categorical exclusion deployed externally, with the boundary justified independently of the scale? If there is, how does it differ from ordinary threshold-setting and from pure gradualism? If not, can you coin and delimit a precise term, specifying what counts as an instance, what nearby patterns (such as simple equivocation or a standard non-identity problem) it excludes, and where its edges lie?
