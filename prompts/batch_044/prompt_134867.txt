I'm working through Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste for a philosophy of art seminar, and I've become stuck on a passage near the middle where he treats what he calls the test of time. He observes that certain poems, paintings, and musical works continue to please across different nations, climates, and historical fashions, and he treats this endurance as a kind of empirical check on merely local prejudice. Yet he insists that this endurance does not establish any objective quality in the work itself—only that a common structure of human sentiment is being reliably activated. At the same time, he argues that correcting for prejudice requires the judgment of true judges who possess refined delicacy of taste, comparative practice, and freedom from parochial bias.

What confuses me is the precise status of this test. Hume suggests—approximately—that when the general verdict of cultivated judges, sustained across generations, favors a particular work, this serves to correct individual deviation, but it still remains a verdict of sentiment rather than reason. The survival of the work functions like an intersubjective filter, yet he refuses to call the result an objective standard independent of human response. I want to understand whether there is a consistent distinction here between saying that this work reliably activates refined sentiment in diverse contexts and saying that this work possesses objective beauty.

To push the point into somewhat unfamiliar territory: if we imagine a collective judgment mechanism—say, an archive that aggregates aesthetic responses across centuries without any single participant possessing the full delicacy of Hume's ideal judge—does Hume's framework allow such distributed calibration to count as a legitimate test of time? Or does his reliance on the cultivated individual make any synthetic, algorithmic aggregation of preference conceptually illegitimate? In other words, can his account accommodate collective, distributed taste-formation without collapsing into the very procedural objectivity he rejects?

Please help me understand this tension. In your response, please say how confident you are in your interpretation of Hume's position, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—particularly regarding whether distributed judgment violates his conditions for true judgment.
