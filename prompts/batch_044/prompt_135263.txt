In our metaphysics seminar, a fellow student circulated a study-sheet summary of the counterpart-theory versus transworld-identity debate that I am trying to evaluate before our exam. The summary runs as follows: David Lewis’s counterpart theory holds that possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated totalities, and that no individual exists in more than one world; consequently, when we say that I could have been a musician, we are not attributing musicianship to me in another world, but rather noting that some other-world counterpart—someone qualitatively very similar to me—is a musician. Because the counterpart relation is primitive and grounded in overall qualitative resemblance, Lewis avoids attributing contradictory properties to one and the same object across worlds. By contrast, the transworld-identity view associated with Saul Kripke and Alvin Plantinga maintains that the very same individual exists in multiple worlds, so de re modal claims are literally about that individual having different properties elsewhere. The summary then asserts that, because counterpart theory denies cross-world identity altogether, it is fundamentally incompatible with essentialism: if I share strict identity with no inhabitant of other worlds, there can be no essential properties that I must share with my world-mates, and the notion of an essential property collapses into a mere regularity among counterparts. Meanwhile, transworld identity is said to depend on every object possessing a complete individual essence—a haecceity—that fixes which world-inhabitants genuinely count as me, and without such essences the view collapses into arbitrariness about modal attribution. Finally, the summary claims that both views face a shared, underappreciated difficulty with accidental intrinsics—properties such as being bent or having a precise mass—because counterpart theory must distribute these across worlds via similarity relations that appear circular (resemblance presupposes shared properties, yet properties are what resemblance is supposed to explain), while transworld identity must allow one and the same object to instantiate contradictory intrinsic shapes in different worlds without any principled boundary between essential and accidental form.

I am inclined to accept the broad architecture, but I am uncertain about the specific claims linking counterpart theory to the rejection of essentialism, the characterization of haecceities as required for every object, and the framing of the accidental-intrinsic problem as a shared circularity rather than an asymmetrical challenge. Could you audit this summary claim by claim? For each segment, please indicate whether it is accurate, whether it represents a defensible simplification, whether it subtly misrepresents the view it describes, and whether the conclusions drawn—particularly about essentialism and the accidental-intrinsic problem—actually follow from the premises given. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of further readings; I want to know what a careful defender of each position would accept, what they would correct, and whether any hidden assumption in the summary makes the two camps appear closer in their commitments, or farther apart in their resources, than they genuinely are.
