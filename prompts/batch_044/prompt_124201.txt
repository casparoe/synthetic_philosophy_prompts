been stuck in a debate with my reading group — mostly rationalist-adjacent folks — about why moral norms like slavery or dueling actually vanish, and it's been going in circles for like a year now. One side keeps citing Appiah's honor-code story: dueling collapsed because elite people stopped caring about being called a coward, not because someone won a syllogism. The other side is pushing what they call the Williams thesis — basically that moral change needs an internal reason, so pure interest shifts or status games can't really explain why people stopped seeing slavery as okay, there has to be some prior normative shift. Then there's the materialist in the group who says both are just dressing up economics, and that slavery ended when it stopped being profitable and dueling died with the professionallization of violence, full stop.

We all agree on the rough timeline, we all agree suffering is bad, but nobody's budged. So I'm trying to figure out what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal — we're using 'explanation' to mean causal mechanism for one person and normative justification for another? Is there a hidden empirical claim about whether honor can shift faster than economic incentives? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether people are ever genuinely moved by status dynamics they don't endorse? I also want to float a niche framing I've been playing with: 'honor liquidity,' the idea that once elite defection crosses a threshold the status value of a norm collapses faster than rational argument could ever push it. Does that actually resolve anything, or is it just a fancy name for Appiah's side? And honestly, what would count as progress here — a formal coordination model, a tighter historical comparison between Britain and the American South, or just admitting that one side is doing descriptive sociology and the other is doing normative philosophy and we should stop pretending it's one debate? We're definately not moving until I understand which of these it is.
