I have come to accept a modified cyclical account of institutional history that synthesises Polybius’s anacyclosis with Ibn Khaldun’s dynamics of asabiyya, but extends both by arguing that democratic regimes enter decline not primarily through external shock or moral corruption, but through the progressive erosion of shared risk-exposure—the very condition that initially made collective deliberation rational—leading to an internal cycle of oligarchic consolidation, populist rupture, and eventual dissolution. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position: which empirical patterns in modern democracies, which conceptual distinctions between cyclical and linear decay, and which formal or comparative-historical arguments should actually move me. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed cyclical theorist would themselves concede as damaging rather than dismissing, and include an honest assessment of whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a defect for a theory of this kind. Finally, please state what would change your mind: which specific finding or argument, if it proved otherwise, would lead you to abandon the cyclical framing in favour of a linear-progress or hybrid account, and identify where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
