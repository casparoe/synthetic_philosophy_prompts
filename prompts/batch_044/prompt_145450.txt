I am drafting an essay on Nietzsche's essay about history and life, and I have built an argument that seems to collapse his framework from within. The conclusion is clearly too strong, but each step feels defensible, and I cannot locate the error.

(1) For Nietzsche, history serves life in exactly three modes: monumental, which inspires present action by offering exemplary figures; antiquarian, which preserves cultural identity through continuity with the past; and critical, which liberates life by judging and destroying a past that has become oppressive.

(2) Any living culture eventually accumulates a past that burdens its present growth; therefore critical history is not a luxury but eventually necessary for any culture that wishes to remain vital.

(3) Critical history, however, requires adopting a standpoint external to the past being judged. Once that external standpoint is established, the past loses its authority not merely in part but in principle: to declare a tradition oppressive is to deny it the normative power that made it a tradition in the first place.

(4) If the past has lost its authority in principle, then monumental exemplars can no longer inspire—since they derive their force from belonging to an authoritative tradition—and antiquarian preservation becomes incoherent, because continuity with a delegitimized past offers no meaningful identity.

(5) Thus, whenever a culture enters the critical mode, it simultaneously destroys the conditions required for the monumental and antiquarian modes to function.

(6) But Nietzsche insists that life needs all three modes, deployed according to present need, and that critical history is especially necessary precisely when the past becomes crushing.

(7) So Nietzsche is committed to the claim that life periodically requires the very conditions that destroy history's ability to serve life.

(8) If history serves life only through these three modes, then at precisely those moments when life most needs history—when critical judgment is required—history ceases to serve it altogether.

(9) By extension, no culture can ever consistently use history for life; every culture must eventually reach a state in which its historical modes cancel one another out, leaving only a kind of mode-lock in which nothing is available.

(10) But this contradicts Nietzsche's explicit view that history can be indispensable for life when used correctly. I therefore assume one premise is flawed, yet I cannot tell whether the error is an equivocation between local judgment and global delegitimization in step (3), a modal slide in (5) that treats sequential availability as simultaneous impossibility, or an unjustified quantifier shift from "some parts" to "the whole past."

If I must preserve (1) and (6)—since they capture Nietzsche's core taxonomy and his claim that critical judgment is periodically unavoidable—which step is least costly to reject? Specifically, does the flaw lie in (3)'s inference from judging part of a tradition to undermining its authority in principle, or in (5)'s claim that the modes are structurally incompatible rather than differentially available to different groups or at different times? And if I reject (3), what do I owe in explaining how critical history can operate without eventually dissolving the tradition it judges?
