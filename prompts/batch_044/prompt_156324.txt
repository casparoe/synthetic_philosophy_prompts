I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about where nudging ends and manipulation begins, and my own verdicts refuse to line up with any simple rule. Here they are.

Automatic enrollment in a retirement plan with an obvious one-click opt-out strikes me as permissible: inertia does the work, but conscious choice remains fully available. By contrast, an overdraft-protection default that conceals fee structures in dense text feels impermissible, even though opt-out is technically present—so reversibility alone cannot be the standard. A cafeteria that rearranges its layout so healthy food sits at eye level, without announcing the redesign, also strikes me as acceptable, despite the absence of transparency; that suggests transparency is not necessary either.

My friend then proposed a municipality painting road markings that create an optical illusion of excessive speed, causing drivers to slow without conscious awareness. I find that more objectionable than the cafeteria case, though neither removes any option. At the same time, I am comfortable with mandatory graphic health warnings on tobacco products—explicit emotional steering, but overt and direct.

The pattern I glimpse is that an intervention is permissible if it either appeals to conscious deliberation or leaves the pathway for such deliberation intact and unmisled; it is impermissible if it either hides information needed for deliberation or feeds false data into non-deliberative processing. Yet this threatens to condemn visceral calorie-labeling designed to provoke disgust rather than inform reasoning, and I am genuinely unsure whether that should be condemned.
