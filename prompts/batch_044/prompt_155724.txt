(1) Dr. Elena Vasquez argued that the backward induction paradox dissolves if we replace the standard common-knowledge assumption with a model of epistemic humility: each player assigns a non-zero credence that their opponent is reason-responsive—capable of recognizing the mutual benefit of continuing—rather than purely strategic. She claimed that with this credence, the first player can rationally stay in, because a probabilistic path to mutual cooperation raises the expected value of continuing above the immediate payoff from taking.

(2) Dr. Marcus Chen replied that this merely smuggles cooperation in through probabilistic noise. Even with a small credence in reason-responsiveness, the conditional reasoning at each reached node remains intact: if you are at node k, you still know the opponent will take at node k+1 given rationality, so you should take at k. The reason-responsive disposition does not alter the prescription at any reached node; it only changes the prior probability of reaching that node.

(3) Vasquez countered that Chen assumes an infinite nesting of belief attribution. If knowledge of reason-responsiveness is only finitely nested—say, second-order—then at deeper nodes the backward chain breaks, because players lack the requisite mutual expectations to justify defection. She added that Chen treats backward induction as governing only reached nodes, but its force at the root depends on the entire tree being transparent.

(4) Chen responded that even with finite nesting, the rational prescription at any node you actually reach is still to take. The paradox concerns what you should do if you are there, not merely whether you arrive. He said Vasquez confuses the ex-ante decision at node 1 with the ex-post prescription at node k: backward induction governs both, and if the ex-post prescription is always take, then the ex-ante expectation must account for that risk, not override it with hope.
