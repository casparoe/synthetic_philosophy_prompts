I'm preparing a graduate seminar on moral responsibility that pairs Harry Frankfurt's original counterexamples with Anthony Burgess's novel A Clockwork Orange, and I want a philosophical reading that treats the narrative as more than illustration. After Alex is subjected to the Ludovico technique, he becomes physically incapable of violence: the very thought of aggression induces nausea. Read the novel's concluding chapters—including the redemptive final chapter absent from Kubrick's film—through the principle of alternative possibilities and Frankfurt's hierarchical model of desire. Does Burgess present Alex as retaining second-order volitions oriented toward the good even when his first-order violent desires are mechanically blocked? Or does the conditioning destroy the internal mesh between levels that Frankfurt takes to be sufficient for genuine personhood? I am particularly interested in whether the novel anticipates what philosophers have called the prior-sign problem: the state does not intervene in any particular choice but only implants a general aversion, so is there genuinely a counterfactual controller present in the Frankfurtian sense, or does Burgess's scenario collapse into ordinary determinism?

Take the reading further and ask where the work resists the philosophical framing. Burgess's theological commitment to free will as a divinely granted capacity that must include the real possibility of evil seems to conflict with Frankfurt's claim that responsibility does not require open alternatives. Does the novel's political satire of behavioral conditioning support that conflict, or does Burgess inadvertently dramatize the very case Frankfurt describes—an agent who acts in accordance with his higher-order preferences and is therefore responsible despite lacking genuine alternatives? Finally, I want an assessment of what the novel adds to the debate that pure argument cannot supply: whether its horror at mechanically produced virtue forces us to distinguish moral responsibility from authenticity, or between being the true source of one's actions and functioning merely as a well-calibrated mechanism. The analysis should remain faithful to both the philosophical distinctions and the narrative arc, avoiding any reduction of Burgess to a footnote in the free-will literature.
