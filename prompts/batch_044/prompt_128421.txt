I recently listened to a philosophy podcast that tackled the old question of whether pleasure is genuinely one thing, given that eating well, finishing a difficult problem, and hearing beautiful music all seem to feel completely different. The host presented the hedonist defense as follows: every pleasure contains an identical hedonic tone, a bare qualitative feel that is precisely the same regardless of context, and the differences we notice come only from the accompanying beliefs, bodily states, and intentional objects that act like packaging around this invariant core. He noted that when Sidgwick discussed a common quality shared by every pleasure, he was pointing to exactly this shared core rather than to some abstract relation. The host then appealed to neuroscience, claiming that since all pleasure activates the nucleus accumbens in the same pattern, brain imaging cannot distinguish culinary enjoyment from intellectual satisfaction—it can only register that pleasure is present. From this he concluded that the heterogeneity objection is essentially a category mistake: critics examine the packaging and overlook the identical item inside. He finished by comparing pleasure to temperature, arguing that just as heat can vary in degree without ceasing to be temperature, pleasures can vary in their accompaniments without ceasing to share one fundamental nature.

The argument sounds elegant, but I am unsure how much of it holds up. I do not know whether philosophers actually defend hedonism by claiming that pleasure is literally identical in every case, or whether the neuroscience is being asked to do work that belongs to conceptual analysis. I am also uncertain whether Sidgwick's notion of a common quality was meant to be this specific, or whether it allowed for genuine qualitative diversity. Could you assess how fair this summary is?
