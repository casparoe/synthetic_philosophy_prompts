Design a 90-minute structured deliberation for a philosophy seminar of 8 to 12 advanced undergraduates or early graduate students. The aim is to make participants experience, before any theoretical naming occurs, the collective-action failure that arises when hypocrisy is treated as a binary disqualifier from standing to blame. The rules, roles, and payoffs should be arranged so that the group runs into the problem itself—widespread minor imperfection disabling collective judgment—before the facilitator supplies the conceptual frame.

The idea to be embodied is this: standing to blame is not merely an individual credential but a distributed group resource. In any community where most members share some minor version of the fault being condemned, applying a strict purity condition does not just excuse particular hypocrites; it systematically collapses the group’s capacity to issue collective blame, even though private judgments might remain intact. Sustaining collective judgment therefore requires either relaxing the purity condition or accepting an arbitrary asymmetry in who may speak—both of which feel unsatisfactory from inside the dynamics.

Activity: The Reprimand Protocol.

Preparation: Create case cards describing a serious, fictional transgression within an academic or professional community—e.g., a colleague systematically taking credit for joint work. Create fault cards distributed secretly; approximately 75 percent indicate that the holder shares the fault in minor form (e.g., once failed to correct a misattribution, once used an idea without explicit acknowledgment), and 25 percent indicate purity.

Procedure, in three phases totaling about 60 minutes, followed by a 30-minute debrief:

Phase 1, silent positioning (10 minutes): The facilitator reads the case aloud. Each participant writes privately whether they believe the group should issue a formal statement of collective condemnation.

Phase 2, structured disclosure and discussion (25 minutes): Seated in a circle, each person may speak once for or against the statement, but must observe the disclosure rule: if speaking in favor, they declare only whether they are tainted or pure, without revealing details. Tainted supporters place a red token on a central board; pure supporters place a green token. Opponents place no token. The facilitator tracks the visible count in real time.

Phase 3, vote and payoff (15 minutes): Participants vote by secret ballot—sign or refuse. The group receives a tangible but minor collective reward, such as selecting the seminar’s next discussion topic plus a shared snack, if at least 75 percent sign. After votes are collected, all fault cards are revealed face-up. Then apply the purity filter: only green-token supporters count toward the threshold. In nearly every run, the revealed taint rate will be high enough that the filtered count falls far below 75 percent, so the reward is lost or severely reduced, even when the raw vote was strong.

Debrief requirements:

Map moments precisely onto parts of the idea: (1) the initial confidence during silent writing, when purity is assumed to reside elsewhere; (2) the first tainted declaration, which typically triggers withdrawal, defensiveness, or strategic silence; (3) the visible accumulation of red tokens, which makes further support feel reckless; (4) the post-revelation collapse, where the group sees that consistency would have required near-total silence.

Predict failures and specify how to handle them: Some participants will reject the binary taint/purity framing, arguing that degrees of fault matter or that acknowledgment restores standing. The facilitator should not defend the framing but should ask whether any non-arbitrary line could be drawn inside the activity’s dynamics, and must supply—explicitly, not through the rules—that real-world standing is diachronic and can be repaired, which the game intentionally freezes out. Others will attempt to separate private judgment from public disclosure by remaining silent in Phase 2 but signing in Phase 3; the facilitator should treat this not as cheating but as data, showing that people intuitively distinguish private blame from public standing, which is exactly the tension the concept highlights.

Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: The game collapses private and public blame into a single public token, and it treats taint as static rather than responsive to apology or character change. The facilitator must supply that in actual moral life private reproach may survive where public condemnation dies, and that the possibility of redemption or changed character is precisely what allows communities to maintain collective judgment without requiring moral purity. The activity also cannot reproduce long-term reputational dynamics; it offers only a single-round snapshot, so the facilitator should note that sustained social standing depends on temporal processes the exercise excludes.

Keep all framing conceptual rather than bibliographic: do not ask for citations, named theoretical frameworks, or reading lists, and do not invoke specific obscure doctrines. The prompt should depend only on the dynamics of the exercise and on broadly familiar moral intuitions about hypocrisy and blame.
