I've been fileing notes from a symposuim on rational choice and ran into this argument from a philosopher I talked to over coffee near the hotel. She laid it out like this, and now I'm stuck trying to write the piece. (1) If rational choice requires a reason for every selection, then Buridan's ass facing two literally identical hay bales can never choose—which is absurd, since both animals and recommendation algorithms break ties constantly. (2) So rational choice must allow picking without a reason. (3) But if picking without a reason is permitted, then any arbitrary tie-breaking rule is rational, including ones that systematically steer you toward worse outcomes just to avoid paralysis. (4) That collapses rationality into bare consistency, which can't distinguish a smart agent from a broken random-number generator. (5) Therefore there must always be a hidden reason—an unconscious bias, a microscopic sensory gap, or a lexical tie-breaker you didn't notice. (6) Apply this to human life: if you're genuinely torn between two career paths or relationships that feel incommensurable, you're not actually torn; there's always a buried preference waiting to be excavated. (7) But that proves too much, doesn't it? It wipes out the idea of true value incommensurability, which seems to be half the point of existantial choice. Where's the slip—between (2) and (3), or is (5) smuggling psychology into normative theory? And if I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) than (6), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying for that.
