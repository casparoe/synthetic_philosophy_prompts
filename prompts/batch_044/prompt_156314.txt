I spent thirty years in experimental physics, and since retiring I have been working through moral philosophy with the same habits—looking for unstated boundary conditions and asking whether a disagreement is about the model or about what counts as data. Peter Singer’s drowning-child case seems to me the clearest example of a stalled debate: the claim is that if saving a child at negligible cost is obligatory, then distance, nationality, and institutional mediation are morally inert, so affluent strangers bear stringent rescue duties; the standard rejoinders invoke demandingness, the killing/letting-die distinction, special relationships, and the limits of moral psychology. Yet for decades the same positions recur—utilitarians adjust the cost threshold, deontologists restrict the scope of positive duty, virtue theorists warn that universal rescue corrupts character—without anyone conceding ground. My hypothesis, drawn from thinking about physical properties, is that the stall is not about rescue but about whether moral obligations are “intensive” (holding with the same force regardless of system scale or proximity) or “extensive” (varying with the agent’s local capacity, relationships, and contextual load), and that the two sides are therefore answering different questions—one about normative structure, one about feasible agency—under the single heading “duty of rescue.” Is this a verbal disagreement about “obligation,” a substantive disagreement about which cases are genuinely analogous to the pond, or a hidden dispute about empirical moral psychology that neither side can resolve without abandoning philosophy? And what, if anything—an explicit formal distinction, an empirical result about motivation, or a reframed question—would actually count as progress rather than another iteration? Diagnose the structure of the impasse using what each side actually says, and tell me whether progress should be expected.
