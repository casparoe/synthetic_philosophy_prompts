I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been studying how hospitals and legal systems declare human death, and I keep seeing what looks like an architectural inconsistency in the protocol design.

In most jurisdictions, there are two distinct institutional pathways for certifying death. The first is neurological, commonly called “brain death,” established through clinical examination—often whole-brain or brainstem criteria—while the heart continues to beat via mechanical ventilation. The second is circulatory, or “cardiac death,” declared after observed cardiac arrest followed by a mandatory waiting period, typically in the range of two to five minutes, during which autoresuscitation must be excluded. The asymmetry that puzzles me concerns the heart itself: in donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) protocols, the heart may be restarted in situ after the declaration of death solely for organ preservation and perfusion, whereas in brain-death protocols the identical mechanical support that keeps the heart beating is interpreted not as sustaining life but as maintaining circulation within an organism that is already dead.

This raises a question about the underlying philosophical commitments. If the brain functions as the organism’s sole integrator, then brain death should be the definitive endpoint—so why does the circulatory pathway require a temporal “dead-state” observation window rather than an immediate assessment of integrative failure? Conversely, if death is fundamentally the irreversible cessation of circulatory function, why is brain death accepted as death even though circulation is artificially preserved? And what does it mean for personal identity or biological continuity that the same organ—the heart—can be treated as dead tissue eligible for retrieval in one pathway, yet as a living pump sustaining a dead body in the other?
