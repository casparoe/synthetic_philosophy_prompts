Two of my collegues got stuck arguing about whether the dirty-hands problem is even alive anymore, now that goverment runs on committees and bureaucrats instead of kings. They were talking about a real local council vote: a member cast the tie-breaking vote to approve a factory that saves 400 jobs but dumps toxins into a river serving a poor neighborhood.

Leo said thats classic dirty hands -- she did something wrong (harming residents) to achieve a greater good (jobs), and she should feel that guilt personally. Sam said thats outdated thinking. In a collective body nobody personally pulls the trigger; she just raised her hand among nine people. The wrong, if there is one, belongs to the system, not to her palms.

Leo came back that she was literally the deciding vote -- without her hand the harm doesnt happen, so causal and moral responsibility are still hers. Sam said if thats the standard then every bureaucrat who stamps a harmful permit has dirty hands, which dilutes the idea into uselessness. Dirty hands is meant to mark a special, direct moral stain, not just being a cog in a machine.

Leo replied that maybe the concept should expand on purpose: modern politics hides violence behind procedure, and recognizing "deliberative dirty hands" -- not just the dictator ordering torture -- is exactly what we need. Sam said then youve just renamed "working in a flawed system." Without a principled line between direct personal wrongdoing and structural participation, the concept loses all its bite.

They asked me to referee but I honestly dont know. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say whos right about what? Be committed -- take actual sides at each step, dont just list both views -- and say whether Leo's expansion saves the concept or kills it by overreach.
