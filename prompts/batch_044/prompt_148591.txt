I work as the acquisitions archivist for a county historical society, and every month I have to decide whether to take in a donated box of papers and, if we take it, how to cataloge it without pretending the collection is neutral. I've been trying to apply some of the theory about archives and power — the idea that silence isn't just missing paper but is actively produced by the same structures that create the record — but I can't run a philosophy seminar each time a donor shows up with a trunk. Could you turn that into an actual procedure I can run in about thirty minutes per collection?

Specifically, I want a step-by-step checklist: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure tells me to flag something in the catalog versus where it forces me into a value call I have to sign off on (like rejecting a donation whose gaps are actively harmful rather than accidental), and the exact points where it stops and says "this needs human judgment, not a rule." It needs to handle hard cases: apply it to a 1950s family photo album full of white middle-class domestic scenes where one corner shows a Black domestic worker with no name or caption, and to the complete administrative files of a local cannery that employed migrent labor but contains zero worker correspondence or injury reports. I also want you to build in a distinction I'm playing with — call it "counter-provenance" — tracking not just who created the record but whose absence shaped its form, and tell me exactly where that idea sharpens the checklist and where it makes the whole thing collapse under its own weight.

If this request rests on a confused premise — like if turning structural critique into a thirty-minute checklist kills the critique, or if "counter-provenance" is just jargon that doesn't do real work — say so plainly instead of politely complying. I actually want the pushback if the task as posed can't be done well.
