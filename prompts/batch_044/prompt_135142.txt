You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Form and Fidelity," held in a university auditorium with two audiences seated together: practicing literary translators who have spent years wrestling with rhyme and meter, and formal linguists and computer scientists who study constraint satisfaction but rarely read poetry. The theme is translation under extreme formal constraint, centered on the problem Douglas Hofstadter explored over many years in his 1997 book Le Ton beau de Marot: how to render Clément Marot's short Renaissance poem A une Damoyselle malade—referred to by Hofstadter as Ma mignonne—into English without destroying its fixed rondeau structure, its syllabic regularity, and its intimate, almost whispered emotional address to a sick young woman. Your speech should do real structural work. Use the specific tension in Marot's poem—between the rigid formal cage, which forces particular word choices and syntactic inversions, and the tender, grief-tinged content—to frame everything that will be discussed over the next two days. You must speak to both groups without condescension: the translators need to hear that you understand why rendering the poem's address as a generic term of endearment misses something irrecoverable, and the formalists need to see that constraint satisfaction is not merely an optimization problem when the objective function includes intimacy and mourning. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Instead, introduce one genuinely under-discussed evaluative criterion—call it tonal inertia—to explain why some translations that preserve every rhyme and every syllable still feel as though they have missed the poem's emotional center. Define tonal inertia precisely enough that a formalist could imagine modeling it, but illustrate it with a concrete comparison between two hypothetical English renderings of the poem's opening address, showing how one obeys all formal rules yet carries the wrong emotional weight. The speech should last about six minutes, be polished but conversational in tone, and should close by posing one open question that both audiences could answer in radically different terms: what does it mean for a translation to be correct when its form is preserved but its tone has drifted?
