For my graduate seminar in the epistemology of mathematics, I want an essay that engages Benacerraf's dilemma without merely rehearsing the standard taxonomy of responses. The problem, precisely stated, is this: if mathematical entities are abstract and causally inert with respect to us, then our robust apparent knowledge of them seems incompatible with any plausible causal or reliabilist account of knowledge. Rather than surveying Platonist modifications, nominalist reconstructions, or structuralist deflations, I ask you to develop and evaluate a less-discussed strategy—what we might call representational anchoring—which distinguishes ontological commitment to abstract objects from epistemic access secured through the modal stability of the representational practices that fix reference to them. Your task is to determine whether this distinction genuinely dissolves Benacerraf's challenge, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty to a prior problem about justifying those representational practices themselves. The essay must reach and defend a substantive conclusion: either that representational anchoring saves a moderate, non-mysterious Platonism, or that it collapses into a form of anti-realism that Benacerraf himself would reject. Write for an audience of graduate students who understand the basic literature but need to see the argument made fresh; do not appeal to bibliographic authority, do not provide reading lists, and do not quote texts from memory—construct the reasoning directly.
