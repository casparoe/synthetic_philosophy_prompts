Political philosophers often defend compatriot priority by analogy to parental partiality: just as parents may legitimately devote more resources to their own children's education without claiming those children are globally more deserving, citizens may prioritize compatriots' welfare without denying outsiders' equal moral worth. Critics object that the analogy imports false symmetries—family bonds are emotionally thick, small-scale, and largely involuntary, whereas citizenship is bureaucratic, territorially arbitrary, and extends to millions of strangers with whom one shares little. Audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the source domain (parent, child, household budget, outsider child, emotional bond, justification for unequal care) onto the target domain (state, citizen, public resources, non-citizen, national identity, justification for compatriot priority). Second, isolate which disanalogies actually threaten the normative conclusion: does the absence of emotional thickness, the scale, the possibility of emigration, or the arbitrariness of birth location break the parallel? Third, examine whether repairing the analogy—say, by comparing citizenship to a voluntary cooperative or to a contractual scheme of mutual advantage—can preserve special claims, or whether the conclusion that compatriots have special claims was carrying all the weight and the analogy merely illustrated it. Fourth, if the family analogy collapses, propose a more defensible analogy that could support moderate compatriot priority without treating global equality as irrelevant, and say what awkward commitment that replacement introduces.
