I have come to hold a particular reading of Tolstoy's theory of art—specifically, the view set out in his late essay—that can be sharpened into a stricter thesis than he himself explicitly defended. On this reading, art is defined not merely by the infectious transmission of feeling from artist to audience, but by the transmission of feeling that is immediately accessible without specialized education or cultural initiation; and this accessibility is not a contingent feature of good art but a normative condition of it, so that any work requiring esoteric training to be genuinely felt is not just poor art but a kind of counterfeit, analogous to counterfeit currency that circulates only among those who have agreed to treat it as valuable. I want to know what would genuinely count against this sharpened Tolstoyan position. Please consider which observations, arguments, or conceptual distinctions should move me: for example, whether there are cases where technical mastery and universal emotional infection are genuinely inseparable; whether art that was initially opaque to the uninitiated but later widely felt undermines the normative claim; whether Tolstoy's own admiration for folk art and his contempt for Wagner can be sustained consistently given his own aristocratic education and the elitist conditions of much folk performance; and whether the view risks collapsing into tautology, defining good art as accessible art and thereby insulating itself from counterexample. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view can qualify it—say, by allowing that some feelings are latent and require cultivation—without rendering it unfalsifiable, and whether such insulation is a strength or a weakness for a normative theory of art. Please rank the defeaters from the most decisive to the merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of this view would concede as damaging and which they would dismiss, and conclude with whether the position is genuinely exposed or well insulated. Respond entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—write as though in a single flowing letter or email, organizing the ranking by the natural order of your sentences rather than by visual structure.
