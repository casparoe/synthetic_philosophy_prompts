In a reading group I run on panpsychism, the argument over the combination problem has reached a peculiar kind of stasis. One faction, call them the integrationists, holds that macro-conscious subjects emerge when micro-experiential parts are arranged in the right informational or causal architecture—roughly on the model of integrated information theory, except applied downward to fundamental physical entities—so that the problem is really just a problem about describing that architecture precisely. The other faction, call them the boundary essentialists, insists that if every basic constituent genuinely has its own minimal phenomenal character, then either those parts retain their experiences and we are left with an uncombined mosaic of subjects, or they somehow surrender them, which seems ontologically arbitrary and violates a kind of mereological essentialism about experience: an experience belongs essentially to the subject that has it, so it cannot be transferred into a larger whole. Both sides have read the same literature on phenomenal unity, both accept that neuroscience offers no direct evidence either way, and both agree that rejecting macro-subjects is phenomenologically implausible; yet after eighteen months, neither side has conceded an inch. The disagreement does not seem to be about empirical predictions—no one proposes a different brain-scanning protocol—nor about logical consistency, since both positions are internally coherent. My own suspicion is that the sides are answering different questions under the single label combination problem: the integrationists are asking what causal conditions are sufficient for a unified subject, while the boundary essentialists are asking whether phenomenal properties can be proper parts of larger phenomenal properties at all, with the causal story being at best a correlation. But I am unsure whether this is a verbal dispute, a clash of bedrock metaphysical intuitions about parts and wholes, or a hidden formal question—perhaps about whether the relation of phenomenal composition is transitive or permits overlap—disguised as a debate about consciousness. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is: do the sides differ about hidden facts, about what counts as an adequate explanation, about which thought experiments should be treated as data, or about starting commitments that neither can justify? And given your diagnosis, specify what kind of progress would be appropriate: a formal result about mereological relations for phenomenal properties, a revised taxonomy that splits the problem into separate questions, an empirical criterion for counting subjects in synthetic neural-bridge cases, or an argument that the impasse is unavoidable and should be dissolved rather than solved. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in the structure of the disagreement itself.
