I've just started volunteering on the acquisitions panel for a tiny museum in Manchester that collects obselete communication tech — telegraphs, early radios, punch cards, that sort of thing. The problem is we're constantly arguing about whether a donated object actually belongs in the collection, because there's no essential definition of 'a communication device'. Is a smoke signal? Is a children's cipher wheel? We need something reusable, not just another arguement that goes in circles.

I'd like you to turn Wittgenstein's idea of family resemblance and language games into an actual decision procedure my panel can run in about fifteen minutes per object. Not a philosophical essay — a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, and explicit flags for where the procedure has to hand over to human judgement because it's a value call, not a fact. It needs to work for people who haven't read Wittgenstein, since half our panel are retired engineers.

The procedure should cover: how to identify the relevant language game (who was using the object, for what purpose), how to check family resemblances to clear cases in our collection, and where to stop and reject. Be honest about where it will misfire.

Then apply it to three donations we're actually debating:
1. A 1920s player-piano roll with punched patterns that encode music — but the holes were also used to control factory looms.
2. A set of naval semaphore flags from the 1950s, perfectly intact, but never actually used for ship-to-ship messages.
3. A 1980s toy decoder ring that only translates a simple substitution cipher for a breakfast cereal promotion.

One of these should come out as 'no, reject it' even though some of us think it's an obvious yes, and I want you to say why the procedure forces that answer and whether that's a feature or a bug.

Please structure your answer like this:
- The procedure (around 350 words): the steps, the judgement flags, and the stop points.
- The three worked cases (around 450 words): run each through the steps and show where it lands.
- What the procedure can't do (around 200 words): its blind spots, the kinds of objects it will misclassify, and where we'd need a different framework entirely.
- A one-paragraph bottom line (around 100 words) on whether this is actually usable for our monthly meetings or just a fancy way of disguising our arguements.

Don't ask for citations or a reading list — I can't have the engineers hunting down books. And please don't refer to super obscure theories; stick to the family resemblance stuff we can actually discuss over tea.
