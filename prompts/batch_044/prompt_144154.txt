Assume you are advising a small interdisciplinary fund that can support about five conceptual researchers for two years, working at the intersection of moral philosophy, decision theory, and animal ethics—no empirical entomology required. The framing problem is this: insects exist in vast numbers, yet the per-individual probability that any given insect possesses phenomenal consciousness or affective sentience is low, perhaps very low, while the biological evidence for nociception, integrative behavior, and central-pattern generation is genuinely ambiguous. Rather than a literature survey, I want a defended agenda: which open conceptual questions, if resolved, would most change whether and how we should take large-scale moral concern for insects seriously, and where should limited philosophical effort go?

Specifically, please prioritize and defend an ordering among the following lines of conceptual work—adding or substituting others if you think they are upstream.

First, the aggregation problem under moral uncertainty: does a low per-capita credence in sentience combine linearly with astronomical population sizes in an expected-value framework, or do non-linear considerations—such as threshold conditions for suffering, the moral irrelevance of expected value below certain cognitive-complexity bounds, or totalist versus averagist population-ethical objections—block or severely weaken the scale argument? How tractable is this for philosophers working with current conceptual tools?

Second, the phenomenal-consciousness versus affective-valence distinction: if insects lack the former but possess something like negatively valenced nociceptive states, does the scale argument apply with full force, partially, or not at all? Is resolving this dependency primarily a conceptual analysis of the moral significance of affect without phenomenology, or does it collapse into an empirical question?

Third, system-level replacement and counterfactual population dynamics: do interventions that reduce harm to one insect population (for example, altering agricultural practices that affect soil arthropods) generate substitution or rebound effects that increase the total number of individuals with some probability of sentience elsewhere? Is this fundamentally a problem in population ethics about identity and replacement, or is it conceptually inseparable from empirical ecology?

Fourth, phylogenetic boundary conditions for moral patienthood: should moral consideration extend continuously across invertebrate taxa, or are there principled conceptual breaks—perhaps tied to specific neural architectures or information-integration capacities—that determine where the scale argument even applies?

Please rank these (and any you substitute) in terms of what deserves the limited research effort, defending the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability for philosophers rather than biologists, how many downstream theoretical and practical questions depend on each answer, and whether any are upstream of the others. State what kind of argument or conceptual clarification over the next two years would cause you to reorder them. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the reasoning that fixes where the effort should go and why.
