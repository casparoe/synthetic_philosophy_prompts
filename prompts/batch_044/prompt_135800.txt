You are delivering the opening remarks—approximately ten minutes—at the inauguration of a new public institute devoted to intergenerational care and relational governance. The audience is divided evenly: half are scholars of Confucian ethics who will recognize every conceptual move, and half are policymakers, caregivers, and institutional donors who have no philosophy background. The speech must make the philosophical idea do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. Its core claim is that the institute’s mission—designing durable structures of care—only makes sense within Confucian role ethics: moral obligations arise not from universal rules or individual rights but from the specific relational roles (parent, elder, steward, sibling) that people actually occupy.

To avoid the usual platitudes, build the address around a precise, non-obvious distinction: separate role-fulfillment—meeting the obligations that belong to a given relational position—from role-constitution, the claim that properly occupying a role is partly constitutive of one’s moral identity, so that sustained failure is not merely a breach of duty but a form of self-dissolution. Use this distinction to constrain what can and cannot be said about empowering individuals: if empowerment is framed as freeing people from relational roles rather than equipping them within those roles, it dissolves the very framework the institute exists to preserve. The speech should therefore argue that the center can support autonomy only as autonomy-within-roles, never as autonomy-from-roles.

Write so that the specialists hear an accurate engagement—acknowledging, for example, that the five cardinal relationships remain the settled backbone of the tradition—without turning the talk into a lecture, and without using technical jargon that loses the non-philosophers. Crucially, separate what is broadly accepted in the field from what remains genuinely contested: for instance, distinguish the centrality of relationality and the five relationships (settled) from debates over whether role ethics permits internal moral criticism of assigned roles, whether it collapses into a form of ethical particularism, and whether it can accommodate modern egalitarian commitments (all contested). Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view. No citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or verbatim quotations are wanted; the argument should stand on its own reasoning.
