I have spent thirty years modeling boundary conditions in thermodynamic systems, and since retiring I have been trying to understand why philosophers treat privacy as something more mysterious than a well-insulated container. I constructed the following argument, but its conclusion strikes me as physically coherent yet ethically intolerable, and I cannot locate the error.

Consider an analysis of privacy I call predictive isolation: an agent A maintains privacy over mental state M just in case no external observer can predict M with accuracy exceeding baseline without A's cooperation, analogous to a black-box system whose internal dynamics are shielded from external measurement.

(1) If predictive isolation defines privacy, then privacy is a relational property between an agent and an observer's predictive capacity, not an intrinsic property of the agent.

(2) Any agent embedded in a shared physical environment continuously emits information—thermal, electromagnetic, gravitational—about its internal microstates.

(3) Given the deterministic or probabilistically deterministic evolution of physical law, an observer with arbitrarily precise instruments and unlimited computation could, in principle, derive any internal microstate of A from these emissions.

(4) Therefore predictive isolation is never absolute; it is only a contingent function of current technological asymmetry between A and potential observers.

(5) Our legal and moral frameworks, however, treat privacy as a categorical, non-gradient right: either a disclosure violates privacy or it does not, regardless of how easily an adversary could have inferred the information.

(6) If privacy is merely technological asymmetry, then advancing neuroscience and computation should gradually dissolve privacy rights rather than threaten them—rights should shrink, not be defended.

(7) But it seems absurd that the moral status of privacy should diminish as our instruments improve; if anything, stronger observation technology makes privacy protections more urgent.

(8) Therefore predictive isolation cannot be the correct analysis of privacy, and we must reject (1).

Yet (1) seemed attractive precisely because it explains why mental privacy feels like insulation: we experience our thoughts as hidden because, practically, no one can decode our neural emissions. I am reluctant to abandon this physicalist framing because it avoids positing some non-physical inner sanctuary.

Where does the argument fail? Is the error in (3)—a hidden assumption that mental content is reducible to microstate predictions? Or in (5)—a false dichotomy between categorical rights and gradient properties? Or in (6)—a misinference about how rights should respond to shrinking asymmetry? Or does the argument actually succeed, and I must accept that privacy is indeed a technologically contingent privilege? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (7), but I want to know what I must pay to do so. Please also indicate what would change your mind—whether a convincing demonstration that mental content is not derivable from physical emissions, or a framework showing how categorical rights can rest on gradient physical properties. Finally, please say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
