As a novelist drafting a scene in which two philosophers of language argue, late at night, about whether any boundary between convention and fact can survive Quine's holism, I need a rigorous specification—not a history lesson—of what a reconstructed analytic-synthetic distinction would have to deliver to count as adequate. I am not asking for a bibliography or a scorecard of every position in the literature; I need the criteria themselves, with the motivating cases behind each, and an honest judgment of which demands are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particular camps.

Please organize your response into the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths indicated.

The Setup (about 80 words): Briefly frame the problem: Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" undermines both the reductionist account of meaning and the idea of unrevisable analytic truths. State what is at stake for someone who still wants a philosophically serious distinction—perhaps graded, contextual, or functional—rather than Quine's flat holism.

The Desiderata (about 450 words): List the requirements a post-Quinean theory of the analytic-synthetic boundary must meet to be adequate. For each, give the concrete case or consideration that motivates it. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable preferences of particular camps. If any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given Quine's arguments or related no-go considerations, say so explicitly—this is the part I most want to see.

Contenders at a Glance (about 150 words): Briefly note how the main existing orientations—Quinean holism without distinction, Carnapian conventionalism reconstructed for naturalized settings, and a graded-centrality view—fare against your list. Keep this brief; the weight should be on the criteria, not the scorecard.
