In contemporary AI governance and product classification, a sharp institutional boundary separates “tool AI” from “agent AI” that tracks the architecture of human authorization more closely than it tracks the system’s actual capacities. A language model that, within a single conversational turn, invokes a function to deploy a persistent cloud worker, initiate a multi-day data pipeline, or dispatch a sequence of external messages is still categorized as a “tool,” because the user technically approved the action in one prompt—even though the effects unfold long after the turn ends and the system exercises no further oversight. Only when the system itself selects, sequences, and revises sub-goals across turns, pursuing objectives without a per-step human veto, does it cross into “agent” territory. Liability frameworks and safety protocols map cleanly onto this divide: deployers of “tools” retain full legal and moral responsibility for all downstream outcomes, while “agents” trigger demands for system-level accountability, mandatory kill-switches, and distributed oversight.

What philosophical conception of agency and moral responsibility does this classification practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it hold that agency is not constituted by internal planning depth, environmental coupling, or the capacity for self-directed reasoning—features that may be present in both cases—but is instead defined solely by the absence of intermediate authorization checkpoints? Is the underlying view a behaviorist criterion relocated to system design, an authorization-based relational account of agency, or a control-based theory of responsibility that has been shifted from the actor’s mental states to the deployer’s veto rights? Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well: could the same liability and safety rules be sustained under a graded, capacity-based conception of agency on which both the single-turn deployer and the multi-turn planner possess agency in different degrees? Finally, if that rival view replaced the institutional binary, what concrete changes would be required in liability assignment, safety-testing protocols, and the design of human-in-the-loop interfaces?
