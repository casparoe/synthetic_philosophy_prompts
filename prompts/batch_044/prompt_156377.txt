Please review the following notes for a short essay on pain asymbolia and whether sensory pain is essentially bad, and offer your comments.

Notes. Philosophers often treat pain as intrinsically pro tanto bad: to feel pain is, by that fact alone, to be in a state that is bad for one. Pain asymbolia challenges this simplicity. In this condition—observed in certain neuropathological and dissociative presentations—patients report that a stimulus hurts, can localize it, and describe its quality, yet they display no avoidance behavior, no emotional distress, and no desire for relief. The sensory-discriminative dimension appears intact while the affective-motivational dimension is disrupted.

From this I want to argue that pain's badness resides not in its sensory quality but in its aversive force. To sharpen the point, I propose a conceptual mirror-image: call it "cryptic aversion." Here a subject vigorously avoids a stimulus, reports intense suffering, and seeks relief, yet consistently denies experiencing any sensory quality—no burning, stabbing, aching, or throbbing. If this is conceptually coherent, then avoidance and suffering can occur without sensory pain, just as sensory pain can occur without avoidance or suffering in asymbolia. The two dissociations together suggest that sensory quality, negative affect, and avoidance behavior are separately dissociable, and that only configurations including negative affect and avoidance carry the normative weight we call "badness."
