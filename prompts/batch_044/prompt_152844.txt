There's this philosopher i argue with who has pretty clear takes on the special composition question — like, when do scattered parts actually form one real object? She said yes for a living human, an assembled clock, a beehive, and a coral colony where all the polyps share nutrients. She said no for two people shaking hands, a sand pile, a bike that's been stripped and spread accross three garages, and a corporation (she calls that pure legal fiction).

Now I'm wondering about three weirder cases. First, a swarm of autonomous drones spread over miles running a shared flocking algorithm — no central controller, but they constantly adjust each others flight paths in real time. Second, a blockchain ledger maintained by nodes on different continents. Third, a human brain where some neurons are synthetic chips that communicate wirelessly inside the skull.

What'd she say about each? I'm actually less interested in bare yes/no answers than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit thier past verdicts just as well, and which of these three cases best tells those rules apart. What's the closest analouge from her earlier list for the drone swarm? And what's one more hypothetical case that'd really force her to choose between the competing rules?
