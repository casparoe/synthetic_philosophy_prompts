My study partner is trying to defend Braudel's longue duree but ended up with this argument that sounds like it wipes out all of political history. I think there's a slide somewhere but I can't pin it.

(1) Braudel treats geography and climate as deep structures that shape societies over centuries, while events and individual choices are just foam on the surface.

(2) If something is genuinely structural in that sense, then it constrains every outcome inside its time span; otherwise it wouldn't be doing structural work.

(3) The Mediteranean climate and geography stay roughly constant from 1500 to 1800.

(4) So every event in that window - the Crusades, the Black Death, some merchant deciding to sail in 1423 - is constrained by that geographic frame.

(5) But "constrained by" here has to mean either the structure determines the event or the event is explanatorily irrelevant to it, since if you could fully explain the event without mentioning climate, climate wasn't really constraining it.

(6) Apply that consistently: either Robespierre's turn to terror is determined by Mediteranean wheat cycles, or it lies outside Braudel's framework entirely.

(7) The first option is absurd, so every specific political choice lies outside the framework.

(8) That collapses Braudel's three-tier model into just geography on one side and unexplained noise on the other.

(9) But Braudel clearly writes political and diplomatic history as if events have their own autonomous explanations.

(10) So there's a contradiction: structures constrain events, yet events explain themselves.

Where's the exit? Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to keep (1) and (9) together if that's possible, but I'm not sure it is.
