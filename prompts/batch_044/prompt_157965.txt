Write a short story that uses Molyneux's problem as its conceptual spine, but displaced into a near-future setting where sensory substitution takes an unconventional form. Your protagonist should be a sculptor who has been congenitally blind and who navigates the world primarily through refined haptic and proprioceptive mapping. At age thirty-four, she receives an experimental cortical implant that does not restore vision in any conventional sense; instead, it translates three-dimensional spatial relations into a structured auditory field—edges become sustained tones, curvature becomes modulation, proximity becomes amplitude. The story's pivotal scene should occur when she is presented, simultaneously, with a physical dodecahedron she has handled for years and with its acoustic projection generated by the implant. She must decide whether the harmonic object in her auditory field and the polyhedral object in her hands are one and the same entity, despite having no prior cross-modal experience that could ground the identification. The narrative should treat this recognition problem not as a medical test to be passed or failed, but as an existential question about whether spatial knowledge is genuinely modality-independent or whether each sensory system constructs its own irreducible ontology. Do not resolve the ambiguity with a definitive philosophical answer; allow it to reshape her understanding of the sculptures she has made and the body she has lived in. Avoid bibliographic references, citations, or appeals to specific philosophical texts.
