Compare Samuel Scheffler’s “afterlife conjecture”—the claim that much of what gives present human lives meaning depends on the expectation that humanity will continue after one’s death, so that extinction undermines the point of present projects, relationships, and cultural efforts—with what I will call the “bounded-arc thesis.” The bounded-arc thesis holds that present lives derive a distinctive form of axiological coherence not from open-ended continuation but from belonging to a complete, narratively closed human history; on this view, extinction is bad partly because it prevents present projects from being situated within a finished whole, leaving them structurally incomplete in the way a novel without its final chapters is incomplete, rather than because it eliminates future people who might benefit from or complete those projects. In which respects do these two views share the same underlying logic about how present value depends on future states, and in which respects do they diverge—particularly regarding whether the harm of extinction is a loss to present agents of a continuing audience, or a loss of narrative closure that renders present achievements fragmentary? Which view, if either, better captures the intuition that human extinction is a unique tragedy that exceeds the aggregate of individual deaths it involves? In answering, please separate what is philosophically settled about future-oriented values and the structure of Scheffler’s argument from what remains genuinely contested—such as whether meaning can depend on outcomes that occur after one’s death, or whether narrative closure is a real axiological category—and do not present any disputed interpretation of Scheffler’s position or of narrative theories of value as if it were the consensus view.
