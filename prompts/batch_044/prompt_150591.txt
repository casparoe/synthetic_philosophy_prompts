Whilst constructing the moral architecture for a novel in which a surgeon declines to participate in a triage protocol requiring the death of one healthy donor to save five critically ill patients, I received the following gloss of deontology's central paradox from a philosopher acquaintance at a dinner party. She maintained that deontology treats all moral norms as absolute side-constraints grounded in the Formula of Humanity: one must never treat a rational being merely as a means, and perfect duties—above all the prohibition on murder—are entirely insensitive to consequences, however catastrophic. The paradox, she said, is that this appears to oblige the moral agent to remain passive whilst millions perish, so long as she does not herself initiate the harm. She further claimed that Kant resolved this tension by regarding the preservation of life as an imperfect duty that may yield in extremis, but that contemporary deontologists have demonstrated that this resolution fails, since agent-relative permissions necessarily allow an agent to favour her own projects over saving strangers. Therefore, she concluded, deontology is doubly flawed: it is too rigid in forbidding minimal direct harm regardless of scale, yet too permissive in licensing catastrophic omission, and the only consistent escape is to abandon agent-relativity and concede that all moral reasons are ultimately agent-neutral. She also proposed a distinction I had not encountered in any standard text, between 'directly structured harm', which violates a perfect duty, and 'environmentally mediated harm', which does not; on her account, this explains why deontologists can condemn individual killing while remaining silent on systemic suffering produced by collective inaction. I suspect that parts of this account are caricature, but I cannot identify precisely where the misrepresentations lie. Is it a fair summary? Please separate what is philosophically accurate, what amounts to a common popular simplification, and what constitutes either a misreading of Kant or an overstatement of modern deontological commitments—particularly regarding imperfect duties, agent-relative permissions, and the distinction between direct and mediated harm. I should also like to know whether her conclusion—that deontology must collapse into agent-neutral consequentialism—follows from the premises she has got right, or whether defenders such as Kamm or Foot would reject both the premises and the inference.
