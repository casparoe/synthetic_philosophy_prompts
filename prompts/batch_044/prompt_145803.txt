Here are draft notes for an essay on how intuition pumps persuade, focusing on Thomson's violinist case. The standard account holds that the pump works through analogical mapping: unwanted pregnancy is like being involuntarily hooked to a violinist, so abortion is permissible like unplugging. I want to challenge this. I propose that the pump's real mechanism is what I call affective colonization: the scenario does not merely share logical structure with pregnancy but induces a specific phenomenological state—bodily sovereignty panic, or the felt loss of agency over one's physical boundaries. This affective framing does not map onto the target; it infiltrates it. Once the panic is activated, it is projected onto pregnancy and treated as if it were evidential rather than imported. That, I argue, explains why listing structural disanalogies rarely dislodges the intuition: critics address analogical logic while the pump operates at an affective-phenomenological level. I am particularly interested in whether this distinction—between structural analogy and affective colonization—adds anything beyond familiar warnings about emotional manipulation in moral reasoning, and whether it applies to other pumps, such as those involving killing versus letting die.

Please comment on three things. First, does the distinction between analogical mapping and affective colonization hold up, or am I merely renaming a standard point about emotional bias? Second, have I mischaracterized how philosophers typically deploy the violinist case—do they genuinely rely on structural analogy, or do they already implicitly invoke something like bodily autonomy? Third, does my framing illegitimately dismiss the possibility that the affective response tracks a genuine moral feature rather than distorting judgment? I would also welcome suggestions for sharpening the concept of bodily sovereignty panic so that it does not collapse into a generic claim about intuition being unreliable.
