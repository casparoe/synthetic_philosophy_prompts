Analyze the corpus callosotomy studies conducted by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga from the 1960s onward—specifically the clinical observation that, after severing the corpus callosum, the left and right hemispheres can process information independently, with the left hemisphere generating confabulatory narratives to explain actions initiated by the right—through the lens of Douglas Hofstadter's theory of the self as a strange loop and his thesis that an 'I' need not be confined to a single brain but can persist, at least in part, within others. First, map the empirical phenomena onto Hofstadter's framework: does each hemisphere contain a partial strange loop; did the pre-operative patient host one loop that subsequently bifurcated, or did two loops always coexist? Second, extend the analysis to Hofstadter's further claim that the pattern defining one person's self can survive, distributed, inside another person's brain. Ask whether the split-brain case offers an analog—two loops within one skull—or a counterexample, since the hemispheres share a body and history in ways that cross-brain persistence does not. Identify precisely which predictions of the model are borne out by the clinical data, which require unsupported analogical leaps, and where the theory remains silent rather than being forced to speak. Conclude by assessing whether this episode strengthens, weakens, or is neutral to the broader claim that personal identity is substrate-independent and can be genuinely distributed. Be disciplined about the mapping—who or what counts as the loop-bearing subject, what integration conditions must be met, and whether functional separation within one cranium is comparable to separation across crania—without inventing empirical details not part of the standard account.
