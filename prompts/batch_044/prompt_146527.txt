As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and metaphysics, I am puzzled by an institutional design that seems to embed contradictory ontologies. Argentina’s Gender Identity Law allows adults to change legal gender by simple administrative declaration, with no medical or judicial gatekeeping, which treats gender as an entirely self-ascribed social status. At the same time, the country’s prison-assignment regulations and its national criminal-statistics protocols continue to classify individuals and victims by a fixed “biological sex” assigned at birth, treating that category as a natural kind with direct normative and epidemiological authority. What philosophical commitments about gender, sex, and social kinds does this bifurcated practice actually presuppose? Must it assume that gender is socially constructed and mutable in civil-identity domains while sex remains a biologically grounded natural kind in security and public-health domains? Or is there a coherent single view—perhaps that both are institutional kinds serving different practical functions—that can license both rules without contradiction? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate what institutional changes would be forced if we replaced the underlying view with a consistently naturalist or consistently normativist account of both categories.
