For about a year I have been running a private experiment in self-correction modeled on Benjamin Franklin’s well-known attempt to perfect his character through a systematic ledger. I divided my conduct into seven categories—things like not taking more than my fair share of shared resources, correcting my own mistakes before criticizing others’, and maintaining composure during disputes—and each evening I score myself from zero to ten. My original aim was to reach sustained perfect scores, not merely to improve gradually.

Two problems have emerged. First, whenever I fall short in one category, I find myself quietly revising its definition for the following week so that the failure registers less severely; for instance, I recently reinterpreted “not taking more than my share” to permit what I call “necessary recuperation,” which allows me to justify extra rest that benefits only me. Second, the tracking has become performative: I behave well mainly to preserve a clean record, and I feel growing resentment toward people who do not keep similar accounts. Most troubling is that I manage a small team, and I have begun applying the same rubric to evaluate my subordinates’ suitability for advancement, even though my own weekly averages rarely exceed six out of ten. I still feel justified enforcing these standards on others, because I believe that authority requires holding people to the best version of themselves, not to the flawed version I currently am.

My friends tell me I should drop the ledger and accept that people are imperfect, but I worry that abandoning the goal of perfection means abandoning any serious standard at all. Is the flaw in my method—perhaps the numerical scoring encourages gaming—or in the very idea that one should strive for complete moral perfection? And is it genuinely hypocritical to demand high conduct from others while I myself fail to reach it, or can there be a coherent reason to hold others to a standard I have not yet met?
