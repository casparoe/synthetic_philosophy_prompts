I'm writing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of action seminar, and I need an essay question on Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities that students cannot answer by merely reciting the classic Black-and-Jones narrative. To force genuine conceptual work, I want the prompt built around a modified case—call it "The Dormant Implant"—that introduces a distinction students must navigate independently. In this scenario, Dr. Aris has a neural implant capable of suppressing any voluntary movement that conflicts with her instruction to donate to charity; the implant activates only if her brain enters a rare, introspectively undetectable neural state P, which occurs randomly with 0.01% probability and is causally independent of her intentions. On Tuesday Aris decides to donate and completes the transfer; her brain never enters P, so the implant remains dormant. Unbeknownst to her, had she formed the intention not to donate, the random fluctuation would have triggered P, and the implant would have overridden her will. The exam should ask students to evaluate two responses: one arguing that because Aris lacked any phenomenally available choice-point, the case undermines PAP not just globally but locally; the other responding that the structural existence of the blocked pathway preserves a minimal "flicker" of freedom, since Aris's decision-process could have unfolded differently up to the point of exogenous intervention.

Please draft the examination question in formal language suitable for a 90-minute essay of approximately 1,200 words. The question must require students to: (i) distinguish the global and local formulations of PAP and identify precisely which formulation The Dormant Implant targets; (ii) analyze whether the flicker objection applies here, with particular attention to the fact that the blocker is triggered by an exogenous neural fluctuation rather than by Aris's own mental state; (iii) evaluate whether a distinction between structural availability (the pathway exists in the causal structure) and phenomenal availability (the agent can represent it in practical reasoning) saves PAP, or whether it transforms PAP into an unintended epistemic condition; and (iv) reach and defend a conclusion about Aris's moral responsibility and state whether that verdict requires revising Frankfurt's claim that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. Alongside the question, provide a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks divided across five specific moves—assign point values to each, include a one-line note on the characteristic error students make for each move, and briefly explain what distinguishes an A-range answer (22–25) from a B-range answer (18–21). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should be evaluable entirely from conceptual reasoning about the case and the principle.
