I am writing a paper for a philosophy of social science seminar on whether values can be genuine causes, and the question has become personally pressing because my own family comes from a religious community that emphasizes ascetic self-discipline; Weber’s thesis therefore feels less like an archival curiosity and more like a live description of how culture shapes economic life. In conversation with a friend who studies sociology, I built the following argument about Weber’s Protestant Ethic, but I find the conclusion intolerable—if it is sound, then culture can never explain anything in its own right, and Weber’s famous thesis collapses into redundancy. I want to know exactly where the slip occurs and what repairing it costs.

(1) For a cultural value to causally explain a macrohistorical outcome, it must operate through individual action, and that operation must be isolable: if the value had not been present, the outcome would not have occurred, holding other conditions fixed. (2) Weber describes the link between Calvinist asceticism and the spirit of capitalism as an elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft), not as deterministic causation; but if elective affinity is not reducible to some causal mechanism, it does no explanatory work, so we must read it as a claim that internalized religious norms causally produce economic behavior. (3) Once we treat the affinity as causal, the explanation runs: Calvinist values generate value-rational action oriented toward methodical labor and capital accumulation, which in turn produces modern capitalist development. (4) But if the causal power resides in the psychological profile—methodical self-discipline, deferred gratification, rational calculation of means—then that same profile can arise from secular education, economic necessity, or institutional incentives without any religious content. (5) Therefore, whenever capitalism develops, the real explanatory work is done by a universal psychological mechanism, and the specifically Calvinist cultural content is causally redundant; culture is merely a contingent vehicle. (6) By the same reasoning, any cultural explanation can be decomposed into generic psychological or material mechanisms, with the cultural form playing no independent causal role. (7) So Weber’s thesis, if taken seriously as an explanation, proves that culture is never a cause in its own right—only an epiphenomenal wrapper around universal causal processes.

I feel that something shifts illegitimately between (4) and (5), or perhaps between (2) and (3), but I cannot name the error with confidence. If I must choose, I would rather preserve Weber’s claim that the religious content matters—that the specifically Calvinist form of asceticism does work that secular discipline cannot—than preserve the demand that cultural explanations be reducible to isolable counterfactual mechanisms. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost for the possibility of explaining with values, and does it require me to abandon the idea that elective affinity can be explanatory without being deterministically causal?
