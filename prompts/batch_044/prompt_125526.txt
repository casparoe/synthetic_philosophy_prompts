I'm compiling prompts for a philosophical-reasoning evaluation dataset, writing from the standpoint of a longtime rationalist-forum reader who expects arguments to be evaluated by their structure rather than by recognition of standard summaries. For the domain of health and disease—specifically the naturalism versus normativism debate—I want an exam prompt of the type that delivers a question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The setting is an upper-level philosophy of medicine seminar final: ninety minutes, with answers expected around seven hundred words. The prompt must be unanswerable by reciting Boorse's biostatistical account and Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis; instead, it should force students to work through a crux. To that end, inject the following niche but conceptually tractable framing: a hypothetical "predictive homeostasis" view, drawn loosely from active-inference ideas familiar in rationalist-adjacent discussions of cognition, which defines health neither as statistical normality nor as absence of dysfunction, but as the condition in which an organism's generative model minimizes prediction error about its own physiological dynamics, and which reconceives dysfunction as a persistent mismatch that the system cannot resolve through allostatic adjustment.

Write the exam question asking students to determine whether this predictive-homeostasis framework genuinely escapes the naturalism/normativism dichotomy, or whether it smuggles normative commitments under the guise of information-theoretic neutrality. Require them to apply the framework to two hard cases—an asymptomatic pathogen carrier whose biomarkers remain within population norms, and a chronic-pain patient with no detectable tissue damage—showing exactly where the view yields a verdict different from both Boorse and Wakefield, and assessing whether that verdict is defensible. Then provide a model answer of about seven hundred words, and a marking scheme totaling twenty points that separates an A answer from a B by naming the specific conceptual moves required: for instance, recognizing that prediction-error minimization is not value-neutral because the generative model is already shaped by evolutionary and developmental norms; showing that statistical normality and predictive stability can diverge in the asymptomatic-carrier case; and explaining why the chronic-pain case threatens to collapse the distinction between dysfunction and maladaptive prediction. The scheme should also flag typical mistakes that cost marks—such as treating the framework as purely naturalist merely because it uses mathematics, or assuming that any information-theoretic framing automatically eliminates normativity.

Keep the prompt formal and polished. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from real texts; paraphrase published positions without quotation marks. Do not lean on obscure attributions, dates, or titles that a model without web access could not be expected to recall precisely.
