Next week I present at my school's philosophy symposium a reconstructed defense of Berkeley's master argument. Rather than resting on the claim that it is psychologically impossible to imagine an unconceived tree, I defend a transparency condition on perceptual intentionality: any representational state with the content "object O exists unperceived" necessarily includes a self-referential index that makes a perceiving subject part of its own conditions of representation, so the content is structurally self-undermining rather than merely hard to visualize. My audience is my teacher, who specializes in early modern philosophy and knows the standard critiques by Winkler and Philipse intimately, plus four classmates who have read the Three Dialogues and tend to think Berkeley simply confuses conception with imagination; one classmate is also a committed physical realist who will press whether my move collapses into a linguistic trick that applies to all abstract thought. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each audience member would phrase them, with what I should concede up front, what I should hold, where each reply is weakest, and the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—especially any reflexive version showing that my transparency condition applies to my own claim about representation and undermines it from within.
