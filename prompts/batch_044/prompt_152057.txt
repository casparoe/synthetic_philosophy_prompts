There's this thing in the modern Stoic revival that drives me up the wall. The old-school Epictetus crowd insists on the hard dichotomy: judgments, impulses, and aversions are in your power; everything else—money, health, whether your kid gets into college—isn't, and if you're truly wise you don't prefer one outcome over another. Then you've got the modern revivalists, Holiday, Pigliucci, the whole self-help wing, who treat the dichotomy as a stress-management heuristic: pursue goals aggressively but don't be crushed if they fail, maybe even use cognitive reappraisal to stay calm.

The argument's been stuck for years. Hardliners call the revivalists CBT with a greek costume, revivalists say they're preserving internal freedom. Nobody budges. I think part of the stall is this framing loophole I keep seeing: modern Stoics claim that while outcomes aren't in your control, the meaning-frame you apply to them is, so you can control your reaction to loosing your job or getting sick. But that seems like smuggling outcomes back inside the boundary through the back door—you're still caring about the outcome, just routing it through cognition.

So diagnose the stall for me. Is it just verbal—control meaning causal power for one group and emotional regulation for the other? Are they answering different questions, like ethics versus therapy? Or is there a real disagreement about whether framing actually severs the emotional link to outcomes, maybe with hidden empirical stuff about cognitive reappraisal? And what would count as progress here—a sharper conceptual distinction, some psych study on framing, or just admitting these are two movements that share a name but not a question?
