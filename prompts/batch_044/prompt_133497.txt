I am writing a novel about collective decision-making under extreme duress, and I have become preoccupied with a philosophical problem I want embedded in the narrative's moral architecture: not the familiar Sartre of the café waiter, but the more obscure machinery of the fused group, seriality, and the pledge developed in the Critique of Dialectical Reason. I want to apply this framework to a real episode—the decisions of the Hungarian Workers' Councils in late October and early November 1956 to maintain the general strike even after the government announced neutrality and the first Soviet withdrawal seemed to open space for negotiation. Please analyze this episode as a test of Sartre's collective freedom. Treat the Council members as existing in severe facticity—tanks returning, international abandonment, the dead of the first days—yet still, by Sartre's lights, radically free. Does their refusal to dissolve the strike represent authentic collective praxis, or is it bad faith disguised as solidarity, perhaps an evasion of the anxiety of choosing a specific, limited future? More precisely, does Sartre's distinction between the fused group and seriality explain how a collective can be free without becoming a super-individual consciousness, or does the episode show that collective freedom collapses back into serial dispersal the moment external coercion returns? I want to know not only what the theory predicts about their choice, but what the Council's eventual suppression by the second Soviet invasion reveals about the theory itself—whether radical freedom survives when facticity becomes nearly total, or whether this case exposes an unresolved gap in Sartre between individual agency and group agency that his account of bad faith never fully addresses.
