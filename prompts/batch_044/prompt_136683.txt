I'm putting together a kind of internal memo for a philosophy-of-mind working group — we're trying to decide where to put our limited energy over the next few years, and I'm stuck on the distribution of consciousness across animals. Everyone knows the octopus-and-bee debates, but I want to go deeper than the usual "do they feel pain?" headlines. Specifically, I'm torn between three open tracks. First, there's the problem of graded consciousness: people talk about "degrees" but never fix whether that means intensity of experience, richness of content, or something like global availability — and without that distinction, comparative claims across species seem kind of empty. Second, there's a weirder conceptual issue about the unit of analysis: should we assign consciousness to individual organisms, or could distributed decision-making in social insects or slime molds support a kind of "collective phenomenal state" that isn't reducible to each part? Third, there's the cross-modal recognition problem in invertebrates — like whether an octopus's distributed arm-control counts as a form of embodied awareness that's genuinely conscious but structurally different from vertebrate integretion.

If you had to advise us on which of these lines actually deserves priority — say, one workshop and one postdoc — how would you rank them? I'm not asking for a survey; I want a defended ordering based on what's upstream, what's tractable for philosophers without lab access, and what resolving it would actually change for scientists or ethicists. For each, tell me what kind of evidence over the next couple years would bump it up or down, and flag which ones you think are overrated dead ends that just recycle old debates. No bibliographies please — I know where the literature is — I just need the reasoning on where the conceptual bottlenecks actually are.
