I've noticed a recurring configuration in reports of sensory restoration—particularly in people who acquire sight after long congenital blindness and eventually learn to match shapes they knew by touch with their visual appearances. The phenomenon is not the classic Molyneux failure of initial recognition; rather, it is what remains after recognition succeeds. Multiple accounts describe subjects who, once they can reliably pair a tactile cube with its visual appearance, still do not experience the two modalities as presenting one object in two guises. Instead, they appear to maintain parallel object-files—one tactile-geometric, one visual—linked by an explicit, almost theoretical translation rather than by fused experience. For instance, a subject might report that "that visual pattern corresponds to the cube I feel," rather than "that is the cube I feel, now seen." A similar separation appears with faces: after learning to match haptic facial structure to visual form, the emotional expression read through sight does not automatically update the affective profile built through touch; it operates like a second registry about the same individual. I am asking whether this precise pattern—successful cross-modal identification combined with persistent modal segregation and mediated integration—carries an established name in philosophy of perception or cognitive science. Is it subsumed under Molyneux's problem, under failures of amodal representation, under some form of cross-modal binding deficit, or should it be treated as its own thing? If no standard term captures it, please propose one and carefully delineate its boundaries: separate it from ordinary learning curves, from visual agnosia, from the original Molyneux ignorance, and from the thesis that all cross-modal unity is necessarily inferential. In your response, please state how confident you are in the identification of the nearest concepts and in the distinctions you draw, and explicitly identify the point—whether concerning the phenomenological description, the relation to amodal theories, or the boundary conditions—about which you are least certain.
