I'm a novelist drafting scenes in which characters face choices between goods that resist common measurement—artistic creation, filial obligation, ecological preservation, human survival. I want to know whether my intuitive verdicts point to a coherent principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

Here are my judgments. A surgeon saves a pianist's life by amputating her hand; the choice is right, yet the destroyed music is not outweighed—it remains a genuine, un-canceled loss. A father abandons his unfinished masterpiece to nurse his dying child; right, yet the art is sacrificed, not traded away at a discount. A state razes a unique, un-replicable oral archive to build a hospital saving hundreds; wrong—not because the archive matters more, but because treating its value as comparable to lives saved is itself a moral error, even when lives are saved. Yet when the state razes an identical digital backup of that archive for the same hospital, there is no wrong and no tragic residue. A woman declines an artistic fellowship to stay with her mother, though she could have arranged care and done both; wrong, and not tragic—merely selfish. A commander who must choose by lottery which of two equally deserving soldiers to rescue: right, with no remainder, only misfortune.

What precise principle distinguishes choices that leave a sacrificial remainder from those that do not, and explains why treating incommensurable values as weights on one scale is sometimes correct reasoning (the surgeon) and sometimes the very source of wrongness (the archive)? Give a committed answer: either state the principle that saves all five verdicts, identify the clause that resolves the tension between the surgeon and archive cases, and predict its verdict on a case I have not listed—say, a society that permits a dying language with twelve speakers to vanish in order to fund universal literacy programs; or admit honestly which of my verdicts the best available principle forces me to drop, with reasons.
