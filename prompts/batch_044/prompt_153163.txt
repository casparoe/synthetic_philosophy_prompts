I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks this Saturday at the reopening of the Marrow Gallery, where nearly half the permanent collection consists of landscapes and portraits by Elias Vore — a painter long regarded in our town as brilliant but difficult, and now revealed to have systematically exploited apprentices and concealed serious harm across decades. The board voted not to remove the works but to reinstall them with revised contextual labels, and the room will hold survivors' family members, collectors who loved these images before knowing the full story, art students, local officials, and neighbors who grew up with Vore's paintings on their walls. I am not an art historian or philosopher — I am a retired electrician who served on the board because my late wife loved this place — and I need these remarks to do more than apologize or defend.

I have been turning over an idea I do not have a proper name for except, in my own mind, the problem of the second gaze: the moment when learning what the maker did does not merely change our opinion of the work but seems to restructure the very act of looking, so that the canvas stops being a wall we face and becomes a window through which we see his cruelty. I want the speech to honor the real beauty people have genuinely felt here without asking anyone to pretend that beauty is innocent, and I need it to avoid both the false comfort of pure separation — the claim that the art is untouched — and the opposite cruelty of insisting the beauty was always a lie. The idea of the second gaze should shape what I can honestly say and what I must leave unsaid, allowing the tension between seeing and knowing to structure the remarks rather than serve as decoration. Please write this speech for me: it should be appropriate for this mixed audience, clear enough for people who have never studied philosophy, and grounded in the specific situation of these paintings hanging behind me.
