I am drafting a long-form essay for a general-interest magazine on the philosophy and historiography of science. The working title is "The Inevitable Now: Whig History as a By-Product of Explanatory Inversion." I am writing as a science journalist who is trying to avoid the very trap I am describing.

Below are the core notes and argumentative moves I intend to develop. I would welcome your critical comments on their coherence, accuracy, and interest, and particularly on whether my central conceptual claim holds together. Please respond in British English, and please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations.

The essay begins from Herbert Butterfield's classic critique of the Whig interpretation: the habit of writing the past as an inevitable progression towards the present, judging earlier figures chiefly by whether they anticipated modern conclusions. I want to argue that this is not merely a moral failing or a lapse in scholarly neutrality, but a structurally incentivised mode of explanation—especially in popular science writing.

My central claim introduces what I call explanatory inversion. In ordinary causal explanation, we trace how earlier conditions produced later ones. In Whig history, I suggest, we implicitly reverse this: we treat the present state of knowledge as an explanatory attractor that pulls earlier events into its orbit, selecting which past developments matter according to their contribution to the outcome we now inhabit. The past becomes a curated pathway rather than a field of contingent possibilities.

I apply this specifically to science communication. When journalists (myself included) recount the development of, say, quantum mechanics or climate modelling, we naturally frame earlier disputes as preliminary stages of current understanding—ignoring conceptual frameworks that were locally coherent but led away from present orthodoxy. The result is not falsehood in any narrow sense, but a systematic distortion of causal weight: dead ends are rendered as necessary failures, and historical actors are interpreted as groping towards concepts they could not have possessed.

I then propose that this inversion violates a principle of interpretive charity. If we take seriously the bounded rationality and local conceptual resources of historical agents, we must allow that many of them were solving problems that do not map onto ours. To explain their actions primarily by reference to our present categories is therefore not just presentist; it misattributes the reasons that actually guided their behaviour.

Finally, I sketch an alternative narrative grammar—one that permits genuinely abandoned research programmes to remain abandoned, without being recuperated as steps toward later success. I concede that such narratives are harder to tell, because audiences need stakes anchored in present concerns, but I argue that the difficulty is rhetorical rather than epistemic.

Please comment on the following:

1. Is the distinction between ordinary causal explanation and explanatory inversion clear and defensible, or does it collapse into a restatement of presentism?
2. Does the application to science journalism overlook important counter-examples—cases where popular writing successfully resists teleological framing?
3. Is my appeal to interpretive charity and bounded rationality consistent with the way philosophers of science actually discuss historical explanation, or am I importing a concept from elsewhere without adequate justification?
4. Is the essay likely to be interesting to a non-specialist reader, or does the conceptual machinery obscure the practical point?

Please answer in British English.
