ok so i'm writing up my view on collective guilt across generations for a seminar and want a consistency check before i present it. here's roughly where i've landed, with a mix of case verdicts and principles:

1. a nation can be politically guilty for past crimes (say, colonial expropriation) long after every direct perpetrator is dead, but only in an institutional sense — not as personal moral blame that sticks to individual citizens.
2. i deny that citizens today are beneficiaries in any morally relevant way, because the gains are too structurally diffused; you can't trace a specific unjust advantage to any particular person.
3. still, i think there's a duty of reparation, but its corrective justice aimed at the institution, not punishment for guilty individuals.
4. i accept the usual four-way split between criminal, moral, metaphysical and political guilt, except i reject the idea that metaphysical guilt is inescapable — i think sustained moral resistance to the legacy can dissolve it.
5. i also think that if a citizen emigrates and fully renounces citizenship, the nation's political guilt doesn't shrink at all, since its tied to institutional continuity rather than membership rolls.
6. but i also think a citizen who stays and actively works against the legacy can individually shed her share of the political guilt, even though the nation as a whole remains guilty.

i feel like parts of this hang together and parts don't, but i can't tell which is which. does tying guilt to institutional continuity (1,5) conflict with letting individual dissent erase one's personal share (6)? does denying personal benefit (2) make the reparations duty (3) look like we're just taxing people for a mess they didn't cause? and is there an apparent tension between rejecting inescapable metaphysical guilt (4) while accepting inescapable political guilt (1) — or is that just mixing up categories?
