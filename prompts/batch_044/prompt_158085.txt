Students were specifically asked to determine which model better captures Kant’s account of moral worth in the Groundwork, to explain how each model treats the honest merchant who returns correct change solely to preserve his reputation, and to identify one internal tension in the model they reject.

The student wrote the following: “On Kant’s account, the performative model is clearly correct, because moral worth requires that the will be determined by the representation of the moral law rather than by inclination alone. This means that if an agent acts in conformity with duty but her action is caused by self-love or sympathy, she lacks moral worth, since the presence of inclination as any concurrent cause necessarily negates the purity required for moral worth. The archival model is therefore inadequate: the honest merchant who knows that honesty is required but is moved by reputation certainly contains the correct principle in his reasoning archive, yet he has no moral worth. By contrast, an agent who acts from a purely de dicto recognition of her duty—without any particular concern for the people affected—fully satisfies the performative condition and therefore possesses moral worth. The main tension in the archival model is that it collapses the distinction between objective and subjective principles of volition, treating mere cognitive possession as equivalent to rational self-legislation. Hence Kant should be read as a performativist.”
