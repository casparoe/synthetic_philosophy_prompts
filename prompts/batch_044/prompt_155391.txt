I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy in earnest. I intend to guide a single ninety-minute discussion for a small group of retired scientists and engineers on the special composition question—specifically, whether there is a principled boundary between mere aggregates and genuine composite objects, and if so, what grounds it. I do not want an exposition of the debate; I want a pedagogical plan. First, specify the exact sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit: for instance, whether the distinction between spatial adjacency and causal-functional integration must be secured before any criterion of composition can be evaluated. Second, identify the single carrying example—drawn from molecular biology or materials science rather than everyday furniture—that should structure the entire session, explaining precisely which feature it must exhibit (say, emergent functional unity) and which it must conspicuously lack (say, mere statistical correlation of parts), and defend why this example succeeds where a crystal lattice or a sand pile fails. Third, anticipate the misconceptions this technically trained audience will carry in—likely that composition is reducible to inter-particle forces, or that quantum entanglement resolves the issue automatically—and state the precise moment in the sequence at which each misconception should be intercepted and neutralized. Fourth, propose one concluding diagnostic question that reveals whether a participant has grasped the metaphysical problem rather than merely endorsed a vague holism. Fifth, indicate which position—perhaps a restricted-composition view indexed to biological organization—should be introduced only as an unresolved provocation and deliberately left hanging, with an explanation of why resolving it would derail the session's purpose. The plan should assume no assigned texts and should avoid bibliographic references.
