I am giving the opening remarks at a workshop marking the retirement of Dr. Miriam Okonkwo, who directed the ethics consultation service at a major teaching hospital for twenty-eight years. The audience is split: on one side are palliative-care physicians, nurses, and clinical-trial coordinators who worked with her; on the other are philosophers of medicine, theologians, and bioethicists who studied her writing. Dr. Okonkwo's guiding principle, which she drew from Augustine's De Mendacio, was that every intentional verbal deception—every mendacium—is a corruption of the speaker's own moral integrity, regardless of the listener's welfare or the gravity of the occasion. She adopted Augustine's eight-grade taxonomy not to rank which lies were excusable, but to show that even the lowest grade, the so-called officious lie told to preserve life or ease suffering, remains forbidden. At the same time, she insisted that strategic silence, truthful ambiguity, and a direct refusal to answer are not lies and carry no such stain. In practice this meant she refused to approve consent language that encouraged therapeutic misconception, she trained residents to say that they did not know rather than fabricate reassurance, and she was known for sitting with families in long, truthful silences instead of offering euphemism. The philosophical idea must do real structural work in my remarks: I need to honor her rigor without implying that her refusal to lie made her indifferent to suffering, and I need to speak in terms that land for clinicians who experienced her compassion as warmth rather than rigidity, while not misrepresenting her Augustinian commitment for the philosophers in the room. Please write a four-minute address that uses the distinction between mendacium and permissible silence to shape both what I praise and what I deliberately do not claim about her legacy.
