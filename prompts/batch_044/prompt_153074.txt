Design a 75-minute structured role-play for a seminar of about 16 upper-level students in political philosophy or comparative ethics. The participants should come away seeing that the line between corruption and legitimate reciprocity is not just a matter of applying a universal Western standard, but depends on whether an exchange is visible, role-embedded, and convertible into material advantage—features that can pull in opposite directions within the same institutional rules.

Center the activity on a fictional municipal planning board in a post-colonial city-state, Mariká, whose formal regulations recognize “kinship credits”: social obligations accrued through neighborhood and family ties that can be registered with the board but carry unwritten norms about when they may be drawn upon to secure land-use variances, expedited permits, or exemptions from density rules. Invent the mechanics concretely—three types of variance (residential height, commercial zoning, heritage demolition), a token economy of reputation points and future-obligation cards, and a public audit mechanism triggered by anonymous complaints—so that the rules themselves generate the tension, not a facilitator’s framing.

Structure three decision rounds with these roles: four board members (each with a different institutional background—elected official, appointed planner, community elder, legal auditor), six petitioners (with different credit portfolios and hidden private obligations), and six observers who hold partial information and can file audit triggers. Between rounds, reveal new information: first, only formal applications; second, the kinship-credit registers; third, which petitioners have undisclosed reciprocal debts to board members. The payoff scheme should use non-monetary stakes—reputation tokens that determine who can serve on future boards, obligation cards that must be honored in later rounds, and a collective “city legitimacy” score that collapses if too many hidden exchanges are exposed—so that honoring private reciprocity and maintaining public legitimacy genuinely conflict.

The rules must be arranged so that in round one students feel confident labeling moves as clean or corrupt, but by round three they discover that the same structural move—a petitioner drawing registered kinship credits to secure a variance—functions simultaneously as legitimate social reciprocity (visible, role-based, long-term) and as hidden exclusion of outsiders who lack such credits. The auditor role should have incomplete information, forcing students to decide whether to investigate based on pattern rather than proof, so they experience the epistemic problem of detecting corruption without collapsing all reciprocity into bribery.

In the debrief, map specific moments onto conceptual distinctions: deviation from formal rules versus violation of role-based reciprocity norms; discrete quid-pro-quo versus continuous obligation; whether Mariká’s formal rules actually encode a different separation between public office and private social life than Western anti-corruption frameworks assume. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time: students often collapse all credit-drawing into bribery once they learn about hidden obligations, or the auditor becomes purely punitive and destroys the reciprocity dynamics. Provide in-the-moment corrections—pause after round two to ask whether the exchange was hidden or structurally embedded, and remind the auditor that legitimacy requires distinguishing exclusion from exchange.

Finally, include an honest note on the feature this activity cannot reproduce: the macro-structural and historical question of whether clientelist systems stabilize democratic representation over decades, or whether they inevitably erode universal citizenship. Supply that limitation verbally after the debrief, clarifying that the game isolates the conceptual boundary problem but cannot model long-term institutional decay. Do not require advance reading of named theorists, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the philosophical problem should emerge from the rules and the forced choices.
