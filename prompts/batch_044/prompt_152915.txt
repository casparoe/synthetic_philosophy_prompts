I'm working through Russell's arguments in his writings on marriage and sexual morality, and I've constructed what looks like a reductio that pushes his rationalist critique past the point he intended, leaving marriage either redundant or unjustified. I'd like help locating the weak step, because each premise feels like a direct extension of his actual view, yet together they seem to dissolve the very institution he wants to reform rather than abolish.

(1) Russell holds that sexual ethics should be governed solely by rational assessment of human happiness and tangible harm—especially harm to children—while restrictions derived from religious authority or irrational emotion ought to be discarded. (2) Among those irrational emotions he singles out jealousy, treating it as a destructive, possessive instinct that rational people should seek to overcome rather than encode in social norms. (3) If jealousy ought to be overcome, then the expectation of sexual exclusivity—which functions as jealousy's institutional expression—lacks rational justification. (4) Russell nevertheless defends a reformed marriage, often described in terms of companionate cooperation, on the grounds that it provides necessary stability for child-rearing and sustained emotional partnership. (5) But if exclusivity is removed, the remaining bond is defined only by cooperative parenting and mutual affection; yet these features can be fully present in close friendships or in non-marital cooperative parenting arrangements that carry no special legal or social status. (6) Therefore, once exclusivity is abandoned, marriage possesses no distinctive moral or institutional content and collapses into a particularly intense friendship—or, if one prefers a more formal framing, into a renewable cooperation contract with no necessary link to romantic exclusivity. (7) Russell, however, insists that marriage should be preserved as a legally recognized and socially distinct institution, not dissolved into friendship. (8) So either Russell must concede that exclusivity has rational value—contradicting (2) and (3)—or he must concede that marriage is institutionally redundant—contradicting (4) and (7).

The conclusion that marriage is redundant strikes me as absurd, not least because Russell devotes considerable effort to defending its legal form and social utility. I suspect the error lies somewhere between (3) and (6), perhaps in the assumption that a partnership lacking exclusivity cannot possess a structurally distinct role, but I cannot pinpoint whether the slide is an equivocation on "privileged bond," a hidden premise about institutional definition, or something else. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up commit me to? If I can preserve (1) and (2)—since they seem to capture the distinctive core of Russell's rationalist critique—I would prefer to do so, though I am willing to revise (5) if that is where the argument overreaches.
