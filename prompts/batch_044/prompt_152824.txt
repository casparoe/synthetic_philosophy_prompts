I help administer a twelve-person cooperative that maintains shared fabrication equipment, and we are deadlocked over our own charter. The charter contains two provisions enacted at different times. The first allows any operating rule to be temporarily suspended for urgent repairs if eleven members agree. The second provision declares that this suspension mechanism itself is never subject to suspension, and it further specifies that this second provision can only be amended or repealed with the unanimous consent of all twelve members—including anyone who would benefit from blocking such an amendment.

Yesterday a core machine failed. Eleven members want to invoke the suspension mechanism to bypass the usual procurement protocol and repair it immediately. The twelfth member refuses. He argues that bypassing procurement could indirectly alter resource flows that sustain the protected suspension mechanism, thereby touching the second provision in a way that violates its protected status. He also points out that if the eleven tried to amend the protection clause first to clear the path, he could withhold his consent, since the clause requires his agreement to change. The eleven counter that they are not touching the second provision directly, only using the first one as intended.

Is there a principled way for the eleven to proceed that respects the charter's internal logic, or does the charter contain a structural paradox that makes consistent self-governance impossible in this case? What should the cooperative actually do?
