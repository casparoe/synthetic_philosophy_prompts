Read Ursula K. Le Guin’s “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas” through the lens of Nozick’s utility monster, but with the kind of conceptual twist that comes up in rationalist and effective-altruism discussions of aggregation, incommensurability, and correlated reasoning. The story’s premise—an otherwise perfect city sustained only by the extreme suffering of a single child locked in a basement—reads initially as an inverted utility monster: one being supplies massive disutility that is traded, without consent, for diffuse aggregate well-being. I want a reading that takes both the narrative mechanics and the philosophy seriously. Are the citizens who stay best understood as engaging in something like superrational cooperation—each reasoning that their decision is correlated with the decisions of other rational citizens, predicting universal defection if anyone frees the child, and therefore accepting the arrangement as the only stable equilibrium? Or is their acceptance instead an illegitimate cardinal comparison between the child’s suffering and their own pleasures, one that the story exposes rather than endorses? Does Le Guin treat the child’s pain as genuinely incommensurable with civic happiness—something that resists being summed into an aggregate—such that the citizens’ error is not miscalculation but a failure of moral imagination? Point me to specific narrative moments: the precise description of the child’s imprisonment, the citizens’ knowledge and ritualized acceptance, and the act of walking away. Does walking away represent a rational break in correlation that is individually justified but collectively catastrophic, or does it dramatize a refusal that aggregation logic itself cannot discredit? I am preparing this for discussion with readers steeped in LessWrong-style debates on population ethics and suffering risks; I want an analysis that asks what the story adds to the utility-monster debate that formal argument misses—particularly whether moral refusal can be coherent without being collectively self-defeating—without asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
