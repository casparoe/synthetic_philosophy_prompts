I am writing a thesis chapter on the epistemology of risk in normative decision theory, and I have become preoccupied with a reconstructive question: how would Frank Knight—who died in 1972, well before risk-weighted expected utility was formalised in its modern decision-theoretic form—have responded to its application in domains of deep uncertainty that he never confronted? Specifically, I am interested in the frameworks now circulating in transformative AI governance, where analysts do not merely assign probabilities to unknown outcomes but calibrate risk-weights using parameters that go beyond subjective credence—most notably, what some researchers call an 'ontological disruption index', a niche metric intended to capture the degree to which a decision might restructure the very space of evaluable states, violating the closure conditions that standard risk-weighted expected utility implicitly assumes. Knight’s own corpus turns on a rigorous separation between measurable risk and true, unquantifiable uncertainty; my question is whether he would have regarded this modern weighting scheme as a legitimate extension of probabilistic reasoning or as a category error that smuggles spurious precision back into the domain of genuine Knightian uncertainty. I want the prediction to be disciplined: which elements of Knight’s actual distinction—his critique of statistical inference in economic life, his insistence that entrepreneurship operates under non-probabilistic ignorance—genuinely carry over, and which are simply outrun by a conceptual apparatus he could not have anticipated?

More concretely, suppose we present Knight with a decision tree for deploying a transformative AI system, where the risk-weight assigned to catastrophic outcomes is determined not by Bayesian credence but by this ontological-disruption metric, and where the utility aggregation itself is treated as endogenous—potentially altered by the very act of deployment in ways that break the independence conditions standard REU requires. Would Knight have modified his taxonomy to accommodate such 'leakage' between the decision and the state-space, rejected the framework as an illicit quantification of the unquantifiable, or argued that the presence of such leakage merely confirms his original thesis that true uncertainty lies precisely where probabilistic weighting fails? Please avoid treating his writings as a static oracle; instead, trace the philosophical pressure points where his commitments genuinely extend, and flag explicitly where the modern problem outruns the conceptual resources he actually possessed.
