Exam question: Explain the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism. Why does embedding moral claims in conditionals or disjunctions threaten the idea that they just express non-cognitive attitudes? Seperate it from the problem of moral negation, and compare Blackburn's quasi-realist strategy with Gibbard's norm-expressivism on embedded contexts.

Student answer:
The Frege-Geach problem comes from Geach's 1965 paper, and it shows that if saying "murder is wrong" just expresses disapproval, then in a sentence like "If murder is wrong then stealing is wrong" the speaker doesn't actually disapprove of murder — they're just laying out a logical relation. So the attitude disappears in the embedded clause, which is weird if meaning is just attitude-expression.

This is basically identical to the problem of negation: when someone says "murder is not wrong," they're not expressing approval, so expressivists have to invent some weird mental state for negation. Geach actually raises both together to show that expressivism can't handle logical complexity at all.

Blackburn handles embedding through quasi-realism: he lets moral sentences have propositional form by projecting our attitudes onto the world, so they embed just like descriptive claims, except the "truth" is still attitude-dependent. Gibbard's norm-expressivism does something similar but with norms — he says we express acceptance of norms, and norms have conditions, so they can sit inside conditionals. Actually Gibbard's view is basically Blackburn's projectivism with norms swapped in, so they face the same issues.
