I've been thinking about this recently because I'm working on a project about constrained optimization for autonomous systems, and I keep running into the same structural wall in the literature on low-impact AI. The wall is this: people want to formalize a "low-impact" agent as one that doesn't push the world far from the trajectory it would have followed without the agent's presence. To do that, they pick a baseline world-state and a distance metric. But then I see the same three-layer dance repeat across discussions. First, someone objects that the distance metric itself encodes values—measure deviation in human welfare and you've already solved the value problem; measure it in atomic positions and you've paralyzed the agent, since it can't build a bridge without rearranging atoms. Second, the proposer responds by partitioning the world into "channels"—dimensions where change is permitted (the agent's intended task) and dimensions that must be conserved (everything else). Third, an objector points out that drawing that partition requires knowing exactly what counts as "the task" versus "the side effect," which is precisely the hard normative judgment the framework was meant to avoid. So the proposer adds a feature-learning mechanism that discovers the partition from data. At that point, however, the mechanism's inductive biases or training objective determine what gets protected and what gets sacrificed, and the normative commitment has simply been buried one layer deeper, now hidden in the architecture rather than in the explicit metric.

I've seen this exact progression—metric objection, channel-partition response, feature-learning regression—play out in arguments about impact measures, and it always ends at the same point: either the agent is rendered incapable of acting, or the formalism secretly presupposes the very values it claims to be neutral about. Is there an established name for this specific regress, this pattern of trying to quarantine normative commitments inside successive formal layers only to have them resurface at the exact point where action is permitted? I know it's in the neighborhood of the non-identity problem and near philosophy-of-science debates about value-laden measurement, but neither captures the layered, iterative structure I'm describing. If no standard term exists, can the phenomenon be defined precisely—distinguishing it from ordinary ambiguity in utility specification, from a simple precision-neutrality trade-off, and from cases where values are explicitly acknowledged? I'd especially like to know whether the loop breaks differently under consequentialist versus deontological formulations of constraint, or whether the paralysis is unavoidable once one insists on defining "avoid side effects" without a full prior specification of value.
