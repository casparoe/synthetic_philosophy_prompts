A recent popular essay summarized Confucian role ethics as follows: "The view holds that moral identity is entirely constituted by social roles—there is no self prior to being a child, parent, ruler, or friend—so ethics is essentially the performance of the scripts attached to those roles (li). Because the five relationships are governed by reciprocal duties, the framework is symmetric in moral weight: a ruler owes a subject the same kind of care, just scaled, as a father owes a child. Critics therefore charge that it collapses moral judgment into etiquette and eliminates any basis for criticizing a role-structure itself. Defenders respond that ren (humaneness) is nothing more than excellence within one's assigned roles. Consequently, role ethics is flatly incompatible with universal human rights, since rights apply to persons qua persons, not qua role-bearers." Something about the symmetry claim, the treatment of ren, and the conclusion about rights strikes me as too neat, but I cannot tell which parts are standard simplifications and which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly regarding hierarchy, the distinction between li and yi (appropriateness), and the function of shu (reciprocity) across unequal roles. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a careful defender would deny; then assess whether the objection about universal rights survives once the view is corrected. Finally, say what would change your assessment: which specific interpretive or textual point—say, about the critical potential of zhengming or the non-scripted nature of yi—would lead you to judge the summary as mostly accurate or mostly unfair?
