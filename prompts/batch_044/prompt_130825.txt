I have recently been appointed secretary to my university's constitutional review panel, which two years ago adopted a 'living statutes' framework permitting any procedural rule to be amended by simple majority, save that amendments to the amendment clause itself require a three-quarters majority. A member has now proposed a substantive equality-policy revision containing an embedded proviso that would lower all future procedural thresholds to a simple majority. The drafting committee maintains that because the proviso is attached to substantive policy, the proposal falls under the ordinary amendment pathway and does not engage the protected-clause safeguard; opponents argue that this is precisely the self-referential stratagem the safeguard was designed to block, analogous to the paradoxes of legal self-reference in which a rule governing its own alteration can be circumvented by embedding the alteration elsewhere. I must circulate a brief written opinion before Thursday's session recommending whether the proposal should be admitted under the simple-majority route, ruled inadmissible absent the supermajority, or referred for external arbitration, with reasoning that takes the drafting committee's interpretive claim seriously rather than dismissing it by fiat. The panel is split between members who treat our statutes as a Nomic-style self-amending system and members who hold that legal self-reference is always resolvable by ordinary canons of construction.
