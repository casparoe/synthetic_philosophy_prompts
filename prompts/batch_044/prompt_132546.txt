I was listening to two friends argue over dinner about whether people in different cultures actually disagree about what's morally right, or whether they just apply the same basic rules to different situations. They'd both read something about WEIRD psychology — the idea that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic societies have moral intuitions that are outliers compared with much of the world.

Maya said that if one culture treats nepotism as a family duty and another treats it as corruption, that's a flat contradiction: at least one side has to be wrong, which suggests there's an objective moral truth that one group is missing. Jonas replied that it's more like arguing about the best diet: both groups share the underlying value of keeping people healthy, but they have different empirical beliefs about what actually works, so there's no deep moral contradiction.

Maya pushed back with what she called a "pure difference" case. She pointed out that in some societies people feel a genuine moral obligation to criticize or cut down anyone who stands too far above the group — not out of envy, she insisted, but as a kind of social-leveling duty. In American culture that sounds like vicious resentment, while celebrating standout success is treated as a moral good. She argued that if one culture makes achievement-celebration a virtue and another makes achievement-leveling a virtue, you can't explain that away by saying they share values but disagree about facts. The values themselves seem to point in opposite directions.

Jonas said she was still sneaking in hidden shared values: both cultures care about fairness, but they define unfairness differently — Americans see unfairness in holding people back, while the other culture sees unfairness in letting anyone accumulate disproportionate status. So it's the same fairness principle, just weighted differently against goods like social harmony.

Maya then asked: if every disagreement can be redescribed as "shared values with different weights," then moral objectivity becomes empty, because there's no possible observation that could prove one weighting wrong. Jonas admitted that was a real risk, but said it doesn't follow that there's no right answer — only that finding it requires comparing total social outcomes over time, not just inspecting people's gut feelings.

Can you go through their exchange move by move? For each of Maya's and Jonas's points, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and point out anything important that both of them missed — especially about whether cross-cultural differences in folk morality actually tell us anything about whether morality is objective.
