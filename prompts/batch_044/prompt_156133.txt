Consider a counterfactual in which Arthur Schopenhauer is alive today and has studied the asymmetry argument advanced by David Benatar in favour of antinatalism: that the presence of suffering is bad for the sufferer, the presence of pleasure is good for the enjoyer, yet the absence of suffering is good even when there is no subject to benefit, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there exists someone who is thereby deprived, from which it follows that causing a person to exist is always a net harm. Now imagine that Schopenhauer also contemplates a hypothetical, wholly painless technology that permits humanity collectively to deny the will-to-live without individual distress. Would he treat Benatar's asymmetry as a genuine philosophical vindication of his own view that existence oscillates between pain and boredom, or rather as a shallow rationalisation that confuses a surface symptom with the deeper metaphysical malady of blind willing? Specifically, how would he assess the asymmetry's emphasis on experienced harms in light of his conviction that the will must be negated at its root rather than managed case by case? Respond in a single paragraph of at most 180 words, in British English.
