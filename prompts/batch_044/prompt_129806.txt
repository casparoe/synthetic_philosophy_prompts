I recently turned eighteen and, for the first time, faced a local election in which every candidate for a council seat struck me as seriously flawed—one credibly endorsed policies I consider unjust, while another seemed personally decent but manifestly unfit for governance. I left that race blank, and since then I have been uncertain whether my abstention was an act of integrity or a failure of civic duty. Next week I am delivering the opening address at my town's annual Civic Responsibility symposium; the audience is split between seasoned local officials and first-time voters who faced the same dilemma. Please write a five-minute speech structured around the ethics of voting—specifically, the tension between voting as an expression of one's values (which selecting a "lesser evil" might corrupt) and voting as a duty that binds us regardless of candidate quality. To keep the argument precise, work through the concept of "expressive asymmetry," which I take to mean that leaving a race blank is not a neutral non-expression but itself expresses a specific kind of acquiescence to the likely winner, thereby collapsing the familiar distinction between the "pure" abstainer and the "compromised" voter. The speech should comfort those who abstained without letting them off easily, and it should challenge those who voted for flawed candidates without condemning them. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific philosophers by name. Write it in a voice that is formal but personally accountable—this is my own attempt to think aloud about something that has become genuinely relevant to me.
