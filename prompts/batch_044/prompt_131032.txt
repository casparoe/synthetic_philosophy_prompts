I cover interdisciplinary science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I repeatedly face the same editorial problem: two peer-reviewed studies in closely related fields appear to assert contradictory propositions—same phenomenon, opposite conclusions—yet both have survived standard peer review, share comparable sample sizes, and cite overlapping methodological literature. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in dialetheism and paraconsistent logic but entirely free of formal notation, that my team and I can run in about fifteen minutes before deciding how to frame a story. The procedure should present an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions: first, whether the contradiction is formal (identical predicates, identical conditions) or contextual (different models, scales, or auxiliary assumptions); second, whether both claims can be jointly affirmed without entailing arbitrary falsehoods, and which structural rules of inference must be restricted to prevent explosion; third, which shared background premises must be abandoned if we treat the contradiction as genuinely real rather than merely apparent; and fourth, whether scientific consistency functions here as a regulative ideal or as an empirically testable constraint, with an explicit flag marking this step as a normative judgment rather than a factual determination. It must specify clear stopping conditions—when we classify the conflict as a real dialetheic tension requiring both claims to be reported as literally true, when we treat it as a resolvable paraconsistent inconsistency that calls for further empirical arbitration, and when we conclude that at least one study is straightforwardly erroneous. The procedure must also state its own failure conditions: where it cannot settle the framing and hands the decision back to editorial judgment about audience understanding and potential misinterpretation.

Apply the procedure to three cases. First, two regional climate-model parameterizations that yield opposite-sign predictions for monsoon intensity change over the same geographic window, both achieving comparable validation scores against historical data. Second, a neuroscience study in which the same dataset both supports and undermines the global workspace theory of consciousness, depending solely on whether a conservative or liberal statistical threshold for network-connectivity significance is applied. Third, a hypothetical case in which the procedure recommends framing the contradiction as genuinely real—both claims true in some non-classical sense—but my editorial judgment resists that framing because presenting it to a general audience would likely be read as "scientists have no idea what they are doing" rather than as a nuanced logical status. In each application, show the path through the questions, identify the point where judgment supersedes logic, and explain what adopting the dialetheic framing costs us in terms of explanatory coherence. Finally, specify one further case—briefly described—that would distinguish whether a user of this procedure is implicitly committed to dialetheism as a metaphysical thesis about reality or only to paraconsistency as a logical tool for managing inconsistent information sets.
