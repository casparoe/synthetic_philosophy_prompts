In my graduate seminar on the semantics of normative discourse, two doctoral candidates—Alex and Jordan—recently reconstructed a debate they had witnessed during a municipal zoning hearing. The controversy concerned a councilor’s opposition to an ordinance that would replace a longstanding community garden with luxury condominiums. In her remarks, the councilor stated: “If tearing down shared green space for private luxury development is unjust, then enacting this ordinance is unjust.” Alex contended that, by uttering this conditional, the councilor had genuinely expressed moral disapproval of destroying the garden even within the antecedent clause, because the emotional and rhetorical force of her intervention made any purely hypothetical reading implausible in that deliberative context. Jordan objected that this conflates pragmatic effect with semantic content: in a conditional, the antecedent is merely assumed for the sake of argument, so the speaker need not actually hold the attitude expressed there; were she committed to it independently, she could not consistently suspend judgment later, yet practical reasoning routinely employs working hypotheses without full endorsement. Alex replied that Jordan’s position makes it impossible to use normative premises in practical deliberation without being accused of hypocrisy should one later revise one’s view, which seems an unreasonably rigid constraint on moral reasoning. Jordan countered that this objection mistakes logical form for psychological commitment—just as a mathematician may assume a contradiction to derive a result without believing it, a speaker can deploy a normative premise as a temporary supposition; any emotional invitation extended to the audience is a pragmatic embellishment, not part of the literal meaning of the embedded clause. They have asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating; then indicate who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both interlocutors are missing about how embedded normative language functions in public deliberation.
