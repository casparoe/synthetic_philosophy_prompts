Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students on political polarization, echo chambers, and whether the other side argues in good faith, with a structural twist: instead of open debate, the session uses anonymized “reasoning transcripts” to separate the evaluation of an argument’s internal coherence from the identity of its advocate. Before class, prepare two sets of self-contained reasoning prompts on a concrete, non-obvious policy question—e.g., whether algorithmic transparency mandates should apply to private recommendation systems—assigning half the students to defend Position A and half Position B, regardless of their personal views. In the first 20 minutes, each student writes a transcript: a step-by-step justification for their assigned position that they themselves need not believe, explicitly labeling premises, inferences, and one point where they think a reasonable opponent might resist. Transcripts are anonymized, shuffled, and distributed randomly in the second phase (30 minutes): each student receives one transcript from the opposing camp and must classify it as either “good-faith reasoning from different premises” or “strategic bad-faith rhetoric,” recording confidence on a 1–5 scale and writing a two-sentence justification of their classification. In the third phase (20 minutes), authors reveal themselves, read the classifications they received, and respond briefly; the group then votes, without further discussion, on whether knowing the author’s assigned position changed their initial classification. The debrief (20 minutes) should map moments onto the conceptual landscape: the transcript-writing phase forces articulation of premises that echo-chamber dynamics usually suppress; the anonymized judgment tests whether structural features of reasoning (explicit premises, acknowledged resistance points) can be detected without identity cues; the reveal-and-vote tests whether polarization operates mainly at the level of inferred motivation or at the level of content evaluation. Provide rules that prevent direct rebuttal during classification, materials (template sheets for transcripts and scoring cards), and a symbolic payoff scheme—e.g., cumulative “epistemic accuracy points” awarded to students whose classifications align with the author’s self-reported intent (collected after the reveal)—rather than real money. Predict what will go wrong: students will likely conflate unfamiliar premises with bad faith, anonymity will be broken by writing style or prior knowledge of peers’ views, and the vote will show near-unanimous identity-driven revision, undermining the intended separation. Handle this by using typed templates with standardized formatting and by framing the failure itself as data. Finally, state honestly what the simulation cannot reproduce: the structural economic and algorithmic incentives that create echo chambers in the wild—platform optimization for engagement, identity-based social sorting, and long-term reputation costs—so the facilitator must supply that context in closing. Ensure the mechanics, not a lecture, generate the experience of doubting whether the other side is reachable.
