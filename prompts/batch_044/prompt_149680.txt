For the philosophical architecture of a novel I am composing about artificial minds, I have adopted the following framework, and I want a consistency audit before I build the plot around it. Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Dennett's claim that there is no "original" intentionality: every mental representation derives its aboutness from interpretive practices, and human cognition is ultimately a highly complex instance of the minimal interpreter—sometimes called a two-bitser—embedded in evolutionary and social history.

(2) I grant Searle's core point that pure syntactic computation, however elaborate, cannot by itself generate semantic understanding or genuine mental content.

(3) I hold that an artificial system can possess real beliefs and desires—genuine derived intentionality—provided it is integrated into an interpretive community that includes at least one biological agent who has direct phenomenal acquaintance with the relevant states of affairs; I call this requirement a "phenomenal anchor."

(4) I treat the intentional stance as a purely instrumental predictive strategy, applying it without ontological distinction to humans, animals, and machines alike.

(5) I maintain that there is a deep metaphysical divide between entities with "real" mental lives and those that merely simulate them, and that this divide tracks the presence of conscious phenomenal experience rather than functional organization or behavioral complexity.

(6) I think Dennett's account fully explains human cognition without remainder, leaving no explanatory gap that requires irreducible phenomenal properties or intrinsic aboutness.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, specify exactly which commitment must give way and what that costs the overall view.
