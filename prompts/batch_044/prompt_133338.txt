I've been reflecting on a pattern I see in workplace rules and licensing boards. In fields like pharmacy, aviation, or surgery, regulators usually sort serious errors into at least two boxes: knowing violations, where someone understood the rule and ignored it, and absent-minded lapses, where someone was running on autopilot and forgot a step they genuinely meant to take. There's also a third category, pure accident, like an unforeseeable equipment failure that no human attention could have prevented. The sanctions differ: knowing violations often bring suspension or revocation; absent-minded slips sometimes bring lighter penalties or mandatory mindfulness training; pure accidents may bring no personal blame at all, though the harm is identical.

This pattern seems to mirror the old philosophical puzzle of the absent-minded driver who intends to turn at the store but drives right past it—he did not decide to keep going, yet he did keep going. The institutional practice treats that middle case as morally different from both deliberate wrongdoing and pure misfortune.

What philosophical commitments about action, responsibility, and the self does this three-way institutional distinction presuppose? Does it assume that an act only belongs to a person in the blameworthy sense when it is accompanied by present-to-mind awareness? If so, how can the same system hold the driver responsible for being absent-minded—doesn't that require that he should have been present, which in turn implies he could have summoned presence? And is there a hidden view of the person as divided—an autopilot self that performs actions without ownership, and a deliberative self that owns only what it consciously directs? Would the practice collapse if we replaced that divided picture with a unified account of agency, or could it be reconstructed on purely consequentialist grounds? I want to understand what theory of the mind and of moral responsibility is quietly doing the work here, whether the practice actually requires that theory or merely fits it, and what would have to change in these regulations if the underlying view were abandoned.
