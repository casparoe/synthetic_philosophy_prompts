I am working through my own commitments on whether pleasure and suffering are genuinely commensurable—whether they can be summed, traded, or weighed on a single cardinal scale—and whether there is a lexical threshold below or above which suffering dominates regardless of the quantity of pleasure at stake. The framing I keep returning to is a lexical-threshold form of negative utilitarianism, but I am unsure whether it can be made coherent with my intuitions about incommensurability, so I want to put my verdicts in order and see what principle, if any, survives without ad hoc repair.

Here are my judgments, given without much further argument. First, a life containing one episode of extreme torture and otherwise vast pleasure seems, to me, categorically worse than a life of moderate pleasure without torture; the suffering is not merely heavier, it appears to operate as a lexical blocker, as though no accumulation of pleasure can redeem the life. Second, comparing two populations—one with a thousand people each receiving one unit of pleasure, the other with a single person receiving nine hundred and ninety-nine units of suffering—I still judge the second world worse, which suggests a strongly non-linear, negative-utilitarian aggregation rather than a simple sum. Third, when the suffering is genuinely sub-threshold, such as a brief headache or a moment of embarrassment, I think it can be compensated by a sufficiently large pleasure, for instance a sustained period of creative fulfillment; so whatever lexical boundary exists is not triggered by all suffering, only by severe or sustained forms. Fourth, I find myself uncertain about a being that experiences pleasure solely as the absence of discomfort, with no independent positive phenomenology: if such a being suffers only a mild, continuous ache, can its loss ever be outweighed by the intense but qualitatively different pleasures of others? This makes me wonder whether pleasure and pain are not just differently weighted but dimensionally incommensurable, like comparing velocity to temperature. Fifth, in a practical policy choice between reducing chronic low-level anxiety across millions and preventing a single catastrophic trauma, I lean toward preventing the trauma, yet I feel uneasy treating this as a multiplication problem rather than a categorical priority.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism qualified by a dimensional-incommensurability clause—that captures all five verdicts without forcing me to drop one? Or must I abandon one of them, and if so, which is the least defensible? Please also state what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not listed: the hypothetical being described in the fourth verdict, engineered so that its only affective state is the gradient between mild discomfort and neutral absence, asked whether its minor pains should be traded away to maximize the vivid pleasures of other, phenomenologically richer beings.
