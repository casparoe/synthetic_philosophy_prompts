I've been arguing with my roommate about whether bad people can make good art, and we got into this weird loop that now feels like it eats itself. Here's the argument I sketched out: (1) Call a work "morally embedded" if its aesthetic form is inseperable from a specific immoral evaluative stance — not just depicting it, but structurally requiring the viewer to adopt it to perceive the form correctly. (2) If a work is morally embedded, then appreciating it fully requires taking up that stance, at least dispositionally. (3) If taking up an immoral stance is required for full appreciation, then the work's artistic value is directly proportional to the depth of moral corruption it induces. (4) So the best morally embedded artworks are the ones that corrupt you the most thoroughly. (5) But that means we should rank, say, a hypothetical film that makes genocide feel sublime as greater art than a technically brilliant but morally neutral landscape painting. (6) Nobody buys that ranking. (7) Therefore either (1) is false and no work is morally embedded, or there's a hidden slide somewhere between (2) and (4).

My roommate thinks the slip is between "adopting a stance for perception" and "being corrupted by it" — like reading a villain's mind in fiction doesn't make you villainous. I think the problem might actually be in (3), treating artistic value as a monotonic function of corruption depth while ignoring that corruption might destroy other aesthetic capacities. But I'm not sure which diagnosis is right. Can you tell me exactly which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs? In particular, if I save the idea that some artworks are genuinely immoral in a way that touches their artistic core — not just their subject matter — do I have to accept that appreciating them makes me worse? I want to keep that core idea if I can, but I also don't want to end up praising the worst things just because they're intense.
