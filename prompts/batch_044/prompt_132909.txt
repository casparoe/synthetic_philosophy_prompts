I am a novelist preparing a manuscript that reimagines the final years of a real, now-deceased public figure, and at a recent symposium a fellow writer summarised for me a philosophical position concerning our obligations to the dead when we dramatise them. She presented it as follows: since the dead lack sentience, they cannot suffer harm in any morally weighty sense, which entails that we hold no direct duties of welfare towards them; instead, we are bound by what she called mnemonic stewardship—a fiduciary obligation to preserve the structural coherence of a subject's self-interpretation within collective memory. On this account, a biopic or historical novel wrongs the deceased not by inflicting suffering, but by substituting an invented narrative architecture for the subject's own meaning-making framework, thereby corrupting the communal record. She further claimed that artistic licence is therefore a form of epistemic trespass unless the work explicitly declares its fictional status, and she concluded that our heightened unease about portraying recently dead figures stems from the fact that living witnesses can still correct such corruption, whereas with distant historical subjects the damage is already settled and irreversible.

I feel this account blends genuine ethical concerns with some questionable inferences, but I cannot locate the precise distortions. Would you audit its fairness? Please examine it in parts: which claims a careful defender of the view would accept, which are overstatements or conflations of distinct arguments—perhaps about posthumous harm, narrative identity, and public trust—and does the conclusion about recent versus distant figures genuinely follow from the premises she advances?
