I have come to accept a continuous-scaling version of the scale argument regarding insect sentience: rather than treating moral patienthood as an all-or-nothing status that applies only where consciousness is certain, I hold that moral consideration should scale with the product of estimated population size and subjective probability of consciousness. Because insects likely outnumber vertebrates by many orders of magnitude, even a per-capita probability of sentience far below one percent implies that the total expected number of sentient insect lives dwarfs that of larger animals, which in turn suggests that diffuse, everyday actions with ecological footprints—such as the routine use of broad-spectrum pesticides or artificial lighting that attracts and kills nocturnal insects—carry moral weights comparable to direct harm against mammals or birds. I want to know precisely what would count against this position. Which empirical findings in insect neurobiology, which conceptual distinctions in moral theory—perhaps between direct and indirect harm, agent-centered versus outcome-centered obligations, or intentional versus foreseeable effects—would genuinely undermine the expected-value framing? Which objections would defenders of this view concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as appeals to intuition that ignore probabilistic reasoning or as misunderstandings of how moral risk should be calculated? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by retreating to only intentional harm or to actions within one's direct control—and whether such a retreat is a legitimate refinement or an evasion. Finally, I want to know whether the absence of a non-arbitrary boundary preventing the argument from extending to bacteria, plants, or artificial systems constitutes a specific defect of my framing, or whether it reveals a general problem for any continuous moral calculus; and if it is specific, what principle could stop the extension without being ad hoc.
