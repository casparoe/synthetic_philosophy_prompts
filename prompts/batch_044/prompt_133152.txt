Here is an argument I built from the frame/inner-message/outer-message distinction in GEB, and it seems to collapse into either universal skepticism about biological meaning or an admission that messages carry intrinsic content—both of which I want to avoid. (1) In that framing, an inner message is syntactically inert; all semantic content is supplied by the outer message, the interpretive frame. (2) Apply this to DNA: the genome is an inner message, and the cellular transcription and translation machinery is its frame; without that machinery, DNA is mere chemistry with no genetic meaning. (3) But the machinery itself is composed of proteins and RNAs—further inner messages—each requiring its own frame (chaperones, membrane context, evolutionary selection history). (4) This generates a regress: every frame is made of messages that need further frames. (5) If the regress never terminates, then no biological message is ever semantically grounded, so evolutionary "information" is conceptually incoherent. (6) If it terminates in a base frame—say, physical law or selection pressure—that frame has no outer message by definition. (7) By (1), then, that base frame is syntactically inert and carries no meaning, so it cannot ground the meanings above it. (8) Therefore either all biological meaning is ungrounded, or some message carries intrinsic semantic content independent of any frame—contradicting (1). (9) To preserve evolutionary biology we must accept (8)'s second horn and reject (1), yet (1) seemed necessary to explain misinterpretation, mutation, and context-dependence. Which step should I reject? I am most reluctant to give up (1) or (5), but I also cannot swallow the conclusion. Is the slip in treating causal interaction as semantic framing, in assuming the regress must be linear rather than circular, or somewhere else—and what does fixing it cost the anti-intrinsicist view?
