I was chatting with some friends on a rationalist forum about how every sci-fi take on determinism just does Laplace's demon and then calls it a day, which feels like it's missing the actually interesting failure mode. I want a short story - maybe 800 to 1200 words - that digs into chaos and predictability in a less obvious way. The setting is a deterministic universe where historians work as 'shadow archivists': they rely on this idea from dynamical systems called shadowing, where even if measurements are garbage and trajectories diverge exponentially, there's still a true orbit nearby that the noisy data actually tracks. The protagonist is reconstructing a specific crisis from corrupted records, but they discover that the corruption itself isn't random noise - it's got it's own deterministic structure, almost a second-order chaos. So every correction they apply doesn't converge on 'the' actual past; it just selects a different but equally shadow-valid trajectory. There's a scene where they have to choose between two reconstructed timelines that are both valid within the error bounds - one where a war breaks out, one where it doesn't - and they realize determinism guarantees only one happened, but epistemically they're seperate and indistinguishable. I want it to feel like rationalist fiction, actually engaging with Lyapunov divergence and the limits of prediction, without turning into a physics lecture or giving the tired 'free will saves us' ending. Also please don't have anyone cite papers or ask for a reading list - I'm writing this for an offline exercise and it needs to stand on it's own.
