Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and converge in the final two lines—on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem as it arises specifically for belief, rather than for the more familiar cases of love or admiration. Let one refrain carry the evidentialist commitment: that belief aims constitutively at truth, so that only truth-related considerations are reasons of the right kind for belief, while practical or pragmatic considerations are structurally misdirected. Let the other refrain carry the opposing claim from the pragmatic-encroachment literature: that practical stakes can bear on belief in a way that is not merely causal but genuinely reason-giving, without being reducible to evidence. Use the form structurally: through the tercets the two refrains should pass each other repeatedly without resolution, and in the closing quatrain they should be forced into adjacency, mirroring the philosophical stalemate over whether belief has a single constitutive aim. Render each position with enough precision that a specialist in epistemology or philosophy of mind would recognize its commitments—specifically, the evidentialist's appeal to the transparency of belief to its truth-aim, and the pragmatist's denial that belief's normative structure excludes non-evidential reasons—without reducing either side to a slogan. The poem should work for a reader unfamiliar with the debate, but should be accurate enough that someone who knows the literature would not find it mischaracterized. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface, commentary, or framing text before it. After the poem, include a clearly separated brief note indicating where the poem takes liberties with the positions or relies on a contested interpretation—for instance, whether pragmatic reasons are best understood as reasons of a different kind rather than the wrong kind, or whether transparency entails that there are no wrong-kind reasons for belief at all. In that note, distinguish what is broadly settled (that there is a meaningful distinction between reasons that engage an attitude's constitutive aim and those that do not) from what remains genuinely contested (whether belief has exactly one such aim, and whether practical stakes can ever constitute reasons for belief rather than only causes of it). Do not present any contested view as the consensus.
