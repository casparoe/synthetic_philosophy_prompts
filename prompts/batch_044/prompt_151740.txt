I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a hospital whose charter requires that, when requested, continuity questions be assessed through Buddhist anatta rather than Western psychological-identity theories. Our committee—meeting without a Buddhist scholar present, with about thirty minutes per case—must repeatedly decide whether an advance directive or prior consent still binds a patient who has undergone radical cognitive or personality change (advanced dementia, severe traumatic brain injury, long-term psychiatric restructuring).

Please write a reusable decision procedure my committee can run in order: the observable clinical questions to ask, what counts as sufficient evidence for each, where the procedure stops with "the prior directive binds," where it stops with "it does not bind," and where it must hand off to human judgment because the issue is values, not assessment of aggregate change. Ground the criteria in the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness—translated into clinical indicators, and include explicit misfire warnings where the procedure will likely yield a counter-intuitive result. State clearly what the procedure cannot do.

Then apply it to three cases: (1) an Alzheimer's patient who retains bodily habits and emotional warmth but no longer recognizes family or remembers the directive, with family insisting it be honored; (2) a post-TBI patient who remembers signing the directive but now holds opposite values, with intact reasoning, and family asking that it be overridden; (3) a late-stage dementia patient who appears content and shows no suffering, but whose prior directive refused artificial nutrition in precisely this state.

In one case the procedure should yield an answer that conflicts with the family's preference; flag that conflict and note the residual moral question the procedure cannot settle. Finally, separate what in your framework is a relatively settled interpretation of anatta from what is genuinely contested among Buddhist philosophers and clinical ethicists, and do not present any contested reading as the consensus view.
