I host a podcast in the rationalist-adjacent philosophy of technology space, and my next guest is an unnamed researcher defending a specific refinement of the competitiveness-constraint argument about AI alignment. Rather than the familiar claim that safety measures impose a performance cost firms won't bear, he defends what he calls the epistemic-reversal thesis: that alignment taxes do not merely reduce capability but systematically degrade a firm's strategic self-knowledge, while competitors who forgo interpretation gain not just speed but a form of organizational black-box fluency—the ability to coordinate around outputs they cannot fully explain. He further claims this creates an arbitrage dynamic in which risk is outsourced to subcontractors who bear the tax indirectly, leaving the primary firm technically aligned but structurally coupled to unaligned subsystems.

I want ten interview questions that avoid asking him to restate the thesis or to recommend papers. Instead, they should press where the literature actually presses. I want at least one question on whether the epistemic-reversal effect is an empirical regularity or a conceptual feature of interpretability—specifically, whether a modular, fully audited system could in principle recover competitive parity through verification speed rather than opacity—and a follow-up keyed to his likely reply that verification latency scales non-linearly with system complexity. I want at least one question that forces him to distinguish organizational black-box fluency from ordinary tacit knowledge, with a follow-up that asks whether his distinction survives the objection that all high-performance engineering involves irreducible tacit coordination. I want one question on the subcontracting-arbitrage mechanism: if risk can be outsourced so cleanly, does the thesis collapse into a standard regulatory-capture argument rather than a deep structural constraint? For each question, include the anticipated standard reply and a second-order follow-up designed to make that reply do more work. Keep the tone appropriate for an audience familiar with the alignment-tax literature but not with this particular refinement. No generic biographical openers, and no requests for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
