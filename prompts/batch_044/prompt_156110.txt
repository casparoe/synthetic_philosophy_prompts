I'm taking a graduate seminar on long-range forecasting that treats the "outside view" as the only legitimate method for institutional durability: we classify polities into structural reference classes visible ex ante—ignoring narratives about leadership quality, cultural values, or contingent events—and then apply base-rate survival curves. Our instructor has applied this framework consistently across five cases, always refusing inside-view adjustments. In 395 CE she assigned the Western Roman Empire to a continental-bureaucratic-declining class and forecast a median remaining institutional lifespan of roughly 80 years (it dissolved in the West by 476); in the same year she placed the Eastern Roman Empire in a maritime-bureaucratic-resilient class and forecast about 950 years (it endured until 1453). In 1750 she placed the Qing Dynasty in a continental-bureaucratic-standardized class and forecast roughly 160 years despite its continental exposure (it fell in 1912, about 162 years later). In 1648 she assigned the Holy Roman Empire to a confederal-elective-sacral class and forecast about 150 years (it was dissolved in 1806, roughly 158 years later). And in 1900 she placed the Austro-Hungarian Empire in a multi-ethnic-federal-dynastic class and forecast roughly 20 years (it collapsed in 1918, about 18 years later).

The case we must now resolve is the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1650, at the height of its territorial reach but just before the Deluge: a continental polity with an elective monarchy, a powerful noble legislature (the Sejm), decentralized provincial administration, no standardized civil-service examination, yet a relatively homogeneous Catholic noble core and defensible interior lines. Predict which reference class she would assign it to, what median lifespan she would forecast, and which of her five earlier verdicts is the closest analog. Then identify at least two plausible structural rules—one weighting geographic exposure and defensive coherence, another weighting administrative standardization or succession institutionalization—that both accommodate her five prior judgments but diverge sharply on this 1650 case, stating precisely what each rule predicts for the Commonwealth's durability. Give your best-guess synthesis with explicit reasoning, and specify the single further historical or counterfactual case that would best adjudicate between these competing rules.
