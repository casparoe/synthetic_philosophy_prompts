Construct a thought experiment involving a naval commander who, at 06:00, must decide whether to deploy her fleet before a potential enemy strike at noon, engineered so that three positions on Aristotle's sea-battle problem deliver three distinct practical verdicts despite identical evidence and causal conditions. Specifically: (1) an Aristotelian non-bivalentist holds that the proposition that the enemy will strike at noon currently lacks a truth-value, so deployment is rational not as a response to a fixed fact but as an open-future action that participates in constituting the outcome; (2) an Ockhamist actualist holds that the proposition is already determinately true or false in the actual world, but because the commander is epistemically isolated from which, she should deploy on standard expected-utility grounds; and (3) a logical fatalist holds that this present determinacy renders deployment either redundant (if the strike is true) or futile (if false), recommending inaction. The scenario must be realistic (no omniscient observers, no explicit branching-world metaphysics available to the agent), confined to one paragraph, and must include the operational detail that the enemy's attack decision occurs at 09:00 using intelligence that physically cannot detect the 06:00 deployment until 14:00, thereby isolating the divergence to each theory's assignment of present truth-value rather than to strategic causation or risk preference. After describing the case, explain what each theory says the commander ought to do and why, identify the precise interaction between temporal-causal isolation and bivalence commitment that forces the fatalist's inaction, state how the case changes if that isolation is removed (e.g., if deployment could deter the enemy), and present how the fatalist would defend her verdict against the charge that it collapses practical reasoning.
