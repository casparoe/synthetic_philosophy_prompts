I'm facilitating citizens' assemblies for regional climate-adaptation boards, and we keep running into the boundary problem — like, who actually belongs to the demos for a given decision? We get proposals to include people living just outside the watershed, seasonal workers in employer housing, remote workers who pay taxes elsewhere, and even corporate entities with local assets. Right now our inclusion calls are basically vibes-based, which is embarassing.

I'm trying to operationalize a hybrid principle I sketched out after reading some deliberative-democracy stuff: for decision D, you belong to the demos if (a) you're either subjected to D's binding authority or significantly affected by D's outcomes, AND (b) you're within the "scope of reciprocal justification" — meaning you can understand and actually contest the reasons offered in the deliberation, given your real epistemic position, not just in principle. The idea is that being affected isn't enough if the decision requires technical knowledge you don't have access to and can't be given in time; but being subjected is always sufficient regardless of epistemic access, since authority over you demands justification.

Please write a decision proceedure my staff can run on a membership proposal: the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a clear "no," and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact. Then apply it to three cases:
1. A permanent remote worker who lives in the watershed, works for an out-of-state company, pays no local taxes, and has no family ties here.
2. A seasonal agricultural worker present four months a year, living in employer-provided housing inside the jurisdiction, who speaks limited English and has no access to the technical hydrology reports.
3. A downstream municipality outside our jurisdiction that will suffer increased flooding risk from upstream retention ponds we're planning.

One of these should come out in a direction that will annoy someone — maybe the remote worker gets excluded despite being physically present, or the worker gets included despite language barriers — and I want the proceedure to flag that tension explicitly rather than smoothing it over. Also say clearly what this proceedure can't do: for example, whether future generations should get proxy seats, or whether non-human animals affected by habitat loss should have representatives. Keep it practical — no reading lists, no "see Dahl 1989," my team has ten minutes per proposal and zero philosophy background. Just the checklist, the three runs, and an honest list of misfire points.
