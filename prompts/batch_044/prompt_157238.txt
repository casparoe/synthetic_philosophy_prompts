I've been thinking about that old story where Zhuangzi wakes up from being a butterfly and genuinely cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it is a man. I tried to turn that into a stricter chain of reasoning, but it seems to devour far more than I intended—it ends up undermining the idea that I am the same person from one minute to the next. Here is the argument as I wrote it down:

(1) When Zhuangzi wakes, he has no internal feature of his present experience—no particular feeling, image, or quality of awareness—that distinguishes being the awakened man from being the butterfly who has just begun to dream.
(2) If two competing explanations cover every feature of a subject's present experience with equal completeness, then there is no rational ground to prefer one, and consequently there is no determinate fact about which is true.
(3) Now apply this to my own case. The explanation that I will persist as the same continuing consciousness five minutes from now covers every feature I will then experience. But so does what I will call the instantaneous-replacement hypothesis: that a numerically new consciousness will arise in this body, equipped with false memories, matching neural patterns, and an identical sense of continuity, while the present me simply ceases.
(4) By the reasoning in (2), there is therefore no fact of the matter about whether I persist across even a brief interval.
(5) If there is no fact about persistence across minutes, then there is no stable subject who can be held responsible for past actions, be the rightful owner of future goods, or be bound by promises made earlier.
(6) Yet denying all of that is practically unavoidable; we organize our lives and moral relations around it.
(7) So one of the steps must be wrong.

I want to hold onto (1) and (6) if I can, and I am willing to pay some price elsewhere, but I cannot see which link breaks. Is the error in (2), where I slide from "no available evidence to choose" to "no metaphysical fact of the matter"? Or is it in (3), where I treat the replacement hypothesis as genuinely on a par with continuity, when in fact it requires an unexplained coincidence that continuity does not? Or does the problem lie in applying a dream-parable to ordinary waking time-intervals at all? I would like to know which premise is least costly to reject, why that premise looked plausible at first, and what giving it up actually commits me to—especially whether I can reject (2) without letting metaphysics float entirely free of any evidential constraint.
