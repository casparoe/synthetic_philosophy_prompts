I'm constructing a taxonomy of positions on whether and why original artworks retain a value that perfect perceptual duplicates lack, for a graduate seminar in aesthetics. Please build a decision tree whose root is the question: "Does an original artwork possess value that an indistinguishable forgery cannot have?" Each branch should split on a concrete diagnostic claim—phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing the philosophical labels—rather than on named theories.

The tree must accommodate at least these leaves: (1) pure aestheticism, which denies any surplus value; (2) causal-historical essentialism, which requires a direct causal link to the artist's intentional act; (3) an institutional or recognition-based view, which locates value in art-world status that forgeries cannot acquire; (4) a restoration-tolerant causal view, which permits extensive material replacement provided a continuous causal chain to the original creative act survives; and (5) any genuinely unoccupied combination in that logical space, marked "unnamed/unexplored" with a brief diagnosis of why it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely overlooked.

The branching nodes should cover four concrete issues: whether perceptual identity is sufficient for artistic value; whether direct manual involvement by the named artist is necessary, or whether mediated production (studio assistants, foundry casting, digital fabrication) breaks the causal chain; whether a restoration replacing roughly ninety-five percent of the original substrate destroys originality; and whether a work algorithmically generated posthumously—trained exclusively on the complete corpus of a deceased artist and executed without human hand—counts as an original, a forgery, or something else. For each occupied leaf, state the view's most awkward commitment: the implausible consequence it must accept. For any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate using only these nodes, flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than papering over it.

The audience is philosophy graduate students; keep the tone formal and analytic. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on verification of specific texts, dates, or named scholars.
