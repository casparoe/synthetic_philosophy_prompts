I'm planning a ninety-minute seminar for final-year philosophy students who have covered basic decision theory but not collective-action problems. I want to teach the unilateralist's curse: the structural problem in which any single member of a group can take an action that produces diffuse, irreversible costs and localised benefits, so that actors with the lowest belief-thresholds for net benefit act first, yielding a collective outcome that a majority might have rejected had they been able to coordinate, without any individual having acted irrationally. Do not explain the concept to me; plan the explanation. Which dependency must be secured first — the distinction between individual rationality and collective choice procedures, or the idea of heterogeneous belief thresholds? Choose one carrying example that runs through the session; I am weighing a scenario in which one municipality unilaterally deploys gene-drive mosquitoes to suppress local malaria transmission, while adjacent regions bear ecological risks they never authorised. Assess whether this example carries the structural logic of the curse or whether it collapses it into a free-rider or tragedy-of-the-commons problem. Lay out the exact sequence of ideas, noting what each step presupposes. Map the misconceptions these students will predictably form — I anticipate they will read it as a variant of the prisoner's dilemma, or suppose that perfect information-sharing resolves the asymmetry — and specify at which point in the sequence each misconception should be defused. Provide one closing question that reveals whether they understand the threshold asymmetry itself rather than merely the bioethical surface of the example. Finally, say explicitly which element of the classic framing — the proposed principle of conformity — should be set aside at this level and why it would distract from the structural point.
