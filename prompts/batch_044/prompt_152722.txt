I'm putting together a closing piece for a philosophy-of-mind reading group, and I'd like a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets plus a closing quatrain, with two refrains in an ABA rhyme scheme—to engage illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. The philosophical content needs to be precise, not just namedropped: the view is that there are no intrinsic qualitative properties ("what it's like"), only functional and representational states that systematically misrepresent themselves as possessing phenomenal character. I want the form to carry the argument. One refrain should capture the introspective conviction (something like "there is something it is like to be here"), and the other the illusionist correction ("there is only the seeming, not the being"). The compulsive return of these lines should mirror how introspection keeps regenerating the illusion every time it checks—specifically, invoke the refrigerator-light analogy, where consciousness only appears continuously present because the act of inspecting activates the representational mechanism. At the volta in the final quatrain, let the refrains collide or shift rather than resolve, leaving open whether introspection has caught itself in the act or merely turned on another bulb. Don't sermonize about intervention or shrug with "it's all an illusion"; keep the tension intact, as our discussion ended. The poem should be recognizable to someone familiar with illusionist arguments (for instance, the treatment of phenomenal concepts as misrepresentational) and still stand as verse for someone who isn't. Please write in American English, about nineteen lines, with no rhyme scheme beyond the villanelle's ABA.
