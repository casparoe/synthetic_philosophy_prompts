A number of jurisdictions have recently adopted what policy documents call "mandatory rest accounting." Under this framework, employers must record not only hours worked but also each employee's "rest deficit"—the gap between statutory leisure entitlement and leisure actually taken. Should the deficit exceed a quarterly threshold, the firm faces an escalating levy, while the worker receives a "rest subsidy" redeemable only through verified non-work providers such as travel cooperatives or community arts collectives. Concurrently, workers who maintain productivity above a specified percentile may "bank" surplus leisure credits toward extended sabbaticals, provided their performance remains stable. The scheme is presented as correcting both the Protestant-derived moralization of overwork and the Japanese karoshi phenomenon, while preserving the efficiency logic behind the 35-hour week. Yet it also treats leisure not as mere absence from labour but as a measurable, redeemable, and even capitalizable good.

What philosophical commitments about work, leisure, rights, and human flourishing does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—particularly regarding whether leisure is an instrumental good, a constitutive element of autonomy, or a form of social capital—distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify internal tensions (for instance, between the coercive enforcement of rest and the banking of leisure as a reward for productivity), and say which features of the practice would have to be abandoned if its underlying view were replaced by a straightforwardly anti-work or purely libertarian conception of free time.
