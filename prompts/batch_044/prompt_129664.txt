Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group — mostly philosophers of mind and decision theorists, with no capacity to run large-scale training experiments — that has secured three years of funding to work on the conceptual foundations of corrigibility and the shutdown problem. They want a defended agenda, not a survey: which open conceptual questions should they treat as upstream priorities, in what order, and which should be deprioritised because resolving them would not substantially alter downstream thinking about alignment architecture? For each priority, explain why it sits upstream of other lines of work, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than machine-learning researchers, and what kind of conceptual or formal result over the next couple of years would cause you to revise its rank. I am particularly interested in how you weigh the tension between two partly niche distinctions: first, between corrigibility of an agent’s goal-representation versus corrigibility of its world-model (and whether these can come apart in ways that matter for shutdown behaviour), and second, between conceiving the shutdown problem as a failure of instrumental reasoning versus as a failure of value alignment under ontological uncertainty. Please rank the main open questions — including, if you judge them relevant, the status of reflective stability for corrigible preferences, the conditions under which an agent can recognise its own shutdown as non-catastrophic, and whether corrigibility requires some form of transparent self-model — and defend the ordering with explicit criteria (upstream dependency, philosophical tractability, and potential to change design assumptions). Throughout, separate what is broadly settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any contested framing as though it were the consensus. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want an agenda whose priorities are justified.
