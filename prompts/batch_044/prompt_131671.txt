I'm preparing a proposal for a six-month postgraduate research fellowship in moral philosophy. I want to work on standing to blame and hypocrisy, but I want to avoid the well-trodden question of whether hypocrites forfeit all standing. Instead, my half-formed idea is that the debate treats "standing to blame" as a single status, when it should be split into at least three dimensions: epistemic standing (being positioned to recognise the wrong), normative standing (being entitled to hold the wrongdoer to account without self-undermining authority), and affective standing (the legitimacy of expressing moral resentment or indignation). Hypocrisy, I suspect, undermines these dimensions selectively and asymmetrically depending on whether the hypocrisy is in-domain (the hypocrite commits the same wrong) or cross-domain (they commit a different but comparable wrong), and whether their blame is directed upward, downward, or horizontally.

Please turn this into a formal proposal of about two pages that includes: (1) a precise statement of the research question that makes it determinately answerable; (2) why the existing literature on standing and hypocrisy leaves this open—specifically, why treating standing as unitary obscures the selective effects of hypocrisy; (3) the method: how I would analyse the three dimensions, construct a taxonomy of hypocrisy cases (private versus public, same-domain versus cross-domain, upward versus downward versus horizontal), and test whether our intuitions about standing track the dimensions separately through structured thought experiments; (4) what a positive result would look like (for example, a principled mapping of which dimensions are undermined in which cases) and what would count as failure (for example, the dimensions collapse into one, or intuitions turn out to be noisy and unprincipled); (5) a realistic scope for six months, with months 1–2 devoted to conceptual mapping, months 3–4 to case analysis and intuition-testing, and months 5–6 to synthesis and writing, together with an honest assessment of what might make the project too ambitious for the timeframe; and (6) a brief statement of why the answer matters for the broader theory of moral responsibility, without appealing to any specific policy application.

Write the proposal in British English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations—I will add those myself. Do not rely on references to highly specialised theories or obscure primary texts that would require verification; work with the conceptual framework outlined above.
