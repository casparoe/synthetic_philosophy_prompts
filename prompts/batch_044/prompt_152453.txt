As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been struck by how multi-stakeholder system design resolves conflicts among genuinely incommensurable objectives—not by discovering a universal metric, but by negotiating trade-offs across distinct modules. This led me to accept a philosophical position I want to stress-test: that moral compromise between fundamentally different value systems operates analogously to economic gains-from-trade. On this view, parties whose core commitments are genuinely plural—say, one treating individual autonomy as lexically prior and another treating aggregate welfare as the sole criterion—need not converge on a shared currency of value. Instead, they can exchange concessions in separate domains, with each yielding ground where it cares least and gaining protection where it cares most, producing an equilibrium that is Pareto-superior relative to non-cooperation for both sides. I take this not as mere pragmatism but as carrying normative weight: value pluralism is best respected not by forcing theoretical convergence but by allowing structured, domain-specific exchange. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including conceptual, empirical, and normative objections. Does the trade analogy secretly smuggle in a consequentialist calculus that undermines the pluralism it claims to defend? If values are truly incommensurable, is there even a coherent sense in which both parties are "better off" after compromise, or does the comparison collapse? How should I weigh integrity-based objections—that compromising parts of one's moral commitments corrupts rather than preserves them—alongside work on taboo or sacred values that resist any form of exchange? Are there hidden power asymmetries that make "moral trade" a euphemism for domination by the stronger party rather than mutual gain? I also want to know which objections a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as damaging and which they would dismiss, and whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability.
