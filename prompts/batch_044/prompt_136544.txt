I am a novelist and serve as one of the literary trustees of the Crane Foundation, which holds the papers of the late author Alistair Crane. Next Thursday we must vote on the fate of a trunk recently recovered from a demolished house in Dorset: approximately four hundred pages of juvenilia, private letters, and unfinished fragments that reveal an early, quite fervent commitment to political and aesthetic positions Crane spent his mature career repudiating. The board is divided. Our chief archivist advocates what she calls a "life-giving critical edition" — every contradiction, failure, and embarrassing enthusiasm should be annotated without mercy and published in full, on the grounds that to hide these documents is to burden living writers with a false monument to genius and to deny them the liberating judgement that the past can be overcome. A second faction, composed chiefly of Crane's former pupils and literary executors, argues for a strictly monumental presentation: a carefully curated exhibition and a selective volume of only the finest fragments, maintaining that artistic culture depends upon inspiring examples and that to expose the raw disorder of a developing mind is to weaken the very aspiration that sustains craft. A smaller caucus urges pure antiquarian preservation — complete digitisation, chronological ordering, and absolute interpretive silence — insisting that our duty is to the record itself, not to any present use we might make of it.

I find myself caught between these positions, and I am conscious that my own novel-in-progress — which concerns an archivist who discovers that the past she is cataloguing begins to reshape her present — has made me unusually sensitive to the question of whether history serves life or suffocates it. I have been discussing the matter with friends who know nothing of philosophy, yet they keep returning to the same worry: that if we publish everything without interpretation, we risk turning Crane into a mere curiosity; if we curate only the triumphant fragments, we risk idolatry; and if we judge the past too severely, we may sever the very continuity that makes judgement meaningful. Before Thursday's meeting I must circulate a short written opinion recommending a concrete course of action — with a clear time frame for implementation — that takes each of these three positions seriously rather than dismissing them. The opinion should not merely restate our legal obligations as trustees; it should engage with the strongest argument on the side I ultimately reject, and it should offer reasoning that the opposing factions could respond to rather than simply overrule. Would you please draft this memo for me? I should be grateful for a formal, polished document in British English that I can adapt and sign.
