i sit on our city's heritage and secularism review board, which sounds fancier than it is. we're deciding whether to approve a restoration grant for the old chapel on mill road — it's municipaly owned now and used mostly for chamber music, but the roof's falling in. the grant comes from this new "dual-stream" local tax we piloted last year: residents tick either "religious heritage" or "secular arts" on their municipal tax form, and the pool for religious heritage is what'd cover about 80% of the repair. the board's split. some say accepting the money violates state neutrality because the building still has a consecrated altar in the back room even if nobody uses it for worship. others say neutrality means we can't discriminate against funding just because the taxpayers who chose that box happen to be religious, especially since the venue is open to everyone. there's also a weird middle argument that the altar itself should be treated as a museum artifact but left physically intact, which creates this strange category of "inactive sacred infrastrcture" that our secularism rules never contemeplated.

please draft the memo i'll circulate before next tuesday's vote. recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a clear timeline for the altar situation. engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly — i don't want it to sound like i'm just restating the legal definition of secularism. also address whether the dual-stream tax model actually solves the church-state problem or just moves the entanglement one step back into the taxpayer's choice.
