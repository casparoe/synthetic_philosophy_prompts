I have recently taken a role on an ethics review board tasked with weighing diffuse, microscopic harms — for instance, a manufacturing process that imposes barely perceptible discomfort on billions — against localized catastrophic failures. In working through the framing, I have come to defend a specific intermediate thesis on the aggregation of tiny harms: that moral weight does not scale continuously with the number of affected subjects, but instead exhibits a discontinuous phase boundary at a particular magnitude — roughly, when the affected population exceeds the total number of discrete conscious moments available to a single human lifespan, multiplied by an order of magnitude — below which aggregation is morally inert and above which the accumulated harm competes directly with severe individual injuries such as torture. This is neither the strict lexical view that one torture always outweighs any number of dust specks, nor the linear aggregation view that numbers eventually dominate. I want an honest map of what would count specifically against this discontinuity thesis. Which arguments, intuitions about scale, formal analogies from decision theory, or policy-relevant cases would show that such a boundary is either arbitrary (movable without theoretical cost) or collapses into one of the neighboring positions? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what defenders of continuous aggregation versus defenders of strict lexical priority would themselves concede as damaging to my view rather than to their own. Finally, assess whether the thesis risks unfalsifiability — for example, by allowing the transition point to be adjusted post hoc in response to counterexamples — and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a position intended to guide real regulatory judgments.
