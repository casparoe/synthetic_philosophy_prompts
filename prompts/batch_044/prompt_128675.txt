My study group has this argument about the modern Stoic revival and I can't find the exit. (1) The core modern teaching is the dichotomy of control: what's up to you (judgments, impulses) is all that matters, and everything else should be met with complete indifference. (2) Modern practicioners extend this to emotional reactions — if grief, anger, or fear depend partly on externals, you should reframe the judgment so the emotion doesn't take root. (3) But here's where it gets weird: if I judge that a policy is unjust, that judgment is technically inside my control, while the policy itself is outside. (4) Stoic epistemology says we ought to assent only to true impressions, so I should assent to "this policy is unjust" if it's true. (5) At the same time, since the policy is outside my control, I should be indiferent to whether it changes, which means I should also be indiferent to whether I maintain the judgment that it's wrong — maintaining the judgment doesn't affect the policy. (6) So I have a rational obligation to assent to the truth, and a rational obligation to be indiferent to whether I do so. (7) These don't technically conflict unless assenting carries some practical commitment, but modern Stoics say it doesn't — you can hold the judgment without passion or action. (8) That means the ideal Stoic can fully recognize injustice, feel nothing, and do nothing, which seems like quietism dressed up as wisdom. (9) So either the modern revival secretly requires political passivity, or there's a hidden line between judgments about values and judgments about externals that the dichotomy doesn't capture. I want to keep (1) and (4), but something between (5) and (7) feels slippery, like I'm recieveing a hidden premise I didn't sign up for. Which step do you reject, and what's the cost? Also, say what would change your mind — like what consideration or argument would flip which step you think is wrong, and where is your own reasoning here shakiest?
