I keep running into this line in my AI ethics seminar — 'your true preferances are just your actual preferances made coherant with each other and with the facts.' People throw it around like it's obvious that coherence cleans up messiness, but I think it's hiding about four different theses. One is just the banal claim that contradictory wants can't all be satisfied at once. Another sounds like a psychological claim about what we'd want if we knew more and weren't tired. A third looks like a normative claim that coherence is constitutive of rational agency itself. And there's a fourth, weirder one that treats coherence as an identity condition across modal neighbors — your 'ideal self' isn't just you-better-informed, it's the endpoint of extrapolating your preference graph into some kind of stable fixed point.

Can you lay out these precise readings — or however many distinct ones you actually see — and say for each whether it's a platitude, a substantive empirical guess, or a metaphysical commitment? I'm especially interested in which version actually does the work in the CEV-style argument that we should build systems that optimize for what we'd want if we were smarter and more patient, rather than what we want right now. And please, no bibliography — I just want the menu of meanings with verdicts on which ones are false, which are defensable, and which one the people quoting the slogan in seminar are probably smuggling in when they say 'just make it coherant.'
