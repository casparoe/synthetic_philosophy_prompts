Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 students in a logic or philosophy of mathematics course that makes them feel the force of the strategy-stealing assumption without naming it in advance. Invent a minimal, symmetric, perfect-information two-player game played with paper tokens—explicitly not Hex, Chomp, or any standard textbook example—where the rules are identical for both players except that one moves first, and where there is exactly one harmless way to waste an opening move. Structure three phases: (1) 20 minutes of paired silent play with win/loss recording only, so students observe that the first player never seems disadvantaged but are given no explanation; (2) 35 minutes of guided worksheet construction in which groups must assume, for contradiction, that the second player possesses a winning strategy S, then articulate step-by-step how the first player could make an arbitrary legal opening, discard it, and execute S, reaching the contradiction themselves; (3) 25 minutes of a "preference auction" in which students bid hypothetical points to choose first or second, followed by a debrief that maps each worksheet step onto symmetry, the dummy move, and the non-constructive conclusion. The debrief should be frank about what the activity reproduces (the counterintuitive feeling that an extra legal option cannot be harmful) and what it cannot reproduce (the actual winning strategy, plus real games in which an extra token can obstruct a critical line). Include an honest note on the predictable failure mode—some pairs will derive an explicit first-player strategy and miss the point of non-constructivity—and how the facilitator should redirect them. No up-front lecture; do not name the argument or cite canonical games until the debrief.
