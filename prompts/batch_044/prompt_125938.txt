A philosophy blog summarizes indirect normativity — the view that normative reasons are mediated through an idealized deliberative standpoint rather than applied directly to particular acts — in three steps. First, it claims the view is restricted to moral reasons alone, since only morality involves a gap between our actual reasoning and a corrected, fully informed version of it; prudential and epistemic norms, by contrast, are said to apply directly. Second, it defines the idealized standpoint as requiring not just full information and logical consistency, but total emotional detachment, so that any motivation rooted in affection, loyalty, or compassion is automatically excluded from the idealized assessment. Third, it presents the standard collapse objection as decisive: once you ask what your idealized self would advise about this very situation, you have merely added an intermediate step to direct evaluation, making the indirect/direct distinction psychologically interesting but normatively empty. The blogger then concludes that defenders can only avoid this collapse by denying that idealized agents have any first-personal access to particular circumstances, which strikes him as implausible. I am inclined to think the emotional-detachment point is overstated and that the collapse objection is being treated as a refutation rather than a challenge to be answered. Is this summary fair? Take a definite position: identify which parts are accurate representations, which are subtle conflations or overstatements, and whether the blogger's conclusion survives once those are corrected; in particular, say whether serious defenders of indirect normativity really require emotional detachment, and whether the collapse objection genuinely empties the view of normative content.
