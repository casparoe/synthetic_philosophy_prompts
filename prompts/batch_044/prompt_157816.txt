I have recently returned to Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's defense of women's intellectual life, not merely as a historical artifact but as a living framework for thinking about access to knowledge. Her insistence that the denial of education to women rests on custom rather than nature, and her advocacy for study through direct engagement with texts rather than mediated authority, have become personally urgent for me as I observe how modern educational platforms deploy algorithmic personalization. If Sor Juana were alive today and confronted with adaptive learning systems that use predictive student models to shape curricula—systems that promise democratization yet operate through opaque recommendation architectures—how might she extend her critique? Would she see these mechanisms as fulfilling the scholarly autonomy she defended, or as a technologically refined version of the ecclesiastical gatekeeping she resisted, substituting algorithmic profiling for clerical prohibition?

More specifically, I wonder whether her commitment to public disputation and visible intellectual labor would lead her to distrust the substitution of human pedagogical judgment with automated tracking that sorts learners invisibly. Could she draw a structural analogy between the silencing strategies she encountered in colonial religious institutions and the exclusionary patterns embedded in data-driven pathways that steer women away from advanced study without explicit prohibition? I am asking not for archival documentation but for a conceptual projection: given her arguments about the mind's capacity for direct knowledge of natural and philosophical truths, which features of contemporary algorithmic education would she accept as instruments of liberation, and which would she reject as disguised constraints on the very rational agency she sought to defend?
