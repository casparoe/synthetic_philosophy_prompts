Consider the institutional practice—now adopted by several large computing consortia—of subjecting jobs that run recurrent architectures with persistent internal state across sessions to a mandatory moral-overhead review, while exempting feedforward inference jobs of comparable energy use, parameter scale, and behavioral output, even when both process identical sensory streams. The policy treats the temporal continuity of computation, rather than observable behavior, resource consumption, or architectural size, as the decisive boundary for ethical consideration. What philosophical commitments about the attribution of sentience, the moral significance of computation, and the boundaries of moral status does this practice presuppose? Must it assume a functionalist view on which sustained information integration is sufficient for interests, or can it be reconciled with a biological-substrate theory? Is it consistent with the same institutions' refusal to extend review to static databases that store identical state representations without active recurrence? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what the protocol would have to change if one replaced the underlying view with one that grounds moral status solely in phenomenal experience or in ecological relational role.
