Self-deception is genuinely possible once we reject the assumption of a unified mental agent. The classic paradox—knowing p while convincing oneself of not-p—only arises within a Cartesian theater where all mental states are globally accessible. In the partner case, the agent receives credible evidence E but never forms the explicit belief that the partner is unfaithful. Instead, E remains in a subdoxastic state that generates anxiety without entering the doxastic system. Meanwhile, the agent explicitly believes that the partner is loyal. Because these occupy different functional roles—one subdoxastic, one doxastic—there is no contradiction in a single global belief box.

This is essentially Davidson's partitioned-mind solution: the mind is divided into rational subsystems with independent access conditions, so one subsystem can retain E while another holds the denial, without either violating logical coherence. However, Davidson also insists that this division is not an intentional strategy but an irrational defense mechanism. The agent does not choose to partition; the partition happens as a structural failure of integration. Therefore, if deception requires an intentional act of hiding, Davidson's model actually eliminates self-deception—it becomes mere irrational division rather than a reflexive deceptive act. Furthermore, because the subdoxastic evidence is unavailable for rational revision, the agent cannot be said to know the truth in any epistemically relevant sense; she merely possesses subpersonal information that lacks doxastic status. Consequently, either self-deception requires intentional compartmentalization—which Davidson denies—or it collapses into non-intentional irrationality. Partitioned models solve the logical problem but destroy the reflexive structure that makes the phenomenon genuinely self-deceptive.
