I'm running a philosophy workshop for people who've been around rationalist forums - about 10 people, 90 minutes, mostly grad students but not all philosophers. We're digging into the dirty hands problem, but I want them to feel an institutional version that's a bit more in the weeds than the usual "should the leader lie" framing. Specifically, I'm thinking about cases where the moral cost is split across three seperate roles - the decider who authorizes, the executor who carries it out, and the denier who keeps the public record clean - and where the system depends on each role not knowing, or at least not being able to say, exactly what the others did. The twist I want to inject is the idea of a moral ledger that doesn't net out: each role can point to their own clean hands, but there's leftover residue that belongs to the system itself, not to any individual, and it can't be cancelled by the good outcome.

Design a runnable in-room activity using only paper, pens, and maybe some tokens. Give them the three roles with rules that force coordination without explicit acknowledgment - like they have to pass messages that are technically true but collectively misleading. Use a payoff scheme with small real stakes (maybe points that buy the group a snack, but distributed so that the decider gets the benefit, the executor takes a hit, and the denier gets neutrality) so they feel the asymmetry. Include a timeline for 90 minutes, a debrief that maps each phase onto the conceptual parts - the split responsibility, the unclaimable residue, and the dependency on silence - and an honest note about which feature this simulation can't reproduce, probably the long-term psychological corruption or the irreversability of actual political violence. Also tell me what will predictably fail - people breaking character to warn each other, or the group refusing to complete the task - and how to handle it without destroying the effect. No pre-reading, no citations, and please don't ask them to produce a bibliography.
