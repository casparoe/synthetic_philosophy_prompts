I'm a third-year philosophy student running a discussion section for first-year interdisciplinary seminar students who have never taken social ontology. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for leading a fifty-minute session on collective intentionality and joint action. The students arrive thinking that "doing something together" just means individual people aligning their private goals, so the opening move needs to make the conceptual problem visible before any technical vocabulary appears. I want the plan structured around dependency: which idea has to be in place before the distinction between a shared we-intention and a bundle of coordinated I-intentions can even make sense, and in what order the pieces should unfold. For the carrying example, I want a single concrete scenario that involves clear role differentiation but no central director, and—crucially—one in which each participant genuinely lacks an individual mental representation of the complete end state, so that if each were operating only with an I-intention the activity would necessarily fail; it should not be the standard "walking together" case. I also need the misconception map: I'm expecting they will conflate collective intentionality with group consciousness, then with strategic cooperation, and possibly with mere behavioral synchronization—tell me exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted and defused. Include one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that distinguishes whether they've understood the irreducibility claim from whether they've just memorized a definition. Finally, specify what parts of the wider debate—say, joint commitment, plural subjecthood, or the relation to distributive versus collective rationality—should be explicitly named and bracketed at this level with a brief note that they exist but need not be resolved here. The audience is skeptical that philosophy can say anything substantive about "just doing stuff together," so the arc should build the need for the concept rather than assuming it.
