I am writing a novel whose central historian proposes that lost national causes—Masada, the Confederacy, Kosovo’s medieval defeat, Dunkirk—persist through what she terms counterfactual loyalty: descendants honor not historical achievement but the ethical world that victory would have realized. In an unnamed research essay I encountered, her view is summarized as holding that this loyalty generates an asymmetric moral framework—locals owe the myth hermeneutic charity, while visitors bear only a negative obligation not to ridicule it within commemorative spaces—and that this distinction avoids relativism by grounding obligations in collective identity rather than in historical truth. The essay then counters that this collapses into moral relativism, since any harmful ideology could claim the same protective structure, and concludes that commemorative ethics and historical denial are therefore inseparable. Before I construct a scene around this exchange, I want an accuracy audit: does the view actually restrict obligations to insiders, or does it extend a thinner duty to outsiders? Is the relativism objection a faithful engagement or a misreading of the identity-based claim? And does the summary conflate the psychological mechanism of the myth with the normative argument about how it ought to be treated?
