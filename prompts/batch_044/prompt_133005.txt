As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have composed some preliminary notes for an essay on Saul Kripke's interpretation of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations. I propose an analogy between the sceptical paradox Kripke articulates and the problem of instrument calibration in metrology: just as a measuring apparatus requires an independent standard to establish whether it is functioning correctly rather than systematically deviating, a speaker applying a rule seems to require an external criterion to distinguish correct application from mere error. I argue that this does not lead to a debilitating scepticism, but rather reveals that rule-following is distributed across a community of practice, analogous to the way measurement standards are established through intersubjective agreement rather than private intuition. To push the comparison further, I suggest that Kripke's sceptical solution bears a structural resemblance to conventionalist theories of measurement, in which the selection of units or reference frames is underdetermined by nature yet constrained by considerations of simplicity and predictive coherence. I am concerned, however, that I may be importing scientific prejudices: scientific conventions are ultimately anchored in empirical predictive success, whereas mathematical or normative rules appear to lack such an external anchor. I should welcome detailed comments on whether this analogy genuinely illuminates the Kripkenstein paradox or merely obscures it, on the accuracy of my characterisation of the sceptical solution, and on whether the comparison collapses once we notice that metrological conventions are revisable in light of new data in ways that fundamental rules of arithmetic or grammar may not be. Please respond in formal British English, and kindly do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I wish to assess the argument on its own merits.
