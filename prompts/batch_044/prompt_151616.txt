I am writing a chapter on modal optimism within a broadly Leibnizian framework—compossibility, law-governed harmony, and the actualization of essences—and I have been testing my advisor’s implicit commitments through a series of hypothetical world-comparisons. So far her verdicts are consistent and sharp. First, she holds that a world of infinite simple substances with minimal perception, perfect harmony, no freedom, and no suffering—governed by a single finitely expressible law—is inferior to a world of large but finite variety in which many substances are rational and free, some suffer local and remediable confusion or moral failure, and the governing laws are simple yet allow genuine contingency, even though the latter contains slightly less total metaphysical perfection. Second, she rejects a world identical to the second except that it contains one substance suffering eternally and irremediably, even if that suffering is the necessary condition for the salvation of infinitely many other rational souls. Third, she maintains that a world with the same total perfection and variety as the second, but distributed among exactly half as many substances—each with double the individual clarity, all actions necessary rather than free, and governed by a law of maximal simplicity—is still worse than the second. I take these three judgments to constrain any interpretation of her view, though they clearly underdetermine the precise principle.

The new comparison I plan to put to her involves two worlds that push in opposite directions. World Epsilon actualizes every possible essence, but sequentially across an infinite temporal progression rather than simultaneously; each epoch is governed by its own maximally simple law, rational agents exist only in those epochs where freedom is compossible with the epoch’s law, and suffering is always local, temporary, and tied to transitions between epochs. World Zeta contains only those essences that are mutually compossible, arranged in a single static hierarchy with no temporal change, no creation or annihilation, and every rational agent existing eternally with perfect freedom and no suffering whatsoever; however, the single law governing Zeta is of such complexity that its complete specification requires an infinite description.

Given her track record, I want to predict her verdict on Epsilon versus Zeta, but I am more interested in the reasoning structure. What are the two or three most plausible rules that jointly accommodate her first three judgments, and what does each rule predict for this new pair? Which rule do you think she is actually running, and what is the strongest reason for preferring it? Finally, if her commitments genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would she be adopting by choosing Epsilon, and what principle by choosing Zeta? To help me design the next test, please also specify a further hypothetical case—one that has not yet been considered—that would best distinguish the leading interpretations from one another. I am not asking for a bibliography or historical exegesis; I want the conceptual architecture that makes her verdicts cohere, together with an honest statement of which prior judgment, if any, each interpretation struggles to accommodate.
