Here's an argument I keep returning to about Gödel's rotating universe and the unreality of time, and I can't tell whether the flaw is subtle or catastrophic. (1) Gödel's rotating universe is an exact solution to Einstein's field equations with a cosmological constant and rotating matter, containing closed timelike curves—worldlines along which an observer can return to an event in their own past. (2) Along such a curve, there are distinct events E and F such that E precedes F on one path and F precedes E on another. (3) If the relation "before" holds symmetrically for any pair, it is not asymmetric, and therefore cannot be the objective ordering relation that distinguishes past from future. (4) Hence, in any physically possible rotating universe, objective temporal succession does not exist; time is not real, as Gödel inferred. (5) But objective causation requires that causes precede their effects in exactly this asymmetric sense. If "before" is not objective, causation is not objective either. (6) Scientific explanation, however, is essentially causal: to explain a phenomenon is to exhibit its causal antecedents. Without objective causation, physics becomes a merely correlational description. (7) Yet Gödel's own derivation of the rotating universe is offered as a causal explanation—the field equations, together with a rotating matter distribution, determine the metric that permits closed timelike curves. (8) If causation is unreal, this derivation cannot be genuinely explanatory; the reasoning that establishes the rotating universe undermines itself. (9) One might block this by saying causation is only locally valid, and the curves involve only global circularity. But if local asymmetry is sufficient for causation and science, then global circularity does not threaten objective time—only a globally linear timeline does, which science never required. (10) That saves causation and explanation, but at the cost of blocking Gödel's philosophical inference: if local temporal order survives, time remains objectively real for all actual observers, and the rotating universe is a topological curiosity rather than a metaphysical refutation of time. (11) So we face a trilemma: either accept (4)-(6) and lose scientific explanation together with Gödel's own argument; or reject (4) by distinguishing local from global order, saving science but making Gödel's conclusion an overreach; or reject (1) by declaring rotating universes physically impossible despite being exact solutions, but then we need a non-ad-hoc principle that rules out solutions to our best equations on metaphysical grounds. Which step is the weak link—between (3) and (4), between (8) and (9), or elsewhere? And whichever premise I surrender, what does it cost: the objectivity of time, the possibility of causal science, or the authority of physical theory? If I have to choose, I would most like to keep both Gödel's reasoning intact and the validity of causal explanation, but I suspect that combination is unstable.
