As a doctoral student in philosophy of science with some training in formal epistemology, I observed the following exchange in our working group on rational disagreement, and I would like an adjudication rather than a general survey. (1) One colleague maintained that Aumann's agreement result entails that two rational agents with genuinely common priors and common knowledge of their posteriors cannot maintain different beliefs about any well-defined event; consequently, any persistent disagreement in scientific practice—say, between climate modelers about the precise value of equilibrium climate sensitivity—must be explained either by hidden private information or by a failure of common knowledge regarding what each party actually believes. (2) Another colleague objected that this overlooks a structural precondition of the theorem: the agents must share the same partition of the state space. She offered a concrete case: Researcher A treats equilibrium climate sensitivity as a continuous parameter on the real line and updates via a Gaussian process prior, while Researcher B, for computational reasons, works with a discretized grid of bins and updates via a categorical prior over those bins. Even if both fully disclose their posteriors and share the same underlying observational data, they can rationally disagree—not because their priors differ in substance, but because their conditioning events belong to different sigma-algebras that are not intertranslatable within a single formal framework. (3) The first colleague responded that this distinction collapses once we embed both representations in a richer meta-model that includes propositions about which formalization is correct; any differential weighting assigned to the continuous representation being appropriate versus the discrete representation being appropriate just is a difference in prior probabilities over that meta-space, which means Aumann's result applies at the meta-level and the disagreement is still prior-driven. (4) The second colleague replied that this meta-embedding begs the question, since neither researcher assigns any positive credence to being in the wrong formal framework—each conditions on the correctness of her own model as a structural given, not as an empirical hypothesis to be weighed—and therefore the disagreement concerns the architecture of reasoning itself, not a divergence in beliefs within a shared architecture.

Please adjudicate each move in turn, rating it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating, with particular attention to whether move (3) successfully dissolves the structural-objection into a prior-objection or whether a stable distinction remains. What, if anything, are both parties failing to notice? I also ask that you state how confident you are in your adjudication overall, and identify specifically the point—likely the legitimacy of the meta-space embedding—about which you are least certain.
