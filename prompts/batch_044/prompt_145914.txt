Write a short story exploring the moral authority of anticipatory self-dissolution in a society that permits assisted dying based not on terminal illness, but on a condition it calls axiological discontinuity—defined as a persistent, irreversible reversal of one's core evaluative commitments, even while cognitive function remains intact. In this world, citizens may file a value-continuity bond specifying that if they later come to hold values diametrically opposed to those they now endorse, an appointed trustee may authorize termination on the grounds that the present signatory, not the future occupant, retains legitimate authority over the body's fate.

Your story should center on a trustee—perhaps a philosopher-physician—who must decide whether a patient's reversal is genuine axiological discontinuity or merely an evolved, legitimate change of heart. Do not resolve the dilemma through a simple medical diagnosis or legal technicality; instead, let the narrative turn on a specific, concrete interaction, such as a shared meal in which a familiar dish is refused for reasons that sound either like principle or like alienation, a misremembered phrase from an old letter, or a dispute over whether loyalty to a dead partner requires maintaining one's old grief or embracing a new joy. Force the trustee to confront whether being the same person in any morally relevant sense survives such a reversal, and ask, without answering conclusively, whether preventing that future self from continuing to live is an act of fidelity or of tyranny.
