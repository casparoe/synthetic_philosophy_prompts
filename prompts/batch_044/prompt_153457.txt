Consider the view, associated with Douglas Hofstadter's work on strange loops, that the first-person self is not a substance localized in a single brain but a high-level pattern that can be instantiated across multiple minds. On this account, when others maintain accurate, running models of me—through memory, understanding, and emotional engagement—the same loop of self-reference genuinely continues in them. The original skull is then one host among potential others, and the death of that particular brain need not entail the cessation of the self, provided the pattern remains actively instantiated elsewhere.

Take this position seriously and ask what would have to be true for it to fail. Which empirical results—concerning, for example, the irreducibility of first-person indexicals, the fragmentation of personality after distributed brain damage, or the failure of intersubjective modeling to preserve narrative continuity—would count as decisive rather than merely awkward? Are there conceptual risks that push the view toward either standard physicalism, if the pattern is reducible to information storage, or toward a non-physical soul, if it is not? What would defenders of the position themselves concede as a genuine defeater—perhaps a proof that a single loop cannot be simultaneously centered in two distinct first-person perspectives without contradiction, or evidence that distributed instantiation always produces competing selves rather than one extended self? Finally, assess whether the view is well insulated: can it absorb counterexamples by retreating to the claim that the pattern was never fully instantiated in the other minds, and is that flexibility a virtue or a methodological weakness? In particular, say whether the view predicts anything distinctive about personal survival, grief, or moral responsibility that rival accounts of identity do not, and whether those predictions are in principle testable without granting outsiders authoritative access to the first-person reports of the distributed subjects.
