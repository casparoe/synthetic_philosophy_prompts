I retired from experimental physics some years ago and have since taken up philosophy of science and decision theory in a more systematic way, mostly through self-study and a small reading group of former colleagues who meet monthly to discuss papers none of us would have touched in our working lives. Last month I sketched an argument that seemed to show that any rational agent applying standard expected-utility reasoning consistently must become paralyzed in ordinary scientific and practical judgment, yet I know the argument must contain a hidden slip because I spent decades making precisely such judgments without paralysis, and I want to locate the exact misstep rather than simply dismiss the whole framework. I present it here with numbered steps, and I ask where the error lies; if I am forced to abandon one premise, I would prefer to keep the claim that rational agents should not assign exactly zero credence to unfalsified logical possibilities, but I want to understand what giving up that claim costs me, and whether there is a middle path involving something like a principle of inductive insulation that excludes structurally disconnected hypotheses from practical reasoning without treating them as impossible. The argument runs as follows. (1) If a hypothesis is not logically contradictory and has not been experimentally falsified, then a rational agent with bounded epistemic resources should assign it a credence strictly greater than zero. (2) For any specific action or belief B, one can construct a logically possible scenario—call it a Pascal configuration—in which an undetectable agent rewards or punishes B by an amount that dwarfs all terrestrial utility, with the reward or punishment contingent precisely on B and on no other feature of the world. (3) Because the scenario in (2) is consistent with all current evidence, it falls under the scope of (1), so its credence, however small, is non-zero. (4) By standard expected-utility calculus, when the utility term is unbounded and the credence is non-zero, the product dominates any competing consideration with bounded utility and comparable or larger credence. (5) Therefore, for every action or belief B, there exist Pascal configurations that dominate the expected-utility calculation, pushing rational agents toward actions designed solely to satisfy these configurations rather than toward ordinary scientific or practical ends. (6) But this implies that rational judgment about B is impossible, since every option is simultaneously paired with an astronomically rewarding and an astronomically punishing configuration that both claim dominance. (7) Rational judgment about B is not impossible; otherwise forty years of laboratory work, peer review, and everyday decision-making would have been systematically irrational. (8) Hence one of the premises must fail. I feel the slide may occur between (3) and (4), involving some hidden assumption about how unbounded utilities aggregate across competing configurations, or perhaps between (5) and (6), involving a quantifier shift that treats separately weighted possibilities as if they jointly command a single decision; but I cannot name the exact misstep, and I would like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it commits me to—particularly whether it forces me into a form of epistemic closure that my scientific training should reject, or into a revision of decision theory itself.
