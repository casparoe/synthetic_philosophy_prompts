Consider the opening framing of Porphyry’s Isagoge, the ancient introduction to Aristotle’s Categories traditionally attributed to Porphyry of Tyre. In it, the author raises the well-known triad about genera and species: whether they subsist in reality or are formed only by the mind; if they do subsist, whether they are corporeal or incorporeal; and, should they be incorporeal, whether they are separated from sensible things or exist within them. He declines to resolve these questions, calling the inquiry too deep and requiring a different kind of investigation.

I am trying to understand whether this framing implicitly presupposes an exhaustive ontological binary: for a universal to be real, it must either be embedded in corporeal sensibles or exist as an incorporeal entity that is either separated from or immanent within them. To probe this, I want to apply a view I will call counterfactual-instantiation structuralism (CISS). On this view, a universal is neither actually instantiated in any particular thing nor separated as an independent substance; instead, it operates as an invariant relational structure that holds across a modal neighborhood of possible worlds, anchoring cross-world similarity without being spatially or ontologically “located” in any of them.

Can you help me parse the passage’s underlying assumptions? Specifically, does Porphyry’s refusal to decide between “separated” and “existing in sensibles” still carry force if one denies the premise that real universals must bear some kind of location—either here among bodies or apart in a non-physical realm? Reconstruct the implicit ontology that makes his triad exhaustive, and then assess whether CISS genuinely escapes that ontology by rejecting location altogether, or whether it merely relocates the problem into modal space without resolving the underlying tension between reality and non-instantiation.
