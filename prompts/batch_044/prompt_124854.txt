I am convening a colloquium session with a political philosopher who advances a qualified defence of lottocracy—specifically, a bicameral model in which an elected legislature shares authority with a citizen assembly selected by lot, the latter exercising exclusive competence over what she calls “structurally opaque” domains of governance, defined as policy areas where causal chains are too complex for meaningful retrospective electoral accountability. She maintains that this division preserves democratic legitimacy because the sortition chamber’s authority rests on statistical mirroring rather than explicit consent, thereby resolving the epistemic deficits of pure representation without collapsing into technocracy. I should like a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of the position. In particular, I want at least one question addressing how statistical representativeness is sustained across staggered, multi-year terms in the face of differential attrition—what we might term representational drift—together with a follow-up anticipating her likely appeal to rotational replacement; one probing whether the boundary between structurally opaque and structurally transparent policy is itself politically constructed, with a follow-up keyed to her probable claim that complexity can be objectively indexed; and one examining the objection that lot-selection does not eliminate elite capture but merely displaces it into the design of stratification criteria and facilitation protocols, with a follow-up pressing on whether transparency about those protocols can ever be democratically ratified without undermining the very insulation the model promises. The questions should be rigorous, mutually responsive, and suitable for an audience of graduate students in political theory.
