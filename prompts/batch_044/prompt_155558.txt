As a novelist, I find myself returning to the question of whether an object or a person remains itself when its matter is replaced or destroyed, and my intuitions cluster in ways I cannot quite articulate. Here are my verdicts.

First, a marble statue is slowly chipped away and restored with new stone over centuries; provided the form and artistic lineage remain continuous, I say it is the same statue. Second, the original clay model used to cast a bronze is destroyed in the casting and never recovered; I say the bronze is a new object constituted by different matter, not the clay transformed. Third, in a narrative I am drafting, a protagonist's body is gradually replaced by synthetic tissue until no original cells remain, yet she retains her memories and social roles; I treat her as the same person. But if her synthetic form is scanned and instantiated elsewhere while she continues living, I feel there are now two claimants, or perhaps the original has been split rather than duplicated. Fourth, an ice sculpture melts into water and is later refrozen into the identical shape in the same room; despite the material being the same water, I judge this closer to the destroyed clay model—an imitation rather than a survivor. Fifth, if a bronze statue is melted down and the very same metal is recast into a different form, I say the original statue is gone, even though the material persists.

These judgments pull in different directions: continuity of form saves identity in the marble case and in the biological replacement case, yet in the ice case form is restored without survival, and in the bronze-to-new-form case material continuity is present but form is lost, and I deny survival. Is there a single principle of material constitution—perhaps involving spatiotemporal continuity of parts, or of form-bearing causal history, or of psychological and social trajectory—that accommodates all five? If so, what does it predict for a case I have not settled: a character whose consciousness is uploaded into a digital substrate that continuously updates in real time, with the biological body left intact and unconscious? And if no principle saves every verdict, which one should I abandon—the judgment about the ice, the synthetic scan, or the biological replacement?
