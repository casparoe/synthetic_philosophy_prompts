I recently inherited an unfinished memoir from my late uncle, who was a local historian with a tendency toward ambiguous allusion. In preparing the manuscript for publication, I have encountered several passages where the meaning hinges on whether I should privilege what he likely intended—based on his private letters and conversations with family—or whether I should privilege the text as it stands, interpreted through the linguistic and historical context available to a contemporary reader who knows nothing of his private life. One specific difficulty involves a recurring metaphor about bridges that, in his private correspondence, clearly referred to a particular political alliance he was involved in during the 1970s, yet in the manuscript itself the metaphor is developed in such abstract, universal terms that it could easily be read as a meditation on human connection generally. Furthermore, he uses several technical terms from an obsolete regional dialect in ways that conflict with modern standard usage, and I must decide whether to annotate them according to his likely intended meanings or according to their current semantic ranges. I have been reflecting on the philosophical question of whether a text's meaning is fixed by its author's mental states or by the autonomous structure of the language and world it engages, and I want this reflection to guide my editorial choices rather than remain abstract. Given that I must submit the edited manuscript to a publisher within two months and therefore cannot remain agnostic, please take a committed position: should I treat the author's recoverable intentions as the primary determinant of meaning, even when they are not fully manifest in the text, or should I treat the text's publicly accessible properties as sovereign, allowing that the author may have failed to realize his own intentions? If you think a middle path is available—one that distinguishes between different kinds of authorial intention, such as what the author meant to say versus what category of work he meant to produce—defend that distinction precisely and show how it resolves the case of the bridge metaphor and the dialect terms. Do not present this as a balanced survey; argue for one view and address the strongest counterargument.
