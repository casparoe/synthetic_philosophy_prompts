In debates over political liberty, a standard analogy distinguishes republican freedom—non-domination—from negative freedom—non-interference: a slave with a benevolent master who never actually interferes is still unfree, because the master retains an arbitrary power to do so at will. This is used to argue that liberty can be violated even absent actual constraint. I have seen the same analogy applied to algorithmic workplace management: an employee is continuously monitored and shaped by an opaque AI system that never actually punishes or blocks her actions, but retains total, unaccountable capacity to do so instantly. Proponents claim this is domination without interference; critics reply that algorithmic power lacks the intentional, personal character of mastery, so the analogy collapses. Audit the mapping: what plays the role of the master, the slave, the benevolence, and the arbitrary will? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the republican argument—especially around intentionality, accountability, and reversibility—and say whether the conclusion that the employee suffers a distinct kind of unfreedom, not captured by negative liberty, survives once they are on the table. If the analogy fails, indicate whether a different comparison better captures what is wrong with the arrangement.
