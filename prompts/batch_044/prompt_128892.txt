The exam question was as follows: “In The Poverty of Historicism, Popper rejects the claim that history is governed by discoverable laws of development. Explain his distinction between trends and laws, and show how both the Oedipus effect and the logic of the growth of scientific knowledge undermine the historicist project of long-range historical prophecy. The student’s answer reads:

Popper’s central argument is that historicists like Marx confuse trends with genuine laws. A law is an unconditional regularity that holds across all contexts, whereas a trend is merely a conditional pattern that depends on specific initial conditions and on the institutional settings in which it arises. The historicist error is to treat a directional pattern—say, the concentration of industrial capital—as an iron law of historical development rather than as a contingent trajectory.

Popper advances two main objections to prophecy. First, the Oedipus effect demonstrates that in social life the publication of a prediction can alter the conditions it describes, making prophecies self-fulfilling or self-defeating. This reflexivity is not unique to the social sciences; it operates symmetrically in natural science as well. For example, predicting an earthquake can change construction norms and thereby alter the damage pattern, so the Oedipus effect undermines long-range forecasting in every predictive domain, not just in history.

Second, Popper argues that the growth of scientific knowledge is inherently unpredictable. If we could forecast the content of future discoveries, we would already possess that knowledge in the present, which is contradictory. Because technological and economic change depends on scientific progress, and because scientific progress is open-ended, the long-term course of history cannot be mapped in advance.

In explaining trends, Popper adopts methodological essentialism: we must grasp the essential nature of collective entities—classes, civilizations, or economic systems—before we can account for particular historical events. This essentialist framework allows him to identify large-scale directional patterns without reducing them to individual psychology. At the same time, Popper insists that trends depend entirely on contingent initial conditions and never on enduring institutional structures, which means they cannot support even conditional predictions about the future. The proper role of social science is therefore limited to piecemeal engineering proposals tested against immediate outcomes, rather than grand historical narratives.
