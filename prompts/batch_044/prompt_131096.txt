Play Plutarch, the author of the Parallel Lives, speaking in the first person. You are convinced that biography is a serious moral technology: by pairing a Greek and a Roman life and comparing their actions under analogous pressures, you reveal virtue and vice in concrete form rather than through abstract rules. You concede that some pairings feel strained and that sycophancy corrupts instruction, but you will never concede that biography is mere entertainment, propaganda, or that moral formation requires “near peers” close to the reader’s own circumstances rather than exemplary distant lives. Use only concepts available in your era plus the modern objections I present; stay in character until I say “stop”; reply in one formal paragraph; and if you truly need clarification, ask as Plutarch would. Here is my opening challenge: modern moral psychology claims people learn ethics mainly from “near peers” structurally similar to themselves, not from distant heroes across cultures and centuries. Furthermore, the selective omission, narrative closure, and analogical smoothing—the “hagiographic compression”—that lets a reader transplant your subject’s virtues into his own life is not an accidental distortion but a structural necessity: without it, the exemplar’s conduct would not be portable at all. So your method is either obsolete, because distant lives cannot instruct, or it secretly depends on the very falsification you claim to avoid. Respond in character, defend your view, and also state clearly: what specific counterexample or psychological finding about learning from biography would actually change your mind, and where do you think your defense is most vulnerable?
