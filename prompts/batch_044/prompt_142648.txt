After retiring from a long career in condensed-matter physics, I have been auditing graduate seminars in moral philosophy and recently encountered P. F. Strawson’s essay Freedom and Resentment in a reading group of other scientists turned philosophers. I was struck by the analogy between reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—and localized forces in a physical field: they appear directional, relational, and largely indifferent to global determinism. Within our group, two stable camps have formed around Strawson’s framework, and rather than continue talking past one another, they have agreed to an adversarial collaboration to produce a joint statement of convergence and precise residual disagreement.

The first camp, the Practice-Naturalists, holds that reactive attitudes are irreducible, biologically evolved interpersonal practices. They accept Strawson’s distinction between participant and objective attitudes and argue that asking whether determinism undermines resentment is a category error, comparable to asking whether statistical mechanics undermines temperature. For them, moral responsibility is a local normative equilibrium maintained by relational expectations, not a metaphysical property requiring libertarian freedom.

The second camp, the Normative-Cognitivists, agrees that reactive attitudes are ubiquitous but contends that they operate as implicit cognitive-evaluative judgments carrying a commitment to a non-deterministic, agential source of action. They introduce a conceptual refinement—moral thermalization—by which reactive attitudes in large, anonymous societies lose their interpersonal specificity and diffuse into institutional structures such as courts and bureaucracies, thereby decoupling Strawson’s participant attitude from its relational anchor. On their view, determinism does not erase reactive attitudes empirically but reveals retributive ones as unjustified; the appropriate response is not elimination but a disciplined restructuring into accountability practices that preserve respect without blame.

Please draft the joint statement that both camps could sign. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including shared readings of Strawson’s core arguments, shared acceptance that reactive attitudes are empirically pervasive, and shared acknowledgment that determinism does not cause their disappearance; (2) precise statements of disagreement, with each camp’s position phrased in language its own members would accept, especially regarding whether reactive attitudes contain an implicit libertarian commitment and whether moral thermalization destabilizes the practice-based defense; (3) a table of cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—such as whether reactive attitudes are essentially relational or can survive generalization, and whether institutional mediation transforms or merely transmits them; and (4) for each crux, the specific case, argument, or conceptual test both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s predicted outcome recorded in advance. Both sides should explicitly address the functioning of reactive attitudes in large-scale penal institutions, where personal relationships are absent.

Present the agreed claims as a numbered list, followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the shared bearing-case, the Practice-Naturalist prediction, and the Normative-Cognitivist prediction. Maintain a formal, philosophical tone appropriate to a co-authored scholarly document.

Please conclude with a final line beginning Summary: that states in one sentence the single deepest crux separating the two camps after this collaboration.
