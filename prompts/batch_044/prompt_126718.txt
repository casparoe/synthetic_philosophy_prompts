Please help me work out the principle behind my judgments about whether the number of people in danger should determine rescue priorities, and then test it against cases I have not yet settled. Here are my verdicts, reported as concretely as I can.

Case 1: I can reach and save either five strangers or one stranger, with equal effort and risk to me. Verdict: I am required to save the five.

Case 2: The choice is the same in structure, except the single person is my closest friend, while the five remain strangers to me. Verdict: Saving my friend is permissible, and I am not required to save the five strangers instead.

Case 3: I can either save one person from certain death, or take an action that independently reduces each of five other people's risk of death from 30 percent to 10 percent. The expected number of lives saved by the second option is greater than one. Verdict: I should still save the one with certainty; the dispersed probabilistic benefits to the five do not override the direct obligation to the certain victim.

Case 4: I can save five people only by using one innocent bystander as a means—specifically, by pushing that bystander onto a mechanism that will halt the threat to the five. Verdict: This is impermissible.

Case 5: I can save five people today, or save one person today and, with the same resources, save five different people thirty years from now. Verdict: I should save the five today, but my sense of obligation feels noticeably weaker than in Case 1, as if temporal distance dilutes the claim without eliminating it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: I can save either five strangers who are each seventy-five years old with an expected ten years of remaining life, or one stranger who is twenty-five with an expected sixty years of remaining life. Sometimes I think the numbers still require saving the five; other times I think the total life-years saved point clearly to the one.

Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes direct pairwise comparison from aggregate calculation, or that introduces a threshold or weighting condition for when numbers dominate—that captures Cases 1 through 5? If no single principle can accommodate all of them, which verdict should I abandon, and what does abandoning it cost? Please also state precisely what your proposed principle predicts for two further cases: first, saving two strangers versus saving one stranger who is currently in the act of rescuing the other two; second, saving five strangers versus saving one stranger to whom I have made an explicit, unconditional promise of rescue. Answer in American English.
