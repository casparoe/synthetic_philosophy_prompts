I have been trying to understand a puzzle I encountered involving two urns: one contains exactly fifty red and fifty black balls, while the other contains one hundred balls in some unknown proportion of red and black. In the standard version of the problem, people prefer to bet on drawing a red ball from the first urn — where they know the chance is fifty percent — rather than on drawing a red ball from the second urn, where the proportion is unknown, even though the unknown urn might contain far more red balls. Most explanations call this ambiguity aversion and treat it as an irrational psychological bias, as if people were simply afraid of missing information. I have come to think this framing is wrong, and I want to defend a different interpretation.

It seems to me that avoiding ambiguity is not a failure of reasoning but a recognition of a specific kind of practical exposure. When probabilities are genuinely unknown, you are not merely risking a bad outcome; you are risking a bad outcome that you could have anticipated had you resolved the missing information differently, which creates a peculiar form of retrospective responsibility. There is also a social dimension that standard treatments overlook. In collective settings — whether a family weighing a risky medical option or a community preparing for unpredictable weather — ambiguity does not affect everyone uniformly; different members resolve the same gap in knowledge in opposite directions based on their prior hopes, fears, or commitments. This produces what I would call coordination fractures: the group cannot stabilize on a shared course because the ambiguity allows each person to construct a different rationalization of the risk. Please argue that ambiguity aversion, as illustrated by the urn choice, should be understood not as a cognitive bias to be corrected but as a rational defense against both individual epistemic overreach and these social coordination fractures. In particular, argue that treating ambiguous choices as if they involved precise subjective probabilities — the standard Bayesian remedy — actually conceals a problem of moral and practical alignment rather than solving it.
