Since I was appointed last autumn to my institution's strategic-risk committee, I have watched colleagues invoke historical analogies—Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap—as if naming one settles the strategic question. The formulation that dominates our debates is that Munich proves appeasement never works, and that we must therefore avoid another Munich at all costs. The phrase is deployed both to reject incremental diplomatic steps and to demand immediate, full-scale commitment, yet it is rarely specified what kind of similarity is being claimed: causal, structural, or merely rhetorical. I need to understand what precise theses are compressed into that one-liner, because I am now responsible for drafting recommendations and I do not want to confuse a diagnostic analogy with a predictive law. Could you lay out the distinct readings of the claim that Munich proves appeasement never works as a menu—ranging from the trivial observation that some past concessions preceded aggression, through defensible empirical hypotheses about deterrence failure under specific structural conditions, to the strongest universal or normative claims that treat 1938 as an eternal template? For each reading, please indicate whether it is a platitude, a testable but contingent claim, a false universalization, or a category mistake about analogical reasoning; and state clearly which readings actually support the policy inference from this resembles Munich to therefore we must commit fully now. I am particularly interested in whether there is a rigorous intermediate position that treats analogies as tools for identifying causal mechanisms rather than forecasting outcomes, and whether such a position can still sustain the conclusions my colleagues draw. Please present this as an ordered list rather than selecting one interpretation, avoid bibliographies or citation lists, and treat the Munich slogan as the sole object of disambiguation—the other analogies are mentioned only to show the environment in which I work.
