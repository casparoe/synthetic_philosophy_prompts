Two friends of mine, Leo and Sam, spent most of last night arguing about Pascal’s well-known fragment on justice and force after a seminar on early modern political thought. I listened to the whole exchange and want an outside adjudication rather than advice for myself. Here is how it went, move by move.

(1) Leo opened by saying Pascal is essentially a legal positivist before legal positivism existed. Pascal observes that justice without force is powerless, and that since we cannot make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just. On Leo’s reading this is not merely descriptive sociology but a reductive claim: normative authority in human societies is nothing but coercive capacity dressed up in moral language.

(2) Sam replied that this ignores Pascal’s theological frame. The fragment describes the fallen world, in which human reason is too weak and too proud to agree on true justice, so societies settle for the justice of the sword. Pascal clearly distinguishes this conventional justice from divine justice; the passage is a lament about corruption, not an endorsement of power.

(3) Leo pushed back: even as lament, the structure is reductive. Pascal treats justice and force as competing currencies of authority and implies that justice lacks any intrinsic motivational power—if it possessed any, it would not consistently require coercive backing. Leo said this is closer to a proto-genealogical account of morality than to Christian moral theology, because it makes the rationalization of power structurally necessary rather than accidentally corrupt.

(4) Sam countered by distinguishing two mechanisms. One is epistemic failure: people cannot reliably discern true justice. The other is psychological rationalization: people call power just to soothe pride. The first explains why convention is needed; the second explains why it is accepted. But neither mechanism reduces justice to power. True justice remains intact in God’s order, and the Christian can recognize the gap between human convention and divine law.

(5) Leo then introduced an implicit stability condition he thinks is hidden in Pascal’s pessimism. If justice-talk is always post-hoc legitimation of power, then a regime survives exactly when the gap between its coercive capacity and the population’s acceptance of its justice-narrative is smaller than the cost of rebellion. Reform movements that appeal to abstract justice without altering the coercive balance are therefore not merely impractical but conceptually confused—like trying to move a shadow without moving the object that casts it.

(6) Sam said Leo has over-formalized Pascal into a modern bargaining-theory model of equilibrium selection, importing disagreement points and outside options that Pascal never uses. Pascal is diagnosing epistemic corruption and pride, not computing stable allocations. Moreover, Sam insisted, Pascal allows an escape through faith and grace, which means the loop is not sociologically deterministic: individuals can recognize true justice and suffer for it, breaking the rationalization cycle at the personal level even if societies remain corrupt.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Leo’s moves (1, 3, 5) and each of Sam’s moves (2, 4, 6) on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state which specific points each party gets right, what both are missing—especially whether Leo’s implicit stability condition genuinely follows from Pascal’s text or is an anachronistic import—and whether Sam’s appeal to faith successfully answers Leo’s loop or merely relocates the problem. Finally, please state how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
