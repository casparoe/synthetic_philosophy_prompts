In a graduate discussion on naturalized epistemology, my peers and I were trying to locate the exact status of normative discourse within Quine's broader philosophical architecture. Quine is well known for rejecting the analytic-synthetic distinction and for treating scientific theories as webs of belief responsive to sensory experience at the periphery. Yet his scattered remarks on ethics suggest that moral sentences occupy a curiously peripheral and causally detached position within that web. I am trying to understand the conceptual structure of this detachment without reducing it to a simple form of non-cognitivism.

Specifically, please explain how Quine's denial that ethical statements have direct empirical content interacts with his physicalist commitment that all genuine knowledge claims must ultimately connect, however indirectly, to observable behavior and physical stimulation. If moral predicates lack the kind of causal-nomological links that tie theoretical terms in physics to sensory input, in virtue of what do they remain within the fabric of belief at all? Is their retention purely a matter of pragmatic tolerance—preserving social coordination without representational aspiration—or can they be reconstructed as having indirect empirical anchoring through their predictive and explanatory utility in anthropology and psychology? I want a careful conceptual mapping of this territory: how Quine's underdetermination thesis applies differently to normative versus descriptive domains, and whether his position on moral values ultimately collapses into a quietist instrumentalism or leaves room for a minimal, naturalized moral realism grounded in behavioral science. Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions; I am looking for an analysis of the internal logic and conceptual tensions within the view itself.
