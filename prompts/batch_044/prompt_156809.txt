Hey, I'm prepping a unit on moral responsability for my grad seminar and I ran into this summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument in some notes a colleague passed around (they're more into philosophy of mind than ethics). The summary goes like this: Strawson thinks true moral responsability requires being the ultimate source of your actions. His argument has three steps. First, you do what you do, in any given situation, because of how you are — your values, reasoning habits, emotional tendencies. Second, if you're truly responsible for an action, you have to be truly responsible for the mental nature that produced it. Third, you can't be responsible for your mental nature, because either it was caused by factors outside you (genes, environment, random chance), or you caused it yourself, which already presupposes a mental nature with which to do the causing. That's either an infinite regress or just conceptually circular, so ultimate self-creation is impossible. From this he concludes that nobody is ever truly responsible, regardless of whether determinism or indeterminism is right.

Then the notes add: 'This is basically why compatibilism fails — compatibilists say you're responsible when you act from your own motives, but Strawson shows those motives were never ultimately yours. And libertarianism doesn't help either, because even an uncaused choice still expresses a mental nature you didn't choose. In fact, Strawson's point is that any attempt at self-shaping, like years of therapy or disciplined study, just pushes the problem back one level: the self that did the reshaping wasn't self-created either.'

I'm suspicious that there's a subtle slippage here. Is this a fair rendering? In particular, does Strawson really think the regress has to be actually infinite, or is his point just that any chain of explanation bottoms out in something not authored by the agent? And does he deny that people can genuinely change their characters, or does he allow self-modification while denying ultimate authorship of the modifier? Also, my colleague seems to treat the Basic Argument and the manipulation argument against compatibilism as the same move — are they actually identical in Strawson's framework, or is that a conflation? Could you audit the summary: what's accurate, what's a simplification, what's subtly wrong, and does the conclusion that 'compatibilism fails because motives aren't ultimately ours' actually follow? I'd like to teach this without misrepresenting Strawson, and I'd rather not send students on a citation chase since not everyone has database access this term.
