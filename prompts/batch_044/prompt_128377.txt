I've been trying on my own to understand Gödel's rotating universe — the solution to Einstein's equations where spacetime itself rotates, allowing paths that loop back in time — and the conclusion he drew from it: not that we could travel to our past, but that if such loops are built into the geometry, then "before" and "after" can't be objectively real properties of the world. I'd like a poem of exactly sixteen lines, in four quatrains with an ABAB rhyme scheme, but with the very first line repeated verbatim as the sixteenth and final line. The repetition should carry the philosophical weight. In its first appearance the line should sound like a simple statement about returning; by the end, after the middle quatrains have evoked rotation without cosmic expansion and the absence of any universal "now," the identical words should feel emptied out — as though the speaker realizes there was never a linear timeline to loop back into. Keep the cosmology accurate: mention that the metric permits closed timelike curves, but that Gödel takes them to undermine objective temporal succession rather than to enable time travel. Don't resolve the tension with comfort or sermon; let the rhyme scheme hold steady while the meaning of the refrain collapses under it. Make it something a reader who hasn't studied physics could feel, but precise enough that someone familiar with Gödel's argument wouldn't wince.
