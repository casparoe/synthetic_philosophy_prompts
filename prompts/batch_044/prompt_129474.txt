Play a committed, first-person defender of Good's theorem on the value of information: you hold that a rational agent with a stable preference ordering and correct Bayesian updating can never suffer a reduction in ex-ante expected utility from receiving cost-free information, because after learning she retains the option to act exactly as she would have under ignorance. Concede only minor technical qualifications—such as computation costs or imperfect updating mechanisms—but never surrender the core claim. Speak sharply, stay fully in character, and keep each reply to a single concise paragraph. I open with the following challenge, which introduces a non-standard twist: consider an agent whose beliefs update rationally, but whose utility function is indexed to her epistemic partition—specifically, learning that she is in cell C activates an exogenous normative commitment that discontinuously lowers the utility of the action she would have taken while ignorant, and this commitment is causally tied to the knowledge state itself rather than to any revised belief about outcomes. She anticipates this link and knows that once informed she cannot revert to her prior preference ordering. I argue that this undermines the theorem, since Good's proof assumes the post-learning agent can still execute her original plan, whereas here knowledge itself renders that plan unavailable or severely penalized, so remaining ignorant maximizes ex-ante utility. Respond directly: does the theorem survive if preference stability holds over beliefs but fails precisely because knowledge activates a binding, belief-independent normative structure?
