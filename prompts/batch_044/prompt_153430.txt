I've been tinkering with a restricted occasionalism that borrows from Al-Ghazali but applies it mainly to mental causation rather than physical stuff. The thought is that the causal exclusion argument in philosophy of mind only works if we assume physical states have real causal powers that crowd out mental ones. If you go occasionalist about the physical world -- so fire doesn't really cause burning, God just produces burning on the occasion of fire -- then mental properties aren't being "excluded" by physical ones, since neither have independent causal efficacy. Mental events become direct occasions for divine causation too, maybe even the central ones. So mental causation is saved not by overdetermination but by denying the rival causal powers altogether.

What would count against this hybrid? Not the standard Humean constant-conjunction complaints or the "science needs causation" objections -- I know those. I want the defeaters that specifically target mental-ocasionalism: maybe neuropsychology showing mental-to-mental transitions are always physically mediated in ways that make occasionalist reconstruction strained? Or issues about moral responsibility if God is the direct cause of every intention? Or maybe the view just collapses into a weird dualism once you try to formalize the cusomary order for mental vs physical domains?

Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a defender concede as real damage, and which can be brushed off by saying the physical mediation is just part of the cusomary sequence God maintains? And is the view too well-insulated -- any empirical result gets reinterpreted as describing the customary order rather than real causation -- and if so, is that insulation a problem for a theory that's specifically about mental causation?
