Following a recent professional setback that I initially interpreted as entirely circumstantial but now recognize may have involved sustained denial on my part, I have been examining whether self-deception is genuinely possible or merely a misdescription of something else. I constructed the argument below and find its conclusion implausible, yet each step seems defensible.

(1) To deceive oneself about P, one must both believe that P and believe that not-P, since deception requires knowing the truth while inducing a false belief, and in self-deception both roles are played by the same agent.

(2) If an agent can simultaneously hold contradictory beliefs about the same proposition, then there is no internal constraint that forces belief revision when confronted with counterevidence; contradiction can be maintained indefinitely.

(3) But agents do sometimes revise beliefs in response to evidence, which requires that contradiction not be permanently sustainable within a single cognitive architecture.

(4) Therefore, simultaneous contradictory belief about P is impossible.

(5) Hence self-deception, understood as simultaneously knowing and not-knowing P, is impossible.

(6) Yet phenomena such as rationalization, motivated ignorance, and persistent denial in the face of clear evidence are well-documented and seem to involve exactly this structure.

(7) So if (5) is true, these phenomena must be reconceived as something other than belief-based contradiction—perhaps as partitioned belief states, strategic pretense without belief, or mere failure of attention.

(8) But this reconception proves too much: it implies that no agent ever genuinely believes against the weight of evidence, reducing all cognitive bias, weakness of will, and motivated reasoning to non-doxastic failures, which eliminates the very category of irrational belief that moral psychology and epistemology take as central.

Where does this chain fail? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (3) if possible, but I need to understand what giving up (4) or (7) actually costs, and whether there is a more precise failure between (6) and (8) that avoids collapsing self-deception into simple ignorance.
