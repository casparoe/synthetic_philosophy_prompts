I've been reading rationalist forums and the adjacent institutional-decay literature for years, and I recently listened to two regulars at my study group argue about democratic backsliding in a way that kept circling back to classification without resolving anything. I want a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, not just an overall winner. Please write the response in American English.

The disagreement started when Sam said we should define backsliding purely functionally: if a state's Polity score or V-Dem liberal-democracy index drops by more than one standard deviation over a ten-year window, that's backsliding, full stop. He applied it to Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, all of which clearly meet the threshold, and said the US hasn't come close, so calling recent American politics "backsliding" is just partisan rhetoric.

Riley replied that this makes the concept circular and measurement-dependent. She proposed we distinguish "hard backsliding"—constitutional or electoral-rule changes that rig future contests regardless of voter preferences—from "soft backsliding," which is norm erosion, court-packing, and executive overreach that remain technically reversible by a future government with enough legislative support. She said Poland's judicial reforms are soft under this framing, because a future parliament could in principle replace the disciplinary chamber and repopulate the courts, even if politically unlikely.

Sam came back that Riley's distinction collapses in practice. The fact that reversal is legally possible doesn't make it politically realistic; treating court-packing as "soft" hides its irreversibility behind a legal fiction. He added that her framework can't explain why we should treat Turkey's 2017 constitutional referendum differently from, say, a left-wing government that centralizes media oversight—both change institutions, yet we call only one backsliding.

Riley then introduced a third category she called "asymmetric backsliding": institutional changes that systematically advantage one side of the political spectrum regardless of which party initiates them. She argued this is the only way to save the concept from being a partisan label—if a change is structurally biased toward one coalition's survival, it's backsliding; if it's neutral institutional reform that happens to help the current government, it isn't. She applied this to Hungary's media-council restructuring and to certain American state-level election-administration changes, saying both are asymmetric because they entrench specific partisan outcomes.

Sam said "asymmetric" just smuggles in normative commitments about which policies are legitimate. He asked: if a government bans foreign funding for NGOs and expands executive oversight of broadcasters, is that asymmetric? It depends entirely on whether you think those NGOs and broadcasters were already ideologically captured. Without an independent standard for "neutrality," Riley's category reduces to "changes I dislike are asymmetric; changes I support are reform."

Neither of them mentioned whether democratic backsliding requires a prior democratic baseline—can a country backslide if it was never fully democratic?—and neither addressed whether the concept should track subjective citizen beliefs about fairness or only objective institutional indicators.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Sam's functional-definition argument, Riley's hard/soft distinction, Sam's reversibility objection, Riley's asymmetric category, and Sam's smuggling-objection from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justifications. Then say what both are missing, whether "backsliding" can survive without becoming a partisan label, and who is more right overall. Flag any point where your own reasoning depends on a contested normative premise rather than on the logic of their exchange.
