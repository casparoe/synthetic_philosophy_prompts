I'm taking a course on genocide and perpetrator studies and we've been fighting about Browning vs Goldhagen. My study group tried to formalize the tension and ended up with this argument that seems to prove hybrid explanations are impossible, which can't be right since every historian seems to blend them anyway. Here's the chain—tell me which link is weakest.

(1) Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 shows the killers were ordinary family men, not ideological fanatics, who murdered mostly because of peer pressure, authority, and incremental escalation—pure situational dynamics.

(2) Goldhagen argues these same men were willing executioners because they inhabited a culture of eliminationist antisemitism that made killing Jews feel morally correct, even enthusiastic.

(3) If situational dynamics are sufficient to turn ordinary people into killers, then a specific cultural hatred is causally redundant—Goldhagen's culture adds nothing that Browning's situation doesn't already cover.

(4) Conversely, if cultural hatred is necessary to explain the willingness and enthusiasm, then these men weren't ordinary in the morally relevant sense; they already carried an extraordinary disposition to kill.

(5) So we're stuck with an exclusivity: either situation explains everything and culture is epiphenomenal, or culture explains everything and the ordinary men framing is just a descriptive mistake.

(6) Moral responsibility should track whichever source actually did the work. If situation dominates, culpability is diminished; if culture or character dominates, culpability is full because they endorsed the killing.

(7) You can't split the difference—if pressure reduces blame, cultural endorsement can't simultaneously amplify it for the exact same act; that's like saying temperature both froze and melted the water.

(8) Therefore any view that blends them—say, ordinary men activated by a toxic culture—is incoherent. You have to choose one frame and reject the other.

We tried to apply this thing my friend called dispositional overdetermination—the idea that a general human capacity for conformity and a specific cultural hatred could both be present and compound—but step (5) seems to block that. I really want to keep both (1) and (2) true in some form, and I don't want to just dismiss (8) without seeing what's wrong with the reasoning. Is there a hidden scope shift between sufficient and necessary in step (3), or is the problem in how (6) assumes responsibility is zero-sum? Please separate what's settled about Battalion 101 from what's genuinely contested among historians, and don't present one side as the consensus.
