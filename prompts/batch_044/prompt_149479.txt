I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of economics, and we've been examining the socialist calculation debate—specifically Hayek and Mises's arguments from dispersed, local knowledge against Lange's iterative, trial-and-error model of market socialism. Rather than asking which side won on efficiency grounds, I want to compare two conceptual frameworks that are rarely synthesized in standard treatments of this dispute: first, the cybernetic conception of economic coordination as a distributed feedback system, in the tradition of Norbert Wiener and second-order cybernetics, where prices function as information-carrying signals within a self-regulating loop; and second, Michael Polanyi's epistemological distinction between explicit, articulable knowledge and tacit, embodied knowing that resists full formalization. How does each framework illuminate the disagreement between Hayek and Lange? In particular, does Lange's planning mechanism implicitly treat economic knowledge as if it were fully explicit and algorithmically compressible—capable of being fed into a central adjustment mechanism—whereas Hayek's critique gains additional force if read not merely as a claim about information dispersal but as a claim about the irreducibly tacit character of much market knowledge? Furthermore, could a Lange-style system be redesigned—say, through highly localized, autonomous planning nodes—to accommodate Polanyi's tacit dimension without effectively reproducing the price mechanism it seeks to replace? Please provide a rigorous conceptual comparison that shows where these two frameworks converge in interpreting the debate and where they pull it in opposite directions; I am not looking for a bibliography or citation list, but for an analysis of the underlying philosophical commitments about knowledge, computation, and coordination.
