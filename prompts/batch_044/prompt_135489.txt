In the United Kingdom, Denmark, and Japan, the enactment of every statute, the formal appointment of every prime minister, and the authorization of executive acts all require a specific, legally indispensable action by a hereditary monarch—royal assent, the signing of a Council of State decision, or an imperial designation—yet by unbroken constitutional convention that action is never withheld against ministerial advice and the monarch possesses no independent democratic mandate. At the same time, these states profess that all legitimate authority proceeds from the people and that no unaccountable individual may obstruct the popular will. What implicit theory of sovereignty, representation, or authority does this institutional bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the view: does it require that democratic consent be delegated upward through an unbroken chain that must terminate in a single, continuous, non-elected person who symbolically holds the state together; does it depend on a distinction between an effective government and a permanent “corporate” crown that makes law legally possible; or can it be sustained without any such metaphysics? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, assess whether it is philosophically consistent with the democratic doctrines these constitutions also affirm, and say what would have to change in lawmaking and executive accountability if the underlying conception of authority were replaced by one that rejects any indispensable hereditary office. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
