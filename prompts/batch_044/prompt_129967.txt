I'm giving the retirement address for my doctoral advisor, Dr. Lena Vasquez, who spent thirty years defending the view that consciousness is a user illusion — not that it is unreal, but that it functions as an interface systematically concealing the underlying predictive machinery from the user herself. The audience is split: half are philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists familiar with her technical work on predictive processing and interface opacity; the other half are family members and university administrators with no background in the field. The occasion is formal but warm, and I have about fifteen minutes. I need the speech to treat the user-illusion thesis as a structural constraint rather than a decorative metaphor: specifically, it should govern what can and cannot be said about her legacy, about the kind of self-knowledge her work makes available, and about the limits of introspection. To that end, please inject a somewhat niche but defensible idea — that if consciousness is genuinely an interface, then there is a principled, design-level epistemic closure regarding our own neural dynamics, analogous to a software user being structurally barred from accessing the kernel, and that this closure is not a temporary ignorance to be cured but a permanent feature of the architecture. The speech must navigate this without ever implying that she, or science, can pull back the curtain to reveal hidden machinery, or that introspection could eventually penetrate the interface. It should honor her work for the experts without losing the family, avoid bibliographic references or reading lists, and contain no verbatim quotations from publications or philosophical literature. Please write the full speech, structured for oral delivery, with clear transitions that allow non-specialists to follow the conceptual argument even when the technical vocabulary appears.
