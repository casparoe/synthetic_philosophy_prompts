I'm trying to work out my actual stance on whether societies should celebrate or resent highly successful individuals, and my gut reactions seem to pull in opposite directions depending on details I can't quite articulate. I was discussing this with friends after a course session on distributive justice, and I realized that neither the American Dream narrative—where upward mobility is inherently admirable—nor the Scandinavian Jantelagen or Anglo tall-poppy syndrome—where standing out invites moral suspicion—fully captures my intuitions. So I'd like to see whether there's a coherent principle behind my verdicts, or whether I just have to live with the tension.

Here are my judgments on concrete cases. A first-generation immigrant starts a regional restaurant chain, is profiled as a hometown hero, and uses profits to fund local apprenticeships. That feels right to celebrate: the success seems to multiply opportunity rather than extract it from the community she left. But consider a scholar from the same working-class town who earns tenure at an elite university, publishes a memoir calling her origin backward, and never returns. There I feel the Jantelagen impulse strongly—her success feels like betrayal, not mere departure. A musician becomes nationally famous, lives modestly, and endows local schools; I think celebration should persist. Yet when a collective of artisans develops a unique ceramic technique and one member patents it individually to license exclusively to luxury brands, I want to cut her down, even though she technically "succeeded" by every conventional metric. Meanwhile, a farmer who scales up, outcompetes neighbors through efficiency, and buys their land: my reaction splits. The achievement looks like classic American Dream material, but the erosion of the local social fabric makes celebration feel hollow.

The pair that troubles me most is the restaurant founder versus the scholar. Both achieved upward mobility, both left, both profited—yet I celebrate one and resent the other. Is the difference that one maintained solidarity while the other severed it? Or is that just post-hoc rationalization? The case I keep circling back to is a star athlete from a poor rural region who signs a massive contract and moves away, but sends substantial money home anonymously. Is the anonymity a virtuous modesty that makes his success palatable—an appropriate check on tall-poppy arrogance—or does it reveal an unhealthy shame about standing out, precisely the pathology the American Dream is meant to reject? And what about the self-taught engineer who patents a genuinely life-saving water filter, becomes wealthy, but refuses to license the design to developing-world manufacturers on cost grounds? I oscillate between seeing this as legitimate property-right success and as extraction masked by innovation rhetoric.

Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing multiplication success from extraction success, or tracking whether upward mobility extends solidarity rather than severing it—that delivers all these verdicts? Or must I drop one, and if so, which? In particular, can any principle explain why I celebrate the founder but resent the scholar without collapsing into pure favoritism? And what does the best principle predict for the anonymous athlete and the patent-holding engineer? Please also say where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what specific argument or observation about communal obligation versus individual merit would lead you to overturn your preferred principle, or to switch which of my verdicts you treat as the mistaken one?
