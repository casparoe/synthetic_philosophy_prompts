I am in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar focused on consciousness and temporal experience. For our session on perceptual illusions and the metaphysics of timing, I need a runnable in-class activity for about 15 students over 75 minutes that makes the tension between Orwellian and Stalinesque interpretations of the color phi phenomenon feel concrete before anyone explains it. Please design a structured exercise with these elements: a rapid multimodal perceptual task—visual color changes paired with brief auditory cues presented in quick succession—arranged so that the physical sequence conflicts with the unified temporal ordering participants naturally report; assigned roles including immediate reporters who must commit after each stimulus, delayed reporters who only respond after the full sequence, and observer-challengers who can question consistency but not change reports; explicit rules governing when a report can be altered, what distinguishes revising an earlier claim from offering a new interpretation, and how cross-examination proceeds; a clear timeline covering stimulus presentation, initial reporting rounds, structured cross-examination, and collective deliberation; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the Orwellian idea of retrospective editing of past experience versus the Stalinesque idea of presentist construction with no prior content to revise. The debrief should also predict what will go wrong on the first run—such as observers conflating report-change with experience-change or immediate reporters anchoring the group—and should frankly state which feature of the philosophical debate, likely the metaphysical status of an unrevised past phenomenal state, the behavioral rules alone cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. No lecture or framing should happen before the exercise begins, and please do not include citations, bibliographies, or required readings.
