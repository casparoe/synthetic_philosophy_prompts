I understand Black's two-spheres universe well enough: two spheres of identical composition and size suspended in an otherwise empty space, separated by a fixed distance. Black uses the case to argue that two objects can share every qualitative property—including every relational property like "being two radii distant from another sphere"—and still remain numerically distinct, which undermines the law.

But I've developed a worry that seems to reverse the case entirely. If we take relationalism about space seriously—denying that there are absolute positions and holding that only spatiotemporal relations exist—then it looks to me as though the spheres do not possess any genuinely distinguishing relational properties either. Each stands in exactly the same relations to the other: the distance is symmetric, there is no oriented background frame, and there are no other objects to ground an asymmetry. So under relationalism they appear not merely qualitatively indiscernible, but relationally indiscernible as well. That suggests Black's scenario does not refute the identity of indiscernibles; rather, it shows that if relationalism is true, there cannot be two such spheres—they must collapse into a single object. In other words, Black smuggles in an absolutist commitment about spatial position to generate the distinction, and once that commitment is stripped away, the law is vindicated rather than defeated.

My specific question is whether this "relational collapse" objection succeeds. Does relationalism actually erase the distinction between the spheres, or is there a resource—perhaps weak discernibility through irreflexive relations, or some symmetry-breaking orientation available even in an empty universe—that allows a relationalist to distinguish them without invoking absolute space?
