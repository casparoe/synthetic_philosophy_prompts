I am a novelist drafting a cycle of literary fictions in which each protagonist reaches a terminal condition presented as ethically complete. My working principle—tranquilism—holds that the only thing making a life go well is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering; pleasure, achievement, virtue, or relational richness count for nothing if either craving or suffering remains, and add nothing if both are gone. I need a reusable decision procedure I can apply alone to a manuscript page, without specialist apparatus, to determine whether a character’s endpoint is genuinely tranquil or only appears so. Set out the ordered questions to ask about the protagonist’s state, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a definite negative, where it stops with a definite positive, and where it must hand the decision to judgment because the issue is interpretive or evaluative rather than factual. The procedure must distinguish the absence of craving from mere satisfaction, resignation, or narrative flatness, and the absence of suffering from numbness, dissociation, or death-in-life—distinctions that fiction writing constantly obscures. Apply it to three draft cases: a widow who has ceased wanting anything and lives in quiet routine; a monk who reports no suffering and no desire but is clearly dissociated; and a protagonist whose every explicit want has been fulfilled yet who feels a lingering, unnamed dissatisfaction. Include the case in which the procedure delivers a verdict I will not wish to accept, and state plainly what the framework cannot settle—particularly whether a life emptied of craving but also emptied of dramatic tension is still one worth depicting, and whether the absence of suffering in a fictional consciousness can ever be verified from the text alone. Keep the steps formal enough to survive across revisions, but mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.
