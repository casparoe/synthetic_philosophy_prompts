Please write a polished five-minute retirement-dinner speech in British English for Dr. Alistair Fenwick, who is stepping down after thirty years directing the sampling methodology unit of a national statistical office and, in his final years, advising on the design of citizens' assemblies chosen by lot. The audience is divided: half are democratic theorists and practitioners acquainted with debates over sortition and lottocracy—particularly the tension between descriptive representation (mirroring population demographics through stratified random selection) and epistemic representation (selecting for distributed knowledge rather than demographic mirroring)—while the other half are family members, retired survey fieldworkers, and local officials with no background in political theory. Fenwick remains sceptical of full lottocracy—he does not believe legislative authority should be transferred entirely to rotated citizens' juries—but he has come to defend a narrower claim: that unbiased random selection within carefully defined domain strata produces a form of collective cognition that meritocratic appointment systematically suppresses, not because experts are wrong, but because homophily in expert networks creates correlated blind spots. The speech must do real structural work with this idea: it should shape what can be said about Fenwick's career (he is not a convert to pure lot-based government, nor is he a defender of unchecked technocracy) rather than using it as decorative reference. The speech should be affectionate, avoid any bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and should contain no verbatim quotations from published texts. It must be intelligible to the non-specialists without being condescending to the theorists in the room, and it should land as tribute rather than lecture.
