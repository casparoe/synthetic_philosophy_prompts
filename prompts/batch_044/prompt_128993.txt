At a philosophy reading group, two members argued about origin essentialism—specifically, whether you could have come into existence from a different sperm and egg. The first member defended the essentialist position: if her parents had conceived with different gametes, the resulting child might share some traits, but she herself would never have existed, so her actual biological origin is essential to her being this particular person. The second member objected that this overweights chromosomes. She noted that if the actual zygote had split into identical twins, both twins trace back to the same sperm and egg, yet they are distinct persons—so being tied to those specific gametes is not sufficient for being this particular individual. The first member replied that the twin case actually strengthens essentialism: one origin can yield multiple people, which shows that personal identity must be anchored in the specific fusion event that actually occurred, not merely in the genetic material available. The second member then proposed a counterfactual: imagine the same parents, on the same occasion, conceiving a child with different gametes but raising it with the same name, relationships, and social history. If psychological and social continuity are what matter, she argued, that child might be her in every meaningful respect, which suggests origin essentialism mistakes biological causation for personal identity. The first member countered that this scenario illegitimately separates social history from its biological cause—changing the gametes changes the entire causal chain, including which conception event actually happened—and that allowing gamete substitution opens the door to allowing different parents entirely, making the idea of “her parents” dangerously contingent. The second member said this was an appeal to intuition rather than argument, and accused the first of sliding unjustifiedly from “my actual parents are essential” to “my actual gametes are essential,” since parenthood is socially defined, not chromosomally determined. They turned to me, an uninvolved listener, and asked me to adjudicate the exchange round by round: for each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct, or clearly right and briefly justify the rating; identify what each party is missing or conflating; and say who is more right overall, without collapsing the dispute into a simple “it depends.”
