Consider the following rulings made by a philosopher across six hypothetical cases discussed in a seminar series on self-deception, with her verdicts noted in parentheses. First, a manager who reads every quarterly report showing losses yet genuinely believes the division is thriving, interpreting each failure as a temporary exception; she rules this self-deception (yes). Second, a spouse whose calendar contains anniversary reminders yet who forgets each year without any cover story; she rules this not self-deception (no). Third, an activist who publicly denies climate risk while privately maintaining a stocked bunker, sincerely holding both the public line and the private plan without feeling conflict; she rules this hypocrisy rather than self-deception (no). Fourth, a patient with a terminal diagnosis who avoids all medical appointments and genuinely insists he is healthy; she rules this self-deception (yes). Fifth, a researcher who applies stricter evidential standards to her rivals' studies than to her own, notices the asymmetry in private notes, experiences brief discomfort, and then never allows the observation to revise her self-image; she rules this self-deception (yes). Sixth, a trauma survivor who has no conscious belief about a past event but experiences intrusive flashbacks and behavioral patterns consistent with it; she rules this dissociation, not self-deception (no).

The new case is this: a quantitative analyst operates with two fully articulated mental models—one used in her institutional reports, which treats markets as efficient and her own predictions as unreliable, and one used in her personal portfolio management, which treats markets as predictable and her own judgments as exceptional. The two models never interact, not because she suppresses either, but because they are embedded in structurally separate contexts—different vocabularies, social roles, emotional stakes, and decision procedures—so that no contradiction is ever experienced. When a colleague points out the conflict, she is genuinely surprised, acknowledges both are hers, yet feels no immediate pressure to abandon either, since neither feels false in its native domain.

Predict her verdict on whether this is self-deception. Identify the implicit principle that best explains her pattern of rulings—distinguishing, for instance, a division-of-agency or compartmentalization model from a motivated-epistemic-irrationality model that requires only non-epistemic maintenance of belief—and explain which rule predicts what about this case. Give your best guess at her actual verdict with the reasoning, and then specify the further case that would definitively settle which principle she is running. In your answer, please state how confident you are in each claim and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
