You are an expert epistemologist and an experienced facilitator of philosophy graduate seminars; before you design anything, take a moment to think carefully about how foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each generate a different lived pressure when people actually try to justify beliefs in real time rather than only on paper. Design a 75-minute classroom game for eight graduate students in epistemology, seated around one table, that makes them feel the structural force of the regress problem across all three positions. Frame the session as a meeting of an “Epistemic Accreditation Board” tasked with admitting propositions into a shared “Knowledge Registry,” running three successive 18-minute rounds—each enforcing one regime—followed by a 15-minute debrief. In Round 1 (Foundationalist), beliefs may be admitted only if they rest on a “Base Certificate” that the board issues but which requires no justification within the round; inject the novel constraint that the board may issue only two such certificates total, so participants must fight over which beliefs become arbitrarily privileged and quickly discover the discomfort of stopping justification by fiat. In Round 2 (Coherentist), no belief may be admitted unless supported by at least two already-admitted beliefs, and the board must achieve a single closed loop with no dangling nodes; participants will feel pressure to reject intuitively obvious beliefs that fail to cohere and to smuggle in hidden premises to close the circle. In Round 3 (Infinitist), every admitted belief must be paired with a distinct, previously unmentioned justifying belief, with no repetition allowed and no termination permitted; use a physical token chain—each justification is a linked card placed on the table—so that by minute fifteen the surface is covered in an unmanageable chain and participants experience the practical impossibility of completing admission. Assign rotating roles—Proponent, Auditor, and Registry Clerk—to prevent dominance by the same voices, and use a non-monetary point system of “Certification Credits” that rewards successful admission but penalizes violations of the round’s structural rule, with credits reset in value between regimes so strategic behavior exposes tension rather than resolving it.

Arrange the rules, roles, and incentives so that participants encounter the characteristic failure mode of each view before anyone names it. After the third round, conduct the debrief by mapping specific moments—the fight over the second Base Certificate, the rejection of an obvious belief to preserve loop closure, the physical overflow of justification cards—onto the formal problems of arbitrariness, circularity, and infinite regress. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong in execution: in Round 1, students will try to justify the Base Certificates retroactively; in Round 2, they will implicitly rely on unstated background beliefs; in Round 3, fatigue will cause repetition, so include an original “Fatigue Protocol” (a designated Auditor checks for repeated cards and calls a halt) to catch this and make the breakdown visible. Finally, include an honest limitation: the activity reproduces the structural logic of justification but cannot reproduce the normative dimension—participants feel administrative pressure, but they do not experience the genuine epistemic obligation that makes the regress philosophically urgent—so the facilitator must supply that normative framing in closing remarks. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke highly specialized recent literature; build the mechanics from the core distinctions themselves, using only the fresh “Knowledge Registry” framing, the two-certificate limit, and the Fatigue Protocol as self-contained scaffolding.
