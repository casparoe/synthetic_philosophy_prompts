**Question:** Compare Browning’s and Goldhagen’s explanations of Battalion 101’s participation in the Józefów massacre with respect to (i) the relative weight of situational/organizational pressures versus ideological conviction; (ii) whether each treats the perpetrators as psychologically abnormal or as representative of a broader population; and (iii) one scope condition or acknowledged limitation each author places on his own argument. **Student answer:** Browning and Goldhagen both study Reserve Police Battalion 101 at Józefów in July 1942, yet diverge sharply on causation. Browning holds that the killers were unexceptional, middle-aged reservists—mostly working-class men from Hamburg—who acted mainly under situational duress: peer pressure, the desire not to abandon comrades, and careerist conformity. He concedes that antisemitism was present in the unit, but insists it was incidental rather than motivating; in effect, he treats ideology as a negligible factor. His model is essentially Milgram-style authority obedience, modified by group dynamics. For Browning, these were ordinary men, and his explanation applies broadly to any similar group placed in analogous conditions.

Goldhagen rejects this framing. He argues that a uniquely German “eliminationist antisemitism”—cultivated over centuries—made the killings not only acceptable but desirable to the perpetrators. Where Browning sees situational coercion, Goldhagen sees voluntary enthusiasm. Crucially, Goldhagen maintains that the men were psychologically abnormal: their willingness to butcher women and children reveals a sadistic or pathological disposition that distinguished them from ordinary citizens. Nevertheless, he claims that this disposition was widespread in German society, so the battalion was representative rather than exceptional in that respect. Both authors, however, acknowledge that direct orders were not strictly enforced—Major Trapp offered to excuse anyone who felt unable to shoot—though they interpret this fact differently: Browning as evidence that refusal was genuinely possible, Goldhagen as proof that the men killed willingly despite the absence of coercion.
