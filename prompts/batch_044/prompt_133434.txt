I'm a phd student in philosophy of science trying to sort out my gut feelings about Friedman's F-twist, not the slogan but the actual cases. Here are my verdicts, and I can't get them all to hang together.

Case 1: Galileo's falling-body law assumes perfect vacuum and point masses. Obviously false for every real object, but the law works. Verdict: totally fine.

Case 2: A climate model uses 100km grid cells, so mountains and cities disappear. Predicts global mean temp well. Verdict: also fine, that's just resolution.

Case 3: A macro forecast assumes agents have infinite computing power and perfect foresight about prices. Predicts GDP growth for the last decade within 2%. Verdict: feels like cheating. The unrealism is in the mechanism that's supposed to generate the result, not in background conditions. But I can't say exactly why case 1 is different.

Case 4: An epidemiological SIR model assumes people mix randomly like gas molecules. Works for urban flu, fails for rural spread. Verdict: okay locally, not as universal law. So maybe unrealistic assumptions are okay only when they're "insulated" from the structure that actually drives the outcome?

Case 5: A neuroscience model treats neurons as binary logic gates. Predicts almost nothing about actual brains, but engineers use it for chip design. Verdict: okay for technology, not for explaining cognition.

Case 6: A bacterial-resistance model assumes mutation rates are constant and environment-independent. Used for hospital dosing, but stress induces mutations. Fits old lab data but gives bad clinical protocols. Verdict: definitely not okay.

Case 7 (the one I keep flipping on): Cosmological models include dark energy as a pure constant lambda with no physical field behind it. The value is fitted post-hoc to match expansion data. Sometimes I think this is Galileo-style placeholder idealization; other times it feels like case 6, hiding ignorance in a parameter that does predictive work. What's the actual distinction?

Is there a principle that gets 1, 2, 4, 5, and either 3 or 6? Maybe something like: unrealistic assumptions are okay in boundary conditions or resolution limits, but not in the causal dynamics that are supposed to do the explanatory work? Or maybe it's about whether fixing the unrealistic part breaks the prediction?

What does your best principle say about case 7, and which verdict does it force me to drop? If I have to choose between saving case 3 and saving case 6, which goes?
