After being asked to contribute to a university working group on distributive justice—an invitation that felt suddenly personal when my younger sibling received a congenital diagnosis and I found myself comparing the costs of accommodating that disability with the advantages I inherited through family wealth—I have tried to fix my philosophical commitments in writing. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept the core luck-egalitarian claim that inequalities traceable to brute luck are unjust, while those traceable to option luck are permissible. (2) I take the brute-luck/option-luck boundary to be a normative construct rather than a metaphysical or causal kind discoverable by empirical science. (3) I reject any binary account of “choice,” holding that adaptive preferences formed under deprivation and socialization blur the line in almost every real case. (4) I believe people should be compensated for expensive tastes or ambitions that are themselves products of brute-luck socialization—being raised in a culture that valorizes luxury consumption, for instance. (5) I maintain that holding someone responsible for a choice requires that they had access to a reasonable alternative that was not itself a product of brute luck. (6) I think reflective equilibrium is the correct method for drawing the boundary, and I do not believe there is a pre-theoretical line waiting to be discovered. (7) Despite all this, I want to preserve the conceptual distinction rather than collapse into a flat equality of outcome. (8) I also hold that “talent” is not brute luck in itself, but that its distributive weight depends entirely on contingent institutional structures that arbitrarily monetize specific capacities—so talent-based pay gaps are unjust not because talent is unearned, but because markets select which capacities to reward. (9) Finally, I propose that option luck should be assessed not at the level of individual acts but at the level of “institutional exit options”: a risk counts as option luck only if the agent had a structurally available, non-deprived alternative path that was not itself blocked by prior brute luck.

Please audit whether these commitments hang together. I am particularly worried that (5) and (9), taken together with (3), might collapse the distinction I want to preserve in (7), and that (8) introduces an institutional framing that pulls against the individual-level analysis in (1) and (5). At the same time, I suspect that (2) and (6) might only appear to be in tension—one sounds anti-realist, the other sounds like it needs objective data—but I am not sure whether that appearance dissolves on inspection. For each pair that is genuinely in tension, rank the tensions by severity, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what philosophical cost it carries, and identify any pairs that only look incompatible. Then tell me which single revision to my set—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the largest number of tensions at the lowest cost, even if it requires weakening my attachment to (7).
