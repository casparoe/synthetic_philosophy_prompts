So there's this pension policy in several EU countries where workers are auto-enrolled in a defaut lifecycle plan that shifts to bonds as they age, but the sign-up sheet literally says "you are fully in control of your retirement destiny" and frames opting out as "choosing to reject long-term security." What's the implicit philosophy here? Is it just sloppy libertarian paternalism, or does it presuppose a split-self model where there's a "real" prudent self and a myopic tempted self? The weird part is the asymmetry: stay in and you get cheerful updates saying "great job trusting the default," but opt out and you have to watch a 20-minute compound-interest video first. So passive acceptance is treated as wisdom, active choice as ignorance needing correction. What view of autonomy or rationality makes that asymmetry make sense — diachronic autonomy? Some kind of epistemic paternalism? And if we replaced that view with a straightforward occurrent-prefference model, what exactly would have to change: the default, the video, the messaging, or all three? I'm also wondering if the practice is philosophically underdetermined — could a pure welfare-maximizer defend the same design without buying into any split-self story?
