I'm a journalist who covers science and conceptual change, and I'm trying to get my own judgments straight about when a system can reason competently even though it lacks comprehension of what it is doing. Darwin and Turing both strike me as having performed strange inversions: Darwin inferred a causal mechanism from phenotypic patterns without knowing the physical basis of heredity, and Turing argued that behavioral indistinguishability alone should license attributions of intelligence, deliberately setting aside questions of internal understanding. I want to know whether the verdicts below hang together under one principle, or whether coherence demands that I give one up.

Here are my intuitions, case by case.

Darwin formulating natural selection through comparative anatomy, biogeography, and artificial breeding, all while having no concept of genes, DNA, or particulate inheritance. Verdict: this is genuine reasoning. The inferential architecture—hypothesis, comparative test, predictive success—holds up, and the fact that the underlying mechanism was opaque to him does not undermine the reasoning itself.

Turing proposing that we judge a machine's intelligence by its conversational behavior alone, bracketing consciousness, understanding, or internal representation. Verdict: this is a valid philosophical inversion of our usual epistemic priority—we normally want evidence of inner life before attributing mind—but it is an argument about the conditions for attribution, not a claim that the machine itself reasons.

A modern protein-folding neural network that predicts three-dimensional structures from amino-acid sequences with superhuman accuracy, trained purely on statistical correlations, with no representation of thermodynamic forces or evolutionary history. Verdict: this is not reasoning. It achieves competence without any inferential structure, and its creators' inability to explain specific outputs confirms that no comprehension, tacit or explicit, is present.

A chess engine that selects moves through brute-force tree search and evaluation heuristics, with no grasp of strategic concepts like initiative or pawn structure. Verdict: closer to the protein folder than to Darwin. There is computation and optimization, but no reasoning about the domain.

A human master sommelier who identifies a wine's vineyard by taste alone and cannot articulate the specific cues she uses. Verdict: genuine competence with tacit comprehension. The understanding is present but inarticulate, which distinguishes it both from Darwin's explicit but mechanism-blind inference and from the network's blank correlation.

The pair that pulls me in opposite directions is Darwin versus the protein folder: both generate accurate predictions about complex biological phenomena from data without understanding the causal machinery, yet I want to call the first reasoning and the second not. Meanwhile, I keep going back and forth on an evolutionary design algorithm—of the kind used to produce unconventional antenna shapes—that evolves an optimal form human engineers later adopt but never fully understand. Is that Darwinian reasoning instantiated in silicon, selection without a selector, or is it just another correlation engine wearing evolutionary clothing?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on the direction and revisability of inference, on whether the system can be surprised and correct itself, or on the presence of an explicit representational framework—that captures all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, explain which clause handles each case, predict what it says about both the evolutionary-antenna case and one further case I have not listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If no single principle saves them all, tell me which one I should drop and why.
