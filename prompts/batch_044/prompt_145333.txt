I'm a retired chemist who recently started reading philosophy of science for fun, and I got pulled into a debate with two people from my local book club — Mara and Jon, both way sharper than me. We were arguing about the Anthropocene: when exactly did it start, and who gets to decide that? They went back and forth a few rounds and then asked me to referee, but I honestly couldn't figure out who was right about what. Here's the exchange as I remember it.

Mara opened by saying the start should be 1610, tied to the Columbian exchange and that CO2 dip — the Orbis spike — because that's when humans irreversibly rewired global biota and carbon cycles. She said the "who decides" part is easy: the International Commission on Stratigraphy decides what counts as a formal geological unit, so if they reject the Anthropocene, it's not a real period, just a buzzword.

Jon said 1945 or thereabouts makes more sense — the Great Acceleration, nuclear isotopes, plastics — because that's when human impact went exponential and left an unambiguous stratigraphic signal. He also said asking "who decides" is the wrong question altogether: geological periods aren't decreed, they're discovered in the rock record. The ICS dosen't "create" the Holocene; they just ratify what's already there. So if human impact is geologically real, it exists whether some committee votes yes or no.

Mara pushed back that this is naive about science being social. The Holocene boundary itself — around 11,700 years back, tied to ice-core data — was contested and ultimately fixed by convention and institutional vote, not revealed by nature alone. So "who decides" always matters, because periodization is partly interpretation, not pure description.

Jon replied that if it's all convention, then "Anthropocene" is just politics in a lab coat. He proposed treating it instead as a "historical epoch" — like "the Age of Discovery" — which sidesteps the ICS entirely and admits it's a useful narrative, not a rock-layer reality. He called this a "stratigraphic fiction" — not fake, just not a formal unit. Mara said that's a cop-out: if we're serious about human impact being geologically significant, we should fight for formal recognition, not retreat to metaphor.
