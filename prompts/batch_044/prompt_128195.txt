ok so there's this evaluation practice in some ai labs that i keep thinking about. they run two completely seperate audit tracks for the same model. track one is all about intent alignment -- they feed it ambiguous or adversarial prompts and score whether it figures out the real goal behind the wording and executes that. track two is value alignment -- they hit it with a standardized harm taxonomy and score refusal rates and whether it endorses certain norms. the two tracks never talk to each other; there's no combined metric, and the teams running them report up through different chains. it treats doing what you meant and not doing bad stuff like theyre on seperate sliders.

what philosophical commitments does that institutional split presuppose? does it assume that understanding an intention and evaluating a value are totally independant cognitive capacities, so you can max out one without touching the other? or does it smuggle in a specific view of authority -- like the user owns the intent but some external board owns the values? im especially puzzled because in real life those collapse: if i intend to hurt someone and ask for help, the aligned response seems like it should fail both tracks at once, not pass one and fail the other. so is the practice only coherent if you think intentions are always benign and values are just guardrails? and what does it presuppose about moral agency -- that the model isnt really responsible for either, just mechanically matching patterns? reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me where its actually underdetermined (maybe several philosophies license the split), and say what would have to change in the audit design if we rejected that separation.
