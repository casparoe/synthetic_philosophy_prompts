A philosophy essay I came across summarizes Daniel Dennett’s argument in Quining Qualia through the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster case, and I want to know whether the author is being fair to Dennett’s actual reasoning. The summary runs as follows. Chase and Sanborn have spent years tasting Maxwell House coffee and now both report that they no longer like it, but they describe the change differently: Chase says he has become a more sophisticated drinker, so his taste has changed, while Sanborn says the coffee itself no longer tastes to him the way it used to, implying that its qualitative character has shifted for him. Dennett then asks each man to determine, purely by introspection, whether he is in Chase’s position or Sanborn’s—whether his inner qualitative state has altered or the object’s appearance to him has changed. Because neither can settle this question from the inside, the essay takes the case to show that there is no introspectively accessible raw feel that could distinguish a change in the subject from a change in the object. The author then claims that Dennett moves from this epistemic limitation to a stronger ontological conclusion: not merely that we are ignorant of our qualia, but that the very distinction between a changed subject and a changed object collapses, rendering talk of what it is like to taste coffee referentially empty. Finally, the essay asserts that this is why Dennett rejects irreducible qualia as implicitly committed to a Cartesian theater—an inner display of private qualitative states—since without such a theater there is no locus for the indeterminacy to arise, and anyone who insists on irreducible phenomenal properties must therefore be defending a kind of inner stage. How fair is this rendering? Please examine it claim by claim: does Dennett actually ask the tasters to perform that specific introspective determination, or is the essay importing a Cartesian framing that Dennett himself rejects? Is the inference from epistemic indeterminacy to ontological incoherence present in Dennett’s text, or is the author conflating the intuition pump with Dennett’s separate critique of phenomenal concepts? Does the argument against the Cartesian theater depend on the Chase-Sanborn case, or has the essay reversed the dependency? And if one corrects these points, does the essay’s conclusion—that defenders of qualia are necessarily committed to an inner theater—still follow? I am inclined to think the author overstates Dennett’s eliminativism, but I am unsure exactly where the line should be drawn between Dennett’s modest claim about the indeterminacy of qualitative change and the stronger claim that qualia are conceptually incoherent.
