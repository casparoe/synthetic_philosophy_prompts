Essay idea and argument sketch: “No Persistent User: Identity as Convention Over Impermanent Processes”

My central claim is that the Buddhist denial of a permanent self is best understood not merely through the five aggregates (skandhas), but through the Abhidhammic distinction between conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca). At the ultimate level, there is only the arising and cessation of impersonal mental and physical events—like processes spawning and terminating in a cluster with no central controller. At the conventional level, however, we treat a continuous stream of these events as a single agent because doing so makes moral coordination and prediction possible—similar to how we treat a service endpoint as stable even though its backing containers are constantly replaced. I want to argue that rejecting anatta does not require rejecting moral responsibility; rather, agency should be relocated from a static metaphysical owner to the causal continuity of conditions, much as state in a distributed system persists through replication and dependency rather than through a master node.

Specific points I develop:
1. The aggregates have no hidden “user account” behind them; they are the full inventory of what occurs.
2. Conventional identity is a pragmatic abstraction that tracks causal dependencies across time, not a discovery of an underlying substance.
3. Moral accountability can therefore attach to patterns of continuity and conditioning rather than to a permanent self, without collapsing into determinism or eliminativism.
