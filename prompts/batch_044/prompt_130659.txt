I'm taking an advanced seminar on rational choice and intertemporal agency, and my study group has been circling a subtle conceptual knot about the sunk-cost fallacy. Most of us accept the standard decision-theoretic diagnosis: once costs are unrecoverable, they ought to drop out of the utility calculus, so honoring them appears to be a failure of forward-looking rationality. But one of my classmates introduced a counter-model she called "project-identity binding," which holds that rational agency over time is not merely a sequence of isolated utility-maximizations, but involves maintaining a coherent narrative continuity across temporal stages. On this view, abandoning a costly project solely because its costs are sunk might sever the continuity that makes past commitments genuinely the agent's own—yet continuing purely to "redeem" the expenditure might violate a different consistency condition, one that binds the agent to her future self's autonomy rather than to the past's financial footprint.

Could you explain the conceptual tension between these two consistency conditions? Specifically, I want to understand whether there is a principled boundary between a rational commitment device—in which honoring sunk costs serves as a credible signal to one's future self that present intentions will not be arbitrarily discarded—and an irrational escalation that confuses the value of a project with the psychological need to justify past losses. Please address whether "narrative continuity" can be distinguished from mere loss-aversion without collapsing into pure consequentialism or an arbitrary deference to prior expenditure, and whether accommodating this distinction requires revising the standard axioms of expected utility theory or only reinterpreting how an agent's preference ordering relates to her diachronic identity.
