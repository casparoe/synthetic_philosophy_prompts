As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and epistemology, I am drafting material for an upcoming feature on whether laboratory calibration practices expose hidden commitments in theories of justification. For a companion piece aimed at graduate readers, I am also designing an assessment for an upper-level epistemology seminar. I want an exam question—answerable in 90 minutes, in essay form—that does not allow students to earn marks by merely labeling foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. Instead, it should force them to apply these positions to a live circularity problem I have constructed: the “bootstrap detector” scenario, in which Particle Detector A is calibrated against Detector B, yet B’s initial reliability was established using an earlier iteration of A, so that no independent ground exists for either instrument’s baseline readings. The question should ask students to determine whether this circularity is best resolved by treating certain instrument outputs as epistemically basic (foundationalism), by allowing mutual coherence across the detector network to justify each node (coherentism), or by accepting an unending chain of successive calibration checks (infinitism), and it should require them to identify precisely where each strategy collapses under the bootstrap conditions—particularly whether infinitism genuinely escapes the circle or merely postpones it indefinitely at the cost of never achieving justification closure.

Please provide the exam prompt, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a detailed marking scheme that distinguishes an A response—one that maps each epistemic position onto the specific circular calibration loop, explains why coherentism risks circular reasoning unless coherence is supplemented by non-epistemic virtues, and shows that infinitism avoids termination but thereby sacrifices practical justification—from a B response that summarizes the three theories correctly but fails to apply them to the detector scenario or misses the trade-off between closure and circularity. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations in either the question or the model answer; the exercise should be answerable from conceptual reasoning alone, without reliance on memorized source texts. Additionally, include a brief reflective note stating what argument or empirical finding about scientific practice would change your own assessment of which position best handles the bootstrap paradox, and identify the specific point in your reasoning—such as the psychological feasibility of non-terminating justification chains or the hidden foundational commitments in coherence metrics—where it is most vulnerable to failure. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an examination setting.
