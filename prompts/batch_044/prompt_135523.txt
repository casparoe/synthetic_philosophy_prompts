Here is an argument I built by extending Parfit’s hitchhiker case, and I now think it proves far more than it should. (1) In the hitchhiker scenario, rationality requires committing in advance to pay the rescuer after being saved, even though once rescued there is no causal benefit to paying. (2) The driver makes this commitment because the rescuer conditions aid on predicting the driver’s future action. (3) So rational choice must sometimes depend on evidential correlations—on what my act reveals about my dispositions—rather than only on causal effects. (4) But evidential correlations run in both directions: my present mental states are also evidence about my past traits and choices. (5) Therefore a rational agent should revise beliefs about her own past behavior whenever doing so improves the predictive coherence of her self-model, since an incoherent self-model makes her a less reliable predictor of future good conduct. (6) By parallel reasoning, in any interaction where anonymity is imperfect—where identification is merely possible, not certain—the agent should still cooperate, because the small evidential risk of exposure makes defection irrational. (7) Thus rationality collapses into continuous self-signaling and retroactive belief-management, leaving no room for genuinely private, causally-driven choice. (8) This is absurd: rationality should not require me to falsify my own memories just to maintain a favorable predictive profile. Where does the overextension occur? Is the move from (2) to (3) illegitimate, or does (3) hold but (5) smuggle in a category error between being predicted by others and predicting oneself? I want to keep the idea that commitments can be rational even when causally useless, but I do not want to pay the price of universal self-manipulation. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
