Over the past year I have begun mediating disputes in a community restorative-justice program, and I have become struck by a practical distinction that seems largely absent from the philosophical literature I have been reading. Participants in these sessions regularly separate emotional reactive responses—resentment, indignation, or gratitude directed at an offender—from what I can only call structural accountability, which involves demands for institutional repair and relational restoration that persist even when emotional anger has subsided or was never present. This observation has led me back to the long-running dispute about P.F. Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes and its compatibility with determinism. For decades, compatibilists have maintained that attitudes such as resentment are essentially practical and interpersonal, and therefore remain rational even if determinism is true, because they do not presuppose that agents are uncaused causes; incompatibilists have countered that these same attitudes implicitly commit us to the false belief that agents are ultimate origins of their actions, so that determinism undermines not their existence but their fairness and justification. Despite extensive refinement on both sides—distinctions between being responsible and holding responsible, between reactive and objective attitudes, between desert and utility—the debate appears stalled, with each camp repeating structurally similar moves. My own hypothesis is that the impasse is not purely substantive but partly taxonomic or even verbal: the two sides may be using reactive attitude to cover both emotional dispositions and normative expectations of repair, so that compatibilists are asking whether emotional dispositions can be rationally sustained under determinism, while incompatibilists are asking whether normative demands for answerability can be justified if no one is an ultimate source. I ask you to diagnose the nature of this disagreement using what each side actually says—particularly about whether determinism threatens the fairness rather than the rationality of resentment, about whether reactive attitudes are revisable practical stances or constitutive conditions of interpersonal relationships, and about whether the objective attitude is a genuine alternative or merely a theoretical abstraction. Is this a hidden empirical disagreement about the psychology of blame, a verbal dispute about the scope of responsibility, a case of two different questions being pursued under one heading, or an ill-posed problem that should not be expected to yield progress? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates emotional blame from structural accountability, an empirical result in moral psychology, a reframing of the determinism question, or an argument that the stalemate is appropriate and should remain? Finally, please treat this framing with appropriate skepticism: if my premise that restorative justice reveals a hidden taxonomy in the philosophical debate is confused, or if asking for a single diagnosis of such a heterogeneous dispute is not something that can be done well, say so plainly instead of accommodating the request politely.
