Right, I'm trying to nail down my profile for a philosophy of value seminar and I'm not sure these actually hang together. Here's where I've landed on Moore-type stuff about beauty and friendship:

(1) I buy the organic-unity idea: the value of a beautiful landscape or of a friendship isn't just the sum of the parts' values. A friendship can be intrinsically good even if it produces no pleasure at all.

(2) I think beauty is a non-natural property we grasp by intuition, but friendship-value is partly natural — it depends on actual psychological relations like affection and mutual recognition.

(3) So the open-question argument works for 'beautiful' but I don't think it works for 'good friend', since friendship can be partly analysed in those natural terms.

(4) But I also think that if two people genuinely care for each other yet are both radically wrong about each other's characters, the friendship still has full intrinsic value. The affection's real, so the organic whole stays intact.

(5) I hold that aesthetic disagreement is always cognitive error — someone's intuition is off — whereas disagreement about whether a friendship is valuable is usually just weighing constituent goods differently.

(6) And I think beauty is fragile: drop one ugly element in and the whole value can collapse. Friendship is resilient — occasional dishonesty doesn't necessarily reduce its value.

Do these cohere? I'm especialy worried about (2) pulling against (4): if friendship-value depends on actual psychological relations, doesn't radical false belief about character undermine the relation? And (5) looks suspicious with (1) — if both are organic unities, why is one disagreement perceptual and the other just weighing? Is there also tension between (3) and (6), or is fragility vs resilience just compatible with the natural/non-natural split?

Can you audit these, rank the tensions by severity, flag which is only apparent, and say what's the least costly fix? I'd rather not drop organic unity if I can help it.
