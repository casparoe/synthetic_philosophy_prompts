There's this split in how hospitals handle life-or-death choices. In a mass-casualty event — like a bus crash — triage protocal tells staff to do quick fixes on the five people who can survive with minimal care, even if that means leaving the one person who needs a long surgery and might still die. The reasoning is obviously "save the greatest number." But in the regular ER, the same doctors don't aggregate at all: if there are ten people in line, they don't say the tenth person matters less because there are already nine; they treat each arrival as having a full, independent claim. So the institution seems to treat numbers as decisive in disasters but irrelevant in routine care.

What philosophical commitments does that split actually presuppose? Does the triage rule assume that moral reasons aggregate across people — that five separate claims somehow add up to one stronger claim — or can a view like Taurek's, where you just compare each person's claim pairwise and the five never collectively outweigh the one, explain why we do it? Maybe it's not about aggregation at all but about institutional role or tractability. Reconstruct the implicit theory: is there one coherent view behind both practices, or are they running on incompatible logics? And tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — what other theories could license the same split — and what triage would actually have to look like if we took seriously the idea that the numbers don't count, not just flipping a coin but redesigning the protocal from the ground up.
