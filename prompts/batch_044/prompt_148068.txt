After a lengthy debate with a colleague about distributive justice, I find myself unable to reconcile my own judgments about several societies I might enter behind a veil of ignorance. I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures all of them, or whether I must abandon one.

Consider these five institutional arrangements, evaluated without knowing which position I will occupy.

First, society Alpha maintains perfect equality of income and social status, but because incentives for productivity are absent, the worst-off live at bare subsistence with minimal healthcare and education. I judge this unacceptable.

Second, society Beta permits large income disparities—some earn fifty times what others earn—but only if those disparities arise from differences in productive contribution and effort, and the worst-off enjoy substantially greater absolute resources (excellent healthcare, education, leisure) than in Alpha. I judge this acceptable, indeed preferable to Alpha.

Third, society Gamma has the exact same absolute condition for the worst-off as Beta, and the same income ratios, but the disparities are inherited: capital and professional credentials pass almost entirely within families, and intergenerational mobility is near zero. Despite the identical bottom-line welfare, I find Gamma unacceptable in a way Beta is not.

Fourth, society Delta enforces near-complete equality in daily consumption, but allows a small group of risk-takers in medical and environmental research to accumulate vast rewards, provided that 100 percent of those rewards are taxed away and converted into universal public goods that disproportionately benefit the worst-off. The worst-off are slightly better off than in Beta. I judge Delta acceptable.

Fifth, society Epsilon mirrors Delta's structure—same tax rate, same worst-off welfare—but the rewards flow not from research outcomes but from zero-sum financial speculation that produces no social benefit. The worst-off are no worse off than in Delta, yet I feel Epsilon should be rejected, though I struggle to say exactly why without smuggling in a judgment about desert or naturalness that I claim to have bracketed behind the veil.

So my pattern is: reject Alpha, accept Beta, reject Gamma despite matching Beta's bottom line, accept Delta, and reject Epsilon despite matching Delta's bottom line. Is there one principle—perhaps a refined difference principle with an added constraint about the source or legitimacy of inequalities, or perhaps a prioritarian rule with a side-condition about mobility and social meaning—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the cleaner principle predict for a case I have not listed: a society where the worst-off are maximally well-off, but only because an unaccountable algorithm assigns every individual to a social role at birth, with no possibility of appeal?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my judgments it cannot accommodate, and explain what it predicts for the algorithmic-assignment society.
