For a graduate political-philosophy seminar, construct a taxonomy tree that sorts positions in the liberalism–communitarianism debate by conceptual commitments rather than by familiar labels. Use three binary diagnostic splits: (i) Is individual freedom conceptually prior to communal belonging, or is it constituted by membership in a particular form of life? (ii) Is deep value pluralism a permanent feature that just institutions must accommodate, a provisional failure that good politics should eventually overcome, or an illusion that conceals an underlying shared ethos? (iii) When communal obligations conflict with an individual’s desire to exit—say, leaving a religious community that mandates collective worship—does the right to exit take unconditional priority, depend on demonstrable harm to those left behind, or not exist at all?

Place the following named positions at leaves: Rawlsian political liberalism, perfectionist liberalism, civic republicanism, Taylor-style recognition communitarianism, MacIntyre-style tradition-constituted communitarianism, and civic-nationalist communitarianism. Add two unnamed leaves for logical combinations that remain unoccupied, and for each diagnose in one sentence whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Every leaf must carry a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment—e.g., that it must treat a particular communal practice as either sacred or disposable with no stable middle ground. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate.

Present the result in four ordered sections: (1) The three split-questions, each illustrated by the concrete case that makes positions diverge (~120 words); (2) The tree with all eight leaves mapped to their answers and awkward commitments (~250 words); (3) Unoccupied spaces and separation failures (~120 words); (4) A brief note on how this taxonomy differs from standard textbook comparisons of liberalism and communitarianism (~80 words). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should work entirely from conceptual structure.
