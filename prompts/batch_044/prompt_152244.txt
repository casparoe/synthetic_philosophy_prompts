I am preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of logic and the structure of deductive justification. For our session on Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," please compose a rigorous philosophical dialogue between two interlocutors—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Bex Morrow—who disagree about whether Carroll's regress demonstrates a genuine gap in the justification of deduction. Both should be equally competent: neither should be a straw figure. Dr. Thorne should defend the position that the Tortoise's refusal to apply modus ponens reveals that logical rules must be treated as premises requiring propositional assent, generating an unavoidable hierarchy of meta-rules unless one accepts an irreducible practical capacity at the base. Dr. Morrow should counter that this misdiagnoses the problem: applying a rule is an exercise of cognitive competence, not an inference from a belief about the rule, so Carroll's regress arises only from a category mistake that treats practical mastery as propositional knowledge. The exchange must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should directly address the other's specific moves, concede minor points where appropriate, and hold firm on the central issue without talking past one another.

To keep the discussion from becoming a stale rehearsal of standard pragmatism versus rationalism, inject the following conceptual distinction and have both characters wrestle with it: the difference between inferential entitlement (the normative status that licenses one to draw a conclusion) and inferential endorsement (the explicit propositional acceptance of a rule as an additional premise). Have them debate whether the Tortoise lacks entitlement, lacks endorsement, or whether the distinction itself collapses under pressure. They should directly address the moment in Carroll's narrative where Achilles asks the Tortoise to accept the hypothetical premise linking the premises to the conclusion, and the Tortoise demands yet another premise. The dialogue should be approximately 900 words, philosophically precise, and accessible to students who have read Carroll's dialogue and understand basic propositional logic. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand entirely on its own conceptual resources.
