I hold the procedural-commitment resolution of Buridan’s ass: when an agent faces two options with exactly equal first-order value, rationality does not require randomization or paralysis, but rather the activation of a stable second-order rule—say, a canonical left-first ordering—that breaks the tie deterministically. I take this meta-rule to be constitutive of rational agency rather than an external crutch, because an agent who refuses every tie-breaking procedure is not more rational but self-defeating, especially in sequential settings where unresolved ties compound and foreclose future paths. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness: formal results showing that any deterministic tie-breaker is vulnerable to exploitation or generates Dutch-book-style inconsistencies across compound choices; empirical or modeled patterns in human and artificial decision-making that show tie-breaking is governed by context-dependent noise rather than by a stable procedural commitment; conceptual arguments that selecting a meta-rule solely for determinacy collapses into disguised randomness once the canonical ordering is recognized as arbitrary; and coordination or social-choice cases where applying a private canonical ordering yields outcomes that are Pareto-dominated or collectively irrational. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders can always retreat to the claim that the meta-rule is arbitrary-but-acceptable, and whether that retreat renders the position unfalsifiable—if so, is that a defect for a normative theory of rational choice, or is insulation appropriate here? I would find it most damaging if the commitment turned out to be unstable under recursive self-modification: if an agent can foresee that her canonical rule will drive her into a dead end in some reachable state space, and she can then revise the rule, then the commitment was never genuinely binding, and the work is being done by a hidden preference for survival or flexibility rather than by procedural determinacy itself. Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed proceduralist should concede from what they should dismiss, and say whether the view risks being qualified into emptiness.
