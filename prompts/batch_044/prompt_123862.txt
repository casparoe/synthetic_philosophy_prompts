I'm serving on a state sentencing review board that meets monthly to evaluate petitions for sentence reduction. Most members have legal training but little formal philosophy, and we have about thirty minutes per case with a written justification required from the chair. In a recent seminar on penal theory I became convinced that the standard hybrid approach—giving some weight to moral transformation and some weight to recidivism statistics—commits a category error: rehabilitative evidence is not a single currency that can be traded between backward-looking desert and forward-looking risk reduction. Desert tracks culpability fixed at the time of offense and the expressive meaning of the sentence already imposed; risk reduction tracks the agent's present and predicted future state. To pool them is to double-count one narrative of change.

Please turn this separation principle into a reusable decision procedure my board could actually run in thirty minutes. Structure it as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a recommendation against reduction, and where it explicitly hands the decision to the chair as a judgment of values rather than fact. It must distinguish strategic remorse from genuine transformation without requiring psychological expertise; flag predictive data confounded by age, selection effects, or programming refusal; and handle direct conflicts between the two tracks. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and include a brief note on where the procedure will systematically misfire.

Then apply it to three petitions: (a) a violent offender who completed extensive therapy and shows a sharply dropped risk score, but whose original offense involved high culpability and whose remorse the board suspects is performative; (b) a low-culpability property offender who refuses all rehabilitative programming but whose statistical risk is minimal; (c) a long-term inmate whose risk model predicts low recidivism primarily because of advancing age, not because of demonstrated attitudinal change. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a recommendation I will find unwelcome—likely granting reduction when intuition resists, or denying it when mercy suggests otherwise—and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle.
