I have just concluded a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about David Hume’s essay Of Suicide—the one he composed in the 1750s and that circulated only after his death. We kept circling the same section, and I would like help seeing whether my reading or his is closer to what the text actually supports.

In the part where Hume answers the charge that suicide violates nature, he asks us to consider a person who diverts a small stream from a great river. He appears to argue that just as this diversion does not offend the natural order—the water still obeys gravity and motion, the river is not destroyed, the cosmos remains intact—so a person who voluntarily ends his own life does not violate nature. The body still decays according to physical law; the agent has merely redirected the course of one particular life rather than overturned the general system that governs all living things. From there he moves to claim that such an act is no more injurious to society or to providence than many ordinary self-regarding choices that no one condemns.

My friend objected that the analogy fails precisely because diverting a stream is reversible, whereas suicide is not. He said we tolerate the stream-diverter only because he can restore the original course; death, by contrast, offers no remedy, so its irreversibility ought to count against it rather than in its favor. I countered that Hume might actually be treating finality as part of the justification—if the act is irreversible, then there is no future subject who remains burdened by it, and therefore no lingering party who can claim to have been wronged. But I worry that I am importing a modern concern about option-value and temporal neutrality that the passage does not contain.

Can you help me understand what work the river-diversion analogy is actually doing? Is Hume claiming that suicide is permissible because it is merely one more neutral, and in principle correctable, modification of one’s natural condition? Or is he implicitly relying on the act’s irreversibility to show that no subsequent harm can accrue? And if the latter, does the passage offer any principled distinction between permissible irreversible acts—those that eliminate suffering without leaving a wronged survivor—and impermissible ones, or does the argument depend on a boundary between ruin and mere inconvenience that Hume never actually draws?
