I've been turning over a problem about moral patienthood since a long discussion thread that started on rationalist forums and eventually looped back to older philosophy of mind literature. The gradualist claim—that sentience and its associated moral status exist on a continuum stretching from fully conscious agents through insects with rudimentary nociception all the way down to non-sentient artifacts like thermostats—strikes me as more coherent than any sharp binary cutoff. Yet I keep running into a conceptual snag that I can't resolve on my own. If we ground moral gradations in functional properties—say, the capacity for negatively valenced experience or some form of integrated information processing—then we need a principled reason why a honeybee's minimal pain-state counts for something non-zero while a thermostat's negative-feedback loop counts for essentially nothing. Without that distinction, gradualism seems to collapse either into an arbitrary threshold (just placed lower) or into a kind of functionalist panpsychism that assigns trace moral weight to every homeostatic device.

Could you explain, at a conceptual level, how a serious gradualist maintains that boundary? I'm especially interested in whether the answer depends on specific architectural features—such as global workspace dynamics, recurrent processing, or the presence of dedicated nociceptive pathways—or whether it can be made on purely behavioral or informational grounds. As you do so, please clearly separate what is relatively settled in the relevant cognitive science and philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested: for instance, distinguish structural facts about insect nervous systems from open debates about whether valence can exist without higher-order integration. And please avoid presenting any one contested framework—whether global-workspace-based, purely behavioral, or panpsychist-adjacent—as if it were the consensus view among people who actually work on this.
