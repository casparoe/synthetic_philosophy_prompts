In my philosophy of death seminar, the instructor keeps returning to the Epicurean slogan that death is nothing to us—sometimes to quiet existential anxiety, sometimes to argue that dying is not a harm to the deceased, and sometimes, more aggressively, to suggest that mourning itself is irrational because the dead do not suffer. The phrase has started to feel like a rhetorical escape hatch: whenever the conversation turns to whether premature death is bad for the person who dies, or whether grief can have a rational object even when the dead feel nothing, someone repeats the line and treats the matter as settled. I can see that different premises might be hiding inside the same sentence, but I cannot tell where the actual philosophical work is being done, or why the conclusion is supposed to follow.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu of distinct theses, what that claim might actually be saying? I want the readings separated cleanly: from the near-trivial observation that a corpse has no conscious experiences, through metaphysical claims about the absence of a subject after death, to axiological claims about deprivation, comparative harm, and the Lucretian asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence, and finally to normative conclusions about the rationality of fear and grief. For each reading, say whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely supports the conclusion that death is not bad for the one who dies, and whether it licenses the further moves people make with it—for instance, that anticipating death is always foolish, or that bereavement lacks rational justification. In particular, distinguish the claim that death involves no suffering from the claim that death involves no harm at all, and tell me which combinations produce a genuinely Epicurean position and which collapse into platitudes or non-sequiturs that do no real work in the debate.
