After a long argument with a friend about whether punishment can ever be genuinely deserved, I want to sharpen the comparison between Daniel Dennett's compatibilist defense of moral responsibility and Gregg Caruso's skeptical rejection of basic desert. Specifically, consider an offender whose actions flow from fully reasons-responsive psychological mechanisms, yet those mechanisms were entirely engineered by external intervention—imagine a precisely calibrated neural restoration that makes the agent responsive to moral reasons without any prior history of autonomous development. For Dennett, does the presence of functional reasons-responsiveness suffice to ground retributive blame, or does his account implicitly require a historical condition of natural development that this engineered case violates? For Caruso, if no one is the ultimate source of their actions, does the artificial origin of this agent's capacities strengthen the case for treating him purely as a risk to be managed, or does it reveal that functional architecture rather than causal origin should determine our social response? Compare the two views on this scenario, and say which position more consistently handles the engineered offender without either smuggling in an unearned historical requirement or collapsing into pure consequentialism about social control.
