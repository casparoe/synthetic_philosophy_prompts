For my graduate seminar on distributive justice, I am constructing a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—to classify normative positions on inheritance taxation, dynastic wealth, and fair equality of opportunity. The instrument should sort views by the concrete judgments they make about cases and mechanisms, not by the political or philosophical labels their proponents prefer. Build the tree from the logical space of commitments, placing named positions only at the leaves, and phrase every branch node as a direct question a student could answer without already knowing which position it produces.

The root question should be: does the intergenerational transmission of substantial economic advantage, considered as a social practice, constitute a structural impediment to fair equality of opportunity, or is it a permissible outcome of individual liberty?

From there, split the tree using these distinctions and case nodes. First, distinguish two mechanisms of dynastic transmission that must be treated separately: capital-lock inheritance (the direct bequest of liquid assets, property, and equity) and status-lock inheritance (the transmission of social networks, exclusive educational access, and institutional gatekeeping advantages that are structurally equivalent to capital but not legally bequeathed). The taxonomy should ask whether each mechanism, considered in isolation, violates equality of opportunity.

Then include these branch points:
1. Should the baseline for assessing opportunity be evaluated within a single life-course (intragenerational equality) or tracked across family lines over successive generations (intergenerational equality)?
2. Consider a hypothetical in which capital-lock inheritance is fully abolished through taxation, but status-lock inheritance remains entirely intact. Would this outcome satisfy the demands of justice? This node should expose whether a view targets wealth per se or the structural advantages it produces.
3. Is the primary wrong of dynastic wealth that it violates moral arbitrariness regarding circumstances of birth (a luck-egalitarian concern), or that it distorts democratic political equality by concentrating influence?
4. A concrete institutional case: a founder liquidates a business, pays all statutory taxes, and endows a private foundation that provides exclusive internships and mentorship exclusively to the descendants and peers of her family network. Does this arrangement count as an instance of objectionable dynastic wealth?

The leaves must include at least the following: strict abolitionist (all substantial intergenerational economic transmission is unjust); meritocratic reset (inheritance is permissible only if it does not alter competitive positions in social cooperation); capital-only restrictionist (capital-lock must be eliminated, but status-lock falls outside the scope of state remedy); democratic-egalitarian (inheritance is objectionable only when it translates into disproportionate political influence); and dynastic libertarian (no restriction on either form of transmission is justified).

Identify any combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf—such as a view that condemns capital-lock but endorses status-lock on democratic-egalitarian grounds—and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere lack of exploration.

Every node must be a concrete claim or case judgment; do not organize branches by citing named theorists. Present the tree so that a student answers the questions first, then sees which position the pattern corresponds to. At each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of that position’s most awkward or counterintuitive commitment.

Finally, conclude with a brief methodological reflection: state explicitly what argument or empirical finding would change your classification—such as evidence that market mechanisms fully convert status-lock advantages into capital-lock advantages, or proof that democratic equality and fair opportunity necessarily diverge in all institutional settings—and identify the specific point in your reasoning where the separation between two leaves is least secure.
