I spent last evening in a long argument with a friend about panpsychism and the combination problem—specifically, about which composite systems possess a unified macro-subject over and above their micro-experiential parts. She is not a professional philosopher, but she has worked through the standard literature and has developed a consistent, if somewhat idiosyncratic, set of rulings across hypothetical cases. To settle our disagreement, I want to see whether her track record genuinely determines her answer to a new scenario I have constructed, or whether it underdetermines the answer and forces her to adopt a further principle she has not yet articulated.

Here are her verdicts so far, as she has stated them across our discussions over the past year:

1. A neurologically typical human brain contains one unified macro-consciousness, constituted by but not reducible to the experiences of its constituent micro-subjects.

2. A mature social-insect colony—say, a large hive of honeybees—contains no unified colony-consciousness; only the individual insects are subjects of experience, however minimal those experiences may be.

3. A split-brain patient after complete corpus callosotomy, with an intact brainstem and thalamus but no interhemispheric connection, still harbors a single macro-subject; the macro-experience does not bifurcate, even though specific cognitive functions are internally disunified.

4. A conventional supercomputer running one distributed algorithm across servers on three continents, linked by fiber-optic cable with negligible processing delay but with light-speed transmission lags of tens of milliseconds, possesses no macro-subject, regardless of how integrated its information processing becomes.

5. A synthetic neural mesh fully implanted within one human cranium, interfacing continuously with native cortical tissue and sharing a continuous electromagnetic field, but composed largely of non-biological nodes each running minimal phenomenal processes, does contain one unified macro-consciousness that genuinely includes the synthetic nodes; substrate is irrelevant provided the integration is continuous and spatially contained.

These five rulings seem to me jointly compatible with more than one underlying principle—perhaps one emphasizing physical contiguity and containment within a single biological or bio-hybrid boundary, perhaps one emphasizing causal-temporal integration within the characteristic window of conscious experience regardless of spatial distribution, perhaps one emphasizing the presence of a direct phenomenal-bonding relation rather than mere functional or causal connection.

The new case is this. A human subject undergoes gradual neuron-for-neuron replacement with functionally identical synthetic analogues, preserving architectural integration exactly. Once replacement is complete, the synthetic tissue is physically divided into three separate life-support vats distributed across different rooms, with no shared container, membrane, or biological boundary enclosing them. However, the three modules maintain continuous high-bandwidth wireless communication with latencies consistently below five milliseconds—well within the temporal window usually associated with conscious integration—so that the informational structure that originally constituted the macro-subject is preserved across the dispersion, without any spatial contiguity or single enclosing boundary.

Given her precedents, what should my friend say about whether there is one unified macro-subject in this dispersed-vat scenario? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and does that analog genuinely settle the case? If her track record underdetermines the answer, identify the two most plausible rules that jointly explain all five prior rulings, state precisely what each predicts about the dispersed-vat case, and say what substantive principle she would be adopting by choosing one prediction over the other. Finally, specify the further hypothetical case—one that differs from the dispersed-vat scenario in only a single controlled respect—that would force a decision between those two rules and reveal which principle she actually holds. Please do not supply a bibliography or reading list; I am asking for reasoning strictly from the commitments she has already displayed.
