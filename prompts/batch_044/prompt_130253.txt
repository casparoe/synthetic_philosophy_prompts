I am a high school student preparing remarks for a philosophy discussion group, and I have become stuck on a sentence our instructor repeats constantly: that freedom is essentially the condition of not being subject to anyone's arbitrary will. She applies it to standard cases—laws that prohibit versus laws that enable—but also, more surprisingly, to claim that people who rely on predictive recommendation algorithms are in a kind of republican unfreedom even when the algorithm never issues a command or blocks an option. Before I present to the group, I need the sentence taken apart into its possible precise meanings. Could you write, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, as though composing a long letter, a careful separation of the different theses hidden in that slogan? I want to see the trivial reading that simply says nobody likes being bossed around; the negative-liberty reading that equates freedom with the absence of actual interference; the republican reading that freedom requires the absence of domination whether or not interference occurs; and the stricter republican variant that domination requires not just unchecked power but an active capacity and disposition to interfere. For each, please indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible philosophical claim, or false, and tell me specifically which of these interpretations actually supports extending the idea of domination to invisible algorithmic influence, and which ones make that extension look like a category mistake. I ask this because I want to know whether my instructor is invoking one coherent doctrine or sliding between several.
