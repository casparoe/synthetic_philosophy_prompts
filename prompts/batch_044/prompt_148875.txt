Write a dialogue between two equally informed historians of late antiquity, Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen, who hold opposing views on macrohistorical contingency. Vasquez defends a fragility thesis: she argues that the assassination of the Roman magister militum Stilicho around 408 CE was a genuine “for want of a nail” moment, because his death eliminated a unique personal network that could have maintained federated military loyalty and prevented the rapid collapse of imperial administration in the Western provinces. Chen defends a redundant-pathway robustness thesis: he contends that by the early fifth century the Western Empire’s fiscal base, its dependence on barbarian foederati, and its fragmented command structure had already created multiple independent failure pathways, so that even had Stilicho survived, some comparable rupture—whether through court intrigue, provincial rebellion, or fiscal default—would have produced essentially the same fragmentation within a generation. In their exchange, they should directly engage each other’s points rather than talk past one another. Specifically, they should clarify whether their disagreement is primarily empirical (about the actual substitutability of Stilicho’s leadership and the empire’s institutional reserves) or conceptual (about whether “contingency” in history should be modeled after sensitive dependence on initial conditions in nonlinear dynamics, or after narrative counterfactual dependence in historiography). The dialogue should be rigorous, avoid strawman arguments, and end without resolution, but with both parties having precisely located the source of their disagreement.
