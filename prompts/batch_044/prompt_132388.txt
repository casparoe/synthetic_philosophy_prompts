I retired from experimental physics about five years ago and since then I've been hanging around with a philosophy group that meets at the local libary. We've been arguing about belief — specifically the lottery paradox and that preface situation where an author says he stands behind every claim in his book but also admits there's probably an error somewhere. I've landed on a view that both puzzles are the same artifact: they only arise if you treat belief as binary and insist it be closed under deduction. Instead, I think rational agents use something like layered working acceptance — you can treat a specific claim as operative for reasoning or action without aggregating that commitment across every logical consequence. Science does this all the time: engineers use quantum mechanics for chip design even though they know it's not literally true in every domain. So neither paradox undermines rationality; they just show that binary belief is the wrong model, and that acceptance is context-sensitive in a way that doesn't sum the same way.

But I want to stress-test this, not just defend it. What would actually count against this layered-acceptance account? I'm not looking for the usual hand-waving about contextualism being messy — I want the serious defeaters. Are there formal results in epistemic logic showing that any acceptance relation that relaxes closure either violates some other rationality constraint or collapses back into a threshold view? Is there empirical work in psychology or behavioral economics — maybe on how people actually handle contradictions in reasoning — that suggests this layered picture is definately descriptively wrong? And here's something specific: if layered acceptance is right, then finding one mistake in a scientific framework shouldn't automatically collapse the whole working acceptance, but is there evidence that in practice scientists do sometimes abandon a theory over a single anomaly in ways that look more like binary belief? I'd like you to rank these challenges from fatal to merely awkward, and to be honest about whether defenders of this view can just keep adding qualifications until it's unfalsifiable — and whether that insulation is actually a problem for a philosophical theory of belief, or just business as usual in science. Also, please don't just list books I should read; I want the arguments and evidence themselves, sorted by how much they should move someone like me.
