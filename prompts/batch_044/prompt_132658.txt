A philosopher of mind I discuss these questions with has been working through whether mental states that occur during sleep or profoundly altered consciousness count as experiences in the philosophically relevant sense — the kind of issue that divides Malcolm-style conceptual arguments (centered on criteria of report and retrospective attribution) from Dennett-style accounts (centered on functional narrative integration, whether continuous or loaded at transition). She has given me her verdicts on five cases, and I want to extrapolate her implicit framework.

Her track record:
1. Ordinary REM sleep: the sleeper reports a complex narrative immediately upon awakening, but made no concurrent signals, had no working-memory access during the state, and describes the report as "like remembering a movie I didn't watch in real time." Verdict: not experienced during the sleep; the narrative is a retrospective construction upon awakening.
2. Lucid REM with pre-arranged eye-movement signaling: the subject signals "now flying, now falling" with precise temporal markers, and upon waking the report aligns exactly with the signal log. Verdict: genuinely experienced during the state.
3. Sleepwalking with eyes open and complex motor execution: appropriate responses to obstacles, but upon waking complete denial of any experience and zero episodic retrieval. Verdict: no experience occurred during the episode.
4. General-anesthesia awareness claim: the patient reports hearing a specific conversation, but intraoperative EEG shows flatline cortical patterns, no arousal markers, and the report contains details that do not match the actual conversation. Verdict: not experienced during surgery; confabulated false memory.
5. Deep meditative absorption with intermittent finger-tap signals: the meditator taps every ~90 seconds to indicate sustained presence, and upon emerging describes a "contentless but luminous" state precisely bounded by the signal intervals, with no narrative content but clear source-monitoring. Verdict: experienced during the absorption, though not structured like a dream or waking perception.

Now the new case: a hypothetical temporary "mnemonic bridge" implant detects narrative-generating patterns in temporal and limbic regions during REM and writes compressed episodic traces directly into prefrontal storage, but actively suppresses all working-memory buffers, global workspace activation, and motor/signaling outputs. The subject wakes with a vivid, coherent dream report that precisely matches the implant's timestamped log. Intra-sleep fMRI and EEG show no prefrontal or parietal activation, no global integration signatures, and no capacity for concurrent reporting. The subject confidently attributes the narrative to the REM period and can distinguish it from waking imagination.

Predict her verdict on the implant case. Then lay out at least two other rules that generate her first five verdicts just as well — for instance, one that makes concurrent access or reportability the criterion, one that makes sustained local or global functional integration the criterion, and one that makes post-state autobiographical attribution with appropriate source-monitoring the criterion — and say what each predicts here. Give your best guess at which rule she is actually running, with the reasoning. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case — perhaps involving partial suppression, split-hemisphere reporting, or cross-state memory contamination — that would best distinguish the rules that diverge on the implant case, without depending on any specific empirical literature I would need to look up.
