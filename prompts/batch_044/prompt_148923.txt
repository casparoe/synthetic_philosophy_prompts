Exam question:
Consider a municipal ordinance requiring all licensed public-school cafeterias to arrange serving lines so that plant-based entrees occupy the first 70 percent of visible shelf space and the primary serving stations, while meat-based options remain available in a separate, less prominent station with no price change and no prohibition. The ordinance explicitly states its purpose: to reduce carbon-intensive consumption by leveraging the default effect and visual salience bias. Critics call this “architectural coercion”; defenders call it “transparent soft paternalism.”

Analyze the ordinance using three specific frames:
(1) Joel Feinberg’s distinction between hard and soft paternalism, especially his conditions that interference must target actions that are not fully voluntary and must be proportionate to the failure of voluntariness;
(2) Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein’s framework of libertarian paternalism, including whether their transparency condition is satisfied when the choice architecture is legislatively public but the point-of-decision layout is not explicitly labeled;
(3) The distinction between “channel factors” (minor environmental adjustments that leave the broader decision framework intact) and “structural nudges” (systemic redesigns of the choice environment that alter the framework within which preferences are formed). Is this ordinance a channel factor or a structural nudge, and why does that classification matter for assessing whether autonomy is genuinely respected?

In your answer, identify the strongest reason to permit the ordinance and the strongest reason to reject it.

Student answer:
This is clearly soft paternalism, not hard paternalism, because meat is still available; customers just have to walk to a different station. Under Feinberg, soft paternalism is okay when people are not acting with full rationality. Here, diners are influenced by visual salience bias, so their choice to pick meat is not fully rational. That means the choice is not fully voluntary in Feinberg’s sense, so interference is permitted. The interference is also proportionate because nobody is actually forced.

Under Thaler and Sunstein, this is a classic nudge: freedom of choice is preserved, so the libertarian condition is met. Their transparency condition says nudges should not be hidden. Since the ordinance is public law, everyone can look it up, so the condition is satisfied.

On the third point, this is a channel factor, not a structural nudge. It only changes the order of food on shelves and which station is closer—small tweaks like putting fruit near a cashier. Channel factors don’t reshape the whole system, so they don’t threaten autonomy in any deep way. That is why the distinction matters: structural nudges change the framework of decision-making itself, but this just adjusts presentation.

The best reason to allow it is that it corrects a cognitive bias without using coercion. The best reason to reject it is that exploiting people’s biases is manipulative and fails to treat them as rational agents, even if the options technically remain open.
