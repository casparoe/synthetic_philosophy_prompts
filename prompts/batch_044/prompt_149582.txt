I've been thinking on my own about those philosophy puzzles where a person is secretly monitored and would be forced to act if they tried to do otherwise, but they go ahead and do it on their own — I read somewhere these are called Frankfurt cases. The usual claim is that they disprove the idea that you need to have been able to do otherwise in order to be truly blamed. But I'm wondering whether that conclusion is too quick. There's a distinction people draw between having an escape route (leeway) and having your action come genuinely from your own mind (sourcehood). Some philosophers argue that sourcehood is enough, even without escape routes. But I keep thinking about cases where someone's brain was gradually altered over years so that they eventually fully own a harmful desire — they identify with it, it feels like theirs — yet the origin was clearly engineered. If sourcehood alone is enough, wouldn't we have to blame them? And if we don't blame them, doesn't that mean we secretly still care about whether they could have avoided the engineering?

Reach a clear conclusion: either argue that sourcehood is sufficient and explain why engineered identification doesn't excuse, or defend a revised principle that retains some form of alternative possibilities specifically to block responsibility in engineered-identity cases.
