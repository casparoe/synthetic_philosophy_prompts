Consider the following argument, which I have been turning over and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems intolerable, yet every premise looks hard to deny.

(1) For any biological system that exhibits nociceptive reflexes without demonstrable global integration of information, the epistemic probability that it is phenomenally conscious is not literally zero, but some small epsilon greater than zero.

(2) Insects exhibit nociceptive reflexes without clear global integration; hence, for any particular insect, the probability that it is a conscious subject is epsilon.

(3) At any given time, there are on the order of 10^19 individual insects alive.

(4) Therefore, the expected number of conscious insects is epsilon multiplied by 10^19.

(5) Even if epsilon is astronomically small—say, 10^-12—the expected number exceeds 10 million, a population comparable to that of all humans.

(6) Moral reasons against causing suffering apply in proportion to the expected number of subjects multiplied by the expected intensity of suffering per subject.

(7) Consequently, unless epsilon is exactly zero, the aggregate expected suffering of insects should dominate our moral calculus, far outweighing the suffering of mammals or humans.

(8) The very same reasoning applies, with appropriate adjustments to epsilon, to nematodes—of which there may be 10^22 individuals with pathways analogous to nociception—and even to the roughly 86 billion neurons in a single human brain, each carrying some vanishing, non-zero chance of being an independent micro-subject of experience.

(9) Taken together, these premises seem to entail that we are required to avoid walking on soil, using antibiotics, or sustaining brain processes that suppress neuronal firing, since each act risks harming vast expected populations.

(10) These obligations are not merely burdensome; they are impossible to satisfy consistently, and intuitively absurd.

(11) Therefore, at least one of steps (1) through (9) must fail.

Tell me which step I should reject, and explain precisely why that step initially seemed defensible. Then price the alternatives: if I reject step (6), what theory of moral aggregation or epistemic risk do I commit to? If I reject step (1) by insisting epsilon is literally zero for insects, what burden of proof do I take on regarding other organisms? If I reject step (8) by blocking the extension to neurons or nematodes, what principled boundary do I need, and does it risk being ad hoc? I would prefer to keep (6) and (8) intact, but I want an honest account of what I pay for that preference. Please answer in American English.
