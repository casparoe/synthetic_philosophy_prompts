I am currently assisting with a policy review for a municipal heritage centre, and I have become genuinely unsettled by a conceptual dispute that arose during our last deliberation session. The practical question concerns whether the institution should enter into a funding arrangement that requires altering its curatorial stance on contested colonial artefacts—specifically, displaying a particular object within an interpretive framework designed by representatives of the affected community, rather than either suppressing it entirely or presenting it within a conventional academic narrative. Two of my colleagues, neither of whom is asking me to take sides on the policy itself, have nevertheless pressed me to help them map the conceptual fault lines, and their exchange has struck me as going to the heart of whether moral values across traditions can ever genuinely be traded without corruption.

The first colleague opens by arguing that moral values are not fungible commodities, and that describing this arrangement as yielding "gains from trade" is a fundamental category error. She contends that if one value system regards the display of the object as an intrinsic violation of dignity, then contextualising it within an indigenous critical framework does not constitute a compromise but rather a corruption: the dignity-based commitment is not partially satisfied but fundamentally betrayed, since the object remains visible and the refusal to suppress—precisely what that system demands—is not honoured. The second colleague replies that this treats value commitments as atomic and non-negotiable, which risks a form of essentialism. He proposes that value systems contain internal resources for what he terms "constitutive revision"—not dilution but restructuring—whereby the affected community might prioritise narrative sovereignty over absolute suppression. He frames the arrangement as a form of "moral arbitrage": the institution gains ethical legitimacy and access to underrepresented audiences it could not secure alone, while the community gains authoritative interpretive control rather than silent exclusion. The first colleague responds that calling this "arbitrage" presupposes a consequentialist metric of exchange that privileges measurable outcomes over integrity, and that not every refusal to bargain is essentialism; some are boundary-setting acts that protect a value system from being translated into terms it does not recognise. The second colleague counters that boundary-setting is itself a strategic posture, not a metaphysical fact, and asks whether the refusal to compromise actually advances the practical interests of those whose dignity is invoked, or merely preserves the purity of the refusing party's self-conception at the expense of goods that could be secured.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the six arguments (the initial category-error claim about fungibility; the corruption-versus-betrayal objection; the essentialism charge paired with constitutive revision; the moral-arbitrage proposal of mutual gain; the boundary-setting defence against consequentialist presupposition; and the final strategic-posture reply regarding purity versus practical goods) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Identify specifically who is right about which point, and say what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether a principled distinction can be sustained between instrumental compromise, where each side receives more of its pre-existing values, and constitutive compromise, where the values themselves are partially redefined through agreement, and whether sustaining that distinction inevitably collapses back into either pure consequentialism or pure deontology. I am not seeking a recommendation on the policy itself, but rather an assessment of where the conceptual fault lines actually lie.
