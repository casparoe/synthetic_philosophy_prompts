Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion threatens to dissolve the coherence of global anti-corruption efforts, yet each premise feels defensible on its own. (1) Corruption is not merely breaking a rule, but the subversion of an institution’s constitutive purpose by private interest. (2) In societies where clientelism prevails, the bureaucratic role is not differentiated from kinship obligations; reciprocally directing state resources to one’s network is part of what makes one a legitimate official, not a betrayal of the office. (3) Therefore, in those contexts, distributing goods to kin fulfills rather than subverts the constitutive norms of the role. (4) Western anti-corruption frameworks treat impartial rule-application as the sole constitutive norm of bureaucracy and classify any network-directed distribution as corruption. (5) Yet Western democratic politics routinely directs public goods to constituents, donors, and allies through earmarking, patronage appointments, and regulated lobbying—practices permitted because they serve the constitutive purpose of democratic representation. (6) So Western frameworks implicitly rely on a distinction between legitimate political loyalty and illegitimate private loyalty, but they provide no principled, non-circular criterion for drawing that line that does not already presuppose Western institutional norms. (7) If corruption is defined universally—as, say, the use of public office for private gain—then applying the definition requires knowing which gains count as corrupting “private” ones, which in turn requires knowing the local constitutive norms. (8) Consequently, any universal application either misclassifies legitimate local practices as corruption by importing Western norms, or collapses into cultural relativism by accepting all local norms. (9) Therefore, universal anti-corruption law is either incoherent or parochial.

I want to preserve steps (3) and (6)—that local norms can render clientelism constitutive and that Western frameworks lack a non-circular criterion—but I do not want to accept (9). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a hidden equivocation between “private interest” in (1) and “private loyalty” in (6)? Or is the error in (7), by assuming that a functional universal definition necessarily requires full local normative knowledge rather than a thinner cross-cultural standard? Or should I give up (2) and accept that kin obligations always compete with rather than constitute the bureaucratic role? Please diagnose the argument in American English: name the specific step or suppressed premise that fails, explain why it initially looked sound, and price the alternatives—especially what I must surrender if I reject (2) versus (6) versus (7).
