I work in software engineering with no formal background in philosophy, but I have settled on a particular interpretation of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox that I want stress-tested. I hold that these cases do not expose a flaw in classical logic or in the probability calculus, but rather reveal a genuine structural feature of rational belief: it is fully rational to assign high confidence to each individual claim in a very large set—such as that each of a thousand lottery tickets will lose, or that each factual assertion in a lengthy technical book is correct—while rationally withholding confidence from the corresponding universal conjunction, and that this is not logical inconsistency but the correct operation of epistemic modesty. I further think that attempts to dissolve the tension by imposing a precise numerical threshold for belief, or by revising the logic of belief closure, are misguided accommodations that miss the underlying point. The pattern strikes me as structurally analogous to reliability engineering in distributed systems: individual nodes can each be 99.99 percent reliable without the cluster as a whole being 99.99 percent reliable, because failures are not independent and aggregate risk scales differently from per-unit risk. So my position is that non-aggregative belief is not a bug to be solved but a necessary feature of rational epistemology, and that the paradoxes only arise from an illegitimate assumption that belief must be closed under conjunction.

What would genuinely count against this view? I want the arguments, formal results, thought experiments, or empirical findings that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be versus merely awkward. Specifically: are there consistent formal frameworks—perhaps non-classical logics, revised probability calculi, or coherence constraints—that resolve both paradoxes without ad hoc restrictions, and if so, do they avoid the cost of abandoning belief closure entirely? Would empirical studies of expert judgment show that people actually maintain this kind of non-aggregative structure in practice, or do they tend to resolve the tension by lowering individual credences rather than rejecting the conjunction? Is there a demonstration that my analogy to distributed-system reliability breaks down in a philosophically damaging way—for instance, because epistemic confidence is not statistically independent in the way node failures are, or because belief aims at truth in a manner that system reliability does not? I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this non-aggregative position have qualified it into unfalsifiability—allowing any aggregation failure to be explained away post hoc as epistemic modesty—and whether that insulation is itself a defect for a view that claims to describe rational belief rather than merely excuse it. Finally, please distinguish which objections a committed defender of this view would concede as genuinely damaging versus those they would dismiss, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, and say whether there is any observation or result that would make the view not just wrong but conceptually empty.
