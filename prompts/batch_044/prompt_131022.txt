I'm running a graduate seminar in philosophy of welfare economics — eight students, one 75-minute session, all comfortable with basic decision theory but none are specialists in social choice. I want a runnable in-class simulation that makes them feel the specific force of the interpersonal utility comparison problem: the moment when collective choice requires ranking subjective states across people, but every available method either smuggles in an unshared metric or collapses into arbitrary projection. The idea should hit them through the rules and incentives before I name it.

Design the activity around the following injected framing. Each student receives a sealed envelope describing a "subjective state profile" for a hypothetical beneficiary: three dimensions — phenomenological intensity, temporal spread, and adaptational resistance. Each student can see all three dimensions for every other beneficiary, but for their own assigned beneficiary, one dimension is redacted (the "private channel"). The group holds a single scarce collective resource — say, one unit of noise-abatement budget or one priority scheduling slot — and must allocate it to exactly one of the four beneficiaries represented in the room. The only permitted decision rule is that they must reach unanimous agreement on which beneficiary experiences the "greatest overall welfare impact." They may share information, but they may not invent quantitative conversion rates between dimensions unless every present student explicitly endorses the rate; otherwise they must decide qualitatively.

Specify: the exact materials (envelope contents, visible vs. hidden information layout, a visible scoreboard); the roles (who holds which beneficiary card, who moderates time, whether anyone has veto power); the payoff or scoring scheme that rewards unanimous agreement with a collective point pool, but also assigns each student an individual penalty if the resource is allocated away from the dimension that was private to their own card — so every student is both an impartial judge and a hidden stakeholder; the precise timeline across 75 minutes including how long they have to negotiate before a forced vote; and the mechanics of the forced vote if agreement fails.

Then provide the debrief: map three specific moments from the simulation onto parts of the philosophical problem — (i) the initial confidence that comparison is straightforward, (ii) the discovery that private channels make direct introspection useless for ranking others, and (iii) the temptation to project one's own visible dimensions onto others. Include an honest note on which feature of the interpersonal comparison problem this activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the long-run adaptive dynamics of welfare, or the absence of any linguistic bridge between experiences). Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time you run it and how the facilitator should handle it without breaking the simulation.

Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure theories or specific publications by name; you may refer to well-known concepts like Pareto efficiency or ordinal ranking if they help structure the rules, but build the exercise primarily from the framing above. Ensure the rules are self-contained enough that a facilitator could run the session with no outside research.
