I have been working through Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief—especially the lecture notes on religious belief and the scattered observations in his posthumous notebooks—and I am trying to help a doctoral student design a four-year research agenda that makes a real conceptual contribution rather than an exegetical summary. Much of the current literature still divides between what might be called a grammatical-dissolution interpretation, which treats religious belief as a self-contained language-game immune to empirical or metaphysical justification, and a form-of-life interpretation, which reads Wittgenstein as describing a distinctive mode of human existence shaped by ritual, image, and commitment rather than by doctrinal assent. Among the open questions that separate these camps—whether religious belief involves any genuinely propositional content, how the imagery of final judgment functions in the grammar of faith, whether Wittgenstein's account allows for epistemic partiality or doxastic voluntarism, and what the relation is between belief and life-form in his later philosophy—which ones deserve priority?

I want a defended agenda, not a survey and certainly not a bibliography: rank these or related issues by tractability for conceptual analysis rather than historical scholarship, by how many other interpretive disputes depend on resolving them upstream, and by the philosophical payoff they carry for understanding religious commitment outside the academy. In particular, is there a single upstream question about the relation between belief and life-form that, if settled in one direction, would transform or dissolve several downstream debates? And what kind of conceptual or analogical evidence—drawn from Wittgenstein's texts or from parallel practices—would most likely shift your ranking over the coming years?
