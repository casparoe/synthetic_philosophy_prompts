As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I've been trying to map ideas from epistemic game theory onto distributed systems design, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show the entire applied framework is vacuous for real-world coordination. I want to know where it breaks. (1) Rational strategic interaction requires that each agent's reasoning be best-response consistent with the others', which demands common knowledge of rationality — infinite-order nesting of "A knows B knows A knows..." — not merely mutual belief at some finite depth. (2) Any physically instantiated multi-agent system, whether biological neural networks or computer clusters, faces hard constraints on recursive representation: working memory limits, communication latency, and computational overhead impose what I call an epistemic stack limit, likely bounded at third or fourth order in practice. (3) Therefore, in actual human societies, strict common knowledge of rationality never obtains; we reach only truncated, finite-order approximations. (4) The predictive results of epistemic game theory — including agreement conditions and equilibrium refinements — are formally derived assuming the infinite nesting in (1) holds exactly. (5) Consequently, those results should not apply to large-scale human coordination, market behavior, or multi-agent AI alignment scenarios. (6) Yet economists and AI researchers routinely invoke these results for exactly such applications. (7) So either the applied literature is systematically mistaken, or one of my premises fails. I would like to preserve (1) and (6). Which step should I reject, and at what cost? Specifically, can a finite-order truncation approximate the infinite case closely enough to rescue the predictions, or does rejecting (4) require rebuilding the theory from scratch?
