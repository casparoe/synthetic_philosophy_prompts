As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for a character who practices what she terms "pure tranquilism," I have drafted the following argument and now find it suspicious, because its conclusion seems to swallow the doctrine whole. The view holds that well-being is constituted solely by the absence of craving and suffering; pleasure, knowledge, love, and aesthetic experience contribute precisely nothing—neither positively nor negatively—provided they do not generate craving. From this, she derives the following chain:

(1) If a state contributes zero intrinsic value, then its presence or absence makes no comparative difference to well-being, ceteris paribus.
(2) Therefore, the presence of rich mental life (pleasure, knowledge, etc.) in a tranquil subject adds nothing; its absence removes nothing.
(3) Compare world W, containing one conscious being in perpetual tranquil awareness with zero craving and zero suffering but also zero positive experience, with world W*, identical except that the being never existed.
(4) In both worlds, suffering and craving are absent; in W, the being's tranquil awareness is present but contributes zero value by (1); in W*, it is absent but, by (1), that absence costs nothing.
(5) Yet W carries a non-zero risk of future craving arising, however small, while W* carries none. By the risk-minimizing logic that tranquilists routinely invoke against hedonism, W* is strictly preferable.
(6) So pure tranquilism entails that non-existence is at least as good as, and safer than, any tranquil existence.
(7) Generalized: no one should ever bring a being into existence, even under guaranteed tranquil conditions; the doctrine collapses into a quietist anti-natalism.
(8) But my character treats tranquilism as an ethics of living, not an argument for extinction. The conclusion proves too much.

Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost her position? If the flaw is in (1), does she have to concede that positive states have at least some instrumental or comparative weight? If it is in (4) or (5), must she deny that we can compare existence and non-existence, or abandon her risk-minimization principle? I would prefer to preserve (1) and the structural soundness of (7) as a logical consequence, but I suspect the slip occurs between (3) and (5). Where exactly does the argument fail, and what is the price of repairing it?
