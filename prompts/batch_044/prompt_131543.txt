I would like a consistency audit of my commitments regarding objective chance, rational deference, and credence, for a reading-group discussion. My current profile is as follows:

(1) I accept the Principal Principle in its canonical form: if I know that the objective chance of an event E is x, and I hold no inadmissible evidence specifically about E, then my rational credence in E should be x.

(2) I restrict its domain to genuinely chancy phenomena—events governed by physical stochastic dynamics—excluding propositions that merely exhibit a statistical distribution, such as the demographic makeup of an arbitrarily assembled room.

(3) I regard knowledge of an overriding deterministic mechanism as admissible evidence that can sever the link required by the Principal Principle: if I discover that a particular coin toss was fixed by a concealed magnet, I should abandon the 0.5 credence even though the coin is fair.

(4) I apply the principle locally, not globally: the relevant law is the one indexed to the specific system and time that generates the outcome in question, rather than any world-level distribution.

(5) I endorse a position I call diachronic chance fusion: when a physical process revises its own transition probabilities as a function of its history, the objective chance at a given time is defined by the fused dynamics, and the Principal Principle requires deference to this fused rate. This applies even if I possess direct archival records of the original, unfused base rate.

(6) In Newcomb's problem, I two-box. I justify this by treating my knowledge of the predictor's accuracy and its past correlation with my choices as inadmissible for the purpose of applying the Principal Principle to the box-filling event—treating it analogously to background noise rather than to part of the governing mechanism.

(7) I deny that there is any meaningful global world-chance across arbitrary propositions; deference is always to a particular, localized law.

Please assess whether this package hangs together. I want you to distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each specify the least costly revision and what it sacrifices. In particular, examine whether (3) and (6) remain compatible once (4) is taken seriously, whether (5) exerts hidden pressure on (1) or (2), and whether the tension between (1) and (5) dissolves once (7) is recognized as blocking a global reading. Focus only on the pairings that actually pull in opposite directions; do not produce an exhaustive matrix. Deliver this as an audit—ordered tensions, each with what must give—not as a general lecture on the Principal Principle.
