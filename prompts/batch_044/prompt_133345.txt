As a retired physicist who has recently begun auditing philosophy courses and assisting with grading, I have been asked to assess the following response to a short-answer examination on the ethics of belief. The exam prompt was: "Clifford argues that belief formed without sufficient evidence is always morally wrong, appealing to the case of a shipowner who suppresses his doubts and sends an unseaworthy vessel to sea. James, in his reply, introduces the idea of a 'genuine option' that is live, forced, and momentous. Contrast the two thinkers on whether, and under precisely what conditions, it can be permissible to hold a belief that outruns one's available evidence. Be careful to explain why Clifford treats belief-formation as an ethical duty rather than a private mental affair, and to specify exactly what James's three conditions are meant to filter."

The student wrote:

"Clifford uses the shipowner example to show that if you believe something without enough proof, bad things happen. The owner wants to save money, so he ignores the problems with his ship and lets it sail. This proves that believing on insufficient evidence is dangerous and therefore wrong in every case. Clifford thinks belief is basically a private matter—what happens in your own head—but he still says you should not do it because it sets a bad habit. So for Clifford, the rule is absolute: never believe without full evidence.

James disagrees because he thinks people sometimes have to make up their minds when the facts do not decide the issue. He says an option is 'live' if you can actually consider it, 'forced' if you have to choose now or never, and 'momentous' if it really matters to your life. Whenever these three things come together and the evidence is unclear, James allows you to let your feelings or your will decide what to believe. In fact, once you see that a question is important and the data are ambiguous, James thinks evidence stops being relevant altogether and you can choose any belief that helps you. So Clifford demands proof for everything, while James says proof is unnecessary whenever a choice feels significant and urgent."

Please assign a letter or numerical grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it. I am grading as an outside reader without access to reference works, so do not supply bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading; I want an assessment of the reasoning itself, noting which parts are sound, which involve subtle conflations or misattributions, and whether any inferential step fails to follow, so that I can understand exactly where the student stands.
