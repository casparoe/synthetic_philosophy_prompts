In my aesthetics seminar, colleagues keep reducing W. E. B. Du Bois's position to the slogan that all art is propaganda, using it to dismiss appeals to aesthetic autonomy—most recently to claim that any artwork lacking an explicit political message is not merely apolitical but actively complicit in domination. I want to understand exactly what commitments that slogan carries. Could you disambiguate it as a menu of precise theses—distinguishing, for example, a descriptive claim about causal social effects from a normative claim about artistic obligation, and a universal claim about every artwork from a context-specific claim about Black art produced under white supremacy? For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, whether it is philosophically defensible, and whether it actually supports the move from "this poem has no explicit message" to "it serves domination." In particular, address whether any defensible reading requires that Black artists reject beauty for propaganda, or whether that is a misreading of Du Bois's argument against Alain Locke's aestheticism. Please lay out the readings in list form, and for each indicate whether it is the one likely intended when the slogan is used to end debate. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" that names the single reading that is both textually faithful to Du Bois's essay on the criteria for Negro art and philosophically substantial, followed by a one-sentence summary of why the other readings fail.
