You are an expert philosopher of mind with training in cognitive neuroscience, and I ask that you think carefully and systematically about the problem below—perhaps beginning with a brief outline of the main positions on the unity of consciousness before turning to the precise diagnosis.

I am a retired experimental physicist who, after decades in laboratory work, have recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest. In a discussion group with fellow scientists turned philosophical amateurs, we constructed an argument concerning split-brain patients that now strikes me as suspicious: each premise seems defensible, yet the conclusion forces an unacceptable choice. I lay it out here in numbered steps.

(1) In patients whose corpus callosum has been surgically divided, the left hemisphere can verbally report an object presented to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere can guide the left hand to select a different object presented to the left visual field, with neither hemisphere able to report the other’s choice.

(2) The prevailing dual-subject interpretation holds that when two cognitive centers within one body maintain contradictory representations and act upon them with no mutual informational access, they qualify as distinct subjects of consciousness.

(3) If the absence of mutual informational access is sufficient for distinct subjecthood, then encapsulation alone—rather than any deeper phenomenal discontinuity—drives the multiplication of minds.

(4) Yet in neurologically intact individuals, modular subsystems such as the dorsal and ventral visual streams, or implicit procedural memory versus explicit declarative systems, routinely maintain contradictory representations. In blindsight, for example, a subject sincerely denies seeing a stimulus that nevertheless guides accurate reaching behavior, all apparently within one unified consciousness.

(5) Therefore, either such modular encapsulation in healthy brains also generates a proliferation of subjects, or interhemispheric separation in split-brain patients is insufficient to generate two subjects.

(6) One might reply that the difference is only one of degree rather than kind, but if so, then split-brain patients possess merely one subject with unusually weak internal connections—directly contradicting the clinical intuition that they behave as though two minds inhabit one skull.

(7) Consequently, the dual-mind view is caught between an implausible explosion of subjects within every brain and a denial that split-brain patients ever hosted two minds; both outcomes seem intolerable.

Please identify the specific step, equivocation, or hidden assumption that permits the slide from plausible premises to this false dilemma. Explain why that move initially looks reasonable—especially to someone with a scientific background accustomed to treating informational isolation as a marker of independent systems—and then articulate precisely what rejecting it costs for our understanding of consciousness unity. If you must preserve one claim over another, indicate which you find least dispensable and justify that preference. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am evaluating the reasoning without reference access and wish to judge it on its own terms.
