I'm trying to determine what I actually think about the ethics of deep-space colonization, not by consulting an existing theory but by testing my intuitions against concrete scenarios. Here are six cases with my gut verdicts.

First, terraforming Mars for permanent human settlement, knowing that this will almost certainly eradicate any native extremophile microbial life. I think this is permissible: the prospect of a self-sustaining human civilization outweighs the loss of organisms that lack subjective experience and complex interests.

Second, deploying an autonomous mining swarm to a distant moon where long-term chemical monitoring reveals a dormant, non-carbon crystal lattice that is self-catalyzing and could, over geological timescales, develop into a form of non-biological cognition. The swarm will consume the substrate and permanently prevent that trajectory. I think this is impermissible: we have a duty not to foreclose the emergence of potential future moral patients, even if they are not biological and are not currently sentient.

Third, launching a generation ship to a nearby star system. The founding crew consents fully, but their descendants will be born into the mission with no practical means of returning to Earth. I think this is permissible, but only if the ship is structured as a self-governing community rather than an authoritarian command hierarchy; the descendants' genuine autonomy transforms what would otherwise be coercion into a legitimate intergenerational social contract.

Fourth, a nation-state using military-enforced satellites to establish an exclusive extraction claim over a large sector of the asteroid belt, on the grounds that it alone developed the propulsion technology. I think this is impermissible: resources outside any planetary body should be governed as a commons, not as territorial property.

Fifth, creating "operational copies" of human minds—complete with the originals' memories and personalities—to control remote construction drones in high-radiation zones. The originals remain safely on Earth and continue living full lives; the copies experience only task-relevant sensory streams and have no autobiographical awareness of being copies. I think this is permissible: there is no harm to a continuous consciousness, since the original subject is unharmed and the copy is not suffering in any meaningful sense.

Sixth, deliberately redirecting a comet to impact a barren, lifeless planet in order to deliver water for terraforming, knowing the impact will obliterate unique geological formations that have existed for four billion years. Here I am genuinely torn: my instinct is that destroying something ancient and irreplaceable is wrong, yet I cannot see why this should be prohibited if terraforming Mars is permitted, given that neither site hosts current life, sentience, or potential moral patients.

The tension that worries me most is between the first and second cases. In both we destroy something for the sake of colonization, but I approve of one and condemn the other. The difference I point to is that the second involves foreclosing a potential future moral agent, while the first involves only simple organisms. But I am unsure whether that distinction is principled or ad hoc, especially since I also approve of the fifth case, where we create new conscious entities for instrumental purposes, and I disapprove of the fourth, which involves no living beings at all.

What I want is the underlying principle, if there is one, that delivers all six verdicts, or the closest coherent approximation. State it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and apply it to two further scenarios I have not listed: (i) seeding a dead world with engineered microbes designed to eventually evolve into sentience, but where the evolutionary path is directed rather than natural; and (ii) establishing a settlement governed entirely by an AI that makes all resource-allocation decisions, but where human residents retain full, costless exit rights. If no single principle captures my verdicts without distortion, tell me which of my six judgments I should overturn and why, and be explicit about any residue—verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.
