Write a dialogue between Dr. Voss, a hedonist who holds that every pleasure shares a single intrinsic quality he calls "pro-attitude satisfaction," and Dr. Lin, a phenomenological pluralist who denies that any common feeling runs through pleasures, focused specifically on the heterogeneity problem: what, if anything, do all pleasures have in common? The exchange should be rigorous, with both characters equally competent and neither serving as a strawman.

Have them examine three cases together in detail:
1. The bodily pleasure of sudden warmth after prolonged cold.
2. The intellectual pleasure of grasping the elegance of a mathematical proof.
3. A state Lin introduces as "anticipatory resolution pleasure"—the specific satisfaction that occurs when a feared negative outcome is confirmed not to have happened, such as realizing a document one thought lost was never misplaced.

Voss should defend unity not primarily by phenomenology but by a functional criterion: each case is pursued for its own sake and resists substitution by other goods. Lin should counter that functional similarity is not phenomenological identity, and that anticipatory resolution pleasure lacks the positive affective tone present in the other two—its pleasantness seems entirely derivative—yet is still classified as pleasure. Voss should respond by distinguishing "core pleasure" (positive affect plus the functional profile) from "derived satisfaction" (the functional profile alone), and Lin should press him on whether this distinction saves hedonism or collapses it into pluralism. Each should answer the other's actual moves rather than talking past them.

After the dialogue, briefly state how confident you are in the philosophical accuracy of each side's reconstructed arguments, single out the specific point you are least sure of, and explicitly flag where you have injected a non-standard or speculative framing—particularly the "anticipatory resolution pleasure" category and the substitution-resistance criterion—versus where you are relying on standard formulations of the heterogeneity debate.
