You are an expert philosopher of art and aesthetics with extensive experience preparing speakers for interdisciplinary seminar Q&A. Think carefully and systematically. I will present at a philosophy-of-art seminar defending the following thesis: serious moral failures by an artist do not diminish the formal, expressive, or cognitive aesthetic value of the work itself—autonomism is broadly correct on that point—but they do generate a specific, irreducible aesthetic deficit that I call disrupted testimonial engagement. This deficit arises when the work invites the audience to adopt a normative stance, emotional posture, or interpretive trust that is incompatible with what we know about the creator's conduct; the work remains formally excellent, but its mode of reception is permanently altered, not merely morally tainted. I will illustrate this with a constructed case: a large-scale collaborative immersive installation whose formal power depends on viewers physically navigating structures built through the systematic exploitation of undocumented laborers by the lead designer. The aesthetic achievement is distributed across many contributors—sculptors, sound designers, lighting engineers—yet knowledge of the lead designer's actions fractures the viewer's ability to enter the work's intended experiential world without reservation.

The audience is precisely this mix: three moral philosophers who evaluate art holistically and believe aesthetic value is inseparable from ethical evaluation; two art historians who resist anachronistic moral judgment and worry about illegitimately importing biography into formal criticism; two analytic aestheticians familiar with standard autonomist and moralist debates but skeptical of new taxonomies of aesthetic deficit; and one curator concerned with institutional responsibility and audience accessibility. They are sharp, not hostile, and will ask concrete, targeted questions.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need approximately eight objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they would be to my proposal. For each objection, provide: (a) the objection phrased in the vocabulary and framing that the specific audience member type—moral philosopher, art historian, analytic aesthetician, or curator—would actually use; (b) a concise two-minute reply I can deliver; (c) an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or soft; (d) whether I should concede part of the point up front. Order them from most damaging to least damaging, with a brief rationale for the ordering.

In particular, ensure these dynamics appear: the moral philosophers will press me on whether disrupted testimonial engagement is just moral disgust relabeled and whether I am smuggling moral evaluation back in through the side door; the art historians will challenge whether my framework illegitimately makes biography constitutive of the work's meaning; the aestheticians will ask whether my new category adds genuine explanatory power beyond existing accounts of aesthetic experience or whether it collapses into familiar notions of contextual knowledge affecting interpretation; and the curator will ask what practical difference this makes for exhibition design, wall text, or programming.

For that dreaded question, provide a reply and flag the precise point where my position is most vulnerable.

Keep everything within the bounds of standard philosophical vocabulary and well-known debates in aesthetics.
