I've been re-reading some stuff on the discursive dillema, you know, List and Pettit's result where majority voting over premises vs. conclusions can flip the group's position depending on the aggregation rule. The standard literature treats this as a design problem: pick premise-based aggregation, or conclusion-based, or restrict the domain, whatever.

But I actually want you to argue for a more contrarian take: that when collective bodies are dealing with normative premises—think values, priorities, rights—we should just abandon the consistency requirement at the group level altogether. Don't solve the dillema; dissolve it. The idea is that forcing logical closure on a democratic body is a kind of category mistake, importing individual rationality norms onto a pluralistic entity that isn't an individual.

Make the case that allowing the group to hold apparently contradictory attitudes—like endorsing both "maximize liberty" and "maximize equality" without committing to a specific trade-off—isn't a failure of collective reasoning but an honest representation of pluralism. Critique the standard procedural fixes for suppressing real disagreement. And be clear: separate what the formal results actually establish (the impossibility of consistent majority aggregation over certain domains) from the normative claim that consistency shouldn't be required here. Don't present the latter as settled—flag it as contested, especially among people who work in social choice.

Write it like you're talking to a grad seminar—casual, but precise where it counts.
