I am preparing remarks for a graduate workshop on collective action, and in rehearsing with colleagues I have kept running into the same argumentative pattern, one that seems structurally consistent but treated inconsistently across domains. In discussions of meat consumption, an interlocutor grants that an individual purchase is causally inert—the supplier’s reorder threshold is far above one unit, so refusal does not reduce suffering—yet immediately accepts that abstaining still carries weight through norm-setting or expressive alignment with a moral community. In parallel discussions of large-scale democratic mechanisms—say, a national referendum on carbon pricing—the same interlocutor insists that a single vote is probabilistically null with respect to policy outcomes, and then rejects the analogous expressive reply, claiming that democratic legitimacy does not convert causal inefficacy into a reason to participate unless the vote is genuinely decisive. The causal architecture is the same in both cases: the agent’s act is embedded in a collective mechanism with a scaling or threshold factor that renders the marginal contribution null, and there exists a plausible non-instrumental reason to act (expressive solidarity, norm reinforcement). Yet in one domain that reason is treated as sufficient to override the inefficacy objection, and in the other it is dismissed as irrelevant.

I am trying to determine whether this selective deployment has an established name in the literature on collective harm, democratic theory, or practical reason. It is not exactly the standard inefficacy objection, since the objection is being applied asymmetrically rather than uniformly; nor does it seem to be a simple instance of motivated reasoning, because the asymmetry tracks a feature of the collective mechanism—whether the agent’s relation to it is mediated by direct market participation versus institutional representation—rather than the agent’s prior commitments. If there is no established term, I would want a concept that specifies the underlying configuration precisely: an act that is causally null within a threshold-governed collective process, coupled with an available expressive or coordinative rationale, where the normative weight of that rationale depends on how the mechanism is categorized (direct participatory harm versus representative democratic procedure). Does such a concept exist, and if not, how should it be defined and bounded so that it distinguishes this pattern from ordinary bias, from the non-identity problem, and from standard discussions of symbolic action? I am not looking for a reading list—just the carving of the phenomenon, its nearest neighbors in the literature, and where its edges lie.
