I have been reading about Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation on my own, particularly the well-known scenario involving the term gavagai, where a field linguist cannot determine from observable behavior alone whether a speaker refers to rabbits as enduring animals or to momentary slices of rabbit-hood. I do not have an academic background in philosophy or linguistics, but the idea has stayed with me because it seems to cut deeper than a technical problem about dictionaries; a friend who works in software even remarked that debugging legacy code sometimes feels like facing the same gap between behavior and the intended meaning behind it. I have started to think that there are at least three different directions in which this idea could matter, and I am trying to figure out which ones deserve serious attention from someone who can pursue only one or two without institutional support.

The first direction is metaphysical: if translation manuals can be empirically equivalent yet assign radically different ontologies, does that imply that meaning is not a fixed feature of the world but rather projected by interpreters? The second is mental and artificial: I wonder whether the same underdetermination applies when we try to interpret the internal representations of other people, or even the outputs of complex artificial systems—whether there is always an unavoidable gap between observable behavior and the specific concepts we attribute. The third is practical and institutional: if shared meaning is never fully secured, how should that affect the design of legal language, scientific taxonomy, or collaborative technology? I have also noticed a distinction that might reorder all of this—between indeterminacy of reference, indeterminacy of concept, and indeterminacy of interpretation—and I suspect that clarifying which kind Quine actually established might change which downstream questions are real and which are verbal.

Given all this, I want to ask which open lines of work, implications, or conceptual distinctions in this area matter most, and in what order. If you were advising someone with no formal training but about three years of focused thinking, where should their effort go? Which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable through careful conceptual analysis rather than empirical science, and which might collapse into misunderstandings that do not change practice once resolved? Please defend your prioritization with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, and how much other work depends on the answer—rather than offering a survey or a reading list. I am especially interested in whether injecting this distinction among reference, concept, and interpretation reshuffles the importance of the metaphysical, mental, and practical directions I outlined.
