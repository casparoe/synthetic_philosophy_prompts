I'm writing up my philosophical profile on reference, modality, and scientific language for a reading-group audit, and I want a consistency check before I present it. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Proper names are rigid designators: 'Kripke' picks out the same individual in every possible world in which he exists, not via any contingent description such as "the author of a well-known modal-logic monograph."

(2) The necessary a posteriori is not limited to identity statements between proper names. Theoretical identifications—'water is H2O', 'pain is C-fiber firing', 'heat is mean kinetic energy of molecules'—are metaphysically necessary if true, even though we discover them only through empirical investigation.

(3) Modal rationalism is false: conceivability is not a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility. The fact that I can coherently imagine water lacking H2O-structure, or a phenomenally identical zombie lacking consciousness, establishes nothing about what is genuinely possible.

(4) Names introduced for entities that turn out not to exist—'Vulcan' for the hypothesized intra-Mercurial planet, 'Nessy' if there is no Loch Ness monster—are empty rigid designators. They lack an actual-world referent, but in worlds where they do refer they pick out the same object; they do not collapse into the failed descriptions that introduced them.

(5) When a speaker uses a definite description that fails to denote uniquely (for example, 'the tallest person in this room' when two people are exactly tied in height), any name that gets established is anchored to the speaker's intended object and remains rigid, rather than becoming description-dependent or non-rigid.

(6) In scientific revolutions where a presumed entity is discovered not to exist (for instance, caloric fluid), the correct analysis is that the term was an empty rigid designator all along. It did not successfully refer to kinetic energy under a false description; it never referred at all.

(7) Fictional names such as 'Sherlock Holmes' are not names of abstract objects or inhabitants of non-actual possible worlds. They are either empty rigid designators or they rigidly refer to actual mental representations—the idea in the author's mind—without thereby being descriptive names of those representations.
