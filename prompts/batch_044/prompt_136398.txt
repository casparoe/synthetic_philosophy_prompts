I'm writing a philosophy-of-technology seminar paper on whether modern artificial intelligence realizes the brain-computer analogy that John von Neumann developed in his posthumous work The Computer and the Brain. I want to predict how von Neumann—who died in 1957, before digital parallelism approached brain scale—would have assessed today's transformer-based large language models, particularly in light of the scaling-laws debate, claims about emergent reasoning abilities, and mechanistic-interpretability research that attempts to reverse-engineer attention circuits and mixture-of-experts routing paths he never could have imagined. In his own lectures he rejected the idea that the brain is a digital computer in the Turing-von Neumann mold: he stressed analog processing, statistical rather than logical precision, extreme fault tolerance through redundancy, and the absence of a clean hardware-software separation. Yet he also maintained that genuine intelligence requires a specific hierarchical, self-correcting logical architecture rather than mere statistical accumulation. Given these commitments, would he regard an LLM trained by gradient descent on next-token prediction across hundreds of billions of parameters as vindicating the analog-statistical brain processes he suspected, or as confirming his fear that correlation without structured self-repair is not computation in the genuinely intelligent sense? Specifically, where—whether in the lack of architectural error-correction, the absence of a distinct memory-processing boundary, or the reliance on brute-scale data—would he locate the decisive gap between these models and the kind of brain-like computation he envisioned? Lead with your bottom-line judgment in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
