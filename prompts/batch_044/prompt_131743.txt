I've been tracking a recurring argumentative structure in debates over whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about a people's shared character. In one common case, a commentator claims that members of a particular nation relate to rules not through fear of punishment but through an internalized sense of procedural dignity. Critics then point to widespread rule-breaking—tax evasion, speeding, corruption—as direct falsification. The defender responds not by denying the behavior but by redefining the trait: the procedural dignity is said to govern how people experience their own transgressions (as shameful exceptions rather than calculated risks), and this experiential grammar is presented as the real national character, with the observable behavior now being interpreted through it rather than testing it. A parallel case occurs with claims about consensus-oriented cultures: when a decision is reached by apparent unanimous agreement, it is cited as evidence; when open conflict is observed, it is reinterpreted as a "deep" consensus that the conflict itself is being managed in a culturally specific way. In both cases the claim survives counterexample by migrating from an empirical behavioral prediction to a hermeneutic or motivational framework that absorbs any observed conduct. Is there an established concept for this specific migration—the protection of a national-character generalization by reconceiving it as an interpretive grammar of motivation rather than a falsifiable behavioral tendency? If there is no precise term, can you name and define the phenomenon, and carefully distinguish it from ordinary unfalsifiability, ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, essentialism, and the "no true Scotsman" maneuver?
