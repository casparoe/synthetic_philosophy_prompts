While drafting a scene in which two philosophers argue through an entire winter evening without either yielding a single premise, I found myself returning to a comparison that seems to stall precisely because it feels so intuitively compelling and so conceptually unstable at once. The comparison is between Darwin's inversion—where functional design emerges through a process that possesses no foresight, no representation of the end state, and therefore no comprehension of what it is producing—and Turing's inversion, in which a system can be said to think only insofar as its external behavior is indistinguishable from that of a thinking being, regardless of whether anything like understanding occurs inside. The debate I am trying to stage asks whether these two inversions share a common logical form, a kind of competence achieved in the absence of comprehension, or whether treating them as analogous collapses distinct phenomena: the removal of intentional causation from evolutionary history, on one side, and the methodological suspension of internal access from the evaluation of intelligence, on the other. Those who defend the analogy argue that both cases dissolve the assumption that successful function requires a conscious grasp of the task, and that recognizing this parallel is necessary to resist anthropocentric theories of mind. Those who reject it reply that Darwin eliminates comprehension from the causal story altogether, whereas Turing never denies it but merely makes it epistemically irrelevant to the observer; hence to call both instances of competence without comprehension is to mistake an ontological absence for a methodological bracket.

What I want is not a verdict on which side is correct, but a diagnosis of why the disagreement has persisted for so long without resolution, even among people who agree on the biological and computational facts. Is this a verbal dispute disguised by the ambiguous term comprehension, with one camp using it to mean phenomenal consciousness and the other using it to denote representational content? Or is there a substantive disagreement hidden beneath the surface—perhaps about whether any explanation of adaptive function can be complete without invoking some minimal form of understanding, or about whether the criteria for attributing mental states must include internal structure rather than behavioral pattern alone? I also wonder whether the two sides are actually answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of design, the other about the epistemology of other minds—while believing they share a single thesis. Given your diagnosis, tell me what kind of progress could actually be expected: a formal distinction that separates ontological absence from epistemic inaccessibility, an empirical result about cognition in simple organisms that bears on the evolutionary side, a reconceptualization of the question that dissolves the analogy altogether, or an honest acknowledgment that no further move should be expected and why. I want the diagnosis grounded in what each position actually commits itself to, not a survey of the literature, and I want to know whether the stall is a sign that the comparison is ill-posed or merely that it is genuinely hard.
