I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of religion working on the metaphysics of personal identity in classical Abhidharma and its interaction with contemporary philosophy of mind. I am trying to determine whether my judgments about survival, interruption, and duplication can be captured by a single principle—perhaps one that exploits the distinction between ultimate (paramārtha) and conventional (saṃvṛti) truth, or specific Abhidharmic commitments to momentariness and causal integration—or whether I must abandon some of them. I want the conceptual architecture, not a historical survey or bibliography.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

1. Destructive teletransportation: A person's neural and psychological patterns are scanned, the original body destroyed, and a functionally identical being created on Mars. Verdict: conventionally, this is the same person; ultimately no self travels, but the causal-functional stream of the five aggregates (skandhas) continues without ontological rupture.

2. Non-destructive branching: The same scan is performed but the original survives, yielding a psychologically continuous copy. Verdict: neither original nor copy is metaphysically privileged as "the" continuing self; both are equally related streams. Yet I feel an asymmetry—if forced to identify with one, I choose the original—which seems to smuggle in a substantial self I officially deny.

3. Split-brain separation (corpus callosotomy): Each hemisphere supports independent preferences and actions. Verdict: there was never one self controlling both; rather, two previously interwoven streams become distinguishable. There is no tragic "division" of a prior unity.

4. Severe progressive dementia: Over years, memory, character, and cognitive integration dissolve, though minimal consciousness and bodily life persist. Verdict: the conventional person designated by the original name is genuinely gone; the remaining biological entity is a new, severely attenuated stream, not the old one hidden.

5. Nirodha-samāpatti (cessation of perception and feeling): A meditator enters a state in which all mental aggregates—including consciousness—cease entirely for hours, then resumes with prior psychological continuity intact. Verdict: upon emergence, this is conventionally the same person; the interruption is like a dreamless sleep, not a death and rebirth of the stream.

6. Post-traumatic moral transformation: A child who suffered severe abuse becomes an adult monastic practitioner who, upon deep insight into anattā, sincerely disowns the child as "not me" in the ultimate sense, yet accepts conventional moral and legal responsibility for actions performed by that earlier aggregate-stream.

The tensions I keep returning to are these. Between (1) and (2): if causal-psychological continuity suffices for conventional identity in destructive copying, why does the untouched original in branching undermine the copy's claim? Between (4) and (5): in dementia the aggregates gradually degrade and I judge the original person lost, yet in nirodha they vanish entirely and I judge the same person resumed—suggesting that physical embodiment or some non-mental factor is doing hidden work.

Is there a unified account—perhaps one that distinguishes degrees of causal integration, or that treats conventional designation as governed by social-practical criteria rather than metaphysical ones—that delivers all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, and predict what it says about an unlisted scenario: a practitioner enters the formless attainment of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana) for an extended period, during which mental activity is reduced to a minimal, undifferentiated residue, then emerges with intact autobiographical memory. Does the stream survive? If no principle can save every verdict, tell me which ones to overturn and why.
