I am a doctoral researcher in the history of international relations, and in our seminar on historiographical method we have been debating whether the question “who started the Cold War” retains any analytical value, or whether it ought to be abandoned altogether. I would like you to make a sustained argument for the following position: the framing of Cold War origins as a problem of blame—of identifying a singular initiator or primary culpable party—rests on a category error that imports individualistic moral psychology into systemic, multi-agent historical processes, and that historians should replace it with a framework of “distributed institutional responsibility” that assigns weight not to the first hostile move but to the structural conditions that made escalation rational for each side at successive decision nodes. In developing this argument, please address the standard tripartite division of Cold War historiography—the so-called traditionalist, revisionist, and post-revisionist schools—not by adjudicating between them on empirical grounds, but by showing that all three share the same flawed metaphysical premise of unitary state agency, and therefore that resolving their empirical disputes would still leave the conceptual problem intact.

Furthermore, inject into your reasoning a specific, somewhat unconventional distinction: differentiate between “initiatory causation” (the discrete event or decision that triggered the first overt hostile move) and “enabling causation” (the institutional and diplomatic architectures that transformed discrete moves into an irreversible systemic confrontation). Argue that blame, if it is to be applied at all in macro-history, should attach primarily to the maintenance of enabling conditions rather than to initiatory acts, and illustrate this with reference to the well-known sequence of 1945–1947—the breakdown of the Grand Alliance, the Truman Doctrine, and the Marshall Plan—without treating any one of these as the “start” in a moral sense. Please write your response in British English, and keep it formally structured, as if for an academic discussion. Do not provide a bibliography or list of readings; I am interested in the conceptual architecture of the argument itself.
