Write an exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy seminar on early modern metaphysics and ethics, together with a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme out of 20 points. The topic is Leibniz's moral philosophy, but the question must be unanswerable by mere summary. Center it on the tension between Leibniz's view that justice is the charity of the wise—understood as rational pleasure in another's happiness, directed toward the perfection of rational creatures and ultimately toward God—and his simultaneous commitment that this is the best of all possible worlds, arranged by divine choice according to a calculus of compossibility in which every permitted evil serves a greater overall harmony.

The essay prompt should ask students, in about 75 minutes and 900–1,100 words, to examine whether this moral philosophy is genuinely action-guiding or collapses into a kind of theological quietism. Specifically, require them to address four things:

First, explain how Leibniz's tripartite theory of the good—metaphysical perfection as reality, moral good as virtue, physical good as pleasure—underwrites the claim that to love God rationally is to take pleasure in the happiness of other rational beings, and why this makes justice an expression of wisdom rather than mere benevolence.

Second, articulate the quietism problem precisely: if the distribution of virtue, happiness, and suffering is already fixed by the optimal divine selection among compossible worlds, what work is left for human moral agency? Can human action genuinely increase the total perfection of the universe, or is moral effort redundant because the calculus is already settled?

Third, evaluate whether Leibniz can block quietism by appealing to distinctions he actually draws—such as that between physical necessity and moral necessity, or between the realm of nature governed by efficient causes and the realm of grace governed by final causes—without rendering moral agency epiphenomenal or reducing it to mere alignment with an unchangeable order.

Fourth, inject and examine a specific conceptual challenge not usually found in standard summaries: ask whether Leibniz implicitly accepts a structural moral asymmetry parallel in form to the procreation asymmetry. That is, does his system treat the existence of a virtuous, happy rational being as carrying a special reason for agents to promote or preserve it, while treating the existence of a suffering rational being as not carrying a corresponding reason to prevent it—precisely because suffering and limitation are metaphysically necessary privations that serve indispensable compossible functions in the optimal world? Ask students to assess whether this charge is fair, given his privation theory of evil and his claim that metaphysical evil (finite limitation) is inseparable from created being, or whether it conflates necessary metaphysical limitation with contingent moral evil (vice and sin).

Provide a model answer that makes the specific moves an excellent response must contain: a correct reconstruction of charity as rational, pleasure-based love aligned with divine perfection; a sharp formulation of the quietism tension rather than a generic statement about determinism; a disciplined use of the physical/moral or nature/grace distinction that shows whether it saves agency or merely relocates it; and a nuanced treatment of the asymmetry charge that distinguishes metaphysical privation from moral evil and either defends Leibniz against the charge or identifies a genuine residual tension in his calculus of reasons.

Then provide a marking scheme out of 20, with point allocations and explicit separation between A-range and B-range answers. For each of the four required moves, assign marks and state exactly what an A answer must do that a B answer typically fails to do—e.g., recognizing that charity involves contemplative pleasure in harmony rather than instrumental welfare promotion; identifying that quietism arises specifically from the compossibility calculus, not just from determinism generally; using the nature/grace distinction to show agency operates within, not against, the optimal order; and addressing the asymmetry charge by arguing that metaphysical suffering is not an object of moral prevention in the same way vice is, or conversely showing that this exposes a gap in Leibniz's account of reasons for action. Include a brief note on common errors that should cost marks, such as conflating Leibniz's privation theory with a theodicy that justifies every particular suffering, or treating the asymmetry charge as obviously correct without engaging the distinction between necessary limitation and avoidable vice. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on obscure textual details that a well-prepared student could not be expected to know without external sources.
