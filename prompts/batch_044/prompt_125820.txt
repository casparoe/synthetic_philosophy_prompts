Consider Kierkegaard's diagnosis of modern anonymity, particularly his claim that the press produces an abstract collective he calls the public — not a crowd of identifiable people, but a phantom that enables every opinion to be held and none to be owned. He treats this as the mechanism of leveling: moral and religious commitment dissolves because the conditions for being a specific individual who answers for a specific view are replaced by subscription, repetition, and interchangeable commentary.

If you are advising a small conceptual research group with three years and no empirical sociology budget — working purely in philosophy and political theory — which open questions in this Kierkegaardian framework should they prioritize? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey, and I want the ordering justified by tractability, upstream dependence on other questions, and practical stakes for moral responsibility in mass societies.

In particular, I am weighing three underdeveloped lines.

First, whether the concept of the public can be reconstructed within theories of collective intentionality without stripping away its specifically moral charge — that is, whether the leveling Kierkegaard describes is best analyzed as a failure of group agency or as a novel, pernicious form of distributed agency.

Second, what implications the press-critique carries for institutional ethics: if communicative structures produce a collective that can neither be blamed nor addressed, does that undermine traditional ascriptions of institutional responsibility, or does it demand entirely new categories?

Third, whether the diagnosis applies differently across communicative architectures — for example, whether decentralized, non-editorial networks avoid leveling by removing editorial gatekeeping, or whether they complete it by eliminating even residual authority and diffusing authorship further.

Rank these, or substitute others you judge more upstream, and defend the ordering. For each line, explain: why it matters for the others (dependency relations), how tractable it is for philosophers rather than sociologists or media scholars, and what conceptual result over the next few years would shift its priority up or down. I am especially interested in which of these questions, if settled, would genuinely change how we assign responsibility in large-scale social coordination, and which are interpretive refinements that leave the practical stakes untouched.
