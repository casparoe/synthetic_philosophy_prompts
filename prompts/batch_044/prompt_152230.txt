You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in the history of quantum mechanics, comparative philosophy, and philosophy of mind. Before you diagnose anything, spend a brief moment brainstorming the different structures a long-running philosophical disagreement can take—verbal, substantive, hidden-empirical, bedrock-intuitive, or structurally misaligned.

Here is the stalled debate. In discussions of Erwin Schrödinger’s engagement with Advaita Vedanta, two camps have been talking past each other for years without movement. The “literal monists” read Schrödinger’s denial of plural minds—often expressed through the crystal-with-many-facets image and through references to the Upanishadic identity of Atman and Brahman—as a serious metaphysical commitment: consciousness is numerically one, and the appearance of separate subjects is an illusion parallel to the appearance of separate particles in a unified field. The “deflationary readers” concede all the same textual evidence—the same essays, the same metaphors, the same acknowledgment of Schopenhauer and Vedanta—but treat these passages as spiritual autobiography, poetic framing, or mystical ornament that bears no conceptual load for his physics or for philosophy of mind. Both sides agree that Schrödinger sincerely rejected a Cartesian multiplicity of minds. Both sides cite the same passages. Neither has budged.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily historical or exegetical. Instead, I suspect a hidden disagreement is doing the work: the two sides differ on what makes a claim “scientific” versus “metaphysical,” specifically on whether a metaphysical commitment must be empirically vulnerable to belong in a scientist’s worldview. But I also suspect there is a deeper, bedrock blockage involving the indexical nature of consciousness—namely, that even if there is only one consciousness, each perspective presents itself irreducibly as “this one” rather than “all of them,” and neither camp has explained whether that indexical residue can be dissolved or must be accepted as fundamental.

Diagnose the disagreement with precision. Is it verbal, turning on different senses of “consciousness” (phenomenal unity within a life versus numerical identity across lives)? Is it substantive but hidden, with the literalists answering a metaphysical question and the deflationists answering a methodological or biographical one? Or is it bedrock, resting on incompatible intuitions about whether personal identity is fundamental? In particular, examine whether the real blockage is an unaddressed reverse version of the combination problem—not how many subjects combine into one, but how one subject generates the irreducibly first-person perspective of being me—and whether that problem is conceptual, empirical, or ill-posed.

Then answer honestly: given your diagnosis, could anything actually move this debate? Would progress require a formal distinction—say, between ontological monism and epistemic monism? A conceptual result about indexicality? A reframing that splits the single heading “What did Schrödinger mean by the unity of consciousness?” into two separate questions? Or should we conclude that the disagreement is structurally inert, that the sides are answering different questions under one name, and that the only honest progress is to stop expecting convergence? If you think the question is ill-posed, say so and explain why. If you think there is a live distinction or experiment of thought that could shift one side, name it.
