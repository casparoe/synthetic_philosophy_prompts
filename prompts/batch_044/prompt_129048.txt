Please describe the most important implications of treating the Church-Turing thesis as an epistemic boundary rather than a physical law—specifically, the view that it does not assert every physical process is discrete, but rather that any process we can justify calling "computational" must be simulable by a Turing machine, because our evidential access to computation is structurally limited to effective, intersubjectively checkable procedures. I am particularly interested in what this framing implies for arguments in philosophy of mind and cognitive science that invoke analog, continuous, or hypercomputational dynamics to explain consciousness or rapid decision-making. Does it collapse the distinction between "computes in principle" and "computes for any epistemic agent with our verification capacities," and if so, does that render anti-functionalist objections that rely on hidden, unverifiable computations methodologically empty rather than empirically false? And what does it suggest about how we should evaluate claims that a physical system performs a computation exceeding Turing computability?
