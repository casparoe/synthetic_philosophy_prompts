I was arguing with my roommate about care ethics for this feminist philosophy course and she said it's not just 'being nice' but involves this whole framework about dependancy and moral attention — like, really seeing the specific needs of the other rather than applying a rule. I kinda get it for one-on-one care, but then I read about people applying care ethics to climate migration policy, treating displaced people as nodes in a web of interdependence instead of rights-bearers. Here's where I'm stuck: if care ethics rejects universal rules in favor of context-sensitive response, then isn't any attempt to formalize it into policy just a contradiction? The proffesor said it's not anti-universalist, just that universals have to be 'situated,' but that sounds like having your cake and eating it too.

So if I'm designing a decision procedure for a hospice triage board, should I treat care ethics as asking 'who is in my circle of concern and how do I expand it,' or is there something structurally different about care-based reasoning that can't be captured by any checklist? I feel like I'm caught between thinking care is non-cognitive receptive attention — which cant be proceduralized — and thinking institutional care is always a betrayal of the real thing. There's probably a false either/or in there but I can't see where the confusion is, especially if care is supposed to scale to strangers without becoming utilitarianism in disguise.
