Rank the following five hypothetical moral-emotion architectures according to how well each addresses what you take to be the central normative problem of moral regulation—whether sustaining cooperation without excessive suffering, preserving individual autonomy, or maintaining social cohesion—assigning each a score from 1 (least adequate) to 5 (most adequate): (A) a pure guilt-culture relying on internalized self-punishment and private conscience; (B) a pure shame-culture relying on external honor codes and communal visibility; (C) a translation culture that institutionally converts shame-experiences into guilt-accounts through formal deliberative procedures; (D) a suppression culture that actively trains citizens to replace both emotions with dispassionate moral reasoning; and (E) a rotational culture that assigns guilt to private domains and shame to public domains according to generational cohort. For each rank, provide a one-sentence justification referencing a specific mechanism—psychological, institutional, or evolutionary—that explains its placement. Separately, rate on a scale of 0 to 1 the plausibility that any culture can genuinely choose its architecture rather than having it emerge from underlying economic or structural conditions, and defend that rating in two to three sentences without citing named theorists or specific empirical studies. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or source citations.
