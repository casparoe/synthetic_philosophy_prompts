After a lengthy argument with a friend about whether we can meaningfully forecast the durability of the current global order, I have tried to formalize my position, but I worry that my commitments pull in incompatible directions. Here is the profile I have landed on.

(1) For forecasting institutional lifespan, the outside view dominates the inside view: specific narratives about culture, leadership, or policy are overfitted, and we should weight statistical base rates drawn from a well-constructed reference class far more heavily than unique causal stories.

(2) The appropriate reference class for a modern global hegemonic order is not other modern nation-states or financial architectures, but pre-modern agrarian empires—Roman, Ottoman, Ming—because the core dynamics of territorial extraction, administrative overextension, and military logistics remain structurally analogous.

(3) At the same time, modern hegemony differs categorically: nuclear deterrence, instantaneous global communication, and fiat-currency regimes generate feedback loops that disrupt the causal mechanisms driving agrarian collapse, so those base rates are mechanistically inapplicable and should be heavily discounted or treated as unreliable priors.

(4) Despite (3), I still maintain that we should use those agrarian base rates as our primary quantitative anchor when forecasting the current order; without them we have no statistical discipline and revert entirely to inside-view narrative reasoning.

(5) I reject the objection that history never repeats, on the grounds that it confuses repetition of causal mechanisms with repetition of statistical regularities; forecasting requires only the latter, so reference-class reasoning remains valid even when mechanisms differ.

(6) Once an institutional system reaches sufficient technological and organizational complexity, it exits every reference class and becomes sui generis, analyzable only through its internal dynamics rather than comparative statistics.
