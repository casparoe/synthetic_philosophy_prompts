I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm trying to work out who actually belongs to the demos in a democratic system. I'm not working from theory; I'm going by gut verdicts on concrete cases and want to know if there's a principle behind them.

My verdicts:
- Every adult citizen physically present in the territory and affected by its laws: clearly belongs.
- A permanent resident with no citizenship, fully integrated, and equally subject to taxation and regulation: belongs.
- A citizen who emigrated permanently decades ago, pays no taxes, and is unaffected by current policy: does not belong to the active demos.
- Unborn future generations who will inherit the climate and fiscal consequences of today's decisions: belong in some representational sense.
- Sentient non-human animals in the territory affected by environmental rules: do not belong.
- AI proxy agents that aggregate and cast votes for living citizens according to explicit instructions: do not belong; they are instruments, not members.
- A secessionist community inside the borders that explicitly denies the system's legitimacy: still belongs, since it remains subject to coercive law.
- Children currently unable to deliberate politically: belong prospectively, with interests that should be represented now.

The case I keep reversing on is a "legacy agent"—an AI system that continues to vote according to a deceased citizen's recorded preference profile years after death. Sometimes I treat it as an extension of that dead person (whom I said is out); other times I see it as persistent software that should be disregarded, yet I still want future generations represented, which feels structurally similar. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about reciprocal agency, affected stakes, or system membership—that captures all eight verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop, and what does the best principle predict for the legacy-agent case?
