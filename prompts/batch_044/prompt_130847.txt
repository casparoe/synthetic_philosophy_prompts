I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar on rational belief and epistemic normativity, and the topic has become unexpectedly personal. In drafting the preface to a monograph I recently completed, I found myself endorsing each chapter’s central claim individually, while simultaneously admitting that, given ordinary human fallibility, at least one of those claims is probably mistaken. Around the same time, I began participating in a large syndicate lottery in which we purchase several hundred tickets each week, and I have been struck by the structural parallel between my willingness to accept each individual chapter thesis and my willingness to accept that any particular ticket will not win. I want the exam to treat the lottery paradox and the preface paradox not as isolated puzzles but as two manifestations of a single problem about how rational agents manage large sets of beliefs under a uniform acceptance threshold.

Please write one essay question, designed for a 45-minute response of approximately 800 words, that requires students to do the following. First, they must reconstruct both paradoxes precisely, emphasizing the role of a Lockean acceptance threshold applied across a large set and explaining why closure under known entailment generates tension in each case. Second, they must evaluate a proposed unified resolution—call it contextual threshold partitioning—which holds that rational agents apply a high, near-certainty threshold to beliefs that carry direct practical stakes (such as holding a single lottery ticket) but apply a lower, globally tolerant threshold to interdependent epistemic commitments that are individually low-stakes (such as the claims in a scholarly preface). Third, they must identify a specific structural weakness in this proposal: either that it collapses rational belief into pragmatic acceptance, or that it fails to explain why the lottery agent should not apply the aggregate-tolerant threshold to the entire pool of tickets taken together. Fourth, they must take a defended position on whether any non-arbitrary version of the Lockean thesis survives once such partitioning is permitted.

Then provide a model answer of about 600 words that demonstrates these moves with conceptual precision, distinguishing the probabilistically independent outcomes in the lottery case from the likely clustered but unknown error distribution in the preface case, and addressing both the pragmatism-collapse objection and the aggregation-blocking problem.

Finally, provide a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that distinguishes an A-range answer (18–20) from a B-range answer (14–16). The scheme should name the specific moves required for full marks—correct reconstruction of both paradoxes with the closure tension made explicit; accurate exposition of contextual threshold partitioning; identification of at least one of the two named weaknesses with precision; and a coherent, argued assessment of Lockeanism—together with a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each point (for instance, conflating the aggregation problem with the closure problem, treating the lottery and preface errors as structurally identical, or defending partitioning without addressing the pool-level objection).
