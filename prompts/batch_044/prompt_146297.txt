I am not a scholar—just an attentive reader with no formal training in philosophy or religious studies—who has been trying to understand Jain anekāntavāda, often rendered as "many-sidedness" or "non-absolutism," and its companion idea syādvāda, the practice of qualifying assertions with conditions like "perhaps" or "in some respect." Popular accounts present these as calls for intellectual humility: reality is infinitely complex, every viewpoint captures a fragment, and wisdom lies in admitting partiality. That reading is coherent, but after sitting with the idea I think there may be a stricter, less comfortable interpretation that popular summaries suppress, and I want to test whether I am inventing it.

In my notes I distinguish three layers. The first, which seems settled, is ontological: an object possesses infinite attributes, so no single predicate can exhaust it. The second is epistemic: human cognition is perspectival (working through naya, or standpoints), so every judgment is necessarily limited. The third layer—what I am tentatively calling "discursive anekāntavāda"—is my own addition. I wonder whether the doctrine implies not merely that we should tolerate other views, but that any unqualified, single-sided assertion structurally conceals the conditions that make it true, and that this concealment is built into the form of ordinary assertion itself. If so, then holding one view consistently requires systematically suppressing the contextual dependencies that validate opposing views, because to articulate all conditions at once exceeds standard linguistic and social formats.

Applying this stricter reading, I have been thinking about modern public disagreement not as a failure of information or goodwill, but as a formal mismatch: the architecture of short-form, adversarial, algorithmically amplified discourse rewards exactly the kind of unqualified, single-naya expression that the stricter interpretation condemns. I am not claiming that ancient Jains anticipated digital media; I am asking whether extending the doctrine to the conditions of discourse reveals something real about why multi-perspectival speech feels structurally awkward in contemporary debate.

My essay sketches this distinction between a "weak" reading (tolerance-based pluralism) and a "strong" reading (structural concealment through unqualified assertion), then applies the strong version to discourse formats. I also ask whether syādvāda functions primarily as an ontological classification or as a linguistic remedy—embedding conditions directly into grammar—and whether my proposed "discursive layer" belongs to philosophy of language, media theory, or is simply an anachronism.

Please comment on the accuracy of my framing, on whether the weak/strong distinction maps onto anything recognized in Jain philosophical literature, and on whether the application to discourse architecture is a legitimate extension or a misreading. Separate what is fairly settled in the tradition from what is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present one interpretive camp as the unanimous consensus. If my "discursive" layer has no parallel in the literature, say so plainly; if it does, indicate where its edges lie and what it risks distorting. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations—without specialized access I could not use them—but I do want to know which parts of my essay rest on solid ground and which parts are speculative extensions I should mark as my own invention.
