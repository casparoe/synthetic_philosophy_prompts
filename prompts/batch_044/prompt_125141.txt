I have been trying to formalise an objection I encountered in a seminar on cultural evolution, and I now have an argument that looks as though it undermines not only memetics but evolutionary biology altogether; I cannot see exactly where the slip occurs. (1) In David Hull's generalised evolutionary account, Darwinian selection requires discrete replicators that preserve structural information across generations, together with interactors that engage the environment and cause differential replication. (2) For memetics to be genuinely explanatory rather than merely metaphorical, cultural items—ideas, practices, representations—must serve as replicators transmitted with sufficient fidelity that selection operates on their variation. (3) Yet every cultural transmission involves active interpretation, evaluation, and often deliberate reconstruction by the receiving agent; consequently, the supposed replicator is rebuilt by the interactor rather than copied with independence. (4) If independence from the interactor is necessary for true replication, then cultural items are not replicators, and memetics collapses into a theory of individual learning. (5) But genes, the canonical replicators, are themselves reconstructed: transcription, alternative splicing, and cellular context all involve extensive interpretation by the organismic interactor. (6) So either biological and cultural processes both fail to be genuinely Darwinian, or the independence condition stated in (4) must be abandoned. (7) Abandoning that condition means that any causal regularity in which an effect resembles its cause—wind eroding rock into similar shapes, or acoustic echoes—counts as replication mediated by an interactor. (8) That renders Darwinian selection ubiquitous and therefore explanatorily empty. (9) Hence, to preserve the explanatory power of evolutionary theory, we must retain the independence condition, deny that genes are true replicators, and conclude that neither culture nor biology involves Darwinian selection. (10) This is plainly too destructive: it dissolves evolutionary biology along with memetics. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? I should prefer to keep (2) and (5), because they seem descriptively sound, but I am unsure whether saving them forces me to weaken Hull's framework or to relax fidelity in a way that invites (8).
