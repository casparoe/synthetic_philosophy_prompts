Write a Shakespearean sonnet about the methodological claim—often associated with Milton Friedman—that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models do not undermine those models so long as they generate accurate predictions. In the octave, construct an explicitly false model, such as treating gas molecules as perfectly elastic billiard balls moving through a vacuum with no intermolecular forces, and use the rhyme scheme to pair each unrealistic premise with its predictive consequence so that the falsehood sounds structurally necessary rather than accidental. At the volta in line nine, pivot sharply: the predictions hold despite the premises being literally false. In the closing couplet, articulate Friedman's position precisely—that the aim is predictive success, not descriptive fidelity—without reducing it to a slogan. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science recognizes the F-twist rather than a generic defense of abstraction. After the poem, include a brief commentary in continuous prose—without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—taking a definite position on whether the poem's framing risks misrepresenting Friedman by eliding the distinction between predictive accuracy and explanatory power, and stating clearly whether that simplification is acceptable for poetic form or genuinely distorts the view.
