I am preparing a research proposal in political philosophy and have been discussing with colleagues whether the conceptual literature on populism has misallocated its attention. I want to focus the next few years of work on the open problems surrounding “the will of the people” that are genuinely upstream of democratic theory, but I am unsure which lines of inquiry deserve priority and which are largely decorative.

The standard debates—whether “the people” is a pre-political entity or constituted by populist discourse, whether populists advance an epistemic claim about distributed wisdom or a purely normative claim about sovereignty, and whether citizens’ assemblies can reveal an authentic will—are familiar. What strikes me as less examined, however, is the conceptual collapse between will-formation and will-expression that is now being accelerated by algorithmic governance: when platforms and state agencies claim to extract popular sentiment directly from behavioural data, they treat the will of the people as something that can be registered without being formed through deliberation. This raises a specific cluster of questions. Is the distinction between formation and expression philosophically robust, or does it presuppose an implausible view of collective intentionality? If it is robust, does the algorithmic collapse undermine the normative authority that populists claim for their mandates, or can a synthetic, data-derived will carry the same democratic weight as one produced through assembly? And how does this affect the older boundary problem—whether popular sovereignty can be limited by constitutional essentials without betraying the populist premise—given that algorithmic extraction tends to present itself as an unmediated, and therefore unlimited, voice?

Rather than a survey, I want a committed, dependency-aware agenda: which of these conceptual problems should be tackled first, and in what order? Please defend your prioritisation in terms of what is upstream of what, which questions are tractable through conceptual analysis rather than empirical social science, and which, despite their current fashion, would change little about democratic legitimacy even if resolved. I ask for this in British English, and I want you to take a definite position rather than laying out options for me to choose between; please also flag the points where your reasoning rests on contested premises rather than treating your conclusions as self-evident.
