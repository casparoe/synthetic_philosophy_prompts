hey im a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but there's this thing in open source thats been bugging me. there's this unwriten norm called semantic versioning where maintainers promise not to break APIs in minor releases, and they run long deprecation cycles that can drag on for years. but then if enough people hate the direction they just fork the repo, and suddenly you've got a zombie branch still recieveing security patches and a new mainline that are basically seperate worlds competing for users. the community treats forking like a coordination failure but also like a totally legitimate democratic exit. so what philosophical view of conventions or norms does that whole dance presuppose? does it need lewis's idea that conventions are just coordination equilibria held together by everyone knowing everyone else will stick with the regularity? or can a pure evolutionary account — norms are just whatever survives fork-pressure — explain the same pattern without that common-knowledge stuff? and is it consistent that we treat breaking changes in libraries as morally bad but celebrate hard forks in blockchain protocols? if the real underlying theory was actually that norms evolve without any central authority, what parts of package repository goverment would have to change? also, is the practice philosophically underdetermined, or does it strictly commit us to one of those views?
