I am designing a final examination for an upper-level philosophy of time seminar, and I need a single 90-minute essay prompt together with a model answer and a marking rubric. The topic must be the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that special relativity supports a block-universe ontology, but I want the question engineered so that students cannot receive top marks merely by reciting the standard relativity-of-simultaneity objection to presentism.

Specifically, the exam should present a modified presentist position—call it "worldline-indexed presentism"—which accepts Lorentz covariance and the denial of absolute simultaneity, yet insists that ontological privilege is assigned only to events on a given observer's worldline at her local present hyperplane, allowing that other observers may have different "presents" without those events being real simpliciter for anyone else. The essay question should require students to determine whether this view escapes the Rietdijk–Putnam inference, and in particular whether Putnam's claim that an event's being present for one observer entails its being real depends on an unstated assumption about the transitivity or inter-observer sharing of ontological status.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, calibrated for roughly 1,200–1,500 words in 90 minutes, that forces students to reconstruct the original argument, articulate the worldline-indexed reply precisely, identify the hidden transitivity assumption, evaluate whether abandoning it collapses into a solipsistic or frame-relative ontology rather than presentism, and reach a defended conclusion.
2. A model answer of approximately 650–750 words that demonstrates each of these moves.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, divided among five specific argumentative moves (each worth 5 marks), with a one-line note for each on the characteristic error that should cost marks—e.g., conflating epistemic perspective with ontological commitment, or treating Lorentz symmetry as automatically metaphysically neutral without argument.

The prompt should avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on knowledge of any obscure post-2000 technical literature.
