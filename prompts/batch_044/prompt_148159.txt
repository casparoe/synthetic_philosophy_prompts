The debate over whether "national character" is a coherent notion, and whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about peoples, has persisted in philosophy of social science and moral philosophy without clear resolution: defenders treat it as a statistical summary of shared institutional norms and behavioral regularities, insisting that qualified, mechanism-based generalizations are no more essentialist than claims about organizational culture; critics respond that any such attribution necessarily treats historically contingent patterns as intrinsic psychological properties, rendering the concept both conceptually confused and politically dangerous. Both sides reject crude stereotyping, both grant that cultures vary, and yet the arguments appear to loop. I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is the stall a verbal disagreement about what "character" means—dispositional versus institutional—are the sides answering different questions (one about explanatory prediction, one about moral legitimacy) under a single heading, or is there a hidden empirical dispute about cultural transmission that neither side has made explicit? I am inclined to think the impasse reflects a failure to distinguish "diachronic institutional stability," the persistence of practices through specific social mechanisms across generations, from "synchronic uniformity," the false presumption that all members of a population share an inner trait; but I am unsure whether that distinction actually dissolves the disagreement or merely relocates it into a debate about which mechanisms count as legitimate explanations. Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement by engaging with what each position actually commits to, and tell me what would count as progress—whether a formal model of cultural transmission, a conceptual distinction that separates permissible from impermissible generalizations, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
