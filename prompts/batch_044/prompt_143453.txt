As a journalist commissioning material for a publication that covers science and ideas, I am seeking a design for an embodied classroom exercise that introduces Arrow’s impossibility result through lived contradiction rather than formal proof. The setting is an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy—approximately sixteen students, one ninety-minute session. Please design a runnable activity, with rules, roles, materials, and a small-stakes payoff scheme using only prestige tokens, in which participants serve as disciplinary delegates on a fictional funding body called the Meridian Committee. The committee must rank four real proposals—Aurora, Boreas, Cygnus, and Draco—for a single grant. You should invent a specific voting protocol: a sequential pairwise-elimination tournament with a recall clause that allows a previously eliminated proposal to re-enter if a cycle is detected. Crucially, inject a non-eligible “shadow proposal” named Echo that appears on pairwise ballots but is ineligible to win. Arrange the preference cards and the tournament brackets so that, in Round One, the group ranks Aurora above Boreas when Echo is absent, yet reverses that exact pairwise ranking in Round Two when Echo is present—despite no delegate altering their underlying preferences between Aurora and Boreas. The reversal must emerge from the participants’ own actions and token allocations, not from facilitator explanation.

Specify the division of roles, the private preference-card distribution that guarantees an intransitive social ordering, the minute-by-minute timeline, the token-payoff structure that rewards sincere revelation, and the precise conditions under which the recall clause is triggered. Then provide a debrief that maps the participants’ experienced moments onto Arrow’s conditions—unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship—making clear which condition the committee was compelled to abandon in each round and why that sacrifice was structurally unavoidable. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the universal proof across all preference profiles and all numbers of alternatives, and the full unrestricted domain with more than three options—and state honestly which feature must therefore be supplied by the facilitator afterwards.

Finally, include a frank prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run—likely that participants will attempt to “repair” the reversal by strategically altering their votes or by rejecting the recall clause, thereby masking the structural problem—and advise how the facilitator should respond without revealing the result prematurely. Present your entire response in British English, using British spelling and punctuation conventions throughout.
