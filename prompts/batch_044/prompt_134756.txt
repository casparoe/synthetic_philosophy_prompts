Hey, I'm drafting an essay for a phil seminar on Pascal's Pensées — specifically the fragments where he says justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyranical, and that since true justice is hidden we just follow custom backed by state power. Most readings treat this as a static circularity: justice needs enforcement, enforcement needs legitimation, legitimation is missing, so we settle for convention. I want to push a more dynamic reading. I think there's a hidden temporal asymmetry in Pascal: the force that establishes a convention is visible and arbitrary (war, conquest, the king's soldiers), but over time that same force becomes invisible through habituation, so the convention stops looking like coercion and starts looking like natural justice. In other words, Pascal isn't just saying might makes right — he's describing a kind of epistemic laundering where foundational violence gets forgotten and replaced by custom. I call this the maintenance vs foundation split. Does that hold up textually, or am I smuggling in Foucault? Also, I argue that this makes Pascal's view more unstable than usually thought: if people ever recover the memory of the founding violence, the legitimacy cascade collapses. Is that too modern? I'd appreciate comments especially on whether the temporal/asymmetry point is defensible from the Pensées, whether it makes Pascal interesting in a new way, and whether I'm being unfair to the standard circularity reading. Also if there's a obivous counter-example in Pascal where he treats the origin and maintenance of law as the same kind of force. Don't worry about formal citations, I'm just looking for conceptual feedback.
