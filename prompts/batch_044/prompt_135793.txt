I am preparing the question sequence for a podcast episode in which I will interview a philosopher of science—call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has recently defended an unorthodox interpretation of John von Neumann's posthumous lectures, published under the title The Computer and the Brain. The standard reading takes von Neumann to be advancing an early version of the brain-as-computer analogy, limited by his era's technology. Voss rejects this framing. She argues that von Neumann's real concern was not architectural similarity but a principle she terms distributed logical equivalence: the idea that neural tissue achieves reliability not by replicating identical signals across redundant pathways, but by allowing physically heterogeneous substrates to instantiate the same functional relation, so that no two pathways need share a substrate-level feature in order to preserve computation. On her view, this makes von Neumann closer to modern connectionist models than to classical stored-program architecture, despite his use of registers, instruction sets, and precision comparisons. She further claims that the well-known ambiguity in the Yale lecture notes—reconstructed after von Neumann's death—actually supports her reading, because the reconstructed passages about error tolerance are more coherent if interpreted as referring to functional rather than structural redundancy.

I want eight to ten interview questions that stay in the weeds of this interpretation and do not allow her to fall back on general claims about interpretive charity. I need at least one question that presses her on how distributed logical equivalence differs from ordinary neural-network redundancy or from standard fault-tolerance in digital systems; at least one that asks how she accommodates von Neumann's explicit comparison between the low precision of neural components and the high precision of vacuum tubes, particularly if she denies that he was measuring the brain against a digital standard; and at least one follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—namely, that von Neumann was using digital vocabulary metaphorically to describe analog functional relations. Each follow-up should make the next answer harder, not merely change the subject. Please avoid generic biographical questions, requests for reading lists, or invitations to cite chapter and verse; the questions should turn on whether the textual evidence and conceptual distinctions can bear the weight she puts on them.
