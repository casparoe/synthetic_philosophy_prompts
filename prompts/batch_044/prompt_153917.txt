I am drafting a literary novel set in a coastal town recovering from a catastrophic storm. In one chapter, the town’s emergency relief board—neighbors, not bureaucrats—must divide a limited recovery fund between two households. The first family lost everything when the storm surge breached an old levee; they had lived in the low-lying district for generations, carried no private insurance because premiums had been priced beyond reach for decades, and stayed in part because an elderly relative required care that could not be relocated. The second family built an unpermitted second story in clear violation of elevation codes, ignored repeated municipal warnings to retrofit, possessed the means to comply, and then lost the structure in the same storm; they are now destitute, and their two young children had no voice in the decision. A board member proposes distributing aid according to a principle of proportional desert: the first family receives full replacement costs because their ruin was a matter of pure misfortune, while the second receives only bare subsistence because they bear direct responsibility for their own loss. Another member objects that this effectively punishes the children for a parent’s recklessness, and notes that even the first family’s presence in the district was not entirely free—cultural obligation and economic constraint shaped their “choice” to remain—suggesting that misfortune and fault are not so cleanly separable.

As a novelist, I want the scene to dramatize a genuine philosophical tension rather than resolve it through sentiment. Could you advise me on the conceptual architecture at work here? Specifically: does the board’s distinction between misfortune and fault hold up under scrutiny, what hidden assumptions about responsibility, distributive fairness, and the separability of choice from circumstance does it smuggle in, and how might I render the children’s position without reducing them to a mere emotional exception? I am particularly interested in whether there is a coherent framework that honors both the intuition that people ought not suffer for circumstances genuinely beyond their control and the intuition that reckless, well-informed choices should not be fully subsidized by the community—and, if such a framework exists, where precisely it fractures in this scenario given the entanglement of prior obligations and structural constraints.

Please conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by a concise judgment on whether the board’s proposed principle of proportional desert is philosophically defensible in this particular case.
