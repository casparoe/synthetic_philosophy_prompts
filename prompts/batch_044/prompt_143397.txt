I'm enrolled in an introductory ethics course at my high school, and for the past several weeks our seminar has been deadlocked over whether hypocrisy actually undermines a person's standing to blame others. The disagreement has become frustrating because both sides seem to agree on all the relevant facts—everyone accepts that hypocrisy is a moral failing and that blame can be misused—yet neither group has shifted its position at all.

On one side, students argue that committing the very wrong you condemn—say, violating a confidence you later criticize—renders your blame illegitimate, as if moral authority requires a kind of personal cleanliness; they treat standing to blame as a credential that hypocrisy revokes. On the other side, students maintain that blame is fundamentally about the wrongness of the target's action, not about the blamer's biography, so hypocrisy might make you a bad messenger but cannot cancel the message itself. To break the stalemate, I have been wondering whether we are really using standing in two different senses without realizing it: one might call it authority-standing, which concerns whether an audience will take your blame seriously without dismissing you as a fraud, and another might call it integrity-standing, which concerns whether your own character allows you to blame consistently without self-contradiction. If these are genuinely different concepts, then our debate might not be about hypocrisy at all, but about which kind of standing blame actually requires.

Given that philosophical discussion of hypocrisy and standing has persisted for decades without obvious resolution, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this stall a sign that we are facing bedrock intuitions about fairness and moral authority that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical question about how blame functions in social groups that we have not addressed? Or is it a verbal disagreement, with one camp asking about the social legitimacy of blame and the other asking about its logical consistency? And if my distinction between authority-standing and integrity-standing captures something real, does it dissolve the disagreement or merely relocate it? Finally, what—if anything—would actually count as progress here: a conceptual clarification, an empirical study of blame practices, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed?
