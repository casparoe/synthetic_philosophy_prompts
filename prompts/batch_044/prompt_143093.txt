Consider the Chase-Sanborn case: two coffee tasters initially agree on the taste of a particular blend, then over years both gradually shift—Chase toward lighter roasts, Sanborn toward darker ones—yet each sincerely reports that his present experience of the original blend is exactly as it was, even though they now disagree about whether it tastes good. Dennett uses this kind of dissociation between first-person judgment and qualitative state to motivate “quining” qualia: denying that there is any intrinsic, directly accessible “what-it-is-like” that fixes the content of our reports.

Now extend the reasoning systematically. (1) If a mental state can change while all introspective access and verbal report remain constant, then there is no fact of the matter about whether the state has changed. (2) Qualia are mental states of exactly this kind: they can shift without notice, as the coffee case illustrates. (3) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether one’s qualia have changed; “qualia” fail to denote a coherent kind. (4) But beliefs are also mental states for which introspective access is fallible and verbal reports can stay constant despite drift—think of slow ideological shifts in which a subject sincerely denies any change in conviction. (5) So, by parity, there is no fact of the matter about whether one’s beliefs have changed; beliefs can be quined too. (6) Yet if beliefs are quined, then there is no stable mental state that constitutes accepting the argument for quining in the first place. (7) Hence the argument, if sound, eliminates the very propositional attitudes required to assert it.

This seems to prove far too much: it threatens not only qualia but intentionality itself. Which step contains the overreach? Is there a principled asymmetry between qualitative states and doxastic states that blocks the extension at (4), or does accepting (3) genuinely commit us to (5)? If you reject (4), explain why the fallibility of belief-report is structurally different from the fallibility of quale-report in the Chase-Sanborn scenario. If you reject (6), clarify how an argument can undermine its own presuppositions without being self-defeating, and what that costs for the original quining move.
