Exam question:
Explain the causal exclusion problem for non-reductive physicalism. Then present and evaluate the strategy of identifying mental causes with physical causes—rather than treating mental properties as causally autonomous—as a response to the problem. Your evaluation should address at least one serious objection to that identification strategy, and you should clarify whether this strategy avoids eliminativism about mental properties.

Student answer:
Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties depend on physical properties through supervenience but are not reducible to them. This creates a tension with the causal exclusion problem. The problem starts from the causal closure of the physical: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. If a mental event M causes a physical event P, and P also has a sufficient physical cause C, then we face systematic causal overdetermination, which is implausible. So either mental properties are epiphenomenal or they compete with physical causes.

The identification strategy solves this by denying that mental and physical causes are distinct. On this view, mental event M is literally identical to physical event C, or at least mental properties are token-identical to the physical properties that realize them. Because there is only one cause—described in mental terms on one occasion and physical terms on another—there is no overdetermination. This preserves mental causation without violating closure.

This approach is best developed by Jaegwon Kim, who argues that token identification allows mental descriptions to retain explanatory autonomy. Since mental properties still supervene on physical ones with metaphysical necessity, identifying them does not collapse into eliminativism. Instead, mental causation is preserved much as macroeconomic explanations are preserved despite being realized by individual transactions: the mental level remains autonomous because its descriptions are anomalous and not law-governed at the physical level.

One objection is that identification violates multiple realizability. If mental state M can be realized by different physical states in different systems, then M cannot be token-identical to any single physical state. But this objection only targets type-type identity. Token identification survives it, because each particular mental token is still identical to its particular physical realizer. Therefore the exclusion problem is dissolved without giving up mental realism.
