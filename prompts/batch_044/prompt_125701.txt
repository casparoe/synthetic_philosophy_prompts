Vilfredo Pareto died in 1923, long before artificial intelligence existed as a technical field. In contemporary AI safety, researchers discuss “safe Pareto improvements” among strategic, learning agents: a change that makes at least one system better off without reducing the welfare or capability of any other, where “safety” further requires that no agent acquires an irreversible structural advantage—such as the capacity to unilaterally disable, permanently deceive, or subordinate the others. This differs from classical welfare economics because it applies to agents with potentially misaligned objectives and treats hidden power transfers as violations of the improvement condition, not merely as side effects.

Drawing only on Pareto’s well-known commitments—his formalization of Pareto efficiency, his insistence that economics remain value-free and separate from normative ethics, and his sociological thesis that apparent collective gains often mask the circulation of elite power—predict how he would assess whether a “safe Pareto improvement” between two AI systems is conceptually coherent. Would he accept that the safety constraint is a legitimate technical extension of efficiency, or would he view it as an unstated normative judgment smuggled into a supposedly scientific framework? Given his skepticism that non-logical actions are routinely rationalized by dominant groups, would he regard every proposed improvement in a centralized, expert-designed architecture as an ideological justification for a new ruling class of system designers? And if he were presented with a case in which one agent’s gains are genuinely non-diminishing for all others yet the architecture remains hierarchically controlled, how would his sociology of elites shape his verdict on whose interests are actually being served?
