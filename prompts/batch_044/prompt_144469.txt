As a journalist who writes about neuroscience and philosophy of mind, I have been trying to articulate my own considered judgments about when pain is genuinely bad, moving case by case rather than adopting a theory wholesale. I want to know whether these judgments cohere around a single principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

My verdicts are as follows. Ordinary acute nociception—a deep laceration, for instance—is intrinsically bad. Phantom-limb pain in an amputee, intense and life-disrupting despite absent tissue damage, is also bad, though perhaps in virtue of a different feature. Pain experienced during a vivid dream feels awful in the moment, but upon waking I regard it as not genuinely bad, since no harm was actually occurring. Protective pain that causes withdrawal from a flame is instrumentally good in context, yet the sensation itself remains bad; the overall action is justified, not the pain within it.

In genuine pain asymbolia, a patient reports accurate sensory qualities—burning, crushing, piercing—yet describes them as neutral or even curious. There is no suffering, no negative affect, and no behavioral avoidance. Here my intuition is firm: this is not genuinely bad. The same holds, I think, for any hypothetical selective dissociation in which nociceptive signals remain intact but affective valence is entirely abolished; what remains is information, not harm.

I am far less settled about chronic pain that has been fully normalized. A sufferer describes decades of back pain as background weather—no distress, no avoidance, no requests for treatment—yet objective mobility is limited and long-term projects are quietly abandoned. One part of me insists that without felt aversion there is no harm, only an altered bodily state. Another part thinks that a persistent, unchosen constriction of life prospects is bad regardless of subjective coloring.

The case I keep reversing involves what I will call reverse-asymbolia: a hypothetical condition in which nociceptive signaling is entirely absent, yet a person experiences overwhelming, sustained distress and suffering in response to entirely benign tactile stimuli. There is no tissue damage, no threat, and no protective function, but the subjective experience mirrors severe pain in every affective and motivational respect. I judge this to be genuinely bad, and in the same way as ordinary pain. Yet this creates pressure on my other judgments: if badness can be present without any nociception, then the absence of affect in true asymbolia should not eliminate badness but merely relocate it—unless I am implicitly treating nociception as necessary after all.

There is a further tension in the behavioral domain. In ordinary severe pain, avoidance is reflexive; in asymbolia, it is absent. I am inclined to treat avoidance as a reliable symptom of badness rather than its source—if I imagine a paralyzed patient who feels full distress but cannot move away, I still think the pain is bad. But then I must explain why the absence of avoidance in asymbolia tracks the absence of badness so closely, without reducing badness to behavior.

What principle, if any, captures these patterns? Must I distinguish sensory-discriminative, affective-motivational, and cognitive-evaluative dimensions, and if so, which dimensions carry the normative weight? Which of my judgments—particularly about normalized chronic pain and about reverse-asymbolia—should be revised for coherence? And what does the best account predict for a case I have not considered: a fully rational agent with constant, low-grade nociceptive signaling who reports no unpleasantness, no distress, no interference with projects, and no desire for relief, yet who insists that the signal remains a bad feature of their existence purely because it indicates underlying dysfunction?
