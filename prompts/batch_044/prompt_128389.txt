Write a two-page research proposal for a six-month independent philosophy fellowship. The half-formed idea is this: Frank Ramsey's well-known deflationary remarks about whether life has "meaning" are almost always interpreted in isolation from his concurrent work on belief, objective probability, and the rational ordering of preferences. I want to examine whether treating those remarks as part of a broader epistemological framework—specifically, his analysis of belief as carrying both propositional content and a measurable degree of commitment—reveals an unrecognized tension. The precise question is whether Ramsey's belief-commitment apparatus can be extended to long-term evaluative judgments in a way that either rehabilitates the "meaning of life" question as a well-formed problem about practical coherence, or instead exposes a quietism that undermines his own commitments to objective value. Existing interpretations tend to compartmentalize Ramsey's ethics and his philosophy of logic, so the interaction between his deflationary move and his rationality conditions for belief remains unsettled.

The proposal should state the question with enough precision to have a determinate answer, explain why standard readings of Ramsey's ethical remarks leave this open, and describe the method: close conceptual reconstruction of the belief-commitment structure applied to evaluative propositions, clarifying the coherence conditions that would distinguish meaningful from malformed life-questions without importing theological or metaphysical premises. Say what a successful result would look like—ideally, a criterion for when a question about life's meaning is well-formed within a Ramsey-style epistemology—and what failure would consist in, such as a demonstration that the apparatus applies only to empirical beliefs and collapses into circularity when extended to value. Provide an honest assessment of scope for six months, including which primary texts and comparative angles with later expressivist or quietist positions must be reserved for future work. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; named positions and arguments may be referred to generally, with formal citations to be added separately.
