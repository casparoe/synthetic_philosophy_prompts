Design a 60-minute, in-class structured deliberation for 15 undergraduate philosophy students that lets them experience—before it is named—the categorization tension central to the white-horse argument associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names, specifically the claim that because horse denotes essential form and white denotes an independent color-quality, a white horse is not identical with a horse. Do not begin with a lecture on the dialogue; instead, arrange roles, rules, and non-monetary stakes so that participants run into the deadlock mechanically.

Setting and mechanics: Divide the class into three committees of five—the Form Committee, the Color Committee, and an Arbitration Board. Provide each committee with an identical deck of twelve described artifact cards, including a white horse statue, a black horse statue, a white marble sphere, a black iron cube, and eight others that combine shape and hue in non-conflicting ways. Give each committee a budget of exactly ten points to distribute among artifacts that fall exclusively within its mandate. The Form Committee’s mandate is to fund all and only horse-shaped objects regardless of surface appearance; the Color Committee’s mandate is to fund all and only white-colored objects regardless of underlying form. The binding rule is that any artifact may receive funding from at most one committee; if both committees allocate points to the same artifact, both allocations are void and the artifact receives zero. The Arbitration Board may propose withdrawals or mergers but possesses no veto—only the committees can retract a claim. Points determine speaking order in the subsequent 20-minute discussion, so stakes are real but non-monetary.

Deliver the following in your response: (1) The exact artifact descriptions and the point-allocation scoring sheet, including how partial allocations work. (2) A minute-by-minute timeline—silent allocation round, simultaneous reveal and conflict identification, negotiation round with the Arbitration Board present, and final locked-in budgets. (3) The physical materials needed (card stock, score sheets, visible timer) and the room layout. (4) A debrief script that maps the inevitable deadlock over the white horse statue onto the separation of universal form and particular quality in the School of Names, distinguishing the extensional overlap that the game reproduces mechanically from the intensional claim about conceptual separability that the facilitator must supply explicitly because sorting rules alone cannot generate ontology. (5) A frank prediction of what will fail on the first run—such as committees inventing hybrid categories like white-horse-form to escape the paradox, or the Arbitration Board dominating negotiation—and precise corrective moves that preserve the experiential logic rather than resolving the tension administratively. (6) One explicit statement of which feature of Gongsun Long’s reasoning—his reliance on a specific ontology of universals and particulars—this exercise necessarily simplifies, and how you will state that limitation aloud in the debrief without dissolving the force of the problem. Keep the prompt self-contained: present the philosophical structure inside the instructions so that no outside retrieval is required.
