In my seminar on rational agency and value alignment, several participants keep comparing coherent extrapolated volition to the calibration of a measuring instrument: just as we correct a thermometer's systematic drift by comparing its readings against a reference standard in controlled conditions, CEV corrects our actual preference-states by comparing them against an idealized agent with full information, logical consistency, and reflective equilibrium. They use this to argue that idealization is not value-substitution but error-correction — we are not imposing foreign values, only removing noise from an existing signal. I want a rigorous audit of this comparison. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the instrument, the raw measurement, the calibration environment, the reference standard, and the corrected output? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the normative conclusion: for instance, that preferences may lack an intersubjective zero-point, that the calibration alters the very entity being measured rather than revealing a pre-existing quantity, and that there is no external validator independent of the preference-forming process itself. Third, determine whether the conclusion — that CEV is purely procedural correction rather than substantive value construction — survives once these fractures are made explicit, or whether the instrument metaphor was smuggling in the normative commitment all along. Finally, if the analogy breaks, evaluate whether a different technical framework — say, statistical estimation with a misspecified prior, or signal extraction from a corrupted channel — captures the dynamics of preference refinement more honestly, and whether any such framework avoids committing us to substantive values that the calibration metaphor pretends to bracket.
