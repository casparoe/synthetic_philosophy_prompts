I'm drafting material for a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and I've been circling a pattern that seems distinct from the usual frameworks, including Karl Jaspers' familiar distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. Here is the phenomenon as it appears in concrete cases. A state formally acknowledges that it, as a collective subject, bears guilt for historical wrongs—issuing apologies, funding memorials, revising curricula—thereby treating moral culpability as something that can be inherited across generations and owned by present citizens who never personally participated. At the same time, the material advantages that flowed directly from those wrongs—land tenure structures, accumulated capital, institutional privileges—are protected under a separate logic: they are treated as innocent property rights or as the neutral background of legitimate state continuity, not as goods tainted by the same collective inheritance. When critics argue that acknowledging guilt should entail restructuring these benefits, defenders typically respond that moral responsibility and distributive justice belong to different registers, and that treating present beneficiaries as liable for redistribution would confuse symbolic atonement with punitive harm to individuals. The result is a stable configuration: the nation is guilty enough to perform remorse but structurally arranged to preserve what the wrongs produced, with the boundary between moral and material inheritance drawn precisely to make both claims compatible.

Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on intergenerational justice or collective responsibility? It is not exactly Jaspers' political guilt, which concerns shared civic responsibility for state actions, because here the acceptance of guilt is symbolic and decoupled from institutional restructuring; nor is it metaphysical guilt, since the present generation actively claims the guilt rather than feeling solidarity with universal human failure. It also differs from standard accounts of structural injustice or historical redress, because the distinctive feature is not merely the persistence of harm but the deliberate compartmentalization—moral inheritance is activated, economic inheritance is quarantined.
