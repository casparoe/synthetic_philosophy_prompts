Design a thought experiment that isolates the tension between the hedonistic imperative—the abolition of suffering through biotechnology—and a rival ethical framework that treats the capacity for suffering as constitutive rather than merely instrumental to moral agency. The scenario should center on a realistic, near-future public-health intervention: a targeted gene-therapy vector that selectively silences nociceptive affect while leaving discriminative touch and temperature sensing fully intact, made available through an opt-in municipal program. Engineer the case so that advocates of the hedonistic imperative are driven, by an externality argument rather than by paternalism, to support compulsory universal administration, while the rival view resists on the grounds that the absence of suffering undermines a specific form of practical reasoning—without that resistance collapsing into status-quo bias or risk aversion. Constraints: present the scenario in a single paragraph; avoid trolley-style framing, hypothetical perfect predictors, or science-fictional elements; and do not invoke named philosophers as authorities within the argument. After the scenario, provide the verdict and reasoning for each view, identify precisely which structural feature of the case forces the divergence—specifically, whether suffering is valued for its causal role in reasoning or for its constitutive relation to it—and explain how a proponent of the hedonistic imperative could attack the rival position by showing that the alleged externality is illusory.
