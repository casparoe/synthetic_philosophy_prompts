I am drafting a scene for a novel in which eight scholars in a fictional academy must revise the very rules that govern revision, and to make the philosophical tension tangible rather than expository I wish to stage this as a live, ninety-minute classroom simulation. The setting is a graduate seminar in jurisprudence—eight participants, one facilitator, no outside audience, one continuous session. The idea to be made visceral is the paradox of legal self-reference: specifically, the impossibility of a constitutional rule that authorizes its own alteration without either presupposing the authority it seeks to dissolve or generating an infinite regress of meta-rules. To keep the exercise from collapsing into a familiar textbook recitation, I have invented a specific mechanism—the Mirror Clause—which participants will encounter only inside the simulation.

Design a structured role-play with precise rules, physical materials, and payoffs that do not require real money. Divide the eight participants into a Constituent Senate of five and an Arbiter Council of three. Provide each group with printed cards: a Fundamental Protocol containing Article One (establishing the polity), Article Two (the Amendment Gate: any change to the Protocol requires endorsement by four of the five Senators and confirmation by the Arbiter Council using the sealing rites of Appendix B), and Article Three—the Mirror Clause—which states that should any proposal alter Article Two itself, the Arbiter Council must apply the old Appendix B rites not to the proposed new text but to the shadow-text of Article Two as it existed before the proposal, and must do so with the Senate seated exactly as constituted prior to the amendment. The Senators’ narrative objective is to lower the Article Two threshold from four-fifths to a simple majority so they may enact a contentious Article Four concerning distributive justice; they must therefore amend Article Two using Article Two itself.

Specify the exact sequence of actions—proposal, deliberation, vote, confirmation—governed by the printed rules, using tangible props: a sealed envelope holding the shadow-text, colored tokens representing Senate seats that can be physically removed only after ratification, and a confirmation stamp that the Arbiter Council may apply only to the old text. Arrange the incentives so that the Senators genuinely want Article Four passed, while the Arbiter Council members have hidden role cards instructing them to defend procedural integrity; the contradiction should emerge from their interaction, not from a lecture.

Then provide the debrief. It should map each moment of deadlock or absurdity onto a logical feature of self-amending rules: the initial confidence that rules are mutable, the discovery that mutation requires the cooperation of the rule being mutated, the temptation to invoke an unwritten meta-rule, and the final impossibility when the old Senate and old Arbiter are required to ratify their own abolition. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time—likely that participants will invent an ad hoc meta-rule to break the deadlock, or that the Arbiter Council will disregard the Mirror Clause to preserve narrative momentum—and advise how the facilitator should handle this without breaking the frame of the simulation. Be frank about which theoretical feature—the formal infinite regress of meta-rules—this physical exercise cannot reproduce, and state precisely what the facilitator must supply in the closing minutes to complete the philosophical picture. Finally, include a brief note on how the novelist might adapt the participants’ recorded dialogue into fictional scene-work.
