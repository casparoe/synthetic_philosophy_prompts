I'm presenting a colloquium paper next Thursday defending what I call frame-sensitive particularism: the claim that moral reasons are not merely variable in weight across contexts, as standard particularists hold, but that the very identity of a feature as a reason depends on an irreducibly situational relational framing. On this view, the same feature—say, withholding information—can operate as a reason for condemnation in one interactional frame and as a reason for praise in another, and this reversal is not recoverable from any general principle, nor from Dancy-style pluralism about reasons. I ground the view in a model of salience calibration drawn from perceptual psychology but applied to moral attention: agents do not apply rules to determine what matters; rather, they attune to a gestalt comprising their emotional posture, the narrative arc of the interaction, and the perceived vulnerability of the other party. This means particularism is not just about the absence of principles but about the positive dynamics of moral attention.
