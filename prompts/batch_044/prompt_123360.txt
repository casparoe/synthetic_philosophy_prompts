I'm delivering the commencement address in June at a graduate school of public policy and administration, where roughly half the audience will be graduates entering government agencies, advocacy groups, and party-affiliated institutions, and the other half will be family members with no background in philosophy. I want the address built around Simone Weil's critique of political parties—specifically her argument that parties are not merely opinion groups but institutional mechanisms that demand a suspension of individual moral judgment in exchange for collective belonging, and that they manufacture artificial passions that displace genuine attention to justice. The idea needs to do real structural work: I cannot offer the usual reassurance that graduates should simply stay true to themselves, since Weil treats that individualist language as naive and inadequate, but I also cannot dismiss collective political action altogether, since many in the room will spend their careers inside organizations. To avoid a generic recycling of her best-known essay, please inject a conceptual distinction rarely discussed in popular accounts: the tension between gravity (pesanteur), the downward conformist pull of institutional belonging, and decreation (décréation), the voluntary emptying of ego that Weil links to genuine moral attention—but apply it specifically to bureaucratic and administrative decision-making rather than to religious mysticism. The speech should run about fifteen minutes, land for both the professionals and the families without being patronizing, and include a moment where I concede that Weil's own life offers an ambiguous example rather than a model to imitate. Finally, please append a brief epistemic note separated from the speech itself: distinguish what is broadly settled in Weil scholarship regarding her anti-party argument from what remains genuinely contested—particularly whether the critique applies with equal force to non-partisan bureaucracies, professional associations, and social movements—and flag any interpretive move in the address that goes beyond the scholarly consensus.
