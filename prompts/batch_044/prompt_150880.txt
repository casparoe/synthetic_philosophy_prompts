After a long argument with a friend about Whig history—not the generic complaint that winners write the record, but the specific epistemic temptation to treat the present as an explanatory attractor, so that earlier events are read as necessary way-stations rather than as outcomes that might easily not have led here—I want a poem that enacts that distortion structurally. Write a Petrarchan sonnet: in the octave, present three discrete historical episodes (for instance, a seventeenth-century religious settlement, a nineteenth-century franchise reform, and a present-day parliamentary session) each rendered in language faithful to its own local logic and deliberately refusing any forward reference; then let the volta at line nine be spoken by the historian who rewrites all three as progressive stations on a single arc toward modern liberal democracy. Make the rhyme scheme perform the coercion: preserve the octave’s rhyme sounds but force them into the sestet’s different pattern, so that the past’s own phonetic vocabulary is recognized yet displaced, as if being pressed into service for a conclusion it never endorsed. The poem should be precise enough that a historian of historiography recognizes the particular error—retrospective determinism, the collapse of counterfactual openness—rather than a vague moral about bias, and it should still work as poetry for a reader who knows none of this. No citations, no references, no bibliography; just the sonnet.
