Using the Wilt Chamberlain argument, explain why Nozick thinks liberty necessarily upsets any patterned principle of distributive justice. Your answer should distinguish historical from end-state principles, explain the role of voluntary exchange, and assess whether the argument demonstrates that patterned theories are false, or only that they are unstable in practice—including whether a pattern that updates dynamically with each voluntary transaction could survive the objection. The student's response read as follows: "Nozick's entitlement theory is a historical principle of justice, which evaluates distributions by their history rather than their structure, unlike end-state principles such as Rawls's difference principle. The theory comprises justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification. The Wilt Chamberlain scenario begins with an equal distribution; people voluntarily pay a small sum to watch Chamberlain play basketball, leaving him with £250,000 and reducing others' shares. Because these payments are free transfers, justice in transfer is satisfied, so the resulting inequality is just, even though it destroys the initial pattern. Thus liberty is incompatible with maintaining any patterned distribution. Nozick also invokes the separateness of persons, an idea he borrows from Rawls's original position, to argue that forcing Chamberlain to redistribute his earnings in order to preserve equality treats him merely as a means to maintain a social pattern. Furthermore, Chamberlain's basketball talent is a natural endowment rather than an acquired holding, which means justice in acquisition does not apply to it. Consequently, the entitlement theory cannot justify his earnings, since the base of the voluntary transfers—his natural ability—lies outside the scope of the theory. This shows that Nozick's argument establishes only the practical instability of patterns, not the falsehood of patterned principles themselves."
