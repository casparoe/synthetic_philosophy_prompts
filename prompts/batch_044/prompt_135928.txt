I take Machiavelli's account of virtù and fortuna to be best read as a theory of ontological improvisation rather than strategic adaptation. On this view, virtù is not merely the skill of anticipating and redirecting contingent forces—fortune as a river to be channeled—but a performative practice that temporarily collapses the distinction between agent and circumstance, so that the prince's act does not defend against an independent flow but actually generates the pattern of events he inhabits. Fortuna becomes, in this reading, an artifact of virtù rather than an external resistance to it, which makes Machiavelli a theorist of non-derivative political creation rather than a classical realist about power. The value of the view lies not in predicting successful rule but in showing that agency can remain constitutive even under conditions of radical uncertainty.

What would have to be true for this interpretation to fail? I want the evidence, arguments, and textual patterns that should genuinely move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Which of Machiavelli's historical examples, his treatments of cruelty and mercy, or his founding narratives do defenders of the standard strategic-adaptation reading treat as direct counter-evidence, and which of these could my view absorb only by ad hoc reinterpretation? Is there a danger that the interpretation has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if any apparent failure of agency can be redescribed as a hidden restructuring of the agent-circumstance relation—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for an interpretive claim of this kind? Which rival frameworks—republican, civic-humanist, or proto-utilitarian—does my view risk collapsing into if its distinctive claim about the generative collapse is weakened, and what would be the least costly revision that preserves something genuinely Machiavellian?
