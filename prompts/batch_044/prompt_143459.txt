I cover philosophy of science and am tracking how one metaphysician—who accepts Leibniz's principle only in restricted form—has ruled on identity puzzles. Their published judgments are these: (1) Black's two spheres in an otherwise empty symmetric universe are genuinely distinct objects, because the spatial relation between them is real and not reducible to intrinsic properties; (2) a single sphere alone in a featureless void lacks determinacy of size or position and is therefore ontologically defective rather than a complete object; (3) two fermions in a perfectly overlapping quantum state, with identical wavefunction profiles, are not two individuals but one system with multiplicity two, since no relational or structural difference can be assigned to each; (4) two bit-for-bit identical computer simulations running on causally isolated hardware are distinct processes, because the causal-substrate relation grounds individuation.

The case I now want to test has not reached them. A proposed mirror-crystal architecture places two macroscopic crystal lattices—perfect structural duplicates—inside separate vacuum chambers with no direct causal interaction. They are linked only by a shared entangled control qubit that forces instantaneous state mirroring without energy exchange, so that any internal change in one is duplicated in the other, yet no observer can determine which lattice is prior or primary.

Would this philosopher judge one distributed crystal or two? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the competing rules that consistently explain all four prior judgments, what each rule predicts for these mirror-crystals, your best-guess prediction with the reasoning that favors it, and the specific further scenario—say, one involving a broken entanglement or an asymmetric external probe—that would definitively separate the rules.
