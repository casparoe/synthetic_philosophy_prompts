I am preparing an interview sequence for a philosophy podcast panel in which the guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a functional-ecology account of corruption. On her view, corruption is not defined by the standard Western legalist formula—abuse of public office for private gain—but rather as any social practice that destabilizes the local normative equilibrium within which it operates, irrespective of whether state officials are involved. This means that clientelist gift-giving inside a patronage network can be entirely non-corrupt when it sustains the reciprocity expectations that stabilize that network, while a technically legal campaign donation in a liberal democracy may be corrupt if it violates the implicit rules governing fair electoral competition. She also argues that Western anti-corruption frameworks commit a category mistake by universalizing an impersonal state-citizen dyad that never characterized many societies, and that applying Western legalist labels to all clientelist exchange therefore amounts to normative imperialism. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go far beyond asking her to restate this position, and avoid any generic biographical or methodological openers. The sequence must include at least one question pressing the boundary problem—specifically, how her view distinguishes corrupt destabilization from benign institutional change or revolutionary transformation that also disrupts an existing equilibrium—along with a follow-up keyed to her most likely answer that anticipates her standard deflection, namely that critics are merely smuggling the Western legalist framework back into the evaluation. Include a second question addressing societies in which the local normative equilibrium is itself morally oppressive—for instance, a patronage system that systematically excludes women or minorities—asking whether sustaining that equilibrium can ever count as genuinely non-corrupt on her account, again with a follow-up that makes the deflection harder. Include a third question about practical redesign: if an international development agency adopted her framework, how should it restructure anti-corruption metrics without simply endorsing the status quo, with its corresponding follow-up. Finally, include one closing question that asks what comparative empirical evidence or case study could in principle change her mind about whether Western legalist standards are genuinely universalizable rather than culturally parochial, framing it so that she cannot answer with a bare appeal to incommensurability or cultural particularism. Present the entire sequence in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices, as though writing a single long-form email or letter in a formal, polished register.
