I've been working through the ethical implications of panpsychism after a long thread on rationalist forums about whether consciousness is likely distributed across physical substrates, and I keep returning to a framing that I think is underexplored. The standard debate asks whether panpsychism implies we have moral obligations to inanimate objects, or whether it catastrophically multiplies the amount of suffering in the universe. I think both of these miss the deeper structural point. If we adopt a version of panpsychism—say, one where phenomenal properties are fundamental but macro-subjectivity requires specific organizational conditions that solve some version of the combination problem—then the relevant ethical question is not whether electrons suffer, but whether the existence of ubiquitous micro-phenomenal properties changes the status of suffering as an intervention target for ethical reasoning.

Please argue for the following position: panpsychism does not expand our moral obligations downward to include rocks or fields, but instead undermines the assumption that suffering is a localized, addressable anomaly that can be efficiently reduced through targeted intervention. Specifically, defend the view that because micro-phenomenal states are causally inert with respect to macro-behavior—much like the thermal motion of individual molecules doesn't determine the macroscopic dynamics of a gas—the presence of widespread low-level phenomenal properties creates a kind of moral background radiation that is real but not actionable. This means the effective altruist framework, which prioritizes suffering based on tractability and scale, remains intact for biological sentience, but its philosophical justification shifts: we care about animals not because they are the only loci of suffering, but because they are the only loci where suffering is organized into causally responsive systems. Address the objection that this view makes suffering ubiquitous yet ethically irrelevant, and explain why causal responsiveness—not mere presence—should be the correct criterion for moral priority in a panpsychist ontology.
