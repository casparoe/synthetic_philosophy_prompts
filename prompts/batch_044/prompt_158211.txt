Two people in my reading group on the historical Jesus—Nadia and Jonas—spent an hour arguing over whether the standard criteria of authenticity can ever settle who Jesus actually was, or whether they only map the layers of tradition that came after him. Nadia opened by saying that convergence across independent streams can do real work: if a saying appears in Mark, Q, and the Gospel of Thomas without clear theological dependence, and if it sounds dissimilar to both Second Temple Judaism and the concerns of the later church—say, a beatitude blessing the poor with no apocalyptic framing—then we have multi-source confirmation that traces back to Jesus himself. Add coherence with other such sayings, plus the embarrassment of Jesus’s baptism by John and the report that his family thought he was out of his mind, and she claimed you can build a cumulative, probabilistic profile: an apocalyptic prophet announcing an imminent kingdom. She admitted that no single criterion is decisive, but insisted that together they shift probabilities enough to settle a specific figure rather than leaving us with an unrecoverable blur.

Jonas pushed back on every front. He said dissimilarity is circular: to judge a saying “unlike Judaism” or “unlike the church” requires knowing exactly what both were like, but our reconstructions depend heavily on the very texts we are filtering, so the criterion smuggles in background assumptions and then uses them to isolate Jesus. He attacked coherence as circular in the other direction—once you accept one questionable saying, coherence just extends the pattern by fitting other sayings into the same assumed profile. On embarrassment, he argued that what looks embarrassing to modern readers might have served an early theological purpose, such as framing John’s submission to Jesus as proof of Jesus’s superiority, or turning the crucifixion into a necessary fulfillment. So the criteria do not isolate Jesus; they isolate whatever survives a filter designed by scholars who already have hypotheses.

Nadia countered that Jonas’s skepticism, taken consistently, would make all ancient biography impossible—no one demands Cartesian proof for Tacitus or Josephus. She pointed to Aramaic linguistic substrata in sayings about the kingdom as evidence of a Palestinian origin in the 30s CE, not a later Greek-speaking invention. She also insisted that some details, like Jesus’s family thinking he was disturbed, are genuinely hard to invent because they undermine his authority in ways the early church had every reason to suppress.

Jonas did not deny the Aramaic point, but he introduced what he called the “distributed voice” problem: even if a saying carries an Aramaic core and circulates in multiple streams, that only proves it belongs to an early Aramaic-speaking community of disciples, not necessarily to Jesus’s own mouth. In prophetic and wisdom movements, followers often spoke for and in the name of their teacher. Because the criteria are designed to detect a single origin point, Jonas argued, they miss this structural ambiguity. They can tell us what the first generation remembered and attributed, but they cannot settle whether that attribution tracks personal biography or collective representation. He added that if distributed authorship was widespread, then Nadia’s claim that the criteria “settle who Jesus was” collapses into the weaker claim that they “settle what the earliest Jesus-movement said.”

Nadia replied that this “distributed voice” alternative is speculative—there is no direct evidence that Jesus’s followers were composing sayings in his name the way later rabbinic traditions attributed sayings to earlier masters. She accused Jonas of replacing one methodological limitation with an unfalsifiable escape hatch. Still, she conceded that if distributed authorship were indeed structural in the movement, then the criteria might be tracking the wrong unit of analysis, and her stronger claim about settling Jesus’s personal identity might fail.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point. Who is right about the circularity of dissimilarity, the strength of the embarrassment criterion, the significance of Aramaic substrata, and the “distributed voice” problem? What, if anything, are both Nadia and Jonas missing—whether about how the criteria actually function together, about the historical context of attribution practices, or about what “settling who he was” would even require? Take a definite position and defend it with reasons: can the criteria of authenticity ever genuinely settle who the historical Jesus was, or do they necessarily stop short of reaching the person behind the traditions? Do not merely list options; commit to a verdict.
