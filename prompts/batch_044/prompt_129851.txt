I convene a postgraduate seminar on philosophy of science—around fourteen students, one ninety-minute session—where I want participants to feel, rather than merely discuss, why Popper’s falsifiability criterion struggles to serve as a demarcation tool once auxiliary hypotheses are permitted. Rather than opening with a lecture, I want them to discover the difficulty through a structured funding-allocation simulation I have provisionally called the Council of Auxiliary Hypotheses. Please design the full activity.

Divide the cohort into four competing research programmes, each initially assigned one core claim and a set of observable predictions. Over three rounds, programmes present predictions; if a prediction fails, the group votes either to reject the programme or to allow it to propose an auxiliary adjustment that protects the core claim but alters future predictions. Introduce a scoring system—reputation points, not real money—that rewards correct predictions but also imposes a hidden cost on adjustments that are not independently testable within the remaining rounds. The rules should be arranged so that, in round one, participants enthusiastically defend failing programmes through clever auxiliaries; by round three, they should realise that the same defensive manoeuvre renders every programme unfalsifiable, yet that banning all adjustments eliminates genuinely progressive science. The facilitator should remain silent about falsificationism, the demarcation problem, or any named philosophers until the debrief.

Provide: the exact rules, roles, and materials (cards, score sheets, or prompt scripts); a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; instructions for how the facilitator should intervene if a group collapses into unanimous rejection or unanimous permissiveness; and a debrief that maps each round onto the logical structure of the demarcation challenge—specifically, the tension between naive falsification and the protection of theories via untestable auxiliaries—without naming those positions until participants have articulated the difficulty themselves. Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: for example, the historical sociology of actual scientific communities, or the distinction between ad hoc and independently motivated auxiliaries, which the facilitator must supply verbally afterwards. Write the response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as the exercise must be entirely self-contained.
