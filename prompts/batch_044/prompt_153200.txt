I'm writing up my position for a graduate seminar on value pluralism and practical reason, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is roughly where I've landed. I hold that value pluralism is correct: there are genuinely distinct values—knowledge, artistic achievement, intimate loyalty, civic equality—that do not reduce to a single master value like welfare or autonomy. I also think these values are strongly incommensurable: there is no common metric, no precise exchange rate between them, and attempts to force them onto one scale distort what they are. In hard choices—say, between devoting one's life to philosophy versus medicine, or between staying near family and accepting a distant fellowship—I reject both the view that one option is secretly best and the view that the options are exactly equal. Instead, I accept the parity thesis: the options are comparable without being rankable, and rational resolution requires a volitional commitment that makes one option better-for-me, not the discovery of a hidden ordering. I also accept the small-improvement argument, that giving one option a slight advantage does not break the parity but merely shifts it. At the same time, I maintain that rational preference ought to be complete and transitive: a rational agent should not have genuinely unranked pairs of outcomes, and her choices should not cycle. Finally, I am a moral realist about value: I believe there are mind-independent facts about what is good, including about which life paths are best, though I allow that an agent's own commitments can partly determine which values carry authority for her. I also think that rational regret is appropriate after hard choices, because the values embodied in the unchosen path are genuinely lost and irreplaceable, not merely unrealized preferences.

My concern is that some of these commitments pull against each other in ways I haven't fully sorted out. The combination of strong incommensurability with the completeness and transitivity requirements on rational preference looks especially strained; and if hard choices are genuinely on a par with no pre-existing fact about which path is best, my moral realism about the best path seems to hang in the air—perhaps I can save it by letting agent-commitment partly constitute the relevant fact, but then I worry I'm compromising mind-independence. By contrast, I suspect the tension between pluralism and realism is only apparent, since pluralism needn't deny that each value is objectively real. Could you audit this package? Please identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each serious one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Don't tell me which single commitment to abandon; just map the fault lines precisely, including whether my account of rational regret is consistent with the parity view or introduces an additional problem.
