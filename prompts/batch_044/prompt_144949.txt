I've been working through the debate between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen over the perpetrators in Reserve Police Battalion 101, and I've started to think that both accounts understate a specific mechanism that isn't quite peer pressure and isn't quite ideological fervor. I'd call it normative delegation through procedural role absorption: the idea that these agents treated the organizational protocol itself as a source of moral authorization, actively displacing first-person evaluation of the act onto the system's design. On this framing, sustained participation correlates less with prior antisemitism or with acute situational stress, and more with the degree to which an agent identifies with the technical correctness of his assigned function—a kind of professional integrity misapplied.

If this is right, then the explanatory conflict between situation and character is partly miscast. What looks like ordinary men adapting becomes ordinary professionals outsourcing moral judgment; what looks like willing executioners becomes agents whose ideological commitments happen to align with a delegated norm that they didn't personally author. I want an honest assessment of what would defeat this framing—not just the standard objections that peer pressure clearly operated, or that Goldhagen documented widespread antisemitism, but specifically: what patterns in testimony, post-war conduct, or comparable bureaucratic violence would make the delegation story untenable? Would it collapse if perpetrators with weak professional identity but high ideological commitment turned out to be the most persistent? Or if role-distance language were absent in other cases of bureaucratic atrocity? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that is merely awkward, say which counterarguments defenders of this view could absorb by redefining professional identity, and tell me whether that absorptive capacity is a feature or a bug.
