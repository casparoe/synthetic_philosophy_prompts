Consider this case. Two scholars co-founded an interdisciplinary research collective governed by a charter requiring that all work in their shared domain be published under the group name, with no solo submissions without unanimous consent. After an irreparable split, they dissolve the collective, yet they preserve one residual obligation: for two years, neither will publish in that domain, and any exception requires both signatures. They now draft the dissolution agreement. One scholar notices that violating this clause feels structurally different from breaching an ordinary bilateral contract—say, a standard non-compete—even though the practical constraints are identical. Specifically, she reports that the wrongness of a unilateral breach seems to involve not merely failing in her duty to her former partner, but damaging something they still jointly sustain, despite the fact that the cooperative "we" that originally produced research no longer exists.

Please advise: is there a genuine philosophical distinction here between a joint commitment and a merely reciprocal pair of obligations? And if so, how should we understand the normative status of such commitments when the shared purpose that originally grounded them has dissolved—particularly in cases, like this one, where the joint obligation takes a negative form (a shared abstention) rather than a shared project?
