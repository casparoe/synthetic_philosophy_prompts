I have been working through the problem of pure time preference with a small intergenerational-ethics reading group, and we have reached a stall that feels structural rather than evidential. One side presses the familiar argument—rooted in Sidgwick and developed through Ramsey to contemporary philosophers—that discounting future welfare solely because it is future is arbitrary in exactly the same way that spatial discounting is arbitrary: if moral reasons are genuinely agent-neutral, they should not weaken with temporal distance any more than with physical distance. The opposing side maintains that this analogy fails because future persons are not presently actual, because democratic institutions represent only present constituents, and because rational agency requires that reasons be causally or epistemically available to the agent who acts on them; on this view, pure time preference is not a moral mistake but a structural feature of practical reasoning under uncertainty and representation. We can all agree on the formal discount functions—exponential, hyperbolic, or zero-rate—and on the climate-economics arithmetic that applies them, yet after months of discussion no position has shifted. My working hypothesis is that we are not disagreeing about normative ethics at all, but about what counts as a reason across time: whether an agent can be genuinely moved by considerations whose objects do not yet exist, or whether all valid practical reasons must be indexed to the agent's present psychological and causal situation. But I am unsure whether this captures the real divide, or whether there is a hidden empirical disagreement about risk and intergenerational obligations, or whether the two sides are answering different questions—one about decision-theoretic rationality, one about the metaphysics of diachronic personal identity—under the single heading "pure time preference."
