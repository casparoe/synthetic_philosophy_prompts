I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I recently joined a casual reading group that is working through some of Aristotle's ethical and political writings. One distinction that keeps surfacing is between oikonomia—household management oriented toward use and limited need—and chrematistike, the art of acquiring wealth without natural limit, which Aristotle associates with treating money (tokos) as if it were fertile despite being barren by nature. This made me think about a feature of modern economic institutions that I had never questioned: defined-contribution retirement systems are legally structured so that fiduciaries must maximize long-term compound returns for beneficiaries, effectively obligating ordinary people to treat their savings as capital that ought to grow exponentially through interest, dividends, or capital appreciation. At the same time, the same legal frameworks often maintain anti-usury statutes or interest-rate caps on consumer lending, which treat charging interest to borrowers in need as ethically or legally suspect.

What implicit theory of value, exchange, or the nature of money does this institutional split presuppose? Does it require abandoning Aristotle's claim that money is a sterile medium invented only for exchange, or can it be reconciled with a hidden distinction—say, between productive investment and consumptive lending—that Aristotle himself might have recognized? More specifically, does the fiduciary duty to compound capital over decades presuppose that chrematistike has become not merely permissible but a moral obligation for the prudent individual, and if so, what does that imply about the concept of "enough" wealth? And if one actually adopted the Aristotelian view that wealth should be limited by use and that exchange ought to serve living well rather than accumulate without end, what concrete changes would be required in the design of retirement accounts, banking regulation, or fiduciary law? I am especially interested in whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether several rival theories of value could license the same split—or whether it quietly commits us to one particular view of money as naturally fruitful.
