I serve on a state education-equity review board that receives funding and policy proposals invoking behavioral genetics, and I need a reusable audit procedure my team can run without a geneticist present. The context is specific but recurring: applicants cite narrow-sense heritability estimates—often from twin studies—to justify tracking systems, reduced investment in universal interventions, or the adoption of polygenic scores for student placement in diverse districts. I want you to translate the actual statistical meaning of heritability—population-level variance partition contingent on a specific environment, not an individual-level prediction, not a measure of immutability, and not portable across populations without validation—into an ordered procedural checklist. For each step, specify the question to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient factual answer, where the procedure stops with a hard rejection (for instance, if the proposal treats heritability as destiny for individuals or assumes environment-invariance), and where it must hand the decision to a normative judgment because the issue is values rather than statistics. The procedure must explicitly handle three hard cases that keep appearing: proposals that correctly cite a population heritability figure but implicitly assume it holds under environmental redistribution; proposals that compare heritability across ancestry groups with different environmental ranges; and proposals that apply polygenic scores developed in one ancestry group to another. Then apply the procedure to two worked cases: first, a district proposal to use polygenic scores for advanced-track placement in a racially diverse urban system, arguing that because educational-attainment heritability is high, environmental investment plays a minor role; second, a legislative memo claiming that because the heritability of a specific cognitive trait is 0.75, universal tutoring is not cost-effective. In your response, clearly separate what is settled in quantitative genetics from what remains genuinely contested—such as the portability of polygenic scores across ancestries, the stability of heritability estimates under environmental change, and whether gene-environment correlation undermines causal interpretation—without presenting any contested view as the consensus. Structure your answer in the following sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths: The Audit Procedure (about 450 words); Case One: The Tracking Proposal (about 300 words); Case Two: The Tutoring Memo (about 300 words); Judgment Points and Value Hand-Offs (about 250 words); and Settled Science vs. Contested Territory (about 250 words).
