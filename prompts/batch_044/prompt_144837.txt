I have been arguing with a friend for hours about Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, and I have ended up defending a particular reading that I want to test for exposure. I hold that Rousseau's objection to establishing a theater in Geneva is not primarily about conventional moral corruption—vice, luxury, or effeminacy—but about a structural mismatch between theatrical representation and republican citizenship. On this view, the spectator's relation to the actor is a form of mediated recognition that feeds amour-propre rather than amour de soi: the audience learns to seek approval through appearance and representation rather than through direct civic reciprocity, and the actor's craft depends on being seen rather than on being. I take this to mean that for Rousseau, theater is not accidentally harmful but structurally incompatible with the transparent, immediate social bond required by a small republic of equals. What would genuinely count against this interpretation? I want an honest map of its vulnerability. Which of Rousseau's own writings or commitments could be read as undermining it—say, his own theatrical compositions, his approval of certain public festivals, or passages in the Letter itself that sound more prudential than structural? Which historical or conceptual objections show that representation can be civic rather than corrupting, and which of these would a defender of this reading concede as damaging rather than dismiss as talking past the point? And where does the position risk being insulated by qualification: if every apparent counterexample—Greek civic chorus, participatory pageantry, modern community theater—can be absorbed as "not really theater" or "not really representation," has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a problem for a claim of this kind? Please separate what is genuinely settled about Rousseau's position and its textual basis from what remains contested among scholars who take the Letter seriously, and do not present a contested interpretive claim—such as whether amour-propre is necessarily theatrical, or whether Rousseau's critique applies only to Geneva's specific conditions—as if it were the consensus view.
