I'm giving the opening remarks at my mentor's retirement symposium next week. She spent forty years tearing apart sloppy appeals to the principle of indiference—Bertrand's chord paradox, continuous parameter spaces, the whole thing. Half the room are philosophers of probability who know the paradox cold; the other half are her family and old neighboors. Write the speech as exactly five short movements, each just a sentance or two, with no separate intro or conclusion. The paradox should structure the whole thing: the point is that "equal ignorance" always hides a choice of how to measure the space, and that's exactly why her career mattered—she wouldn't let people hide behind false neutrality when weighing evidence or lives. Don't cite papers or give a bibliography; it needs to work from reasoning alone. Keep it warm and affectionate, not a lecture, and make sure both sides of the room can hear it.
