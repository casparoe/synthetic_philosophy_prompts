I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am helping a researcher prepare for an interdisciplinary seminar hosted by a quantitative social-science institute. The speaker will defend the thesis that there is no single fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, because violence operates across incommensurable registers—acute kinetic harm, structural deprivation with delayed mortality, and what the speaker terms institutional latency violence—such that per-capita rates, absolute casualty aggregates, and categorical definitions can diverge without contradiction. The audience will include demographers who regard per-capita normalization as the only scientifically respectable metric, conflict historians who track absolute death tolls across state collapses and wars, and conceptual philosophers who treat definitional disputes about violence as resolvable through rigorous conceptual analysis rather than pluralistic acceptance. Please prepare the speaker for the objections: what each subgroup will actually raise, phrased in their own disciplinary vocabulary; which objections carry real force and should be conceded upfront; where the standard replies are weakest; and identify the one hidden objection—most likely from the philosophers—about whether this incommensurability thesis collapses into a trivial linguistic pluralism that evades rather than answers the normative question. In your response, clearly separate what is settled in the relevant scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among informed researchers, and do not present any particular framework—such as the structural versus direct violence distinction, or the normative weight of institutional latency—as if it commanded consensus.
