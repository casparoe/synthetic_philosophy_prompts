For a philosophy of medicine and law seminar, I have drafted the following package of views on what makes consent valid, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(1) Valid consent has two independent necessary conditions—informational adequacy and voluntariness—such that failure of one does not automatically entail failure of the other.

(2) Informational adequacy requires grasp of the act's fundamental nature and its immediate physical or legal consequences; it does not require comprehension of remote causal chains, probabilistic risk distributions, or the underlying scientific mechanisms.

(3) If a person consents while laboring under a false belief about the identity of the other party or about the fundamental category of the act—for example, believing a procedure is therapeutic when it is purely experimental—the consent is void, regardless of whether the false belief was deliberately induced.

(4) By contrast, false beliefs about the other party's motives, private values, or future intentions—provided they are not deliberately induced—do not invalidate an otherwise adequate consent, even if the consenter would have refused had they known the truth.

(5) Competence is graded and domain-specific rather than global: an individual may be fully competent to consent to a routine interpersonal arrangement yet incompetent to consent to a structurally isomorphic arrangement embedded in a bureaucratic or technological framework that obscures the stakes through procedural complexity.

(6) There is a form of authorization I have been calling asymmetric consent escrow: a person can validly pre-authorize a specific class of future actions they will not understand at the time of execution, provided they understand the governing decision-procedure and explicitly accept that the procedure may select an outcome they would reject if they understood it in detail.

(7) Coercion undermines consent only when the threatened harm is both illegitimate and eliminates every reasonable alternative; intense structural pressure, economic necessity, or social expectation that makes refusal costly but not catastrophic does not count as coercive for these purposes.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severely. For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it costs me. Rank the tensions by severity. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if revised or dropped, would clean up the largest number of conflicts, and whether the remaining package is then stable or still contains hidden fractures.
