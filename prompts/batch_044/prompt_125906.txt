For a seminar handout on low-impact artificial intelligence, write a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on a single question: can an agent be constrained to "avoid side effects" without possessing or accessing a full specification of what is valuable?

Construct the tree from logical commitments rather than from author names. The root node should be the acceptance that unintended physical or informational changes caused by an agent's actions are distinguishable in principle from its intended changes.

From there, build exactly four branch levels, each phrased as a concrete diagnostic claim that a respondent can evaluate without knowing which leaf it produces:

First branch: is the boundary between intended and unintended change defined by the agent's internal plan, by the difference between the actual world and a physically nearest counterfactual in which the agent did not act, or by a normative baseline specifying which states ought to be preserved?

Second branch: if one rejects full value specification, can a non-axiological proxy—such as minimizing the irreversible entropy production of the environment, or minimizing the Kolmogorov complexity of the state-change description—adequately substitute for "do not harm what matters"?

Third branch: in an open-ended environment where human values remain partially unarticulated, should side-effect avoidance be treated as a hard constraint on the action space, as a scalar penalty in an evaluation function, or as a meta-level prohibition on the agent updating its own model of value?

Fourth branch: does the claim that side-effect avoidance requires value specification symmetrically apply to human operators, or is there a relevant asymmetry in how responsibility is assigned for unanticipated outcomes?

Each leaf should carry either an established label (for example, "counterfactual-baseline minimalism," "entropy-footprint constraint," "value-agnostic impact metric," "meta-level preservationism," or "unnamed") or be explicitly marked "unnamed," followed by exactly one sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment—specifically, a commitment it must accept that makes it difficult to apply in real-world deployment.

Then map the actual positions found in the low-impact AI and alignment literature onto the appropriate leaves. Identify any combination of answers that produces an unoccupied leaf, and briefly diagnose whether that vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere lack of exploration. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—where two conceptually distinct positions collapse onto the identical pattern of answers.

Limit the tree to no more than eight leaves. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or attributions to specific texts or authors. Write for an audience of philosophers of technology and AI safety researchers who need to diagnose their own commitments rather than identify themselves by familiar labels.
