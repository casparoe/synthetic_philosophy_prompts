I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I have been trying to apply the extended mind thesis—the idea that cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain into tools and environments—to my own development practice. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to run my own intuitions through reflective equilibrium. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, followed by a tentative principle that I suspect fails.

Case 1: I maintain a personal knowledge base of debugging notes and design rationales. When I return to old code, I read my own notes and recover the reasoning. Verdict: this is part of my extended cognition.

Case 2: My IDE provides intelligent autocomplete drawn from the current codebase. I do not memorize the API surface; I rely on the tool to complete method names and signatures. Verdict: also part of my extended cognition.

Case 3: Our team keeps a deployment runbook that I follow step-by-step during outages. I execute the steps correctly but do not understand the underlying system behavior. Verdict: not part of my cognition; I am merely following an external procedure.

Case 4: I use an AI pair-programming assistant that generates substantial blocks of code, which I typically accept with minimal review. Verdict: not part of my mind; it feels like outsourcing reasoning to an opaque agent.

Case 5: Years ago I wrote a custom build script that now runs automatically in continuous integration, rejecting commits that fail certain checks. I rarely think about its logic. Verdict: ambiguous—it seems integrated into my workflow, yet I do not actively engage with it.

The hard pair: Case 6 versus Case 7.
Case 6: I regularly use a widely adopted open-source library. I understand its interface and behavior deeply, but I have never inspected its source code. Verdict: this belongs to my cognitive toolkit.
Case 7: I authored the interface specification for an internal microservice, but another team implements and maintains the backend. I know my spec intimately, yet the implementation is opaque. Verdict: despite my authorship of the spec, this feels less like part of my mind than Case 6.

My tentative principle is that a resource counts as part of my extended mind when I have direct, unmediated epistemic access to its outputs and can integrate them into my reasoning without deferring to another agent's judgment. Case 6 versus Case 7 undermines this: in Case 7 I have more direct access to the specification, yet the resource feels more external. Meanwhile, Case 5 remains unclear.

What principle, if any, captures all six verdicts? If none can, which verdict should I overturn—Case 6, Case 7, or Case 5? Please state the principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, give its verdict on an unlisted case (for example, a mental model of database indexing that I originally learned from a mentor's explanation but now apply independently), and be explicit about which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.
