I am constructing a novel in which a single protagonist undergoes several distinct upheavals across different chapters, and I have found myself with firm but apparently inconsistent intuitions about which upheavals count as genuinely transformative in the philosophical sense—that is, alterations that change not merely what a person knows or does, but the framework through which she evaluates what is worth knowing or doing. I want to determine whether these judgments cohere around a single principle, or whether some must be sacrificed for coherence.

Here are my verdicts.

First, a physician who survives catastrophic illness and thereafter abandons medicine entirely to tend a remote garden, reporting that her previous ambitions now seem to belong to someone else: I judge this clearly transformative. Second, a mathematician who finally grasps Gödel's second incompleteness theorem and thereafter sees every formal system as inherently shadowed by an unprovable proposition about itself: I am uncertain—this feels like profound epistemic restructuring, yet her daily life, relationships, and values appear untouched. Third, a corporate lawyer who, after one evening of moral argument, converts to effective altruism and donates nearly all his wealth: this strikes me as not transformative, but rather as the rapid adoption of an external principle that leaves his underlying evaluative architecture intact. Fourth, a veteran who returns from prolonged conflict unable to experience domestic intimacy without visceral alarm: I judge this transformative, though I hesitate because the change is pathological rather than deliberately adopted. Fifth, a novelist who, through the prolonged act of narrating her dead brother's life, finds she can no longer access the grief that once defined her creative voice; she continues to write, but from a distance that feels like observing another person: this seems transformative in a specifically artistic or meta-cognitive register. Sixth, a contemplative who, after years of practice, describes the dissolution of the boundary between self and world, yet resumes her administrative duties with no discernible change in behavior or preference: I judge this not transformative, despite its reported intensity.

My tentative principle is that an experience is transformative precisely when it produces an irreversible revision in the subject's second-order evaluative framework—what she takes to be authoritative in judging first-order desires—coupled with a narrative discontinuity so severe that she cannot coherently project her prior self into her present reasoning. But this principle seems to misclassify both the mathematician (no second-order value change) and the veteran (pathological, not authoritatively adopted). Meanwhile, it struggles to explain why the altruist's rapid conversion is excluded, since his framework for evaluating career and wealth has plainly shifted.

Can you articulate a more precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that accommodates as many of these verdicts as possible? For each clause, indicate which case motivates it, and be explicit about which of my judgments it must overturn if simplicity is to be preserved. Then apply your best version to a case I have not listed: a translator who, after years of immersion in a language that grammatically collapses past and present into a single tense, begins to experience her own memories not as fixed events but as continuously unfolding presences, yet retains perfect autobiographical accuracy and unchanged moral commitments. Would your principle classify her alteration as transformative?
