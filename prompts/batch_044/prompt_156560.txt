As a doctoral researcher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and clinical neuropsychology, I am trying to establish the criteria of adequacy for theories of phenomenal unity in split-brain patients before adjudicating among existing accounts. Rather than asking which view is correct, I want the specification of what any philosophically adequate theory must deliver. Write the desiderata: what a theory of interhemispheric phenomenal unity—specifically one that addresses the under-discussed configuration in which the anterior commissure remains intact, permitting affective but not propositional cross-hemispheric transmission, while subcortical arbitration mechanisms may still coordinate response—must be able to explain, distinguish, and accommodate.

For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it: which experimental phenomena it must cover (e.g., lateralized presentation tasks, rivalry suppression, and cross-hemispheric priming without reportable access); which conceptual distinctions it must preserve (for instance, between access unity, phenomenal unity, and narrative unity, but also the distinction I am emphasizing between affective integration and cognitive integration across hemispheres); which pathological variations it must handle (complete callosotomy, partial section with and without anterior commissure sparing, and cases of gradual rather than acute disconnection); and which philosophical commitments it must either entail or remain neutral on (whether unity is a primitive relation, a constructed achievement, or an emergent property of subcortical dynamics).

Flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether accommodating graded subcortical arbitration is compatible with maintaining a strict binary between one subject and two— and indicate which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate account versus which are camp-specific preferences (e.g., demands for first-person reportability, commitments to mereological essentialism about persons, or insistence on empirical equivalence with standard neuropsychological descriptions). Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing views, though you may briefly indicate how standard positions—the unified-field view, the dual-stream view, and graded-integration approaches—fare against the list. My purpose is to use the resulting specification to structure my own evaluation of whether phenomenal unity can survive partial disconnection when affective channels remain open but cognitive ones do not.
