Please argue that the classic heterogeneity problem for hedonism—how pleasures as diverse as tasting chocolate, solving an equation, and feeling relief from pain can all belong to one category—rests on an unnecessary assumption that pleasures must share either a uniform phenomenal quality or a mere family resemblance. Instead, defend the position that pleasures are unified by what we might call axiological anchoring: the structural feature of being states that an agent's practical-evaluative framework treats as carrying non-derivative, self-reinforcing positive weight, regardless of whether that weight is mediated by explicit judgment, bodily feedback signals, or sub-personal reward mechanisms. Show how this avoids both the reduction of pleasure to a single sensory tone and the dissolution of pleasure into an arbitrary disjunction, and address the specific challenge that purely bodily pleasures—such as the diffuse warmth of sunlight on skin—lack the cognitive or intentional structure that anchoring seems to require. In doing so, treat phenomenal heterogeneity as genuine rather than illusory, and explain why axiological anchoring is not simply a disguised form of attitudinal hedonism.
