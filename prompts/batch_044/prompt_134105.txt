In all three countries the monarch is a constitutional figure with limited real power. In the UK, the Crown retains reserve powers like dissolving Parliament, giving royal assent, and appointing a prime minister during a hung parliament. By convention these aren't exercised independently, but formally they exist, so Britain has a reserve constitutional circuit in which the Crown is a dormant sovereign. Norway represents the Scandinavian rejection of that model. The King formally appoints the government and sanctions legislation, but these are automatic on parliamentary advice, and there's no constitutional mechanism for royal intervention in deadlock; the monarchy is purely symbolic legitimation with no latency. Japan is structurally closer to Norway than to Britain, but with an important twist. Under Article 1 the Emperor is the symbol of state unity, and under Article 9 he renounces all state acts, including war-related ones. Because sovereignty resides with the people rather than any institution, Japan has no dormant sovereign at all — the Diet and Cabinet hold all procedural authority. One could even say that British parliamentary sovereignty and Japanese popular sovereignty are functionally identical, since both ultimately derive authority from democratic election; the only real difference is whether that authority is vested in Parliament or in the people directly. So the UK retains a reserve circuit, while Norway and Japan have eliminated it through explicit constitutional design.
