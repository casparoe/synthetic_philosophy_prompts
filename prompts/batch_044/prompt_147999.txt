I'm delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Moral Weight Without Thresholds: Insects, Algorithms, and Thermostats." The audience is split: on one side are philosophers of mind and animal-ethics researchers; on the other are robotics engineers, climate-systems designers, and family members of the organizers. The guiding premise — that sentience and moral status come in continuous gradations rather than as an on/off property — has to do real structural work in what I say, not just serve as a decorative theme. Specifically, I want to navigate between two errors that both sides are prone to: treating the absence of a sharp boundary as a reason to abandon protective norms altogether, and treating every gradation as requiring an impossible calculus of precise moral weights for each entity encountered. I want to acknowledge, without condescension, that for the engineers in the room a thermostat's feedback loops are genuinely different from an insect's nociceptive integration, but that neither comparison resolves where obligation begins. The remarks should run about twelve minutes, welcome people who have never encountered this literature, and avoid giving the impression that science has settled whether insects suffer or whether climate-control systems have any moral standing. In particular, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view — such as the claim that gradated status necessarily undermines human rights, or that it automatically extends rights to artificial systems — as if it were the consensus view.
