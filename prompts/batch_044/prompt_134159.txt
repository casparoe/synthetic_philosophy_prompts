I am a retired physicist who has, since leaving active research, taken up philosophical ethics with a particular interest in applied questions arising from digital environments. I am serving as an external grader for an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral status in virtual worlds, and I wish to verify my own assessment of one student submission against an independent evaluation.

The examination question was as follows: Consider the moral status of a procedurally generated non-player character in an open-world sandbox simulation—specifically, a villager whose reactive behaviors (seeking shelter during storms, fleeing aggression, maintaining an inventory) emerge from algorithmic rules rather than from a predetermined narrative script. The entity possesses no phenomenal consciousness, no autobiographical memory, and no scripted narrative arc; it is phenomenally empty yet causally responsive. Analyze whether killing this NPC constitutes a direct moral wrong, an indirect wrong through character formation or representational harm, or no wrong whatsoever. In your answer, distinguish carefully between scripted and emergent behavior, address whether Peter Singer’s capacity-for-suffering criterion applies differently to entities that are responsive but sentience-empty, and discuss whether representational harm can occur even when the player knows the entity is not real.

One student submitted the answer printed below. Please grade it with precision: assign a specific letter grade or numerical score, and provide point-by-point comments that justify that grade. Identify where the reasoning is sound, where distinctions are conflated, where a view is misattributed to its originator, and where an inferential step does not follow. Integrate your overall judgment into the commentary rather than offering it separately; do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with the student’s conclusion except insofar as it bears on the accuracy of the reasoning.

The student’s answer:

Because the villager lacks phenomenal consciousness, it is not a moral patient in Singer’s sense, since Singer requires the capacity to suffer as a necessary and sufficient condition for direct moral consideration. Therefore, there is no direct wrong in killing it. However, because the villager’s behavior is emergent rather than scripted, it exhibits a form of functional autonomy that scripted characters lack; this autonomy makes it more analogous to a non-sentient animal, and thus we might extend indirect obligations to it, not because cruelty to it harms anyone, but because cultivating cruelty is vicious regardless of the victim’s consciousness. But since the player knows the entity is not real, no vice is actually cultivated, so there is not even an indirect wrong. Furthermore, if we applied Singer’s principle consistently, we would have to grant moral status to any sufficiently complex AI—such as a large language model—that displays responsive behavior, which is absurd. Therefore, the correct view is that only scripted characters with explicit narrative significance can be wronged representationally, while emergent NPCs are morally neutral, and the action of killing them is permissible without moral cost.
