After arguing with a friend for nearly two hours about whether the extinction of humanity would be an actual catastrophe or merely a large-scale tragedy, I have tried to formalize where I stand and want a consistency audit before presenting it in a seminar. My commitments are these: (1) Human extinction is profoundly harmful even if it is entirely painless and involves no suffering; the harm is not reducible to the aggregation of individual deaths, since there is no survivor left to experience the loss. (2) The harm is partly constituted by the frustration of what Samuel Scheffler calls the "afterlife"—the continuation of collective projects, cultural transmission, and intergenerational relationships—though I think this idea needs supplementation rather than direct invocation. (3) I reject strong actualism about future value: I do not believe future people must actually exist for there to be present reasons to prevent extinction, but I also deny that merely possible people have moral standing. (4) I maintain that if humanity were replaced by beings with far richer inner lives—say, post-biological minds with substantially greater capacities for well-being—this would not fully compensate for our loss, because part of the value lies in the specific historical trajectory and the particular forms of meaning we have developed. (5) At the same time, I hold that if humanity had never existed at all, there would be no harm; the badness of extinction is conditional on our prior existence and the projects we have actually begun. (6) I want to defend all of this without appealing to a mystical "human dignity" or species essentialism, and I think evolutionary continuity with other life-forms makes any hard normative boundary between us and other sentient beings arbitrary.

Do these hang together? Specifically, does (3) undermine (2) by denying that the absence of actual future people can constitute a present harm? Is (4) merely disguised speciesism in light of (6), and if not, what distinguishes legitimate attachment to our particular trajectory from illegitimate essentialism? Does (5) weaken (1) by making the harm conditional rather than unconditional, and is there an apparent tension between (2) and (4) that dissolves once we distinguish collective-project value from replaceability? Please audit these commitments in order of severity, identify which pairs are genuinely incompatible versus only apparently so, specify the least costly revision for each real tension, and tell me which single change would resolve the greatest number of conflicts. Answer in American English, and be precise about what each revision costs.
