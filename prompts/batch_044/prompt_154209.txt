I am preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on how historians account for mass atrocity, and after a long conversation with a historian colleague about explanatory pluralism I realized that the standard summary—Browning’s organizational account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 versus Goldhagen’s culture-specific thesis of eliminationist antisemitism—collapses a much richer logical space into a false binary. For the interactive portion of the piece I need a sorting taxonomy, closer to a decision tree than to a quiz that asks people which label they prefer. The reader should answer concrete counterfactual and diagnostic questions about the Battalion 101 record—whether the same policemen would have participated at comparable rates if placed in identical organizational conditions but raised in a society with no particular history of anti-Jewish prejudice, whether organizational dynamics can explain both reluctant compliance and enthusiastic initiative, whether resistance is best read as moral exception or statistical noise, and whether killing at a distance through bureaucratic division of labor operates as an independent mechanism or merely as a situational intensifier. Each branch must split the space by a claim answerable without prior knowledge of the position names, and the path should eventually land on a named or unnamed leaf.

Please build the tree with six internal nodes and seven to eight leaves. The required leaves are: a pure organizational or situationist position in the Browning vein; Goldhagen’s culture-specific “willing executioners” view; an interactionist hybrid in which organizational mechanisms require only a minimal, generic cultural permission rather than deep ideological hatred; a structural or bureaucratic explanation that emphasizes division of labor and role-distance; a multi-causal overdetermination position holding that culture, situation, and structure are each independently sufficient; and a character-modulated view that uses individual psychological variance—empathy differences, authoritarian disposition, or moral disengagement capacity—to explain the spread between eager perpetrators and the small minority who resisted. In addition, inject at least one less-discussed distinction—perhaps incremental moral disengagement as a cognitive mechanism distinct from generic peer pressure, or technological mediation of killing distance, or an evolutionary in-group framing—as either a separate branch or an unnamed leaf. For every combination of answers that the tree allows but that no one defends, note briefly whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; specifically flag combinations such as pure character explanation with no organizational or cultural contribution, or pure culture with no organizational mechanism. You should also flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate—most likely pure organizational situationism and a purely bureaucratic role-distance view if both deny culture and character—rather than papering over the overlap. The audience is an intellectually curious general readership, so do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, and do not lean on precise publication years or page numbers; state positions by their substantive commitments. Deliver the tree with labeled branch questions, leaf descriptions including each view’s most awkward commitment, a clear scoring key mapping answer patterns to positions, and the required annotations on separation failures and empty cells.
