I am a sixth-form student invited to deliver the opening address at my school's annual History and Philosophy Symposium, which this year examines interpretations of the French Revolution. The audience is mixed: history students and teachers familiar with class-struggle, ideological, and contingency-based accounts; philosophy staff comfortable with conceptual analysis; and parents and guests who know only the broad outline. I want the speech to centre on whether revolutions devour their own children, treating this not as rhetorical decoration but as a live constraint on what can be said about revolutionary change. Specifically, I want to argue that the Revolution's turn against its founders is neither structurally inevitable nor purely accidental, but arises from a tension internal to any system that institutionalises perpetual self-renewal — a framing that should shape both the content and the limits of my remarks, preventing me from presenting the Terror as either predetermined or arbitrary. To avoid a familiar rehearsal of well-known interpretations, I want to inject a somewhat niche comparison: treating the Revolution's self-cannibalising phase not through standard historiographical categories but through the logic of rule-systems that contain the conditions for their own transformation. The address should be about five minutes long, should be composed in British English, should perform genuine philosophical work for the specialists without losing the non-specialists, and should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
