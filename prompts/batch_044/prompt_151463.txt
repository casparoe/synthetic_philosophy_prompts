Design a classroom activity for a postgraduate philosophy seminar of about 14 students, lasting 100 minutes, focused on Spinoza's theory of democracy as presented in the Tractatus Politicus. The activity should make participants feel the structural tension between Spinoza's claim that democracy is the most natural form of government — because it maximises the potentia multitudinis, the power of the multitude — and the highly constrained, exclusionary institutions he actually describes: property qualifications for citizenship, the council of syndics that checks the popular assembly, and the abrupt, unfinished treatment of women and servants in the democracy chapter. To avoid a generic lecture on Spinoza's politics, inject a niche conceptual mechanism: use Spinoza's doctrine of common notions (notiones communes) from the Ethics as the formal rule governing democratic deliberation in the simulation. Arrange the exercise in three rounds with assigned roles, hidden incentives, and physical materials (role cards, proposal slips, and a hidden deck of passion cards). In Round 1, students vote by pure majority on a distributive law, each acting from private-interest cards they must not reveal; the result should feel unstable or arbitrary. In Round 2, they must form pairs that can articulate a shared common notion before submitting a joint proposal; only paired proposals reach the floor, and unpaired citizens are sidelined. In Round 3, a council of syndics — three students selected not by vote but by demonstrated success in forming common notions in Round 2 — receives veto power over the assembly's decision. The group then faces a forced choice: either accept the syndics' veto and preserve the commonwealth's rational order, or override it and trigger a faction collapse that ends the simulation. Provide a precise timeline, a non-monetary payoff structure tied to the survival of the commonwealth, rules for the facilitator, and a debrief that maps each round onto specific Spinozist distinctions — potentia versus potestas, reason versus passion, and the claim that in democracy right and power coincide. Be explicit about which feature of Spinoza's metaphysics the activity cannot reproduce — namely, that rational agreement is not merely procedural but expresses the same idea in all minds under the attribute of thought — and indicate how the facilitator must supply this. Include an honest forecast of what will predictably go wrong the first time: students are likely to interpret Spinoza's exclusions of women, servants, and foreigners as contingent prejudice rather than as structurally necessary conditions for rational citizenship in his system, and advise how to redirect this misconception without delivering a lecture. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; assume the facilitator knows the text.
