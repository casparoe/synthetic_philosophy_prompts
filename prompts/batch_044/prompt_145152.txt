For a metaethics seminar I am writing up my views on moral demandingness and want a consistency check before presenting. Here is my package: (1) I accept the demandingness objection to act-utilitarianism—morality cannot require the perpetual subordination of an agent’s personal projects to aggregate welfare. (2) I defend what I call a “moral budget” model: each agent bears a fixed, non-renewable quota of required self-sacrifice distributed across her obligations; demands within the quota are binding, but further consequentialist demands become supererogatory once the quota is met. (3) I hold that direct rescue obligations—saving a nearby drowning stranger at moderate personal risk—are mandatory and do not draw on the budget at all, because they involve immediate causal responsibility rather than general beneficence. (4) I reject global calibration of the budget (counting all suffering one could prevent anywhere) and insist on local calibration restricted to one’s actual causal network. (5) I deny that evolutionary debunking arguments undermine our intuitions about demandingness, yet I also maintain that those same intuitions are unreliable guides to the precise magnitude of the budget. (6) Finally, I claim there is no intrinsic moral difference between failing to rescue through direct omission and failing to prevent harm through distant non-intervention; the only relevant distinction is that direct rescue falls outside the budget while distant aid falls within it.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision in each case? In particular, check whether (3) and (6) can stand together given that both reject standard proximity- or omission-based distinctions, and whether (4) undermines the motivation for (2). Rank the tensions by severity, specify exactly what must give in each case, and identify any hidden commitment I am implicitly signing up for that I have not listed.
