A recent essay on consciousness and complexity summarizes Douglas Hofstadter's framework of tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level in the following way: every complex system—whether a brain, an ant colony, or a computer—contains a base level whose elements are strictly inviolable. Neural firing patterns, individual ants, and logic gates never acquire properties belonging to higher levels, regardless of how elaborate the system becomes. Escher's Drawing Hands captures this precisely: each hand draws the other, yet both remain hands; neither transforms into a meta-hand or transcends its own level. From this, Hofstadter derives that strange loops generate genuinely novel causal powers at the top that are sealed off from the bottom—meaning consciousness in a brain cannot be explained by neuroscience at the neuronal level, and an ant colony's collective behavior operates in a domain entirely separate from the ants' individual actions. The essay extends this to artificial neural networks, arguing that should they ever become conscious, that consciousness would constitute an inviolable-level phenomenon, causally independent of the underlying weights and activations, and therefore inaccessible to interpretability methods that probe those lower-level parameters. How fair is this summary? Please separate the parts that accurately reflect Hofstadter's distinction between hierarchical levels and his use of strange loops from the parts that misrepresent the relationship between emergence and reduction—particularly whether higher-level phenomena are causally sealed off rather than merely described at a different scale. Also evaluate whether the extension to machine-learning interpretability imports an assumption about causal closure that Hofstadter's original framework does not contain, or whether it follows naturally from the concept of an inviolate level.
