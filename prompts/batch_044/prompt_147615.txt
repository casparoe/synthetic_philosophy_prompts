Compare the relational account of practical reasons—according to which a normative reason is necessarily a consideration that counts in favor of some particular agent performing some particular action under particular circumstances—with the thesis that there can be free-floating rationales: normative structures that obtain even in worlds that contain no agents capable of being guided by them. To make the comparison concrete, consider the idea of normative residue: the claim that if a state of affairs is genuinely good, then there persists a counterfactual directive structure specifying how an agent ought to bring it about should one exist, and that this structure is not reducible to present-tense relational facts about actual agents. Can the relationalist accommodate residue by treating it as a modal projection of actual agent-involving relations, or must the relationalist deny that there is any genuinely normative content in agentless worlds? On the other side, if free-floating rationales are admitted, can they maintain genuine prescriptive force, or do they collapse into bare evaluative descriptions lacking practical authority? Furthermore, what distinguishes a rational structure without a reasoner from a mere pattern of value? Identify the strongest argument on each side, indicate which commitment in the relationalist's rejection of residue is least costly to abandon, and say precisely what consideration—whether a complete translation of residue-talk into relational counterfactuals or a compelling case of normative failure in the absence of any possible agent—would change your assessment, as well as where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
