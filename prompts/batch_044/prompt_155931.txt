For an upper-level normative ethics seminar (advanced undergraduates or first-year graduate students), draft a single 90-minute essay exam on moral uncertainty that cannot be answered by reciting the standard maximization-of-expected-choice-worthiness framework. Introduce a specific, invented resolution mechanism—call it the Regret-Translation Protocol (RTP)—which attempts to bridge competing moral theories by translating a deontological prohibition into a consequentialist cost: the “moral regret weight” equals the first-person disutility the committed deontologist herself reports at the prospect of violating the constraint, and outcomes are then aggregated using ordinary expected-utility reasoning.

The essay question should require students to do four things: (i) explain precisely why standard expected-choice-worthiness approaches face an intertheoretic comparison problem (not merely that theories disagree, but that their value structures, ordering relations, and outcome descriptions may lack a common metric); (ii) identify the specific normative assumption RTP makes about the epistemic or practical authority of first-person moral regret, and how that assumption is supposed to supply the missing metric; (iii) argue whether RTP genuinely resolves moral uncertainty in a theory-neutral way, or whether it instead collapses the deontological theory into a weighted consequentialist framework and thereby begs the question; and (iv) assess whether, if RTP succeeds, moral uncertainty has been reduced to empirical uncertainty about our own reactive attitudes rather than normative uncertainty about which theory is correct, and what this implies for the coherence of the original problem.

Provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit analytical moves (4 marks each). For each criterion, state the specific conceptual achievement required for full marks and, in one line, the typical failure mode that should cost marks (for example, conflating intertheoretic comparison with simple disagreement over probabilities, or treating RTP as unbiased without addressing the framework-imposition objection). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should stand on conceptual analysis alone. Ensure the difficulty comes from forcing students to diagnose a structural feature of the proposed protocol rather than to summarize familiar positions.
