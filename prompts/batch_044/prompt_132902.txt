I'm currently taking a course in distributive justice, and I've become puzzled by an apparent inconsistency in our university's financial-aid policies. The office applies a sharp income threshold: students just below the cutoff receive substantial need-based grants, while those just above receive nothing, with no sliding scale. They defend this by saying priority must go to the worst off. Yet when a proposal was made to fund a sliding scale by slightly reducing large merit scholarships for high-achieving students—who would remain well above the threshold—the office rejected it as impermissible leveling down, arguing that reducing anyone's absolute position is never justified, even to benefit the near-poor. At the same time, they approve reducing those identical merit awards to fund larger grants for the very poorest students, calling that required by priority to the worst off. What theory of value does this combination of practices presuppose? Does it require a version of prioritarianism that accepts the leveling-down objection, or can it be reconstructed without that commitment? Does the practice implicitly depend on a distinction between Pareto-compatible and non-Pareto-compatible redistribution, or does it smuggle in an egalitarian concern about relative standing that pure prioritarianism was meant to avoid? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical view, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, say whether it is philosophically underdetermined or compatible with rival theories, and explain what the aid policy would have to look like if we replaced its implicit framework with a straightforward prioritarian calculus that weights only absolute welfare levels and ignores relative losses entirely.
