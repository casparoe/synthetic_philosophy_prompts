I've come to think that Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology, read strictly, commits us to a discontinuous model of rational revision. Hinge propositions—say, that the earth existed long before my birth, or that I am not systematically deceived about my own hands—aren't just very high-confidence beliefs. They're structurally immune to local counterevidence: they can only be displaced by a global framework shift, never incrementally updated. So belief revision is bimodal: either you're tweaking beliefs inside the scaffolding, or you're tearing down and replacing the scaffolding entirely, with nothing in between. I think this captures what's actually going on in scientific revolutions and in personal epistemic crisis, and it distinguishes hinges from mere priors with extreme values.

What would genuinely count against this? Not just the familiar objection that hinges can slowly erode. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which patterns in actual belief change, which formal results about belief revision, and which intuitions about gradual skepticism show there's a middle ground—hinges being adjusted piecemeal without total collapse? Which of these would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed as misdescribing the hinge? Also, does this view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent gradual hinge-change gets redescribed as either local or catastrophic? Is that insulation a feature or a bug?

Lead with the bottom line: is this discontinuous-reading well-insulated, and is that insulation itself a problem? Then map the exposure—fatal, awkward, and dismissible defeaters—within about 450 words.
