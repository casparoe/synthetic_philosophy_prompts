Imagine you are advising a three-year research program in the philosophy of physics that can fund four philosophers, with no empirical laboratory component. The program is specifically devoted to Gödel's rotating-universe cosmology and its bearing on the metaphysics of time, but it wants to avoid two well-worn ruts: repeating standard expositions of closed timelike curves in the Gödel metric, and rehearsing generic A-theory versus B-theory debates that never engage cosmological structure. The directors want an agenda of genuinely open conceptual questions—questions a philosopher without graduate physics training could make progress on, but that are not answerable from existing textbooks. Propose the three or four lines of inquiry that deserve the bulk of the resources, rank them in order of priority, and defend the ordering. For each line, explain: (a) why it is upstream of the others—specifically, which other questions depend on it being settled first; (b) what philosophical (not mathematical or observational) progress would look like, and what kind of result would cause you to revise the ranking; (c) what you are deliberately excluding—particularly any problem that sounds relevant but actually requires resolving unsettled physics that philosophers are not positioned to adjudicate. In addition, inject one niche conceptual framing of your own—perhaps connecting Gödel's Platonist skepticism about temporal becoming to the indexicality of observers in rotating frames, or linking the possibility of causal loops to a specific account of counterfactual dependence that has been underexplored in the literature—and show how that framing reshuffles the priorities. Your answer should be a defended agenda, not a survey and not a bibliography.
