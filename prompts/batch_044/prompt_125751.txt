Write a proposal for a three-month philosophy-of-decision-theory workshop project on risk-weighted expected utility. The precise research question is this: when an agent applies risk weights to avoid catastrophic outcomes, but the boundary of the catastrophic category is structurally ambiguous—the agent knows there exists a cluster of severely bad outcomes without being able to draw a sharp demarcation—which component should bear the risk weight: the probabilities of falling within the fuzzy region, the utility values assigned inside it, or the decision procedure's overall variance? Explain why existing treatments leave the question open: they generally presuppose a well-defined outcome space with a clear catastrophic threshold, and they treat risk-weighting as either a probability transformation or a utility transformation, but not both, and they do not address cases in which the categorization of outcomes is itself part of the uncertainty. Outline an approach that compares three responses—first, sharpening the boundary by stipulation and applying conventional risk-weighted utility; second, extending the framework to outcomes with graded membership in the catastrophic region; third, adopting a process-based risk penalty that operates on the decision rule rather than on outcomes. Defend the choice of this comparative method over the more obvious strategy of simply lowering risk tolerance within standard expected utility. Specify what the deliverable would be, such as a formal framework paired with a concrete case study in autonomous-system deployment; state exactly what result would count as failure, for instance demonstrating that all three responses collapse into standard expected utility under plausible rationality constraints; and assess whether the scope is realistic for three months, identifying which comparison is tractable and which step risks expanding the project beyond the available time.
