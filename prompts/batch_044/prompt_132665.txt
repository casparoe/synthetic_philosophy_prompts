I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, and I find myself caught in a practical decision that seems to sit between empirical reasoning and conceptual analysis. Last month, while helping my granddaughter design a school science-fair demonstration about heat exchange between bodies at different temperatures, I became troubled by the status of irreversible change in physical artifacts, and I wonder whether you can help me see the philosophical structure of my situation more clearly.

The concrete case is this. In my basement I keep an old thermal-demonstration apparatus I built in the 1970s: two large copper blocks separated by an insulating barrier, with thermocouples that recorded temperature gradients continuously over months. Several years ago a plumbing failure flooded the room. The apparatus was submerged, the insulation degraded irreparably, and the paper records—ink traces of years of measurements—were smudged into illegibility. A restoration firm has offered to rebuild the setup precisely: new copper, new insulation, fresh recording rolls, calibrated to reproduce the original temperature gradients. They argue that since the fundamental laws governing molecular motion are symmetric with respect to temporal reversal, a reconstructed apparatus running forward is physically indistinguishable in principle from the original running in reverse, and therefore there is no scientific reason to preserve the corroded originals.

My family agrees, adding that if I authorize the reconstruction, I will have recovered everything that mattered functionally. But I feel a strong resistance. The original apparatus embodied a particular low-entropy state that had evolved over decades into a specific macroscopic configuration; the flood destroyed that evolutionary path in a way that cannot be undone. A perfect replica might reproduce all future measurements, yet it seems to me that it lacks the historical trajectory—the actual path through state space—that made the original meaningful. It is not merely sentimental attachment; I feel that there is a difference between a system that has genuinely undergone an irreversible history and one that has been assembled to mimic the endpoint of such a history.

I must decide whether to authorize the reconstruction and discard the damaged pieces, or to preserve the useless, corroded originals despite their functional worthlessness. The practical stakes are modest, but I sense that my reluctance touches on something deeper: whether the asymmetry we observe in processes like heat flow, degradation, and record-formation is merely an artifact of our macroscopic descriptions, or whether there is an ontologically genuine distinction between a system that has evolved through a particular irreversible past and one constructed to imitate its present state.

Could you advise me on how to think through this decision? I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of readings, or citations—I want to understand whether there is a philosophically coherent position that respects my reluctance without reducing it to nostalgia, and if so, what that position would imply about how we ought to treat physically irreversible artifacts in general.
