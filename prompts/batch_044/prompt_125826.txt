I am drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on rational choice and philosophical paradox, and I need one essay prompt designed for a 90-minute sitting with an expected response of roughly 800–1000 words. The topic must be Buridan’s ass and choice without preference, but I explicitly do not want a question answerable by recounting the haystack parable or by listing the standard resolutions—randomization, hierarchical ordering, or voluntarist breaks. Instead, I want the exam to present students with a constructed decision architecture: an artificial agent must select between two policies, Alpha and Beta, that are strictly identical in expected utility, deontic status, and every first-order evaluative property, yet they differ in the meta-structural form of their justification chains. Alpha’s reasoning contains a symmetric, reflexive self-reference loop that closes back on itself without external termination, whereas Beta’s reasoning follows an asymmetric dependency chain that terminates in an externally anchored premise. Crucially, the agent’s preference function is defined only over outcomes and first-order properties, not over the symmetry or structural form of its own reasoning processes. The exam question should ask students to determine whether Buridan’s paradox genuinely applies in this modified case; whether introducing a second-order preference over reasoning symmetry is a legitimate resolution or merely an ad hoc relocation of the problem; and whether this scenario ultimately supports or undermines the completeness axiom for rational preference orderings. Please provide three things: first, the exam question exactly as it should appear to students, with clear sub-parts; second, a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite position, distinguishes outcome-level incommensurability from structure-level incommensurability, evaluates the legitimacy of second-order preferences, and connects the analysis to broader debates about rational choice; and third, a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific conceptual moves that must be present—such as recognizing that the paradox is relocated rather than dissolved by meta-preferences, and assessing whether completeness can be restricted without collapsing into arbitrariness—together with the common errors that should cost marks, for instance treating symmetry as an outcome-level property or conflating the inability to choose with the absence of a reason. The difficulty should come from forcing students to navigate the boundary between preference and rational architecture, not from recalling historical labels.
