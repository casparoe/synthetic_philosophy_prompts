The exam prompt given to the students was: "Does Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge satisfy the principle of epistemic closure? Specifically, if S knows that P, and S competently deduces that Q is entailed by P, does S thereby come to know Q? "Nozick's tracking theory holds that S knows P iff (1) P is true, (2) S believes P, (3) if P were false S would not believe P (sensitivity), and (4) if P were true in nearby worlds S would still believe P (adherence). Epistemic closure states that knowledge is preserved across known entailment: if S knows P and knows P→Q, then S knows Q. Nozick denies closure. For instance, I know I have hands, and I know that having hands entails I am not a handless brain in a vat. Yet I do not know I am not a BIV, because if I were a BIV I would still believe I had hands and therefore still believe I was not a BIV, violating sensitivity for the BIV proposition. Hence tracking conditions are not transmitted through entailment.

That said, the rejection is not absolute. When both P and P→Q are individually tracked, belief in Q can inherit modal stability through transitive modal adherence, provided the worlds in which Q fails lie no closer than those in which P fails. In ordinary deductive contexts—deriving that a shape has four sides from knowing it is a square—the sensitivity conditions share the same modal radius, so closure is preserved. Only in skeptical scenarios, where the entailed claim involves distant possibilities outside P's stabilizing neighborhood, is closure blocked. Thus tracking theory offers a nuanced, domain-sensitive account rather than a blanket denial."
