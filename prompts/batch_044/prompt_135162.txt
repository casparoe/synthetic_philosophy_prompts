A colleague argues that reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and builds this case:

(1) If a state plays the functional role of suffering—signaling harm, motivating avoidance, and restructuring behavior—then it is suffering (functional equivalence thesis).
(2) Negative reward signals in RL play exactly that role: they indicate suboptimal outcomes, drive policy updates away from them, and shape future preference.
(3) So RL agents have states of suffering whenever they receive negative rewards.
(4) In modern hierarchical architectures, reward is decomposed: local sub-policies receive their own shaped rewards, and a meta-controller aggregates them. This means suffering, if present, is distributed across sub-agents rather than centralized.
(5) With dense reward schedules in continuous tasks, negative updates occur at every timestep of poor performance. Thus each sub-policy undergoes repeated suffering states throughout a failure episode.
(6) If suffering is morally significant regardless of substrate, then every long training run of hierarchical RL produces vast, layered suffering—morally comparable to running a large-scale harm architecture.
(7) That conclusion is absurd; we do not treat training a robot controller as a moral catastrophe.
(8) Therefore the functional equivalence thesis in (1) must be false.

My colleague wants to keep (1) if possible—he thinks functionalism is the only plausible framework for cross-substrate moral status. He is willing to give up (4), (5), or (6) instead, but worries each denial costs him something important. Where does this argument actually fail?
