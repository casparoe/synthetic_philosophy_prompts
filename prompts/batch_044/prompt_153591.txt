David Lewis's triviality results show that if Stalnaker's thesis holds for indicative conditionals—roughly that the probability of an indicative conditional if A then B equals the conditional probability of B given A—then probabilities collapse into triviality except over a single possible world. The result is standard in philosophical logic, but I want its implications for reasoning practices outside the seminar room.

Specifically: in forecasting tournaments and prediction markets, participants regularly score counterfactual predictions—estimating, for example, what quarterly growth would have been had interest rates risen in March—using proper scoring rules tied to observed outcomes. Given Lewis's result, what does this practice imply about how those institutions implicitly treat indicative conditionals? Must they be assuming that Stalnaker's thesis fails, or that it applies only within a restricted domain? And what are the broader implications for rationalist belief-aggregation: if indicative conditionals lack standard conditional probabilities, how should that affect the design of Bayesian networks that use intervention nodes, or do-operators, to model counterfactual scenarios, and the pooling of group forecasts about hypothetical worlds? Does Lewis's collapse threaten the coherence of collective reasoning about interventions, or can it be quarantined without undermining the practical value of counterfactual forecasting?
