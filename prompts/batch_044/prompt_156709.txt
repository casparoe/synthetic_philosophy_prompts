I'm running a 90-minute seminar on democratic legitimacy and rational choice for about eighteen graduate students in political philosophy. We've covered the standard Downsian paradox and rational ignorance, but I want the group to feel why the problem doesn't dissolve when you move from mass electorates to nested representative structures—specifically, how delegation itself can become a rational cascade. At each tier, passing the decision upward seems individually sensible, yet the cascade produces a legitimacy deficit that the simple "probability of being pivotal" calculation doesn't capture.

Design an in-class game—call it the Delegation Cascade—for these eighteen students, arranged in three nested tiers: six local cells of three, two regional councils drawn from delegates, and one national assembly of two. Use only paper ballots and a token economy (no real money): each student begins with ten tokens, direct voting costs one token, and there is a collective payoff of eight tokens per person if the final decision reaches the national tier and is "approved" (a predetermined beneficial outcome), but if the cascade breaks—too many abstain or delegate at a tier—the decision defaults to "rejected" (zero collective payoff) and everyone keeps unspent tokens. Crucially, at each tier, students who choose "delegate" avoid the cost but enter a lottery: with probability 1/n (where n is the number of delegators at that tier), they are forced to represent and pay the token anyway, while the rest free-ride. This makes delegation risky but usually cheap. The game should run for about fifty minutes, with students playing through all three tiers twice so they learn the mechanics; in the second round they should discover—without being told—that delegation spiked at the local tier, broke the cascade in one region, and left the national assembly with only two representatives who felt burdened and questioned whether the outcome was legitimate.

In the debrief, map each tier's behavior onto the rational-choice logic (cost, expected pivotality, free-riding) and onto the legitimacy problem (representation without authorization). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely social-pressure coordination to vote in round one that suppresses the cascade—and specify the anonymity mechanism (sealed envelopes, simultaneous choice) needed to prevent it. Be frank about what feature of the idea this activity cannot reproduce: it captures the cascade of delegation and the legitimacy deficit, but because the "issue" is only the token payoff, it cannot reproduce the epistemic dimension of rational ignorance (voters lacking information about complex policies); note that I will have to supply that separately in discussion. Also include a timeline for the fifty-minute run, the materials list, and an honest note on which student roles (the forced representatives) are most likely to generate the emotional reaction that makes the legitimacy problem feel real rather than theoretical.
