Zygmunt Bauman argues in his study of modernity and the Holocaust that the genocide wasn't a relapse into premodern savagery but a product of distinctly modern conditions—bureaucratic rationality, division of labor, and instrumental reason. By breaking killing into administrative steps, modern organization created moral distance: people could participate in mass murder without feeling personally responsible. Technology and efficiency weren't incidental; they made the scale possible.

This relates to the Historikerstreit because if the Holocaust is modern, comparison gets tricky. Ernst Nolte compared it to Soviet terror, describing the Holocaust as an Asiatic deed reactive to Bolshevik terror, trying to spread guilt away from Germany alone. Jürgen Habermas rejected comparison, insisting the Holocaust remains metaphysically singular—literally incomparable in any respect—since any comparison risks normalizing the crime and weakening its moral weight for postwar West German identity.

Bauman complicates both positions. Since modern bureaucracy is universal, Nolte's comparison to Stalinism makes some sense: both regimes used modern administrative machinery. But Bauman also undermines Habermas, because if modern conditions are everywhere, then the Holocaust isn't singular—it's a warning that any industrial society could reproduce it. So Bauman implicitly sides with comparability, showing it's not a unique German deviation.

There's tension in Bauman's own argument, though. He sometimes writes as if modern rationality necessarily produces genocide once moral constraints are removed, which sounds deterministic. That clashes with Habermas's metaphysical uniqueness claim—if it's just bureaucratic autopilot running on modern logic, it can't be singular. Actually, Habermas probably meant normative singularity tied to democratic culture rather than metaphysics, but Bauman's universalism still undermines the uniqueness defense either way.
