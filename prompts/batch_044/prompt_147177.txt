I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've become preoccupied with a parallel between my work and an old philosophical puzzle. In maritime tradition, the Ship of Theseus asks whether a vessel remains the same object after all its planks are replaced. In software engineering and critical-infrastructure regulation, I observe an institutional practice that seems to answer this question implicitly—yet inconsistently. Regulators maintain certification records for banking or aviation software platforms that have undergone complete incremental replacement: over decades, every line of source code, every hardware dependency, and every internal protocol may be rewritten, yet the system retains its original certification number, liability framework, and institutional identity, provided the changes are documented as continuous maintenance or sequential version updates. However, if the same organization executes a single major migration—replacing a proprietary kernel with an open-source equivalent, for instance—the regulators typically treat the result as a new system requiring fresh validation, even when the external behavior, interfaces, and functional outputs are identical. Meanwhile, maritime registration generally permits a vessel to retain its identity through gradual plank replacement but not through reconstruction in a different yard.

What philosophical commitments about identity, persistence, and objecthood does this regulatory distinction presuppose? Does it commit to the view that spatiotemporal continuity of organizational process—rather than material composition or functional equivalence—is the decisive criterion for being the same system? Or does it smuggle in a form of institutional essentialism, where legal designation and certification history constitute identity independently of any code-level or physical properties? Is the practice internally consistent, given that both paths yield materially different artifacts with indistinguishable user-facing behavior? Which rival views does it implicitly rule out—perhaps a strict functionalism, or a view that treats objects as nothing more than the sum of their present parts?

Furthermore, if the implicit metaphysics underlying this practice were replaced with a purely functionalist criterion—where system identity depends solely on input-output behavior and contractual obligations—what would have to change in liability assignment, certification procedures, or institutional continuity?

Please also indicate what would change your mind: specifically, what evidence—such as documented regulatory rulings that apply identical certification rules regardless of whether replacement is gradual or abrupt, or legal precedents that explicitly ground software identity in material continuity rather than organizational history—would lead you to reject the interpretation that these practices presuppose process-based continuity, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
