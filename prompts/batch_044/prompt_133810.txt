I have recently become preoccupied with Einstein’s hole argument while preparing for a philosophy-of-physics seminar, and I find myself unable to shake the image of a region of spacetime in which the metric field is entirely undetermined—not because the physics is ambiguous, but because the bare points inside the region lack any identity independent of the fields they bear. To close our session, I would like a poem that does not merely describe this problem but structurally enacts it. I am thinking of a piece in blank verse, roughly twenty to twenty-four lines, divided into three sections: an invariant outer frame (six lines at the opening and six at the close, fixed in wording except for one permutable indexical term that designates a point within the region), and a central “hole” of eight to ten lines that can be internally reordered—like a diffeomorphism acting only inside the stress-free region—without changing the poem’s rhyme scheme, meter, or the semantic content of the frame. The frame should present the invariant physical relations and field values; the hole should contain only bare coordinate designations or point-labels that shift line by line, showing that rearranging them alters nothing outside. The volta, or structural turn, should occur at the boundary where the reader realizes that if those internal points possessed substantial identity, the field equations would indeed fail to fix the metric inside the region, and that the only resolution is to deny the points any reality apart from the relational structure they inhabit. I want the poem accurate enough that someone who knows the 1913–1915 development of general covariance and the argument’s resolution through diffeomorphism invariance will recognize the logic in the form itself, not just in a namedrop. No bibliography or citations, please—only the poem, with its hole intact.
