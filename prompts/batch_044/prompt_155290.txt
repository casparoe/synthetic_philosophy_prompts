I have recently found myself preoccupied with these questions after revising my pension contributions and arguing with relatives about climate obligations, and I am trying to understand whether my reactions form a coherent view of pure time preference or collapse into contradiction.

Here are my verdicts on specific cases.

First, regarding my own future: I believe it is rational for me to accept a materially lower disposable income now in order to secure adequate resources when I am seventy, despite feeling almost no emotional connection to that older person and being unable to imagine his experiences vividly. I treat this not as altruism toward a stranger but as prudence.

Second, regarding distant strangers: I do not feel a comparable obligation to forgo present income to fund carbon removal that will benefit people living two centuries hence, even if the magnitude of harm prevented is comparable to the poverty I would avoid in old age. The temporal distance seems to extinguish the claim, or at least diminish it radically.

Third, regarding short-term impatience: I judge it clearly irrational to prefer £100 today to £120 in twelve months, and I regard my own tendency to make exactly such trades for trivial goods as a cognitive failing rather than an expression of legitimate preference.

Fourth, regarding promises to myself: If I resolve to save £200 each month and then break that resolution to purchase something unnecessary, I experience the breach as something closer to a broken promise than to mere imprudence. Yet I also think that if I genuinely anticipate a substantial shift in my values—say, I will come to prioritise mobility over security—then spending now is permissible, because I am not betraying a self whose preferences I still endorse.

Fifth, regarding medical risk across time: I am strongly inclined to undergo an unpleasant screening now that reduces my risk of serious illness in thirty years. But I am uncertain whether this inclination is justified if the statistical risk reduction is identical to that of an anonymous peer of my age; I seem to grant my own future health a special status, yet I struggle to explain why that status does not extend to distant future strangers in the climate case.

Sixth, regarding long-term investment in identity: I think it rational to endure three years of low-paid training for a career I will enter only in my forties, provided the work aligns with values I expect to retain. However, if I believed the career would satisfy only a temporary version of myself—someone I will have outgrown—I think the investment would be wasteful, not merely risky.

These verdicts pull in different directions. Cases one and five seem to endorse a robust concern for one's own distant future, while case two denies a parallel concern for distant others. Case four introduces an exception for anticipated value change that case six applies to career investment but that seems absent from the retirement case. Case three treats near-future preference as bias, yet I am unsure whether my long-horizon preferences are genuinely rational or merely bias at a different scale.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving psychological continuity, value stability, or the scope of rational agency—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict must be abandoned for the sake of coherence? Furthermore, please apply the principle you propose to a case I have not listed: suppose I am offered a well-paid position now that carries a cumulative occupational risk manifesting only after retirement; should the fact that the harm falls on my own future self make me more reluctant to accept than I would be if the identical risk fell on a present colleague? And please provide your answer in British English.
