I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of collective remembrance, and we've been working through the tension between an ethics of memory—where communities owe fidelity to past wrongs, especially when victims are present—and the countervailing concern that modern culture dissolves authoritative tradition through strategic forgetting, themes we have studied through Margalit and Rieff. Over the term my professor has delivered clear verdicts on several cases, but I am trying to determine whether she is running a principle about preserving the content and accessibility of harmful records regardless of medium, a principle about preserving the fixed, authoritative material form of public memorials, or something closer to a proximity-based rule tied to the presence of survivors.

Her verdicts so far are these. First, she held that a city should not remove a monument to a founder who also authorized mass displacement; the right response is contextualization, not erasure, because removing the physical object betrays the duty to remember the wrong. Second, she argued that descendants of survivors have a legitimate claim to know traumatic family histories, but that no one should be compelled to internalize that knowledge as an identity-defining burden; disclosure is owed, forced identification is not. Third, she maintained that a state which declines to erect any public memorial within living memory of an atrocity commits a moral failure, regardless of whether private mourning continues. Fourth, she endorsed a religious community's decision to cease commemorating a centuries-old schism once the ritual had become exclusively a vehicle for present resentment rather than for genuine historical understanding. Fifth, she approved a policy of automated deletion for private digital archives after a fixed term, but insisted that permanent, unalterable preservation applies specifically to institutional records of public harm.

The new case is this. A municipal council proposes to destroy all physical bronze memorial plaques documenting a local genocide and replace them with a single interactive installation powered by generative AI. The installation synthesizes verified survivor testimonies into responsive, personalized narratives: visitors ask questions and receive tailored accounts drawn from both documented testimony and algorithmically reconstructed inferential material. The survivors' association is divided. Some argue the installation keeps memory dynamically alive and reaches younger audiences; others argue it dissolves a fixed, authoritative record into interpretive fluidity and substitutes algorithmic reconstruction for material witness.

If she wishes to be consistent with her own record, what should she decide about the proposal? Which earlier verdict is the closest analog? I believe her judgments are jointly consistent with at least two rules: one that permits transformation of medium so long as the content of the harm remains fully preserved and publicly accessible, and one that treats the fixed material form of collective memorials as itself carrying moral authority that algorithmic personalization undermines. Predict her verdict, identify which candidate principle best explains the pattern across her five judgments, name the further case that would best distinguish these competing rules—perhaps one in which the content is preserved in static digital form without generative reconstruction, or one in which the physical plaques remain but their inscriptions are subtly rewritten—and state clearly what moral principle she would be adopting if she approves the installation versus if she rejects it, particularly regarding whether the duty to remember binds a community to the material form of the record or only to its faithful transmission.
