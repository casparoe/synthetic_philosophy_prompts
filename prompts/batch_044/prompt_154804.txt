I am writing a seminar paper for my course on historiography and the philosophy of history, and I would like your comments on the central argument before I develop it into a full draft. I am not asking for sources or a bibliography—only for conceptual and structural feedback.

The standard critique of Whig history, associated with Herbert Butterfield, treats it mainly as an evaluative failure: historians write the past as an inevitable march toward present liberal institutions, distributing praise and blame according to proximity to modern values. I want to argue that this misses a more pervasive and less obvious variant that I will call structural Whig history. Here the historian need not endorse any narrative of moral progress; she may even regard modern outcomes with deep skepticism. Nevertheless, she organizes the past as a series of attempts to solve a problem that is defined entirely in present terms. The past is not praised for reaching us, but it is rendered intelligible only as a precursor to our current conceptual framework.

My case study is the historiography of early modern natural philosophy. Even scholars who reject triumphalist narratives—those who stress the heterogeneity of alchemy, astrology, and natural magic—frequently frame these practices as contributing, however obliquely, to the demarcation problem: how to distinguish genuine science from pseudo-science. Yet the demarcation problem, in the precise form familiar to philosophers of science, belongs largely to the twentieth century. To treat Boyle or Newton as implicitly wrestling with it is not necessarily to claim they anticipated Popper or Lakatos; it is to assimilate their concerns into ours. I call this retrospective conceptual assimilation. The result is not a simple factual anachronism but an organizational flattening: the past’s own categories are subordinated to a present-centered taxonomy that its actors would not have recognized.

I distinguish structural Whig history from normative Whig history, which judges past agents by present values, and from mere anachronism, which is an isolated error of attribution. Structural Whig history is a framing failure: it selects and arranges evidence according to a problem-space that only became explicit later.

My specific questions are three. First, is the distinction between normative and structural Whig history genuinely stable, or does any structural assimilation necessarily smuggle in normative commitments about which present problems are legitimate objects of backward tracing? Second, in applying this to philosophy of science historiography, am I being unfair to recent scholarship that aims at historical recovery—for instance, work that treats alchemical practice on its own terms? Or does even that recovery fall into structural Whig history when it frames itself as a correction to a previously distorted narrative, thereby preserving the same present-centered problem-space? Third, I am considering extending the argument to claim that structural Whig history is unavoidable for any historian who wishes to communicate with present readers, since all narration requires present concepts. If that is correct, then my critique collapses into a truism about the conditions of intelligibility. Is there a defensible middle ground between unavoidable presentism and the kind of retrospective assimilation I am criticizing?

Please comment on whether the distinction holds up, whether the application to historiography of science is convincing, and whether the argument risks self-undermining in its third step.
