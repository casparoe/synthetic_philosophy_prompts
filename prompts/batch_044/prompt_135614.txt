I am preparing an essay for my advanced high school philosophy seminar on the Hart-Fuller debate, and my instructor has asked us to avoid repeating the canonical illustrations—such as the Grudge Informer scenario or the problem of Nazi statutes—in favor of constructing our own conceptual test cases. I have been working with a hypothetical framework I call prospective consent statutes: a constitutional mechanism that permits future courts to recognize as legally valid certain past actions that violate existing positive rules, provided an approved predictive model demonstrates that a fully informed majority of citizens would have consented to them, and provided the justification is drawn from a latent structural principle said to be embedded in the constitution's preamble but never articulated in any enacted rule.

Could you construct for me a concrete example of a conceptual issue that arises within this framework—specifically, a case in which a court applies such a statute to validate a previously unlawful act, such as a whistleblower's disclosure of classified material or an emergency redistribution of resources—where Fuller's eight principles of legality appear formally satisfied, yet the result undermines the very conditions of human agency that Fuller's internal morality of law is meant to preserve? At the same time, I want the example to show why Hart's hierarchy of primary and secondary rules, including his rule of recognition, cannot straightforwardly accommodate this validation without either collapsing legal validity into moral evaluation or leaving a gap that positive law itself authorizes. Please present the scenario with philosophical precision, explain briefly why it exposes a shared vulnerability in both positions rather than simply adjudicating between them, and do so in a manner suitable for citation-free use in a seminar paper, without offering bibliographies or references to secondary literature.
