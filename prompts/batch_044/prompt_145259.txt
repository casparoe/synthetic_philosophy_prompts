Compare Karl Jaspers’s distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt with the idea of diachronic collective agency—the view that a nation is not merely an aggregate of present citizens but a single continuing agent that persists through time with its own intentions and responsibilities, analogous to an individual across a lifetime. If we treat the state as such an enduring agent, does Jaspers’s claim that political guilt can be inherited by citizens while moral guilt remains strictly individual and non-transferable still hold? Or does modeling the nation diachronically collapse his separation, forcing us to say that moral guilt, too, can be carried forward by the collective agent? Please compare the two frameworks on whether a nation can be genuinely guilty—rather than only politically liable—for wrongs committed by prior generations, and identify exactly which feature of Jaspers’s taxonomy blocks inherited moral blame and whether that feature survives once collective diachronic agency is granted.
