Analyze the August 1971 suspension of dollar-to-gold convertibility—the Nixon shock—as an episode in the social construction of institutional kinds. Use the conceptual machinery of collective intentionality, status-function assignment, and the distinction between constitutive and regulative rules. Map precisely: what brute facts changed (the Treasury's gold window), what institutional facts changed (the dollar's status as redeemable currency), and what remained constant (the physical notes, legal-tender statutes, collective acceptance). Then run the analysis in both directions. First, does the framework explain why the institution persisted rather than collapsed once its brute anchor was withdrawn—does this confirm that money is constituted by social agreement rather than intrinsic properties? Second, does the episode expose a weakness in the theory: can it distinguish a revision of an institution's constitutive rules from the dissolution of the institution itself, or does it blur the line between changing the rules of chess and declaring that chess no longer exists? Conclude by considering whether a fiat regime requires a different form of collective intentionality—perhaps one sustained by state authority and macroeconomic belief rather than by reference to a tangible commodity—or whether the underlying social mechanism is identical across both regimes.
