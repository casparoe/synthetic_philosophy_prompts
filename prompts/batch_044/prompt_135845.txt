Consider the long-running debate over whether Germany bears special moral responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War, crystallized in the Fischer controversy about deliberate imperial expansion and systemic war aims. Much of the literature treats this as either a historical-causal question—did specific state leaders form and execute an intention to initiate general hostilities?—or a collective-agency problem—can a nation-state, rather than its members, be the proper subject of blame?

I want to work within a sharper conceptual framework that distinguishes three rarely separated dimensions: (i) agent-responsibility, which asks whether a discrete state apparatus formed an intentional plan to trigger war; (ii) structural culpability, which asks whether institutional arrangements—military planning autonomy, constitutional secrecy, alliance dynamics—made escalation highly probable regardless of any particular cabinet’s intentions; and (iii) successor liability, which asks whether present-day political communities inherit moral, commemorative, or institutional obligations from predecessor regimes, even when democratic transformation, territorial division, and the absence of personal fault among current citizens break direct continuity.

Within this framework, what are the most important open conceptual questions, and which lines of work deserve priority for philosophers of collective responsibility, political theorists, and historians of ethics? Rank the following four in order of importance, defending your ordering with explicit criteria—upstream dependence on other questions, tractability without new archival discovery, neglectedness relative to the existing literature, and normative consequence for how societies handle historical injustice.

First, whether agent-responsibility and structural culpability are genuinely independent explanatory categories, or whether every plausible account of the former collapses into the latter once institutional context and counterfactual decision-paths are fully described. Second, whether successor liability requires a defensible theory of transgenerational political identity that can survive regime change and democratic transformation without reducing to individual guilt. Third, what practical normative implications—if any—follow from establishing either form of responsibility for a century-old conflict, particularly regarding education, memorial architecture, or institutional reform rather than material reparations. Fourth, whether the standard framing of “blaming a nation” mistakenly assumes that collective moral responsibility must be distributed to every individual member, rather than borne by symbolic institutions, legal personalities, or collective practices that persist independently of membership turnover.

For each line, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what kind of conceptual progress over the next several years would elevate or diminish its priority, and which is most likely to absorb effort without altering either theory or practice. I am especially interested in whether resolving the independence question—agent versus structural—would render the successor-liability debate easier, harder, or largely irrelevant, and what that dependency implies for where a small research group should direct its limited resources. Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey, and be explicit about where judgment must replace argument because the criteria themselves involve value choices.
