In a graduate seminar on moral metaphysics, we have spent several sessions on Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument—the regress according to which, if you are genuinely responsible for an action, you must be responsible for the mental states that produced it, and then for the mental states that produced those, and so on, with true moral responsibility therefore requiring that you be causa sui, which is impossible. The compatibilist responses in the room consistently return to sourcehood and character: they argue that responsibility only requires being the source of action in a non-ultimate, explanatory sense, that the regress legitimately stops at our rational capacities or practical identities, and that Strawson illegitimately demands self-creation ex nihilo. The defenders reply that each such response merely relocates the problem—if my rational capacities, values, or deliberative processes are not themselves up to me, then I am not ultimately up to me—and that the critics are quietly substituting explanatory sourcehood for desert-based responsibility, changing the subject rather than answering it. After weeks, the discussion has stalled: the same examples (the abused child turned abuser, the compulsive agent) are recycled, the same distinction between “being responsible” and “being truly or ultimately responsible” is redrawn, and neither side has found a reason to abandon its framing.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about determinism or free will in any empirical sense, but a hidden clash over whether moral responsibility requires a kind of vertical self-constitution that relational or structural accounts of agency explicitly deny—in other words, that the two sides are answering different questions about what “source” means, one metaphysical and one functional, under the single heading of “moral responsibility.” I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is the stall verbal (centered on the word “true” or “ultimate”), substantive but buried beneath shared examples that both sides interpret differently, or a case of two research programs answering different questions—one about explanatory attribution, one about basic desert—without noticing the divergence? And given your diagnosis, what specifically—a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal model of agential levels, a shift in the framing of the question, or an acknowledgment that no further progress should be expected—would count as genuine movement, or should we treat the impasse as principled and permanent?
