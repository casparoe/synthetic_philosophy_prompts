Assume Alexis de Tocqueville were presented with the modern comparative-political debate over democratic backsliding—specifically the dispute over whether the concept functions primarily as a partisan label applied selectively to governments in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, or whether it can be operationalized through procedural indicators independent of ideological content, such as executive aggrandizement, strategic manipulation of electoral rules, and the incremental erosion of horizontal accountability. Drawing on his analysis of administrative centralization and the risks of democratic despotism he articulated in Democracy in America, articulate how he would likely respond to the following. First, would he regard the contemporary emphasis on judicial independence and media pluralism as institutional safeguards consistent with his framework, or as superficial metrics that obscure a deeper willingness among citizens to surrender autonomy to an expansive administrative apparatus? Second, given his distinction between revolutionary rupture and gradual bureaucratic encroachment, how might he classify regimes that preserve formal electoral rituals while systematically hollowing out checks and balances—would he interpret this pattern as a qualitatively new form of authoritarianism that exceeds his nineteenth-century typology, or as the precise realization of the soft despotism he feared?
