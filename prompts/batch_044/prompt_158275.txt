I'm giving the opening remarks at a retirement workshop honoring Dr. Miriam Kowalski, a physician-philosopher who spent her career arguing that health and disease cannot be reduced either to bare biological dysfunction or to social valuation alone. The audience is divided: half are clinicians and philosophers of medicine steeped in the naturalism-versus-normativism debate; half are family members, former patients, and nursing staff who have never encountered that literature. Miriam developed a view she called biographical functionalism: a condition is pathological only when a biological variation conflicts with a person's practically situated life-plan, not with a universal species-typical function and not with an arbitrarily imposed social ideal. She returned constantly to one comparative case — a professional cyclist with a congenital cardiac rhythm that is statistically abnormal and would be disqualifying in her sport, yet allows her to train safely and pursue the endurance life she has built, versus an office worker with the identical rhythm who suffers debilitating fatigue and clear impairment in a sedentary occupation. Miriam insisted that both judgments — healthy-in-context for the cyclist, diseased-in-context for the worker — are objective rather than subjective preferences, but depend on evaluating biological function against individual life-structures rather than against biology alone or culture alone.

Write a twelve-minute address that makes this case do structural work for both halves of the room. For the non-experts, explain why Miriam refused both the physician's answer that health is only a matter of biology and the sociologist's answer that it is only a matter of social valuation, showing how the cyclist and the worker prove medicine requires both factual and evaluative judgments without reducing one to the other. For the experts, show — without using the technical terms naturalism or normativism, and without citing specific paper titles — exactly where her biographical functionalism diverges from Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory and from Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis: specifically, that she rejects universal species-typical statistical norms as the health baseline and also rejects the requirement that dysfunction be harmful according to some culture-independent welfare standard. The tone should be warm, celebratory, and intellectually honest: do not claim her view is now mainstream, and close by asking the assembled room what clinical responsibility means when the identical biological fact can constitute health for one life-plan and disease for another.
