As a retired marine ecologist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I find the so-called nonidentity problem more disorienting than many of my colleagues admit: in empirical science we routinely compare outcomes across treatments, yet here the very identity of the affected subjects seems to shift with the treatment itself, and the comparison dissolves in a way that feels different from ordinary uncertainty. I would like you to design a 90-minute workshop activity for fourteen advanced philosophy students—split evenly into two boards of seven, seated at separate tables with movable chairs in a seminar room—so that they experience this dissolution from the inside before anyone names it. The participants should come away seeing, first, why an institution genuinely committed to identifying specific victims of policy choices can be brought to a complete administrative standstill, and second, that this standstill is not a failure of information but a structural feature of comparing worlds that contain different people.

The scenario should be institutional rather than personal: a regional planning authority must select, for the next fifty years, either a dense, nuclear-powered urban consolidation with restricted migration patterns (Trajectory Alpha) or a dispersed renewable grid with open settlement and labor mobility (Trajectory Beta). Both trajectories should be described with plausible climate, economic, and demographic projections, but the facilitator should present the task as an ordinary policy evaluation—participants must identify serious welfare deficits and assign harm—so that the philosophical trap is hidden in the procedure, not announced in the framing. To inject the necessary conceptual machinery without importing an obscure academic theory, please construct the rules around an invented instrument called the Transworld Harm Ledger, which permits a board to record any deprivation only if its members can identify the harmed individual in both trajectories using a fictional “Biographical Substrate Continuity” criterion—this demands that a victim share specific formative conditions (place and window of conception, parental pairing, and early ecological context) with a counterpart in the other world. After each board selects and commits to one trajectory, they receive a sealed packet containing ten detailed citizen profiles drawn from the 2075 registry of that trajectory, each profile describing a real life with one serious deprivation. They have thirty minutes to complete the Ledger. They will discover that the two registries are entirely disjoint: no profile satisfies the continuity criterion across worlds. The boards then attempt to present cross-claims—that Board Alpha has harmed Board Beta’s citizens, and vice versa—which the facilitator must reject administratively, since without filled Ledger rows no harm is registered.

Please include a precise timeline, a materials list, role instructions, and a debrief that maps three moments onto the philosophical idea: the initial confidence that harm can be assigned (the intuitive pull of person-affecting morality), the failure of the Biographical Substrate Continuity test (staging the nonidentity of future people across worlds), and the administrative void that results (demonstrating that without shared identity, comparative harm judgments collapse rather than merely become uncertain). You should also specify what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—participants will likely try to circumvent the Ledger by appealing to “impersonal” welfare losses, averaging across populations, or inventing hypothetical hybrid identities to serve as matched victims—and advise how to handle it, for instance by ruling that any harm claim without a completed Ledger row is procedurally inadmissible and must be set aside. Finally, be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: it stages the practical paralysis of comparison, but it cannot demonstrate the metaphysical claim that in the other world there literally is no one who is the same person, so that the absence of harm is ontological rather than epistemic. The facilitator must supply that distinction verbally after the activity ends, because the rules alone can only show that the comparison fails, not why it fails in principle.
