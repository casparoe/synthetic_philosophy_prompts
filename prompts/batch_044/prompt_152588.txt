I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of physics, and for this week's session I've been working through James Clerk Maxwell's original 1867 letter to Peter Tait, in which he introduces the thought experiment now known as Maxwell's demon. Alongside it, I've read Leo Szilard's 1929 paper extending the scenario into an explicit argument about information and entropy. What I'm struggling with is whether Maxwell's own framing actually contains anything like an information-theoretic concern, or whether that reading is entirely a projection introduced later by Szilard, Landauer, and Bennett.

In the passage, Maxwell asks us to imagine a being with capacities delicate enough to observe the velocity of individual molecules in a divided chamber and to operate a frictionless gate, allowing only faster molecules to pass one way and slower ones the other, thereby producing a temperature differential without any expenditure of work. He then considers whether this shows that the second law of thermodynamics is merely statistical rather than absolute, and he qualifies his being as finite in its powers. I find myself uncertain how to parse this qualification: does Maxwell mean that the being is physically small, that its sensory apparatus is limited, or that its knowledge is necessarily incomplete? More broadly, he seems to treat the act of discerning molecular velocities as if it required no thermodynamic cost, yet he also describes the being as physically manipulating a gate. I'm trying to determine whether Maxwell is genuinely suggesting that some form of intelligence or observation can circumvent physical law, or whether he already implicitly assumes that any process of discrimination must itself be embedded in the physical dynamics of the system, even if he lacks the conceptual vocabulary of bits, measurement, and erasure that later authors used to resolve the paradox.

Could you help me understand the passage on its own terms? Specifically, I'd like to know whether it is anachronistic to read Maxwell as an unwitting precursor to the physics-of-information resolution, or whether his argument remains strictly within statistical mechanics and never touches on the epistemic or informational dimensions at all. If possible, please clarify how the modern distinction between acquiring information and erasing it relates to Maxwell's description of the demon's activity, and whether his reference to the being's finitude is better interpreted as a physical constraint or as an early, inchoate acknowledgment that observation itself must have thermodynamic limits.
