I hold a functionalist view of 'Western values': the phrase is coherent not because it tracks a shared cultural heritage, but because it denotes a specific cluster of normative commitments—individual autonomy, procedural rationality, and secular governance—that modern liberal-democratic states must invoke together to justify universal rights claims without collapsing into pure proceduralism. This makes the category necessarily thin: thinner than cultural conservatives claim and thinner than critics of Western hypocrisy assume. What would genuinely count against this account? I want defeaters that attack the coherence of the cluster itself, not merely examples of political hypocrisy or historical counter-examples from outside Europe. Specifically: could a state satisfy all three functional conditions yet be intuitively outside the category, or reject one condition yet remain intuitively inside? Would empirical evidence that these commitments routinely dissociate—for instance, robust secular governance paired with explicit rejection of individual autonomy—refute the cluster view or just show that multiple stable packages exist? And has the functionalist position been qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing the cluster's membership to shift over time? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those a committed functionalist should concede from those they should dismiss, and assess whether insulating the view from counter-example is intellectually costly or defensible.
