I was arguing with my study group about whether self-deception is even a real thing, not just denial with extra steps, and someone threw this chain at me and now I can't sleep. Here goes:

(1) If I'm genuinely self-deceived that my partner is cheating, then part of my mind registers the evidence and guides my avoidance behavior, while another part sincerely denies it. (2) Call the first an implicit beleif and the second an explicit one; both are about the same proposition. (3) Now, a belief isn't just a brain-state; it's a commitment I'm answerable for, so holding both is holding contradictory commitments. (4) Rationality doesn't let you park contradictions in seperate compartments forever — they generate epistemic friction that must resolve. (5) So self-deception isn't a stable state; it's just rapid oscillation between believing and not-believing, like a flickering bulb. (6) But then apply the same logic to implicit bias, repressed memories, or tacit knowledge: if they're sustained states where I both have and lack a commitment, they're also just flickering. (7) That collapses the conscious/unconscious distinction into "what I'm attending to right now," which wipes out the conceptual basis for implicit association tests, subliminal priming, and half of clinical psychology. (8) I don't want to trash all of cognitive science, but I also can't see the exit. Is the mistake in (3), treating sub-doxastic states as full commitments? Or in (4), assuming rationality forces immediate resolution? Or is (6) an overgeneralization — maybe self-deception is special because the contradiction is about my own mental states? Which step do I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) and (7) if possible, since they match my intuition, but I know I probably can't have both.
