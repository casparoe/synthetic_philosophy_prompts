Two colleagues in my reading group had a sustained disagreement about whether Nozick’s tracking conditions actually deny epistemic closure in the way he intended, particularly for entailments that are not skeptical hypotheses but ordinary statistical or logical inferences. I was only listening; please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right at each step, what both parties are missing, and who is closer to being correct overall.

Here is how it went.

Elena argued that tracking clearly breaks closure even for mundane entailments. She took the case of knowing that a fair die landed on 4, tracked via direct observation. From this she knows that it did not land on 6, since 4 entails not-6. But she said the tracking condition for “it did not land on 6” fails: in the nearest world where it did land on 6, Elena’s perceptual apparatus might still register 4 because of a brief visual glitch, so she might still believe it was 4 and therefore believe it was not 6. Hence she does not track the negation of 6, despite knowing the premise and knowing the entailment. For Elena, this shows closure fails for completely ordinary logical consequences, not only for brain-in-vat scenarios.

David responded that Elena applies the counterfactual to the wrong belief. The belief to test is not “it was not 6” considered in isolation, but “it was not 6” as formed through the valid inference from “it was 4.” Nozick’s conditions apply to the actual belief-forming process. In the nearest world where the die shows 6, Elena would not form the premise belief “it was 4” in the first place—her tracking of the premise remains intact—and therefore she would not form the conclusion either. So the conclusion belief is also tracked, not because she independently checks for 6, but because the inference is part of the belief’s etiology.

Elena came back that this collapses tracking into a closure-preserving account by letting the premise belief “carry” tracking to the conclusion. But Nozick explicitly denies that knowledge is closed under known entailment. If David is right, then knowing any proposition automatically gives you tracking for all its logical consequences, which restores full closure and eliminates Nozick’s distinctive anti-skeptical payoff—he wanted to say we know we have hands but do not know we are not brains in vats. If tracking is inherited through inference, then knowing we have hands should let us know we are not brains in vats whenever we competently infer it, which undermines the whole point.

David replied that there is an important distinction between entailments that are “nearby” and those that are “distant.” For nearby entailments like not-6 given 4, the worlds where the conclusion is false are the same worlds where the premise is false, so tracking is preserved through the inference. For distant entailments like not-brain-in-vat given hands, the worlds where the conclusion is false are not worlds where the premise is false—you can be a brain in a vat with hands—so the premise belief does not protect the conclusion. This is exactly how Nozick preserves closure locally while denying it globally, and Elena’s die example actually supports this rather than undermining it.

Elena then said that this “distance” metric is doing all the work but is never formally defined in Nozick’s account. She proposed a more precise framing: call an entailment “method-sensitive” if the counterfactual worlds that falsify the conclusion are a subset of those that falsify the premise, given the agent’s actual reasoning method. Under this framing, closure holds only for method-sensitive entailments. But she argued this is an addition to Nozick, not an interpretation—Nozick never talks about reasoning methods in his conditions, only about belief-world correlations.

David conceded that “method-sensitive” is not Nozick’s own term, but insisted it is implicit in condition (4), which requires that in nearby worlds where p is true, S still believes p. If S’s belief in p is formed via method M, then condition (4) already checks whether M is stable across nearby worlds. Applying M to derive q means q inherits that stability. So Elena’s framing is not an external addition; it is just making explicit what is already in the tracking conditions when they are applied to inferential beliefs.

They stopped there, with Elena insisting that this makes tracking indistinguishable from a reliabilist closure principle, and David insisting that the distance-based asymmetry still saves Nozick’s anti-skeptical result.

Please adjudicate: who is right about the die and not-6 case; about whether tracking is inherited through valid inference; about whether this restores closure globally; and about whether “method-sensitive” is an implicit feature of Nozick’s conditions or an external theoretical addition. Say also what the strongest point is that each side is missing.
