I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend over whether ‘democratic backsliding’ remains a coherent analytical category or has collapsed into a partisan epithet. We kept returning to three issues: what counts as backsliding as distinct from ordinary democratic contestation; who has the authority to declare that a threshold has been crossed; and whether the concept can be rescued from the charge that it is applied asymmetrically across the political spectrum. Rather than asking for a survey of the literature, I want you to treat this as an agenda-setting problem. Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary working group — political theorists, constitutional lawyers, and comparative sociologists — with a three-year funding programme and no obligation to produce empirical case studies. They must decide which unresolved conceptual distinctions in the backsliding debate deserve clarification first, and which practical implications of those clarifications matter most for how journalists, judges, and citizens should apply the label. In particular, focus on the tension between procedural erosion (the manipulation of electoral rules and oversight institutions) and substantive erosion (the contraction of civil liberties and minority protections), and on the further complication that these two dimensions can diverge — for example, when a government expands direct democratic mechanisms while simultaneously weakening judicial review. Rank the open conceptual questions that arise from this divergence. For each, explain why it is upstream of others, how tractable it is for conceptual rather than empirical work, and what evidence over the next few years would move it up or down your list. Then identify the two or three implications that, if resolved, would genuinely change how practitioners distinguish backsliding from legitimate democratic change in contexts such as Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the United States. Defend your ordering with explicit criteria — importance, tractability, neglectedness, and the risk that an unresolved question feeds partisan misuse — and avoid offering bibliographies or treating citation counts as evidence. I am interested in the reasoning, not a reading list.
