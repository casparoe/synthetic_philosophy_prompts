In my comparative politics course, Professor Reed has sorted four constitutional arrangements into parliamentary or presidential systems. She placed the Westminster model—where the legislature selects the prime minister and may dismiss the government by a simple vote—in the parliamentary column. She placed the United States model—where voters separately elect a chief executive for a fixed term and removal requires impeachment—in the presidential column. She placed the German chancellor system, in which the legislature elects the head of government who serves until a constructive vote of no confidence succeeds, also in the parliamentary column. Finally, she placed a Latin American model with a separately elected president who appoints ministers serving solely at presidential pleasure in the presidential column.

Our student government is now debating a proposal for a new school-wide executive: a single chancellor chosen by direct popular election using ranked-choice voting for a fixed two-year term, but who may be removed at any time by a two-thirds vote of the student senate, and whose cabinet secretaries must each maintain individual senate confidence to remain in office. Predict whether Professor Reed would classify this as presidential, parliamentary, or an unstable hybrid; identify the two general rules—one centered on the electoral origin of the executive and one on legislative survival in office—that both account for her four prior classifications; explain what each rule predicts for this chancellor proposal; state your best-guess verdict with reasoning; and describe one additional institutional feature—say, a change in removal threshold or election method—that would definitively show which of the two rules she is actually applying.
