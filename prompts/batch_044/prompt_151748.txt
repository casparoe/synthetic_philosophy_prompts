I've been trying to work out my actual position on whether cross-cultural moral variation undermines objectivity, moving past the standard WEIRD-psychology summary and the reflexive "relativism is self-defeating" reply that dominates rationalist forums. I have a tentative framework: moral disagreement is "shallow"—compatible with a single correct account—when it stems from (i) different empirical beliefs about outcomes, (ii) different jurisdictional allocations of moral authority (who enforces, who is held responsible), or (iii) context-sensitive weighting of shared values under different social conditions. It is "deep"—genuinely threatening to objectivity—only when one culture denies that a class of beings possesses basic moral status at all, rather than merely holding that harming them is overridden by other considerations. I want to know whether this principle actually captures my judgments, where it breaks, and which verdicts I should abandon.

Here are my concrete reactions. The familiar divergence in moral-foundations weightings—WEIRD populations emphasizing harm and fairness, other societies weighting loyalty, authority, and purity more heavily—feels shallow: it is a difference in salience and social scale, not in whether harm or fairness are genuinely normative. Societies that delegate punishment to ancestors or spirits rather than human courts also seem shallow, differing only in jurisdiction, not in whether the underlying act is wrong. Historical infanticide practiced under extreme resource scarcity strikes me as shallow if driven by false empirical premises about suffering or survival prospects, but deep if grounded in a genuine denial that newborns have moral standing. Funerary cannibalism versus burial or cremation also feels shallow: the same underlying value—respect for the dead—is expressed through symbolically coherent but incompatible rituals. By contrast, lying to comfort a dying patient is permitted in some traditions and forbidden in others, and I classify that as shallow virtue-weighting—yet I am unsure what structurally distinguishes it from harder cases.

The trouble case is a small-scale society with strict clan boundaries and no individual concept of moral agency: within the clan, all judgment is delegated to elders, and individuals speak only of "breaking rules" rather than "being wrong"; outside the clan, harming strangers is not overridden but literally unthinkable as a moral category—there is no concept of "wronging" them at all. I initially classify this as deep ontological divergence, because outsiders appear excluded from moral status. But I worry I am being inconsistent: if all moral authority is structurally centralized in the clan, the absence of cross-group obligations might just be extreme jurisdictional boundary-drawing, analogous to legal citizenship, rather than a denial that outsiders can be wronged. Meanwhile, I think the infanticide judgment should stay deep only when status is denied, not when it is empirically mistaken—yet I have no clear criterion for telling the two apart in practice. Can you articulate a precise principle, or a small family of them, that saves as many of these verdicts as possible? Be honest about which of my intuitions the principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc modification.
