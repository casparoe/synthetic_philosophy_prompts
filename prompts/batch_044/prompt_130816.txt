I am preparing a feature for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I want to close with a poem that performs—rather than merely summarizes—the central structural tension in Keynes's interwar essay on economic possibilities and the future of work. I am not looking for a historical pastiche or a sermon about work-life balance; I want a formal poem, specifically a Shakespearean sonnet in iambic pentameter with the rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, whose architecture enacts the very pivot Keynes describes. In the octave, build the optimistic picture he offered: the economic problem solved, absolute needs satisfied, machinery delivering a radically shortened workweek, and humanity finally freed to pursue contemplation, art, and the good life. At the volta in line nine, turn sharply to what Keynes underestimated—not technological failure, but the insatiability of relative needs and the institutional tendency to convert freed time into new forms of labor. Specifically, imagine a present in which the shortened week exists on paper but has been colonized by algorithmic productivity metrics, platform micro-work, and status competition that manufacture scarcity where none exists. The closing couplet should state exactly what Keynes's framework missed: that solving material scarcity does not dissolve the human impulse to invent new scarcities. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of economic thought recognizes the distinction between absolute and relative needs, and should treat the love of money not as a private moral failing but as an institutional proxy for the love of living that Keynes feared. Please write it in the voice of one of Keynes's hypothetical grandchildren—someone who inherited the machinery but not the leisure—so that the form's turn mirrors the argument's turn, and the rhyme scheme carries the reader from promised liberation to its quiet reversal.
