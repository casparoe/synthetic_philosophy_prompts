A colleague extended Chesterton's fence and Burkean prescription into what looks like a reductio, and I need the diagnosis. (1) Chesterton's fence principle says that if you do not understand why an institution exists, you should not tear it down, because it likely serves hidden functions. (2) Burkean conservatism holds that inherited institutions embody practical wisdom exceeding any generation's theoretical grasp, making preservation the rational default. (3) Add an opacity thesis: for any proposed reform, there will always remain some tacit, structurally hidden function of the existing arrangement, so complete understanding is not a temporary gap but permanently unattainable. (4) Therefore, for every reform, the epistemic condition required to justify change is never met. (5) Hence no institutional reform is ever rationally permitted; total stasis follows. (6) This applies even to the legal and social institutions that protect inquiry and property—the very conditions that allow Burkeans to defend the view—so if those were ever reformed into existence, the view forbids them too. (7) The result is either self-defeating paralysis or an obligation to preserve every harmful practice whose origins are obscure. I'd prefer to keep (1) and (2). Which step fails—perhaps an equivocation between not being fully understood and not being understood well enough to justify change, or a scope shift from some hidden function remaining to no reform ever being licensed—and what does surrendering that step cost? Does it reduce the position to ordinary risk-aversion? Price the alternatives honestly. If this argument rests on a confused premise, or if diagnosing it well requires assumptions I haven't made explicit, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
