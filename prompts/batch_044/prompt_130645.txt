In an interdisciplinary working group on taxonomy and social ontology, a participant recently invoked the School of Names slogan that a white horse is not a horse — rendered from bai ma fei ma — to argue that any compound category formed by adding an attribute to a base kind is ontologically distinct from that base kind. She applied it specifically to resist reducing intersectional classifications, such as categories formed by combining gender and racial descriptors, to the corresponding base categories plus accidental modifiers; on her reading, the compound term picks out an entirely different kind, just as 'white horse' allegedly picks out something that is not a horse at all. I am trying to map out exactly what logical, semantic, or metaphysical commitments that move could carry, because the argument seemed to slide between several distinct positions without marking the shifts. Could you lay out the different precise claims that the slogan 'a white horse is not a horse' might express — for instance, as a thesis about the extension of compound terms versus simple terms; about essential versus accidental properties; about the failure of substitutivity salva veritate; about the ontology of universals and their modes of instantiation; about the semantics of attributive modification; or about the independence of conceptual content from linguistic form — and for each reading say whether it is a trivial linguistic point, a substantive but defensible position, or simply false? In particular, indicate for each reading whether it actually supports her anti-reductionist conclusion that a compound category is ontologically separate from its base, and flag which readings are likely being illicitly conflated when the slogan is used to shut down reductionist explanations in taxonomy. Please present the options as a clear menu I can bring back to the discussion, with verdicts attached.
