I've been taking this historiography course and we're deep in Braudel's Meditteranean work, and my study group cooked up an argument that seems to collapse his whole three-level framework into either useless determinism or self-contradiction. Here are the steps:

(1) Braudel divides history into the longue durée (geography, climate, slow social structures), the conjuncture (economic cycles, decades), and the événementielle (individual actions, battles, diplomacy).
(2) He says the first is the "true" history and the others are just foam on the waves — surface disturbances that don't change the deep current.
(3) If the deep current is genuinely causal and the surface is just foam, then the surface should be fully determined by the deep layer, like ripples being fixed by the tide.
(4) So if we understand the Mediterranean's climate, trade routes, and soil patterns well enough, we shouldn't need Philip II's psychology or court politics to explain why the Spanish Armada failed in 1588; the long structures should do all the work.
(5) But Braudel himself spends hundreds of pages on Philip's psychology and specific diplomatic moves, treating them as if they actually do explanatory work.
(6) So either those narrative chapters are redundant bad history, or the deep layer doesn't actually determine the surface.
(7) If it doesn't determine the surface, then calling events "mere foam" is false — they have independent causal weight.
(8) Therefore Braudel either has to admit events matter (giving up his hierarchy) or admit that half his own book is redundant.

Where does this go wrong? I really want to keep (1) and (5) — I think there's something right about slow structures being primary, and I also think Braudel was right to narrate Philip's court. What's the least painful premise to drop, and what does giving it up cost? Please answer in American English, I'm sharing this with a friend in Boston who gets annoyed by British spellings.
