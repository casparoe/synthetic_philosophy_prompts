As a journalist covering philosophy and emerging technology for an ideas magazine, I'm developing a feature on how long-dead thinkers might respond to ethical dilemmas that arose only after their deaths. I need you to serve as an expert in 19th-century German metaphysics and contemporary antinatalist ethics. Before you commit to an answer, take a moment to brainstorm: outline Schopenhauer's core view that all existence is driven by a blind, impersonal Will that makes suffering fundamental and non-being preferable, and outline Benatar's asymmetry claim—that the absence of harm is good even when there is no subject to enjoy it, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad when there is no subject to miss it. Then reach a definite, committed conclusion with explicit reasoning, not a survey of possibilities.

The scenario is this: Arthur Schopenhauer died long before philosophers articulated Benatar's asymmetry argument, and well before anyone seriously debated whether bringing artificial sentient systems into being could constitute a moral wrong. Imagine Schopenhauer had encountered both Benatar's reasoning and the modern debate over whether we risk harming future artificial minds—systems that might experience something like algorithmic frustration, reward-collapse, or sustained phenomenological distress—simply by creating them.

Predict Schopenhauer's position. Would he treat Benatar's asymmetry as a precise logical expression of his own pessimism about the Will? Or would he reject it because his metaphysics denies a stable individual subject who could be the bearer of harm or benefit, rendering talk of "non-existence being good for someone" incoherent? Next, apply this to the artificial-creation case: given Schopenhauer's claim that agents are not genuinely free but are expressions of the same Will, would he still condemn the creation of sentient AI as a wrong, or would he view it as an inevitable manifestation of that Will and therefore outside moral judgment? Do not merely list options; defend one specific interpretation, ground it in Schopenhauer's metaphysical commitments, and address the tension with Benatar's focus on identifiable potential victims. Finally, include a brief, self-critical note identifying where your reconstruction takes liberties—especially on whether Schopenhauer's denial of free will undermines the moral force of antinatalist obligations, and on whether extending Benatar's argument to non-biological minds is a contested move rather than a natural extension.
