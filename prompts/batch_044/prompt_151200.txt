I have been observing a particular kind of negotiation that arises when two communities hold genuinely incommensurable moral frameworks—frameworks that lack any shared metric of value and that each regards the other’s core commitments as mistaken, perhaps even harmful. Rather than seeking a common justification or splitting differences through mutual concession, the communities redesign an institutional arrangement so that each can secure absolute protection for its own non-negotiable constraints while simultaneously receiving a benefit it values highly, a benefit that the other community can supply without violating its own foundational principles. To make it concrete: one community might treat a specific class of actions as categorically impermissible within its own jurisdiction, while the other evaluates every institutional feature solely by its contribution to aggregate welfare; the redesigned system allows the first to maintain an unconditional prohibition inside its domain, while the second achieves a welfare gain through mechanisms operating outside that domain. Both sides continue to reject the other’s basic premises, neither revises its internal values, and neither treats the arrangement as morally correct from its own point of view—yet both are unambiguously better off than they would be under pure isolation or direct conflict.

This does not seem to be an ordinary modus vivendi sustained only by a temporary balance of power, because the institutional structure is stable and deliberately engineered rather than contingent on threat. Nor does it appear to be reflective equilibrium or an overlapping consensus in the familiar sense, since there is no attempt to justify the arrangement through shared reasons or to derive principles that both frameworks could endorse from within. Is there an established concept—perhaps in political philosophy, the literature on value pluralism, or the theory of moral compromise—that precisely captures this structural possibility of generating mutual, Pareto-improving gains across incommensurable value systems without any convergence of belief? If such a concept exists, what are its boundaries: how does it differ from cases where one framework is merely subordinated to another, and what distinguishes it from simple practical coordination that leaves both sides’ moral judgments entirely untouched? If there is no established term, I would welcome a careful delineation of the phenomenon’s edges—what conditions must hold for a compromise to count as this specific kind of cross-framework trade rather than as an unprincipled bargain or a disguised victory for one side.
