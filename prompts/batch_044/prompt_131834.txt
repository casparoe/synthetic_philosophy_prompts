I'm working through some ideas for a philosophy of normativity course and I keep getting stuck on this argument my study partner sketchd out about free-floating rationales. She thinks it shows that if you allow reasons without reasoners you end up with something she calls "normative vaccum pressure" -- the idea that reasons exert force on reality even when there's nothing to feel the push. Anyways here's her chain:

(1) If a reason R is genuinely free-floating, then R doesn't depend for its existence on any agent, actual or possible.
(2) The fact that gratuitous pain is bad is a candidate free-floating reason: it doesn't seem like anyone has to exist for pain's badness to be real.
(3) So in a world with no agents at all -- call it an empty world -- there's still a reason against gratuitous pain.
(4) But reasons aren't just static properties; they have a direction. A reason against X is a reason for Y (avoiding X, preventing X, etc.).
(5) If there's a reason with direction but no agent to be directed toward, then the direction has to land somewhere: on the world-state itself, or on nothing.
(6) If it lands on nothing, then free-floating reasons are causally and practically inert, which makes them mysterious and maybe not reasons at all.
(7) So it lands on the world-state: empty worlds with pain are normatively defective in a way that worlds without pain aren't, even though no one exists to care.
(8) Now apply this across all possible worlds: every world gets assigned an objective normative score based on its free-floating reasons.
(9) But then moral reasoning isn't about what agents should do; it's about computing the hidden normative score of reality. And that seems to drain morality of its practical point entirely -- it becomes cosmological accounting, not guidance.
(10) Therefore, either (2) is wrong and pain's badness depends on reasoners after all, or free-floating rationales collapse morality into something useless for agents.

She says this proves free-floating rationales are self-defeating, but I want to keep (2) if possible -- I really think pain's badness is just there, not invented. I can't tell if the problem is in how 'direction' is being used between (4) and (5), or if (6) is sneaking in an assumption that reasons must be actionable, or maybe there's a scope issue I'm missing with (8). Which step do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (2) and (4) and lose something in the middle, but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
