For my high school philosophy coursework, I am writing about Derek Parfit’s hitchhiker thought experiment and the idea of rational irrationality—the notion that it can be rational to bind oneself to actions that will appear irrational once the moment arrives. I want to apply this framework to a real historical episode: the development of American nuclear deterrence doctrine in the early 1960s, specifically the institutional commitment to second-strike capability and mutual assured destruction, using the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis as the critical test case.

Treating the nuclear-armed state as the hitchhiker and a potential aggressor as the driver, please analyze whether the actual institutional mechanisms—automated response protocols, dispersed command authority, and the public doctrine of massive retaliation—function as credible pre-commitments in Parfit’s sense, or whether they fail because they still depend on bureaucratic interpretation and human judgment at the point of execution. Then examine the crisis itself as the “arrival at town”: once the immediate threat had passed and Soviet missiles were being withdrawn from Cuba, the United States declined to invade and simultaneously arranged the quiet withdrawal of its own Jupiter missiles from Turkey. Does this sequence represent the rational post-arrival defection that a truly credible commitment should have made impossible, or does it show that the commitment was never genuinely binding?

I am especially interested in whether the case exposes a structural gap in Parfit’s account. His scenario treats the problem as primarily psychological—the hitchhiker will be tempted to break his word. But nuclear deterrence seems to require not just a psychological disposition but a literal organizational and physical inability to reconsider. Does the Cuban Missile Crisis reveal that such structural barriers are always porous, and that rational irrationality therefore demands something closer to an actual elimination of future choice rather than a well-designed institution? Please offer a disciplined two-way analysis: use the theory to clarify what the actors faced and whether the doctrine solved their credibility problem, but also use the crisis to show where the framework assumes conditions—such as a unitary decision-maker or perfect commitment credibility—that real political systems do not satisfy, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
