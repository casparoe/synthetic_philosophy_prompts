A few months ago I was drawn into a family decision about whether to authorize an experimental cortical prosthesis for a relative with progressive dementia. In the discussions, someone repeatedly invoked philosophical analogies—first the ship of Theseus, then a gradual teletransportation thought experiment—to argue that if functional equivalence is preserved, personal identity is preserved, and therefore the prosthesis poses no special ethical risk. I found myself initially persuaded, but on reflection I realized that my agreement came from tracking a narrative-continuity intuition in the imaginary scenarios, whereas the actual stakes in the clinical case involved discontinuities in subjective experience, legal personhood, and biological vulnerability that the analogies had quietly stripped away. The argument seemed to work not by resolving those practical concerns but by making me feel that they were “the same question” as the analogy, so that answering the analogy felt like answering the case.

This has made me notice a broader pattern in how intuition pumps persuade. There is a move—common in philosophy of mind and ethics—where a thought experiment is constructed so that a specific continuity criterion (functional, material, or narrative) feels salient and appropriate within the imaginary framing. The argument then applies that criterion to a real-world or clinically analogous situation, but in doing so it suppresses other criteria that are actually operative in the practical context. The listener transfers an intuitive judgment from the imaginary case to the real one, not because the same reasoning applies, but because the verbal description of both cases (“the same person,” “the same ship”) collapses them into a single object of evaluation. The result is that one feels one has engaged with the practical problem when one has merely exercised an intuition calibrated to a fictional constraint set.

I have seen this in at least two forms. In one, the classic cell-replacement argument for psychological continuity: we are told that natural cell turnover already makes us “slow teletransporters,” so we should accept that rapid scanning and reconstruction preserves identity. Here, the intuition activated in the imaginary rapid case—discontinuity of first-person narrative—is different from the intuition activated in the natural case—biological process continuity—yet the argument treats them as instances of the same principle. In the other, the prosthesis analogy I mentioned: the ship-of-Theseus framing highlights functional preservation across material change, but in the clinical context the relevant question is whether subjective experience might be interrupted or legally fragmented, concerns the analogy renders invisible by design.

Is there an established name for this particular failure mode—where an intuition pump persuades by activating a criterion appropriate to the fiction and then transferring its verdict to a practical case via shared verbal labeling, while the practical case actually depends on criteria the fiction has excluded? I am not asking about false analogy in general, nor about framing effects in decision theory, nor about motte-and-bailey defenses, since in the cases I have in mind both versions are genuinely believed and the shift is not strategic retreat but an unnoticed change in what is being evaluated. If there is no precise term, I would like one coined with clear boundaries: what distinguishes it from simple equivocation, from analogical reasoning done well, and from cases where the imaginary scenario genuinely illuminates the practical one? In particular, I want to understand whether this is a cognitive bias that can be corrected, or whether it is an inevitable feature of using modal imagination to reason about practical ethics.
