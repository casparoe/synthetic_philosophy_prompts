I am preparing material for a seminar on metaphysics and decision theory, and I have reconstructed the following argument linking supertasks to a collapse of either physical determinism or individual moral responsibility. Each step seems locally defensible, yet the chain arrives at an absurd disjunction—either continuous motion is impossible or collective harm can arise without any individual being culpable—so I am certain a misstep lies hidden within it, though I cannot tell whether the error is an illegitimate analogy, a modal slide, or a quantifier shift masked by the limit construction.

(1) Suppose a lamp is toggled at times t_n = 2 − 2^(1−n) minutes, for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., within a two-minute interval. (2) At the limit time t = 2, the lamp is either on or off. (3) By the toggle rule, each state is the opposite of its immediate predecessor. (4) There is no final toggle; hence there is no immediate predecessor to the state at t = 2 within the sequence. (5) Therefore, the state at t = 2 is not fixed by applying the toggle dynamics to any preceding state. (6) If a macroscopic physical state at a precise time is not fixed by the system’s dynamics, then determinism is violated at that point. (7) But determinism ought not to fail for ordinary macroscopic objects. (8) Hence, the scenario is impossible: discrete supertasks with an accumulation point cannot occur. (9) Now consider continuous motion through space, which involves occupying every point in a continuum with an accumulation point (the endpoint). (10) By direct analogy with (8), if discrete convergence is impossible because no dynamics reach the limit, then traversing infinitely many points should also be impossible. (11) Therefore, motion through continuous space is impossible, and Zeno’s paradox is vindicated rather than dissolved. (12) But motion is manifestly possible, so (10) must be rejected. (13) Rejecting (10) requires accepting that a system can have a well-defined “limit state” that is not inherited from any finite stage. (14) Apply this to collective action: if each agent in an infinite chain can pass a burden to the next without individual failure, the total harm at the limit is still catastrophic, yet no individual act caused it. (15) Thus, if limit properties are genuine, collective moral failure can arise without any individual being culpable, undermining the principle that moral obligation requires individual causal efficacy. (16) So we must either deny motion (by keeping (10)) or deny that individual responsibility requires individual causation (by accepting (15)).

Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? I should prefer to retain (13) and therefore reject (10), but I want to know whether that commitment genuinely forces me into (15), or whether there is a more local repair—perhaps in (6) or in the analogy between physical dynamics and moral aggregation—that saves both motion and individual responsibility. Please answer in British English, with explicit reference to the step numbers, and say which premise you would least wish to surrender if you had to choose.
