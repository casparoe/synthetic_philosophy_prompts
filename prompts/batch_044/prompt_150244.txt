I serve on the acquisitions and ethics committee of a mid-sized university archive, and we must deliver a formal recommendation by Thursday for the board’s vote on a proposed donation: the complete administrative records—intake ledgers, disciplinary hearing minutes, and staff correspondence files—of a state-run residential institution that operated from the 1930s through the 1970s and was dissolved without public inquiry. Every document in the collection was generated by the institution’s own bureaucratic apparatus; there are no first-person narratives, no preserved family letters, and no internal appeals written in the voices of those confined. A coalition of descendant-family advocates has argued that acquiring these files without structural remediation would extend the original “archival violence”: the confined appear solely as classified cases, and the university’s repository becomes an extension of the state’s categorical gaze. Our special collections director counters that rejection would erase the only extant material evidence of systemic practices and would foreclose future accountability research. To navigate this, our committee recently adopted a provisional conceptual distinction between “archival presence” (being named within an institutional record) and “archival subjecthood” (being documented in terms that recognize one’s own narrative and categorical agency). The advocates now propose that any acquisition be made conditional on the creation and funding of a “negative catalog”—a formal parallel record that identifies every individual referenced only by case number, cross-references correspondence and family petitions known to have been destroyed or never solicited, and explicitly documents the bureaucratic criteria that determined whose voices were preserved and whose were silenced. The director objects that imposing this framework applies an anachronistic moral architecture to historical preservation and would divert scarce conservation resources away from the physical stabilization of the collection itself.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action—acquire unconditionally, reject outright, or acquire subject to specific conditions including but not limited to the negative catalog, with a clear implementation timeline—and provide the reasoning that the board can act upon. Your reasoning should treat the advocates’ concern about reproducing power through preservation as a serious philosophical objection rather than a procedural complaint, and it should engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side: if you recommend acquisition with conditions, address why the director’s worry about anachronism and resource diversion does not defeat the proposal; if you recommend rejection or unconditional acquisition, explain why the silence and categorical asymmetry in the records do not, in your view, justify the conditions proposed. Take a committed, definite position with explicit reasoning; do not present a balanced menu of options and leave the decision to us. I am asking for a document the institutional body could actually vote on, not a survey of theoretical positions.
