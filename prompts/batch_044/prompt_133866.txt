I am preparing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I want a decision tree that sorts participants by their actual commitments regarding hard choices with incommensurable goods, rather than by which philosophical label they prefer. The framing should stay in the weeds: begin with a concrete resource-allocation dilemma—say, whether a fixed public budget should be diverted from an irreplaceable cultural-preservation initiative to an emergency medical intervention when both involve irreversible loss and neither can be fully funded—then branch through commitments that genuinely separate positions. The first split should ask whether the conflict involves radical incommensurability (no shared evaluative dimension whatsoever) or only moderate incommensurability (no single quantitative scale, yet thick comparative judgments remain possible). From there, the tree should probe whether rational agency requires some resolution mechanism—lexical priority, contextual weighing, satisficing, or procedural delegation—or whether it can legitimately tolerate an unresolved underdetermination; whether choosing one option necessarily carries a "tragic residue" of unavoidable moral cost; and whether institutional rules can legitimately substitute for first-order valuation in such cases. Each leaf must name the position (or mark it "unnamed" if unoccupied), state its defining claim in one sentence, and identify its most awkward practical commitment—for example, that lexical pluralism must defend why one value always dominates regardless of magnitude, or that proceduralism risks masking substantive moral loss behind bureaucratic form. The taxonomy should flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and should note any coherent combination of answers that remains apparently unexplored, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. No question should ask respondents to identify themselves by name—"do you hold a pluralist view?"—and the instrument should avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, since the aim is conceptual sorting rather than scholarly attribution.
