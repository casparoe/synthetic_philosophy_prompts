The Gould–Lewontin critique of the adaptationist programme has circulated for over four decades, yet evolutionary biology still cycles through the same standoff: genome-scale studies claim to partition adaptive from non-adaptive sequence, and critics reply that the statistical models already presuppose the distinction they claim to measure. I want a diagnosis of why this particular disagreement has stalled, together with an honest assessment of whether my framing of it is confused.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not mainly empirical—there is no hidden dataset that would settle it—but conceptual and methodological, with both sides using the same vocabulary to ask different questions. One camp treats an adaptive explanation as a historical claim about the selective regime that originally constructed a trait; the other treats it as a functional claim about the trait's present contribution to fitness, regardless of evolutionary origin. At the same time, the category of "spandrel" has fragmented: some biologists use it for any feature not directly shaped by selection, while others reserve it for structurally necessary byproducts of other adaptations—like the triangular spaces between arches in San Marco—thereby excluding mere evolutionary accidents or exaptations. Meanwhile, ideas that have entered the literature more recently—constructive neutral evolution, evolutionary capacitance, and the distinction between exaptation and spandrel—suggest that non-adaptive origins and later functional co-option are routine rather than exceptional. If we adopted a stricter taxonomy that separated spandrels (developmentally entrenched architectural side-effects) from exaptations (traits that acquired new functions after originating for different reasons or for none) and from capacitance (cryptic variation revealed only under stress), would the original debate dissolve, or would it simply relocate to disputes about developmental entrenchment?
