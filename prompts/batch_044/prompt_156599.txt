You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A after a workshop presentation on democratic legitimacy and religious diversity. The speaker is defending the thesis that Rawls’s “proviso” — standardly read as requiring religious citizens eventually to translate their political arguments into shared public reasons — applies solely to institutional justification (the reasoning that grounds coercive legislation) and not to advocacy within civil society. They distinguish sharply between what they call the “forum of justification” and the “forum of advocacy,” arguing that religious arguments offered in the latter never breach the duty of civility, since that duty regulates only the former. The speaker further claims that this reading resolves a tension in Rawls’s corpus between the comprehensive liberalism of A Theory of Justice and the political liberalism of Political Liberalism, by allowing religious comprehensive doctrines full expression in democratic culture without threatening the legitimacy of basic institutions.

The audience comprises: (i) two political philosophers who are committed defenders of the standard Rawlsian interpretation and regard the proviso as binding on citizens in the “public political forum”; (ii) one critical theorist sympathetic to deliberative democracy but sceptical of public reason as an exclusionary mechanism; (iii) a constitutional lawyer whose work centres on religious accommodation within liberal legal frameworks; and (iv) an empirical political scientist who studies whether deliberative forums actually achieve mutual justification in practice.

Please draft the speaker’s defence preparation sheet. Order the objections roughly as they will arise in the session, phrased in the vocabulary and framing each interlocutor would use. For each objection, indicate whether it targets the conceptual distinction, the textual interpretation of Rawls, or the normative upshot; note precisely what the speaker should concede, what they must hold, and where their reply is weakest. Include the objection the speaker is hoping nobody asks — namely, that the distinction collapses because sustained advocacy in civil society inevitably shapes legislative agendas, thereby smuggling untranslated religious reasons into the forum of justification. Provide brief replies suitable for a two- to three-minute exchange, with a note on the softest point in each. Finally, propose one concrete, original test case — not drawn from the existing literature the speaker relies upon — in which religious advocacy is explicitly organised to pressure legislative change, and ask what the speaker’s distinction predicts about whether the duty of civility has been violated.
