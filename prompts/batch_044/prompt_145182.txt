In a philosophy-of-AI reading group, Aria and Ben argued over whether Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem applies to large language models. Aria claimed that if a model’s reasoning is fully captured by a consistent formal system S strong enough for arithmetic, then S cannot prove its own consistency; hence no such model can ever furnish an internal, formal guarantee that it will not eventually generate contradictions. Ben replied that this misapplies the theorem: LLMs do not derive theorems inside S but predict tokens via statistical weights, so Gödel—who concerns formal derivability—simply has no purchase on their operation. Aria countered that even if inference is statistical, any complete formal specification of the architecture and rules is still a formal system, and it is that specification which would need to certify consistency. Ben said that confuses the description of a system with the system being a formal theory in Gödel’s sense; he added that humans also cannot prove Peano arithmetic consistent within PA yet reason consistently, so the limitation is not distinctive to machines. Aria responded that humans escape the loop by appealing to semantic intuition or stronger meta-systems, whereas a model is architecturally confined to its specification. Ben said that presupposes an unverified empirical claim about cognition—namely that human reasoning is not itself a formal system at a higher level—and that Gödel’s result licenses no such conclusion about minds or machines. They asked me to referee: point by point, who is right about what, where each argument overreaches, and what both sides overlook about what Gödel’s theorems actually do and do not show outside mathematics.
