Read Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go through the lens of the hedonistic imperative, the project of using biotechnology to abolish suffering by recalibrating hedonic set-points and eliminating the biological substrate of pain. In the novel, biotechnology produces human clones for organ harvesting; the clones possess full emotional and artistic lives, yet their suffering is structurally necessary to prolong the lives of the non-clone population. Do not treat the novel as a generic dystopia about exploitation. Instead, examine it as an inverted hedonistic imperative: rather than engineering suffering away, the society engineers suffering into a subclass and distributes its benefits upward. Concentrate on the Hailsham scenes that emphasize art and creativity as evidence of an inner life, and on the donation process that ends in death. Does the clones' refined capacity for emotional suffering undermine the hedonistic imperative's premise that suffering is a biological glitch to be technically eliminated? Or does the novel demonstrate that suffering is inseparable from the very capacities—love, artistic aspiration, narrative identity—that a project of genetic hedonic calibration might wish to preserve? Be precise about where the metaphor of paradise engineering breaks down when applied to Ishiguro's world, and identify what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about biotechnology and well-being that a direct argument could not supply.
