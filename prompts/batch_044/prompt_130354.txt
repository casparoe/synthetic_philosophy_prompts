I recently had a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about which national myths built around defeat are morally salvageable and which are irredeemably toxic. She was quite firm on four cases. She holds that the American Confederacy’s Lost Cause narrative is poisonous and ought to be retired from public commemoration; that the Masada story of the Zealots’ final stand against Rome is a legitimate and noble myth of collective dignity; that the Serbian Kosovo Polje myth is dangerous because it sustains irredentist grievance and should be actively demythologized; and that the British “Dunkirk spirit” is benign, even healthy, as a narrative of democratic resilience rather than revanchism. I think she is running at least two different principles that happen to coincide on these four, but I cannot tell which one governs her judgment.

To test her consistency, I proposed a hypothetical but structurally realistic case: in 1961, citizens of a divided capital marched unarmed toward a fortified barrier, suffered casualties, and were turned back; the barrier eventually fell in 1989 through peaceful transition, and the state now teaches the 1961 event as proof that patient, non-violent persistence eventually prevails, without calling for any reversal of the 1989 settlement. At the same time, the official narrative assigns central blame to a local commander who allegedly betrayed the marchers to the central authorities, and a present-day separatist movement uses precisely that betrayal to argue that the post-1989 political order is illegitimate because it derives from the original treason.

Given her four earlier verdicts, do you think she would judge this new myth redeemable or toxic? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the underlying rules: what are the two or three most plausible principles that jointly fit her judgments on the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk, what does each principle predict about this 1961 case, and which prediction should I expect if I want to remain consistent with her track record? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would she be implicitly adopting with each possible choice? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—described precisely enough that no outside research is needed—that would best distinguish the leading rules from one another.
