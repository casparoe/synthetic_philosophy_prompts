Following a recent conversation with my physician about predictive risk algorithms that assign specific clinical endpoints to defined time windows, I have been returning to the parable of Death in Damascus—the servant who, upon seeing Death make a threatening gesture in Baghdad, borrows a horse and rides to Damascus to escape him, only to learn that Death was surprised to find him in Baghdad because their appointment was in Damascus that evening. In clinical ethics rounds, colleagues invoke this story as an analogy for algorithmic prognosis: the model, like Death, merely registers an inevitable endpoint rather than causing it, so that attempts to alter care pathways or relocate treatment are as futile as the flight. Please audit this analogy precisely: map the explicit correspondences (the flight, the appointment, the prediction, the outcome, and the agent of inevitability); distinguish disanalogies that are merely decorative from those that undermine the ethical conclusion that intervention is pointless; examine whether the comparison smuggles in logical fatalism—treating a predictive correlation as a modal necessity—while obscuring the causal structure of clinical decision-making; and assess whether a different analogy, perhaps drawn from meteorological forecasting rather than narrative destiny, could preserve predictive insight without importing determinism.
