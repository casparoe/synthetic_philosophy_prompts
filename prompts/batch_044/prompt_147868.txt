A recent popular essay summarizes Parfit's teletransporter argument as follows. Parfit is said to begin by assuming that personal identity requires continuous physical brain tissue. He then imagines a scanner that destroys your brain, transmits the information to Mars, and reconstructs an exact molecular replica. Because the replica remembers your life and behaves as you do, the essay claims, physical continuity is shown to be irrelevant and psychological continuity takes over entirely. The essay defines "Relation R"—the relation that actually matters—as a direct mental link whose strength can be graded continuously, like temperature or mass, so that psychological continuity comes in measurable degrees. Applying this to a branching case, where two replicas are constructed, the essay argues that each bears Relation R to the original, but since identity is strictly one-to-one, neither can be identical to you. The author therefore concludes that Parfit shows there is no determinate fact about survival in branching; instead, you survive only "partially"—for instance, half in each replica—because Relation R divides like a physical substance. From this, the essay infers a reductionist view of the self: there is no underlying substance or owner of mental events, only a bundle of experiences linked by stronger or weaker connections, and asking "will I survive?" is therefore as empty as asking whether a social club survives the complete replacement of its members. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate to Parfit's view, what is a standard simplification, and what is a misrepresentation—especially regarding whether Relation R is quantitative in the way described, whether branching produces partial survival or rather demonstrates that identity is not what matters, and whether the reductionist conclusion about the self follows from the teletransporter premises alone. Also, assess whether the essay's final inference—that the question of survival becomes meaningless—actually follows from the corrected premises.
