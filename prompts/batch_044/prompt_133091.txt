A fellow doctoral student in philosophy of technology proposed the following argument to me, and I find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the coherence of our field. (1) Define a problem as unitary if success on any proper part does not constitute partial progress toward the whole, and as composite if partial successes accumulate independently. (2) The alignment problem—ensuring AI systems pursue intended objectives rather than proxies or misinterpretations—is either unitary or composite. (3) If it is unitary, then an advance in value specification without corresponding success in scalable oversight or robustness is not partial alignment but a different achievement altogether; yet researchers routinely treat such advances as genuine progress, which is rational only if the problem is composite. (4) If it is composite, then value specification, robustness to distributional shift, and scalable oversight share no essential feature that makes them all instances of alignment rather than separate engineering challenges; the phrase "the alignment problem" functions merely as an umbrella term, like "the science problem." (5) But if (4) holds, then asking whether we have solved the alignment problem is conceptually empty, analogous to asking whether we have solved science. (6) Researchers clearly treat "the alignment problem" as a substantive singular question, not an empty label. (7) Therefore the alignment problem is unitary. (8) Yet if it is unitary, then (3) applies: since full success is absent, there has been no partial progress at all, which flatly contradicts the observable research trajectory. (9) Hence the alignment problem is neither unitary nor composite; it must be a pseudo-problem generated by an ambiguous use of "alignment." I want to resist (9). I am inclined to keep (6) and the intuition that specification advances are real, if incomplete, progress. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
