Write the full list of desiderata that an adequate normative theory of constitutional precommitment must meet, where precommitment is understood not merely as rational self-binding in the Ulysses-contract sense but as the democratic practice of entrenching provisions that constrain future majorities. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual tension behind it: for example, the theory must explain asymmetric entrenchment, in which some constitutional clauses are amendable by ordinary legislative majorities while others require supermajoritarian or multistage procedures, and it must show whether this asymmetry tracks a morally relevant distinction or merely reflects contingent historical path-dependency. It must also handle intergenerational moral risk, the problem that a present generation may lock in governance structures for long-range problems such as climate or technological regulation that may become obsolete or actively harmful under conditions the present generation cannot foresee. Include a requirement addressing the reverse-Ulysses scenario, in which a future majority wishes to preserve a constraint that the present generation seeks to remove, and state whether the theory treats temporal direction as morally asymmetrical or applies the same legitimacy conditions regardless of which generation initiates the binding. The theory must distinguish legitimate precommitment from illegitimate domination of future citizens, and it must respect the distinction between having the authority to bind and having the authority to bind in perpetuity. Identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, particularly between credible long-term commitment and democratic revisability, and note whether this tension is a fatal flaw or a trade-off that must be managed. Briefly indicate how the two broad families of account—those that treat constitutions as pre-political contracts that establish the rules of democratic engagement, and those that treat them as revisable frameworks for ongoing democratic deliberation—fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than on scoring the competitors. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the evaluation should rest on conceptual coherence and the coverage of cases.
