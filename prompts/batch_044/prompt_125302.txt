Write a poem in exactly five numbered stanzas, each stanza consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Take up Hume’s “Of the Standard of Taste” and its central loop: beauty is not in the object but in sentiment, yet we correct sentiment by appealing to an ideal critic who possesses delicacy, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice—only to recognize that critic by his agreement with the judgments he was meant to correct. Make the loop structural by letting the opening line of stanza one return, altered, as the closing line of stanza five, so the poem’s form enacts the circular definition; let the middle stanzas move through the subjectivity of taste, the attempt to derive a general rule from particular agreements, and the hidden dependence of the standard on the very sentiments it adjudicates. Paraphrase Hume’s conditions rather than quoting them, rendering them accurately enough that a reader familiar with the essay recognizes the four marks of the true judge without encountering them as a list, and leave the tension open—do not resolve whether the standard is genuinely external or merely the circle of taste closing on itself.
