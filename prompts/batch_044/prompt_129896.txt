I direct a grant program that funds joint empirical research between groups that are publicly and sharply opposed on high-stakes policy questions—for example, collaborations between climate economists who favor carbon pricing and community organizers who oppose those instruments on equity and procedural grounds, or partnerships between AI safety researchers advocating pre-deployment evaluation mandates and open-source advocates who treat such mandates as regulatory capture by incumbents. Before committing funds, my board needs a reusable decision procedure to determine whether both sides are arguing in good faith in a sense strong enough to make collaboration intellectually productive rather than merely performative. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical distinction between doxastic openness—genuine willingness to revise one’s beliefs in light of evidence—and dialectical openness—willingness to represent the opposing view in terms that its own proponents would recognize as accurate—while also accounting for the structural problem that echo chambers and institutional filtering can produce sincere participants who nevertheless systematically misrepresent counterevidence and opposing arguments. Please construct a step-by-step checklist that my reviewers, who are not philosophers, can run in approximately thirty minutes per proposal; specify the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the explicit stopping points where the procedure returns “do not fund,” and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be referred to a senior board member for a values-based sign-off. The procedure must handle the hard case in which one side is individually sincere but embedded in an institutional environment that filters out disconfirming information—what might be called structural bad faith—and must include two worked applications: first, to a proposed collaboration between carbon-tax proponents and their equity-focused critics, and second, to a proposed partnership between AI alignment researchers and open-source advocates. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the board will find unwelcome, and conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot determine, such as whether good faith guarantees productive outcomes or whether hidden strategic motives are present.
