I am a senior undergraduate in a seminar on ancient moral philosophy and the uses of biography, and I am preparing an interview for our department podcast with an invited speaker—Dr. Samuel Okonkwo—who has recently advanced a defense of what he calls hagiographic pedagogy. Okonkwo argues that Plutarch’s Parallel Lives should be read not as flawed historiography but as a deliberate moral technology: he claims Plutarch practices characterological magnification, selectively amplifying virtues and vices so that the reader can achieve proximate exemplarity, a form of moral learning that requires aspirational identification rather than balanced judgment. He further holds that the synkrisis sections do not aim at neutral historical evaluation but generate productive moral tension through asymmetrical pairing, in which the Greek and Roman figures are not equivalents but foils calibrated to different ethical failures.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press the actual content of this position rather than asking for biography, overview, or methodological preliminaries. The tone should be formal and appropriate for an academic colloquium.

I want at least one question addressing the epistemic danger that a learner might internalize a distorted portrait as factual belief about a historical agent; include a follow-up anticipating Okonkwo’s likely reply that moral knowledge and historical knowledge have different validation conditions, and press whether that distinction collapses when the learner cannot separate the two.

I also want at least one question on Plutarch’s well-known distinction between writing lives and writing histories—specifically, whether that distinction licenses the kind of selective distortion Okonkwo defends, or whether it merely marks a genre boundary without normative weight. Include a follow-up that challenges him if he appeals to authorial intention.

Another required cluster: ask how this model survives modern skepticism about hero-worship and the great-man theory of history—particularly whether magnifying individual character obscures structural and institutional conditions of virtue. Anticipate that he might respond by saying biography is micro-ethical rather than macro-political, and prepare a follow-up that presses whether that separation is stable once the magnified life is offered as a normative model.

Finally, include a question about vice: if hagiographic pedagogy magnifies virtue for identification, must it also magnify vice in the paired figure to maintain narrative coherence, and does that risk making vice aesthetically attractive or morally contagious? Give a follow-up keyed to his probable claim that vice in Plutarch functions as a cautionary mirror rather than a seductive model.

Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references. The questions should assume familiarity with Plutarch’s paired structure and with contemporary debates about moral exemplars, but should not depend on knowledge of obscure texts beyond what a student in this course would have encountered.
