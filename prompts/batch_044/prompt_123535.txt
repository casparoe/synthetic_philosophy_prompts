I have been asked to deliver the retirement toast for a longtime newspaper editor at a dinner attended by her colleagues, several philosophy students who know Kierkegaard's writings on the press, and family members with no academic background. I am not a scholar; I worked under her for a decade and have been turning over in my mind his idea that the modern press manufactures an abstraction called the public—a faceless, collective entity that allows real individuals to speak loudly while escaping personal accountability. Her career, as I see it, was a sustained refusal of that abstraction: she always signed her editorials with her full name, answered critical letters personally rather than through institutional replies, and never addressed her readers as an anonymous mass.

I want the toast to take this idea seriously as its structural backbone, not merely mention it in passing. That means I must avoid speaking in the voice of the public myself: no sweeping pronouncements about society, the culture, or all of us, and no appeal to an invisible audience. Instead, I should speak as one particular person to the other particular people present, perhaps naming two or three guests directly and addressing the retiree by name throughout. To sharpen the point without turning the event into a seminar, I also want to reference a specific feature of the venue: the organizers have installed an anonymous digital message board where attendees can post congratulations without signing their names. I see this board as a contemporary illustration of the very problem—responsibility dissolved into an abstract crowd—and I want the toast to acknowledge it briefly, then contrast it with her practice of named, direct reply.

The speech should last about five minutes, honor her without false sentiment, and work for both the specialists and the relatives without ever citing a text, asking for further reading, or using jargon that requires explanation. Most importantly, the Kierkegaardian distinction should shape what I can and cannot say from the podium: if I fall back on broad generalizations about the media or the age we live in, the toast will betray the very virtue it is meant to celebrate.
