In anthropic reasoning, the dispute between defenders of the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption has persisted for decades with remarkably little convergence, despite both camps working from identical cosmological models and the same formal probability calculus. The stalemate is sharpest around arguments of the presumptuous-philosopher kind: one side treats the vastness of the cosmos, and the corresponding abundance of possible observers, as strong evidence against theories that permit enormous populations; the other treats our own existence as boosting precisely those theories that make observers plentiful. Yet neither camp seems able to move the other, and standard surveys only rehearse the same exchanges without explaining why they stall.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely about probability calculus at all, but about a hidden normative question—whether an observer is permitted to treat her own existence as a neutral datum or must treat it as an evidential token that favours populous worlds. But I am unsure whether that diagnosis holds, or whether there is instead an unacknowledged formal divergence about how reference classes are individuated, or whether indexical information can ever be probabilistically irrelevant.

Imagine a cosmological model in which conscious observers exist in two epistemically isolated partitions: Type-A observers have direct access to cosmological data about the size and structure of the universe, whereas Type-B observers lack any such access, yet both partitions are equally populated and both contain reasoners applying anthropic reasoning. If a Type-B reasoner applies the self-sampling assumption strictly within her own partition, she reaches one conclusion about the expected total number of observers; if she applies the self-indication assumption across both partitions regardless of epistemic access, she reaches another. The disagreement between the two assumptions might therefore turn on whether the correct reference class is defined by epistemic accessibility, by physical instantiation, or by some further criterion neither side has made explicit.
