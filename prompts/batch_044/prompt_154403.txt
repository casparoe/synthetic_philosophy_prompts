I have been observing a recurring pattern in modern knowledge institutions—universities, hospitals, and research labs—that I do not have a precise name for, and I want to know whether the literature has carved it out already.

Here is what I see, described concretely. An organization introduces a transparency or accountability mechanism—say, public productivity dashboards, open peer-review metrics, or real-time impact tracking—originally justified as a way to correct hidden bias or inefficiency. At first, staff simply adjust their behavior to score well on the metric. But then something further happens: the activities that are easily measured are gradually reclassified as the legitimate core of the profession, while the activities that resist quantification—exploratory failure, informal mentoring, clinical judgment that depends on tacit knowledge—are redefined as ancillary, personal, or even unprofessional. The hidden work does not disappear; it migrates into private conversations, off-hours labor, and unrecorded spaces. Crucially, the institution then treats its own blindness in these hidden domains as a sign of rigor—as if the absence of data proves that nothing important is happening there. The result is not merely distorted incentives but an ontological inversion: the visible layer is taken for reality, and reality is relegated to the shadows.

This feels adjacent to several things I can name—Goodhart's Law, the sociology of audit culture, and Foucault's account of the examination and disciplinary visibility—but none of them quite captures the specific move I am describing: the redefinition of the profession's core ontology through the metric, combined with the institutional interpretation of its own blind spots as virtues.
