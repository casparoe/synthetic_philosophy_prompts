As a journalist covering science and philosophy, I have been tracking a curious institutional split in how climate and environmental agencies treat predictive modeling. For adaptation policy, these institutions now routinely reject any single best-guess forecast and instead require ensemble robustness: strategies must perform adequately across dozens of structurally different simulations, with no one model treated as the true description of a future state. Success is measured by decision-relevant stability rather than by correspondence to an independent reality. At the same time, the funding bodies that support these models evaluate them by comparing outputs to historical temperature records, treating discrepancies as failures of representation that must be corrected so the model can mirror nature more accurately. What philosophical commitments about scientific knowledge, truth, and representation does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does the policy-facing half tacitly adopt an antirepresentationalist, neopragmatist stance in the vein of Rorty, treating models as tools for coping rather than mirrors of an intrinsic reality? Does the validation half retain a representationalist framework that assumes there is a correct fact of the matter about past temperatures that the model should approximate? Reconstruct the implicit theory of scientific knowledge that allows both practices to coexist within the same institution, distinguish what the split strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and determine whether the two halves are philosophically consistent or whether holding them together demands that one framework be abandoned.
