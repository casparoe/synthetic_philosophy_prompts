Across most jurisdictions, criminal perjury is narrowly defined: it requires a statement made under oath that the speaker believes to be false. A witness who answers every question with technically accurate but selectively arranged information, thereby inducing a false overall impression, usually escapes liability. By contrast, professional disciplinary codes for scientists, journalists, and auditors frequently treat such selective framing as a serious breach, often equivalent to fabrication. Meanwhile, consumer-protection and contract law regularly prohibit ‘misleading’ commercial behaviour even when no individual utterance is literally false.

What philosophical commitments about language, belief, and moral responsibility does this institutional divergence presuppose? Must one hold that lying violates a specific commitment internal to the speech-act of asserting, whereas misleading exploits a structural gap in that commitment? Or can the divergence be explained without any deep conceptual distinction — for example, by differences in evidentiary tractability, or in the state’s legitimate interest in coercing truthful testimony?

Consider further: if the fundamental wrong of deception is not the utterance of a false proposition but the manipulation of another’s belief-forming processes, does the law’s exclusive focus on literal falsity become philosophically incoherent? And is there a hidden tension in a system that punishes negligent misstatement in tort, yet excuses deliberate but technically truthful misdirection in the witness box?

Reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — that render this pattern consistent. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and indicate what would have to change in legal or professional ethics if the underlying view of deception were replaced by one that treats misleading and lying as morally equivalent.
