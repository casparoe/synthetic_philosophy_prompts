In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind we have been working through Dennett’s contrast between original and derived intentionality—particularly the “two-bitser” case, in which a minimal representational system acquires content only through the interpretive practices of its designers, carrying no intrinsic aboutness of its own—set against Searle’s argument that syntax is never sufficient for semantics and that original intentionality requires a biological, conscious substrate. A student recently observed that our university’s AI ethics oversight protocol seems to straddle this divide in a way that is rarely made explicit. Specifically, the board’s technical documentation requirements mandate that researchers describe large language models and autonomous planning systems using full-blooded intentional vocabulary: the system believes its outputs are truthful, desires to minimize user frustration, intends a particular sequence of actions, and so forth. These are not presented as metaphorical shortcuts but as necessary transparency disclosures. At the same time, the board’s liability and moral-agency framework explicitly denies that any such system possesses consciousness, subjective experience, or original intentionality, and it assigns all legal and ethical responsibility back to the human operators and designers.

What philosophical commitments about the ontology and function of derived intentionality does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it require a Dennett-style view that derived intentionality can be functionally and explanatorily robust—sufficient to ground predictive and normative discourse—without ever graduating into original intentionality? Or could a Searlean treat it consistently by holding that all such attributions are purely observer-relative heuristics with no ontological weight? Furthermore, is the practice internally coherent: can an institution genuinely require intentional descriptions as epistemically indispensable for governance while maintaining that nothing in the system has the kind of intentionality that could ground moral responsibility, and if so, does that commit the institution to an intermediate status for derived intentionality—something more than a fiction but less than original aboutness?
