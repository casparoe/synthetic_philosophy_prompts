I am writing to ask for your help in adjudicating a disagreement that arose between two of my classmates, Ravi and Nina, during a sixth-form philosophy discussion on moral responsibility for collective harms. I was present but did not take sides, and I should be grateful if you could analyse their exchange point by point, indicate who is right about each specific claim, identify what both parties are missing, and conclude with an overall assessment of whose position is stronger. Please provide your answer in British English.

The disagreement concerned whether individual actions—specifically voting in large-scale elections, avoiding meat, and reducing one's carbon footprint—can be morally required when each action appears causally inefficacious.

Ravi began by arguing that in cases such as climate change, factory farming, and national elections, the contribution of any single person is so minute that it makes no detectable difference to the outcome. He claimed that if an action cannot change whether a harm occurs, then blaming or obliging individuals to act differently is irrational. He added that this is not mere cynicism but a logical point about causation: moral responsibility typically requires that one's conduct actually makes a difference, and here it does not.

Nina replied that Ravi was conflating causal efficacy with moral complicity. She said that participating in a harmful collective practice can be wrong even when one's withdrawal does not stop the practice, because the wrong lies in being part of the collective mechanism rather than in being the decisive cause. She pointed to voting as an example: even though one vote almost never decides an election, democratic citizenship involves an expressive duty to contribute to collective decision-making, not merely a strategic calculation of influence.

Ravi then introduced what he called the 'non-identity problem' applied to climate ethics. He argued that if he chooses to live a carbon-intensive life, the particular future people who suffer as a result are not the same individuals who would have existed had he lived differently, because different reproductive choices and timelines would have occurred. Therefore, he said, he cannot be said to harm specific future people, and the harm-based argument for individual climate action collapses.

Nina responded that the non-identity problem might undermine harm-based reasoning about distant future generations, but it does not apply to present suffering—such as that of animals currently in factory farms—where the victims exist independently of his choice. She also said that Ravi's focus on causation ignored the 'market signalling' effect of individual consumption choices: even if one skipped burger does not alter supply, widespread patterns of refusal shift demand curves and can influence institutional behaviour over time.

Ravi countered that market signalling is also diluted to the point of inefficacy at the individual level. He noted that supply chains operate on macro-level forecasting, so one person's abstention is statistically invisible and produces no evidential value for producers. He then proposed a more general principle: if an obligation requires action that is both causally and evidentially inert, then the obligation reduces to a purely symbolic gesture, and morality should not demand symbolic gestures unless they serve some further purpose—such as cultivating virtue—which he said was not established in this case.

Nina concluded by distinguishing between 'outcome responsibility' and 'participatory responsibility'. She argued that even when outcomes are overdetermined, refusing to participate expresses a rejection of the collective wrong and maintains one's moral integrity. She admitted that this does not solve the inefficacy problem entirely, but she maintained that moral life is not solely about optimising consequences.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about what, explain what both are missing, and tell me who is more right overall.
