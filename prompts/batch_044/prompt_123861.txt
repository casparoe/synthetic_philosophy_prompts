You are a senior philosopher of value theory and an experienced seminar facilitator with particular expertise in ethics, metaphysics, and pedagogical design. Before you propose anything concrete, please think carefully and work through a brief internal brainstorming stage: map out the exact phenomenological arc you want students to experience—first the failure of comparative ranking, then the forced moment of commitment under parity, then the distinctive cost of reversing that commitment, which is not ordinary regret but a kind of normative self-fracture. Once that arc is clear in your mind, design a fully runnable classroom activity.

The setting is a closed graduate seminar room with 12 advanced philosophy students, one 90-minute session, movable seating, paper, markers, and no digital devices. The conceptual target is incommensurable values and hard choices. The idea must emerge through what students do, not through any opening lecture; by the end they should feel that rational choice does not collapse into maximization, yet it is not arbitrary whim either—there is a structured experience of commitment that generates reasons.

To keep the prompt meaningful without relying on external lookup, I am injecting a specific niche framing for you to build around: call the scenario the Archive of Unmeasured Goods. The case involves a single scarce public building that can become only one of four things: a preserved historical monument (continuity), affordable housing (distributive justice), an ecological restoration site (repair), or an open deliberative commons (democratic presence). These four dimensions are not weighted; they are irreducible.

Design the activity with these mechanics:
- Assign each student a visible role (e.g., steward of one dimension) and, secretly, one hidden “threshold card” that grants a silent veto if that student’s dimension is entirely eliminated. Only two students hold cards; neither knows the other’s identity unless a disclosure rule is triggered.
- Provide one “parity token” to the group, playable exactly once by any student. When played, it formally declares that two remaining options stand on a par—neither better, worse, nor equal—without resolving the choice.
- Prohibit all numerical ranking, cost-benefit language, and trade-off calculus. If students invoke comparison, they must articulate it in purely qualitative terms; the facilitator should note that such articulations typically collapse or stall.
- The group must reach a unanimous collective decision within 30 minutes of structured deliberation.

Your deliverable should include:
1. Setup and role assignment, including the hidden cards and the parity token.
2. The rules of deliberation, the disclosure mechanism, and the prohibition on common metrics.
3. A precise 90-minute timeline.
4. A debrief protocol that maps specific moments in the deliberation onto philosophical distinctions—incommensurability versus incomparability, parity versus equality, commitment-generated reasons versus discovered reasons, and the specific cost of reversal.
5. An honest note on which feature of hard-choice theory the physical activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in closing remarks—specifically, the phenomenological sense that the agent herself becomes the source of normative authority through commitment.
6. A predictive failure analysis: what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., students smuggling in implicit metrics, hoarding the parity token, reaching premature consensus) and how to correct it without breaking the immersive illusion.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask students to research specific authors or texts. Write so that everything required is contained in the scenario and the rules you invent. Start with your brief internal brainstorming note, then present the full activity design clearly and formally.
