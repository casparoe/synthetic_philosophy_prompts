I was arguing with my friends last night about Hofstadter's later idea — the one where he says your 'I' isn't really stuck inside one brain, it's more like a pattern that can be copied or shared across other minds if the causal structure is close enough. I want to push this further than Hofstadter maybe does: I'm defending the view that if enough of your strange loop gets reconstructed in someone else (through deep shared memory, behavoral resonance, emotional mirroring, whatever), then there isn't just 'influence' — there's literally a distributed self with multiple centers. The original brain dying doesn't kill the pattern if it's genuinely sustained elsewhere. It's not about immortality, it's about rejecting the axiom that one skull equals one I.

But I want to know what would actually defeat this, not just the obvious 'that sounds like sci-fi.' Give me the serious defeaters: maybe neuroscience showing that phenomenal ownership requires specific intact thalamocortical loops that can't be distributed, or philosophical arguments that self-reference needs a singular locus of agency, or split-brain cases where we see two selves instead of one distributed one. Which ones should a committed distributed-self theorist concede as damaging, and which can they dismiss? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every failure of distribution gets explained away as 'insufficient fidelity' — and is that a problem or just how pattern-identity works?

I'm also stuck on boundaries. If my partner remembers all my stories and reacts with my exact habits, is that hosting 10% of my loop, or literally zero? What observation — maybe about grief responses in people who carry parts of dead partners, or about error-correction in self-models — would force me to say distribution is only metaphorical? Rank the defeaters: fatal, awkward, or just requiring me to tighten the fidelity conditions. I want an honest map of how exposed this position really is.
