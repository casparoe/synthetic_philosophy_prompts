I've been reading about teh sunk cost fallacy since I retired from biochemistry, and I'm frustated by how the debate hasn't budged. The economists say honouring sunk costs is always irrational — only future payoffs matter. The philosophers reply that abandoning a long project can wreck the coherence of your commitments. Both sides accept the same utility maths and the usual examples — concert tickets, failed drug trials — yet nobody shifts. My working theory is that they aren't disagreeing about rationality proper, but about whether reasons can be backward-looking in a binding way, not just sentimental nostalgia. Is this a hidden factual question about agent-identity over time, a verbal muddle over 'rational' (local maximisation vs life-plan consistency), or bedrock intuition? And if it's substantive, could a formal model of diachronic commitment, or maybe a psychological result on narrative identity, actually move it? I want a diagnosis using what each side actually says, plus an honest view on whether progress is possible.
