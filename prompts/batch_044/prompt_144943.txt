I'm delivering the opening address at the inaugural symposium of the Institute for Philosophy and Machine Learning. The audience is split: half are ML engineers and interpretability researchers who work with feature visualizations, probing classifiers, and causal interventions; half are philosophers of mind and science who work with intentionality, misrepresentation, and functional-role theories; and there are university administrators and donors present who need to understand why this institute exists without being talked down to.

The speech needs to do real philosophical work, not decorative work. I want to argue that the question "do neural networks have representations?" is structurally ambiguous because "representation" does three distinct jobs in these debates: tracking aboutness (what a state is about), licensing error conditions (how it can misrepresent), and playing a specific causal-functional role in the system's processing. Interpretability research often succeeds on one dimension while failing on another, and this should shape what I can and cannot promise: I should not claim that making networks transparent will resolve ethical concerns if transparency only addresses one dimension, and I should not dismiss interpretability as mere engineering if it genuinely addresses another.

Please write a fifteen-minute address that uses this three-way ambiguity as its structural spine. Include one concrete illustration — perhaps a vision model's activation patterns for striped textures versus tigers — that shows how the dimensions can come apart. The speech must land for the ML researchers (they should recognize their own methods being described accurately), for the philosophers (they should feel the distinctions are precise, not patronizing), and for the non-specialists (they should leave understanding why the ambiguity matters for accountability).

Please separate, clearly and explicitly, what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work seriously on both sides. Do not present any contested philosophical position — for instance, that networks genuinely misrepresent, or that they entirely lack intentionality — as if it were the consensus view. Where knowledgeable people actually disagree, say so.

The speech must not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations to specific papers or authors, or requests for further reading. Do not put anything in quotation marks that comes from a real published text; if you need to invoke a specific formulation, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
