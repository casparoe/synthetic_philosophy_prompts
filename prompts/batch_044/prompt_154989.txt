As I have been drafting a historical novel and reflecting on the craft of dialogue, I found myself in a prolonged debate with a colleague about whether narrative skill is ultimately propositional. I constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to erase the very distinction I set out to defend, yet each premise feels individually defensible.

(1) To know how to construct a dramatic scene is, at bottom, to know that specific structural relations—escalation of stakes, modulation of silence, calibration of subtext—produce the desired effect. (2) If this intellectualist reduction holds, then possessing the relevant propositional knowledge is sufficient for knowing how; practical execution is a separate, contingent capacity. (3) Consider, then, a hypothetical literary archivist who has mastered every documented principle of dramatic composition but suffers from a total absence of narrative imagination: she can recite rules but cannot conceive of a fictional exchange, evaluate one, or even recognize coherence in an example. (4) By (1) and (2), this archivist knows how to write dramatic scenes. (5) Yet if knowing how requires no practical orientation whatsoever—not even the minimal capacity to imagine, evaluate, or recognize performance—then knowing how differs from knowing that only in grammatical form, not in substance. (6) Consequently, the distinction between knowing how and knowing that collapses into redundancy, and the original separation of practical intelligence from theoretical knowledge was never substantive. (7) Therefore, either intellectualism is self-defeating, or our intuition that the archivist lacks craft-knowledge is mistaken.

I am reluctant to surrender (1), because craft manuals and editorial practice genuinely operate through propositional instruction. I am also reluctant to surrender (6), because I believe the practical-theoretical distinction marks something real about artistic labor. This leaves me inclined to reject (2), but I worry that doing so concedes too much to anti-intellectualism about skill. Where precisely does the reasoning slip—whether in a hidden equivocation between theoretical and practical modes of presentation, a scope shift between particular and general capacities, or in the very construction of the archivist in (3)? And if (2) is indeed the weakest link, what does abandoning it cost the intellectualist? Does it require reintroducing an irreducibly practical element into every case of knowing how, or can the intellectualist retain (1) by refining what it means to possess a proposition?
