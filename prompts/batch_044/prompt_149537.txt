I have spent about a year thinking through a problem on my own, without formal training, and I now want to shape it into a six-month independent research proposal suitable for a small unaffiliated fellowship or a philosophy workshop pitch. The subject is sphexishness — the mechanical rigidity of behavior, like the burrow-checking wasp that repeats its inspection ritual even after the object it was checking for has already been removed. Most discussions treat sphexishness as the simple absence of flexibility and ask whether human free will is undermined if our actions are sphexish. I think that framing is too flat, and I want to build the proposal around a sharper distinction.

My working idea is that we need to separate first-order sphexishness, which is rigidity in the action itself, from second-order sphexishness, which is rigidity in the rules that govern how one updates one's actions. A system could change its behavior constantly — appearing flexible — while being entirely sphexish at the second level, because it applies the same mechanical update-rule in every context. The real question, then, is not whether we are rigid in what we do, but whether the mechanism by which we become flexible is itself open-ended or mechanically fixed.
