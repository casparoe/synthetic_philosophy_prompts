So I was reading about pain asymbolia for a class on philosophy of mind and I got stuck on something that feels like it should be simple but isn't. People with asymbolia apparently feel the pain sensation but don't have the usual aversive reaction to it — they might say "yeah that's painful" but it doesn't bother them. The usual philosophical take is that this splits the sensory-discriminative part from the affective-motivational part, which sounds neat. But here's where I'm confused: if the badness of pain is suposed to be what makes it bad, and in asymbolia that badness is just... missing, then is the remaining sensory component still "pain" in the full sense? Or is it more like a neutral bodily signal that happens to be intense?

And then there's this other angle I can't shake. Some people argue that pain's badness isn't just an added emotional layer but is built into how we conceptualy identify pain in the first place — like, if you had the sensory profile without any negative valence at all, you'd be talking about something else, maybe just pressure or heat. But if that's true, then asymbolia patients aren't actually feeling pain without the badness; they're feeling something pain-adjacent that we mislable because they still use the word "pain." I want an explanation that actually gets into the weeds of this: is the badness of pain a contingent psychological reaction that can be peeled away, or is it conceptually necessary? And if it's necessary, how do we make sense of asymbolia without just dismissing the patients' reports as confused? Don't just give me the standard two-factor story — I already know that. I'm looking for the conceptual tension about whether "badness" is part of the essence or an attachment, and what that implies for whether pain is intrinsically bad or only instrumentally so. Maybe bring in the idea that if badness can be absent while sensation remains, then pain's normative weight might come from somewhere other than its intrinsic quality — but I want that worked out carefully, not just asserted.
