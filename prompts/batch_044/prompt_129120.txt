Please adopt the role of a senior philosopher working at the intersection of environmental ethics, philosophy of biology, and normative decision theory. Before answering, take a few moments to brainstorm the conceptual tensions between ecological dynamics and sentient welfare, then proceed with careful, structured reasoning.

I recently joined a conservation NGO and have been pulled into a strategic debate that feels philosophically deeper than my colleagues realize. Some staff argue that habitat destruction is, counterintuitively, a net welfare improvement for wild animals, because reducing an ecosystem’s carrying capacity prevents large numbers of future sentient beings from being born into lives dominated by predation, starvation, and parasitism. Others insist that preservation is always welfare-positive because disruption increases suffering among existing populations through starvation events, trophic cascades, and loss of shelter. A colleague recently proposed that we should evaluate any habitat intervention using what she calls a “sentient biomass welfare integral”—essentially weighting ecosystem biomass by estimated sentience capacity per unit mass, then adjusting for ecological dependency ratios—to determine whether a given landscape change is welfare-improving. I am skeptical that such a metric can do the work she wants, but I cannot yet articulate what an adequate theory actually needs to deliver.

Please write the full specification of desiderata: what must any adequate theory of the net welfare impact of habitat change on wild animals be able to deliver? For each requirement, state the motivating case or distinction behind it, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and explain briefly why it matters for policy. In particular, the list should include but not be limited to: (1) how to handle the replacement or future-lives problem—whether preventing births counts as a welfare gain and under what conditions; (2) a clear distinction between different sources of suffering in wild populations (predation, disease, starvation, parasitism) and how habitat change affects each differentially; (3) an account of ecological dependency and trophic cascades, so that removing one species does not obscure welfare effects on others; (4) consistency with basic population ecology and carrying-capacity dynamics, without reducing welfare solely to population size; (5) a principled separation between individual-level welfare aggregation and ecosystem-level functional properties, so that “ecosystem health” is not illegitimately collapsed into sentient welfare; (6) a decision framework for conservation policy that can compare preservation, managed degradation, and active restoration; and (7) compatibility with the kind of “sentient biomass welfare integral” framing my colleague proposed—either by showing how it satisfies the prior desiderata or by identifying exactly which ones it fails.

Then, indicate whether any subset of these desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether respecting ecological dependency and maintaining a simple individual-level aggregation can coexist. Finally, provide a brief, non-citational scorecard of how the main contending views fare: the strong “wild suffering dominates, so reduce populations” view; the “ecosystem stability minimizes suffering” preservationist view; and a hybrid view that tries to use something like the sentient biomass integral to navigate between them. Keep the emphasis on the criteria themselves, since the scoring is only meant to help me apply the list in our next meeting.
