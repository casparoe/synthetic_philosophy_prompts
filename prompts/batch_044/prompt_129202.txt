I have been reading Friedrich Nietzsche’s autobiographical writings and correspondence—particularly the sections in Ecce Homo where he discusses climate, digestion, and bodily regimen—without any formal training in philosophy or intellectual history. What strikes me is not simply that he suffered from migraines and stomach ailments, but that he treats the digestive system and dietary choice almost as philosophical instruments: he suggests that heavy, “German” cooking clouds the mind, that a particular lightness in food correlates with intellectual agility, and that his own careful calibration of meals, climate, and rest is part of how one cultivates the strength to overcome inherited values. I have come to think there might be a hidden “physiology of thought” in his work, one that makes the body not a metaphor for the spirit but an actual site where philosophical discrimination happens.

For a small podcast I am preparing, I would like to interview an unnamed defender of the stronger claim that Nietzsche’s dietary and health practices are not biographical background but are integral to his philosophy—specifically to his critique of ascetic morality and his idea of self-overcoming. I am not looking for generic biographical questions about what he ate or whether he was hypochondriacal. Instead, please draft six to eight interview questions that turn on the actual philosophical content of this position: I want at least one question on how the stomach can function as an organ of philosophical discrimination rather than a mere symbol for spiritual conditions; one on how this physiological reading distinguishes Nietzsche’s self-discipline from the Christian-ascetic denial of the body that he attacks; and one on whether this view commits him to the idea that some philosophical disagreements can ultimately be traced to differences in digestion, climate, or bodily regimen rather than to reasoning alone. For each of these three, include a concrete follow-up keyed to the answer the scholar is most likely to give—anticipating, for instance, the reply that the body is only an allegory for psychic states, or the reply that Nietzsche’s prescriptions were purely personal medicine with no universal philosophical import. I also want one final question that asks what kind of evidence or argument could genuinely weaken this physiological interpretation, and a follow-up that presses on whether the interpretation risks reducing Nietzsche’s ethics to a matter of gastroenterology.
