I lead a software engineering team, and I am trying to decide what to write in the annual review of one of my senior developers, Jordan. During sprint retrospectives and pair-programming sessions, Jordan is remarkably transparent about technical mistakes—he openly discusses flawed design choices in his pull requests and actively encourages junior engineers to point out errors in his own code. Yet in cross-functional meetings with product and design leadership, he consistently reframes collaborative outcomes to center his individual contributions, and during the recent promotion cycle he submitted a self-assessment that substantially understated the architectural work of two teammates on a shared module. At first I interpreted this pattern as evidence of a stable personality trait—perhaps a form of intellectual vanity that coexists with technical honesty—but I then noticed that in informal one-on-one mentoring conversations he is genuinely generous with credit and actively advocates for others’ visibility to management. The discrepancy is stark: his behavior seems tightly coupled to whether the social setting is competitive-evaluative or cooperative-technical. Before I finalize his evaluation, I want to understand whether it is conceptually sound to label him as lacking integrity in a global, person-level sense, or whether the fact that his conduct shifts so sharply across contexts undermines any legitimate inference to a fixed moral character. How should I think about judging someone’s nature when their actions appear highly situation-dependent?
