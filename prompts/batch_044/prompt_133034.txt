I'm working through the literature on doxastic transparency for a seminar on self-knowledge, and I've constructed an argument that begins with Moore's paradox and ends in an implausible place—perhaps showing that fully rational agents must possess omniscient access to every level of their own mental architecture, or that human cognition is globally irrational. I want to locate the slip precisely.

(1) Moore's paradox demonstrates that sincerely asserting that it is raining while simultaneously denying that one believes it is raining is absurd, because the act of assertion expresses the very belief it denies. (2) The standard diagnosis is that first-person belief is transparent: to sincerely judge that p is, in part, to be disposed to assert p, and to deny believing p undermines that judgment from within. (3) Now extend this to graded belief. A rational Bayesian agent assigns credences, and there is an analogous transparency condition for credal states: if one's credence in p is 0.8, then one's second-order credence that one's credence in p is 0.8 should also be 0.8, on pain of a graded analogue of the paradox—holding that one's credence in p is high while holding that one's confidence in that very assessment is low. (4) By parity of reasoning, transparency must hold recursively at every level—first-order, second-order, third-order, and so on—so that no rational agent can misalign her higher-order attitudes with her lower-order ones. (5) Therefore, a fully rational agent must have perfect, error-free access to the entire hierarchical structure of her doxastic states. (6) But empirical psychology shows that human introspection is systematically unreliable: subjects misreport confidence levels, exhibit the Dunning-Kruger effect, and harbor implicit biases they cannot detect. (7) So either humans fail to be rational agents in the Bayesian sense, or transparency does not apply to graded belief. (8) If transparency fails for graded belief, however, then the original Moore-paradox diagnosis—grounded in transparency—was merely a surface feature of binary assertion, not a deep constraint on rational belief. (9) But if there is no deep transparency constraint, then there is no principled reason why binary belief should be transparent either, and Moore's paradox collapses into a mere conversational impropriety rather than a window into the nature of belief.

The conclusion feels wrong: I do not want to abandon transparency entirely, yet I also do not want to commit to the claim that rationality requires omniscient self-access. Which step should I reject—(3), (4), or (8)—and what does each rejection cost? I am especially interested in whether there is a scope or quantifier shift between the normative claim that rational agents ought to be transparent and the descriptive claim that all actual humans are transparent, or whether I have smuggled in an equivocation between belief and credence. If this framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if graded belief simply does not support transparency conditions, or if the argument illegitimately moves from normative rationality to descriptive psychology—please say so plainly rather than forcing an answer within the frame. I would most prefer to keep the idea that Moore's paradox reveals something structurally important about first-person belief, but I am willing to pay a price elsewhere if that is what consistency demands.
