Write a short, rigorous dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors: Dr. A, a care ethicist who defends what she terms "proximity-gradient obligations"—the view that moral weight scales non-linearly with the density and history of interdependence between caregiver and recipient—and Dr. B, a critic who contends that any attempt to formalize these gradients for hospital triage or state welfare policy either collapses into unprincipled partiality or covertly relies on aggregative consequentialist reasoning. Have Dr. A defend her position using the specific case of a chronic-care ward where two patients present identical clinical needs, but one has resided in the unit for three years while the other arrived yesterday, arguing that the longer-tenured relationship generates a non-substitutable obligation that cannot be captured by need-based or rights-based frameworks. Dr. B should respond directly to this case, not by dismissing relational values, but by demonstrating that any consistent weighting rule for proximity either reproduces standard deontological side-constraints or smuggles in a hidden calculus of welfare maximization across relationships. The exchange should include genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must acknowledge the strongest version of the other's point and identify a specific tension in their own view. Conclude with both interlocutors agreeing on the exact hypothetical case—specifically, a fully autonomous robotic care assistant with sophisticated clinical capacity but zero emotional or historical connection to the patient—that would force one of them to abandon a core commitment, and have them explain precisely why that case functions as the decisive test. Avoid strawman representations; both positions should be developed with philosophical precision.
