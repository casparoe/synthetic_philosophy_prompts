As a journalist who reports on the intersection of economics, philosophy, and institutional design, I have been trying to understand whether the objections to commodification can survive translation into the formal language of market engineering. I want you to restate the core normative arguments against kidney sales and vote-selling markets—specifically the concerns about corruption of meaning, crowding out of civic or altruistic motivation, and distributive injustice—using only the vocabulary of mechanism design and auction theory: strategy-proofness, incentive compatibility, allocative efficiency, information revelation, Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanisms, and externalities. Do not smuggle in philosophical terminology to bridge gaps; if an objection has no counterpart in this framework, identify it precisely and explain whether the gap lies in the vocabulary or in the original claim.

Then provide a residue report. For each major objection, indicate whether it maps onto a well-defined mechanism failure, whether it can be approximated only by distorting the economic concept—for instance, treating moral repugnance as a negative consumption externality or treating intrinsic motivation as an unobservable type—and whether it disappears entirely. I am especially interested in whether treating kidney sales and vote markets through the lens of divisible versus positional goods in auction design captures any real structural difference between the two cases, or whether that distinction depends on normative premises that mechanism design is structurally incapable of representing. Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue points to a deficiency in the economic framework or to an ambiguity in the philosophical objections themselves.
