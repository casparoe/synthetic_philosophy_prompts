I am a retired experimental physicist who spent three decades in condensed-matter laboratories and have only recently begun auditing philosophy-of-mind seminars; I find that most classroom treatments of Von Neumann reduce The Computer and the Brain to a superficial analog-versus-digital contrast that misses the deeper structural tension in his argument. For an upper-level undergraduate or first-year graduate seminar of about twelve students—meeting for ninety minutes, with this particular essay to be answered in roughly forty-five minutes of writing—please draft a single examination question that asks candidates to do far more than summarize Von Neumann’s comparison between unreliable neural components and the error-correcting architectures of digital machines. Specifically, the question should center on the apparent contradiction between his claim that the brain is essentially a statistical, analog device operating with high component failure rates and his simultaneous conviction—derived from his work on self-reproducing automata—that a complete formal, logical description of cerebral function is not only achievable but necessary for any rigorous scientific psychology. The prompt should require students to evaluate whether Von Neumann’s statistical conception of neural computation can genuinely escape Turing-machine equivalence without abandoning the formal rigor he demands, and whether his analogy between neural redundancy and digital reliability ultimately collapses the very distinction he seeks to maintain.

Please provide the exam question together with a model answer and a marking scheme structured around five specific argumentative moves that a top response must make, assigning marks out of a total of twenty, and include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake candidates typically commit at each point. The assessment should be fully answerable by a student who has engaged with the primary text and seminar discussion alone, with no need for web access, bibliographic research, or citation of secondary literature; its difficulty should come from identifying the conceptual crux rather than from recalling labels. I am especially interested in whether the model answer treats Von Neumann’s framework for self-replication as a hidden commitment to digitalism or as a genuinely neutral formal scaffold capable of accommodating statistical, analog computation.
