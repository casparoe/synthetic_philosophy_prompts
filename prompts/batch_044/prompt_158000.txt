I am drafting an essay for my seminar on biography, moral philosophy, and classical reception, and I would appreciate your comments on its conceptual structure and historical plausibility. The essay argues that Plutarch’s Parallel Lives should be read less as nationalist comparison or biographical cataloguing and more as an exercise in what I call “distributed moral cognition”: the deliberate pairing of a Greek and a Roman life forces the reader to hold two culturally specific virtue-claims in suspension, and the pedagogical value lies precisely in the friction between them rather than in any resolved verdict. I contrast this with hagiographic biography, which typically collapses such horizontal pairing into a single, vertically oriented life measured against an implicit universal standard. In particular, I focus on the pairing of Alcibiades and Coriolanus—both figures of exceptional talent, both destroyed by the political bodies they served, both ending in exile and violent death. Rather than asking the reader to decide whether Alcibiades’s flamboyant instability or Coriolanus’s rigid integrity is the graver failure, Plutarch leaves the comparison structurally open: Alcibiades is hunted down in Phrygia, Coriolanus is murdered by Volscian allies he had previously betrayed. My claim is that these parallel terminations do not resolve into a moral ranking but instead illustrate how different political cultures generate distinct modes of self-ruin. Hagiography, I suggest, rarely permits this kind of unresolved structural pairing; even when medieval and early modern lives of saints are grouped together, the comparison tends to serve hierarchical ranking rather than productive complication.

I am trying to ground this in Plutarch’s own stated purpose for writing biography—he describes the enterprise as holding up lives like mirrors for self-examination rather than treating them as legal cases to be adjudicated. I want to argue that the mirror metaphor actually supports my reading, since a mirror requires an internal contrast to function, and the parallel structure supplies that contrast without leaving it to the reader’s external experience. At the same time, I worry that I may be over-reading the symmetry in the Alcibiades and Coriolanus pairing, or that I am imposing a modern cognitive framework onto an ancient rhetorical form. I also wonder whether my contrast with hagiography holds up, given that some comparative frameworks do exist in saintly biography, even if they serve different ends.

Third, is “distributed moral cognition” a useful analytical term here, or does it obscure the classical vocabulary of exemplarity and imitation? Fourth, should I strengthen the essay by engaging closely with specific passages in the Lives, or is the conceptual framing sufficient for a philosophical rather than philological argument?
