I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with a close friend about whether Camus's absurdism can genuinely coexist with contemporary objective-list accounts of meaning in life, particularly the kind of view Susan Wolf advances under the heading of fitting fulfillment. The disagreement became sharp: my friend insisted that any attempt to synthesize cosmic indifference with local meaning collapses into either dishonest self-deception or a covert return to objectivism. To organize my thoughts, I sketched an essay tentatively titled Structured Revolt: Recursive Valuation Between Absurdism and Objectivism. In it, I argue that meaning need not depend on either universal cosmic endorsement or on pre-given objective values that exist independently of engagement. Instead, I propose what I call recursive valuation: a process in which an agent commits to a local project or relationship, then treats the stability and coherence of that very commitment as providing its own secondary justification for continued investment, without ever claiming that the universe validates the initial choice.

The essay applies this framework to artistic practice. I suggest that the absurdist artist does not need to believe her work contributes to some transcendent human progress; she need only recognize that her sustained attention to form and craft generates an internal feedback loop—each act of care makes the object more worthy of further care, not because the object possesses hidden cosmic significance, but because the pattern of commitment itself becomes the site of meaning. I contrast this with Wolf's view by arguing that fitting fulfillment is too static: it requires that an objective value exist prior to and independent of the agent's engagement, whereas recursive valuation allows the value to be constituted through the engagement itself, provided the engagement is sustained, reflexive, and open to revision rather than dogmatic.

I would value your comments on several specific points. First, does recursive valuation genuinely avoid Camus's charge of philosophical suicide—does the feedback loop amount to smuggling in a hidden teleology, or can it remain genuinely tension-filled? Second, is the distinction from Wolf's fitting fulfillment substantial, or does it merely rename the same phenomenon of active engagement with goods that are objectively valuable? Third, regardless of philosophical merit, is this framework interesting enough to sustain an essay, or does it risk collapsing into a restatement of existentialist authenticity dressed in systems-language? Any suggestions on sharpening the conceptual architecture, identifying hidden inconsistencies, or clarifying the boundary between recursive valuation and mere psychological habituation would be particularly welcome.
