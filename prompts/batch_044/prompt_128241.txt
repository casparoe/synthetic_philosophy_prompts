I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though since working on computer vision systems I have developed a side interest in philosophy of mind—particularly in whether our models' reliance on context has any meaningful parallel in human perception. At a dinner last week, two friends—both with graduate training in cognitive science but no philosophy credentials—fell into a sharp disagreement about cognitive penetration, and they asked me to adjudicate. I found myself unable to do so, and I am writing to ask you to work through their exchange point by point.

My first friend argued that cognitive penetration is not merely possible but obvious: she cited the well-known case of radiologists who, after years of training, can detect anomalies on scans that complete novices overlook, and she insisted that the experts are not just interpreting the same image differently after the fact, but are literally experiencing a different visual field. My second friend pushed back by saying she was conflating perception with judgment: he claimed the radiologist's retina and early visual cortex process the same raw data as the novice's, and that only the downstream belief or report changes; therefore, calling this "penetration" is a category error that mistakes post-perceptual inference for altered experience. The first friend then introduced a subtler example—participants who are told a geometric shape is made of cardboard rather than metal consistently report it as lighter in color—and argued that this shift occurs too rapidly to be a separate reasoning step, which implies that cognition reaches directly into the perceptual module itself. The second friend conceded that prior knowledge might direct attention or prime feature detectors, but he maintained that this is not penetration of the qualitative character of experience, only selective processing; he added that if we allow every top-down influence to count as penetration, the thesis becomes trivially true and loses all philosophical interest. The first friend replied that he is hiding behind an arbitrary anatomical boundary between "early vision" and "cognition," and that the substantive question is whether mental states can alter the phenomenal content of perception, not whether they do so inside some specific brain region. They went back and forth without resolution.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the five positions outlined above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating; identify what, if anything, both parties are missing; and say whether there is a principled way to draw the line over which they are fighting. Please write your response in British English.
