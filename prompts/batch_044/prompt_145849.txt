Last weekend my roommate Jordan and his sister Sam got into this long argument after we watched some documentary about animal rights and history, and I mostly just sat there listening. Jordan said the whole "expanding circle" idea people talk about — like how moral concern went from family to strangers to maybe animals — isn't really progress so much as moral inflation. He used this analogy: it's like taking a camera sensor and adding pixels without making the sensor bigger, so each pixel gets smaller. Now we "care" about factory-farmed chickens and climate refugees, but the actual intensity per being is way lower than in a small tribe where people literally risked death for each other. Sam said that's a false analogy because moral concern isn't a fixed-size sensor. Jordan said okay, but it's not about total quantity, it's about "normative resolution" — the level of detail and specificity in our obligations. He pointed out that global human rights law is super vague compared to specific kinship duties, so expanding the circle actually flattens the moral landscape.

Sam countered that vagueness isn't weakness, and tribal obligations were often violent to outsiders, so nostalgia for precision is dangerous. Jordan admitted that, but said atleast they were precise, and proposed we should call the shift "boundary redistribution" rather than "progress" — we're trading deep local obligations for shallow global ones, not upgrading anything. Sam said that assumes a zero-sum that's never been proven, and that we can have both local communities and global norms without losing either, though she admitted it takes alot of institutional work. Then Jordan brought up AI rights as a kind of stress test: if we expand the circle to include synthetic minds, the resolution problem gets extreme because we can't even locate their suffering in a meaningful way. Sam said that's pure speculation and doesn't undermine actual progress with animals and marginalized humans, and that Jordan was using an edge case to dismiss a real pattern.

They turned to me and asked who was right about what, and whether either of them was missing something big. Can you go through their exchange move by move — rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify the rating, say who's right about which specific point, and then tell me who is more right overall? I'm especially curious about the "normative resolution" idea and whether the boundary-redistribution framing actually holds up.
