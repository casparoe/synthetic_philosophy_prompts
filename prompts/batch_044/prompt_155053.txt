Two months ago I served on a jury that returned a guilty verdict in a case of aggravated assault, and the experience left me with a philosophical problem I have not been able to resolve. During deliberations I felt the pull of what seemed like a straightforward retributive intuition: the defendant had wronged his victim in a way that seemed to call for suffering as a kind of moral balancing. At the same time, I have become convinced by arguments—particularly those associated with Daniel Dennett's compatibilist defense of moral responsibility and Gregg Caruso's hard-incompatibilist rejection of it—that the metaphysical foundations for that intuition may be unstable.

Write an essay that weighs Dennett's attempt to reconstruct 'just deserts' without libertarian free will, emphasizing his reliance on the capacity for rational self-control and the social utility of holding agents accountable, against Caruso's claim that such capacities justify at most a public-health model of containment and rehabilitation, not punitive suffering. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: can a non-retributive framework genuinely preserve the moral distinction between wrongdoers and mere victims of misfortune, or does Dennett's reconstructed desert necessarily commit us to a form of punitive practice that collapses once we accept Caruso's premise that reactive attitudes are evolutionary artifacts rather than moral detectors?

In developing your argument, address specifically whether the distinction between 'desert as fairness in social exchange' and 'desert as intrinsic moral accounting' survives Caruso's critique, and whether Dennett's appeal to the social function of blame can be sustained if we treat blame not as tracking genuine culpability but as a mechanism of behavioral regulation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an argument that can stand on its own reasoning.
