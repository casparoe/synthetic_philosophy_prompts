I have been discussing with colleagues in my department's working group on technology and value whether the emerging governance architecture for artificial intelligence rests on a coherent philosophical picture, and I keep returning to a specific institutional pattern that strikes me as conceptually undertheorized. In current practice—spanning the evaluation protocols of major AI laboratories, the risk-classification procedures of the EU AI Act, and the safety benchmarks published by national institutes—"alignment" is operationalized as a single, quantifiable property of a model. Diverse failure modes—deceptive reporting, sycophantic agreement, value drift across contexts, and the emergence of instrumental sub-goals—are routinely aggregated into a unified "alignment score" or composite "safety rating" that determines deployment eligibility. The design treats these pathologies as fungible manifestations of one underlying deficit and permits trade-offs among them, much as one might trade off speed against energy consumption in an engineering specification.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of the alignment problem does this institutional form presuppose? Must it assume that alignment is strictly one problem rather than a family of conceptually distinct problems—for example, representational misalignment (a divergence between the model's world-model and reality), motivational misalignment (a poorly specified objective function), and relational misalignment (interactive dynamics that erode human autonomy over time)? Or can a pluralist ontology, which treats these as incommensurable failures with different normative structures, still accommodate the practice of aggregation? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in these evaluation protocols, distinguish what they strictly require from what they merely fit well with, and tell me what a governance framework would have to look like if it rejected the unitary assumption—say, by imposing separate, non-tradable thresholds for each failure mode and refusing any composite index. Would such a framework be intelligible within current regulatory logic, or would it presuppose an entirely different conception of what it means for a system to be "aligned"?
