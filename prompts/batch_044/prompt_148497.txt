I am an undergraduate reading political theory, and in our seminars on democratic legitimacy I have observed a recurring argumentative move that I struggle to classify precisely. A populist figure asserts that democratic institutions — elections, courts, administrative bodies — possess authority only when they function as transparent channels for 'the will of the people'. When an institutional decision coincides with the figure's position, it is presented as direct, unmediated proof of that will; when it conflicts, the very same institution is dismissed as a distorting apparatus — captured by elites, corrupted by procedural rules, or structurally alienated from the people — so that the true will is said to lie hidden behind it. Notably, the figure offers no independent mechanism by which this hidden will could be ascertained, nor any account of its specific content apart from the figure's own policy preferences. The asymmetry is therefore complete: agreement with the speaker renders an institution epistemically transparent, disagreement renders it systematically opaque, with no consistent criterion of legitimacy that does not ultimately reduce to the speaker's own judgement.

I am familiar with the literature on populism as a 'thin-centred ideology', with Schmittian distinctions between a homogeneous people and pluralistic state structures, and with recent work on the epistemic claims of populist movements — for example, the idea that ordinary citizens possess a direct, non-inferential grasp of political truth. Yet none of these quite isolates the pattern I am describing, especially the absence of any positive alternative procedure or representational theory. Is there an established concept that captures this specific asymmetrical appeal to popular sovereignty? If so, please take a committed position: identify the single best-fitting concept and defend that identification rigorously, explaining exactly why Schmittian decisionism and epistemic populism are inadequate descriptions of the move, and marking the boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary strategic hypocrisy. If, on the other hand, you judge that no established term fits, then coin a precise term with a rigorous definition and defend that judgement against the nearest alternatives, specifying where the phenomenon begins and ends. Please answer in British English, and do not merely survey options — argue for one.
