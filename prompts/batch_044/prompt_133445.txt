I'm writing a feature on evolutionary metaphors in cultural theory and want to close with a short poem—about twenty lines, blank verse—that stages Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes (bottom-up, cumulative mechanisms that build without a designer) and skyhooks (explanations that hang unexplained from above), but applies it to a less common tension: whether cultural evolution operates as a genuine crane, assembling complex norms from low-level imitation and selection, or whether it quietly imports a skyhook whenever theorists appeal to irreducible collective intentionality. I want the poem's own architecture to do the philosophical work: begin with short, monosyllabic lines that gradually lengthen and interlock into syntactically complex sentences, mimicking cumulative selection; then break the buildup with a single grammatically suspended line—no verb, no foundation—to embody the skyhook; close with a couplet that asks whether greedy reductionism, pushed to its limit, dissolves even the crane's own structure and leaves nothing but bare scaffolding. The reference to Dennett should be precise enough that a philosopher of biology recognizes the terms being used correctly, yet the image should stand on its own for readers who have never encountered the metaphor. Do not resolve the tension with a sermon or a shrug; let the form leave the question hanging exactly where the line breaks.
