me and my friend had this long arguement last night about whether nations are actually old or if we just made them up, and i can't stop thinking about it. i want to know if there's one principel that captures what i actually think, because my gut keeps giving me different answers on specific cases.

here's where i land:

- the scottish kilt and clan tartans were basically invented in the 19th century by romantic nationalists, but now they feel ancient and bind people together for real. my verdict: it's an invented tradition in hobsbawm's sense, but that doesn't make it fake. the present practice is what gives it weight, not the origin.

- people saying france was a nation back in 843 or whatever. verdict: that's anachronistic projection. there was a kingdom and shared culture, but not a modern nation with mass consciousness.

- meiji japan reconstructing the emperor as an unbroken divine line and presenting it as recovery of ancient ways, when it was actually modern state-building. verdict: this feels different and worse — it's hiding its own modernity, imposing false continuity from above.

- the armenian diaspora maintaining language, rituals and collective memory for generations without a state or territory (pre-1991). verdict: that's anderson's imagined community, but it's not state-invented. it works horizontally, through shared practice, not vertical myth-making.

- the usa, founded 1776, everyone knows it's new, built on printed constitutions and shared narratives rather than ancient blood. verdict: openly modern, horizontally imagined, and it functions perfectly well without pretending to be old.

- but then there's the case that keeps breaking my principle: imagine a post-colonial state that literally hires historians to craft a 500-year pre-colonial national story, teaches it in schools, and after two generations people genuinely live inside that story and forget it was fabricated. is that more like the scots (authentic because lived) or like meiji japan (false because imposed and hidden)? i keep going back and forth.

i feel like the difference might be whether the imagination runs vertical (claiming ancient roots) versus horizontal (acknowledging simultaneity and shared present), or maybe whether it's bottom-up versus top-down. but then i also think that if people genuinely believe and live a narrative, that belief itself creates the reality, regardless of origin. so my verdicts might not fit together cleanly.

is there one coherent account that gets all of these? if not, which verdict should i drop? and what would the resulting principle predict for a diaspora that suddenly gains a state and then starts manufacturing traditions for itself — does it become "real" in a different way, or does it stay horizontal?
