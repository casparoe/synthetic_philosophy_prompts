I'm on my university's AI governence oversight committee, and we're voting tues on a stakeholder-preference aggregation tool for campus policy — housing allocations, speech codes, that kind of thing. It uses something called coherent extrapolated volition: basically it tries to model what people would want if they were smarter, more informed, and internally consistent. The vendor wants to include a 'pre-filter' that deletes any stakeholder input with logical contradictions before the extrapolation step even runs. They say incoherent preferences aren't part of anyone's true volition, so it's just noise reduction.

One faculty rep is furious. She argues CEV was meant to resolve contradictions through extrapolation, not erase them upstream, and that this filter lets the designers smuggle in their own values under the cover of coherence. She also thinks that if people learn their messy, contradictory inputs are being 'cleaned up' by an algorithm they don't understand, institutional trust will collapse — especially among groups that already feel ignored.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting. Give a concrete recommendation — approve only if the filter is replaced by X, reject, delay for review, whatever — with a realistic timeline. Take her objection seriously; don't just say coherence is obviously good. Also address whether a secular, non-idealized preference system can actually sustain trust in a pluarlistic institution when people know their raw preferences are being edited. Note the strongest point for the side that loses. Write it in American English, and keep it formal enough for a committee but not like a law review article.
