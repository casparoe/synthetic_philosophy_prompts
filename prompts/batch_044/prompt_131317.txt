i'm trying to understand what we're actually committed to philosophically when we split the vocabulary for mass violence across legal and historical contexts. There's this practice in international law and human rights work: prosecutors stick strictley to the UN Convention's protected groups — national, ethnical, racial, religious — and refuse to apply the genocide label to the destruction of political groups or to forced cultural assimilation like banning languages and destroying schools, even when the scale and intent look comparable. Meanwhile historians and some national truth commissions use "cultural genocide" for residential school systems or systematic language suppression, and they reference Lemkin's original broader idea that included cultural and political destruction, not just the four groups in the final convention text. So the same event can be "genocide" in a museum exhibit or historical report but not in a courtroom indictement.

What implicit theory of moral categorization does that split practice presuppose? Does it assume legal naming should be narrower because it triggers specific international obligations, or that group identity is the only morally relevant feature that distinguishes genocide from other atrocities? Is the practice internally consistent — can you claim destroying a culture is genocide for memorial purposes but deny it's genocide for prosecution without contradiction? And if we replaced this bifurcated system with a single taxonomy that used Lemkin's broader categories everywhere, what parts of current international law or human rights reporting would have to change?
