For some years now I have seen the alignment problem described through the analogy of a genie granted three wishes: the master states the desired end-state, the genie executes it with literal, unanticipated efficiency, and the catastrophe arises because the specification was technically correct but normatively hollow. This image is widely used to claim that alignment is essentially one problem—getting the specification exactly right—rather than a family of separate difficulties. Having retired from experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy of action and social ontology, I find the analogy seductive but suspect it conceals more than it reveals. I would like you to audit it thoroughly. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: in this mapping, what serves as the master, the wish-formulation, the genie, the literal execution mechanism, the unanticipated consequence, and the hidden normative gap? Second, identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the argument: does the genie picture falsely assume that values can be fully articulated prior to interaction, does it treat the system as an instrument rather than an agent with its own ontology, and does it import a magical-realist framework that obscures the technical reality of optimization? Third, take a committed position: once these breaks are registered, does the conclusion that alignment is a single specification problem still stand, or does the analogy collapse in ways that show it to be several distinct problems—perhaps better analogized to constitutive rule-negotiation or to the calibration of an instrument that alters the phenomenon being measured? If you think the genie analogy fails, propose a more accurate analogy and defend why it carries the load better. Please do not hedge: I want a definite judgment on whether the "one problem, just specify correctly" thesis survives, together with the reasoning that forces that verdict.
