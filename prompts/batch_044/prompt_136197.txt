You serve as the methodological referee for a federal interdisciplinary review board that evaluates joint proposals spanning ecology and machine learning, and panels frequently deadlock when domain experts assign incompatible probabilities to the same outcome—say, the likelihood that a deployed model will cause irreversible ecosystem harm within ten years. Design a reusable thirty-minute audit protocol, derived from Aumann's agreement theorem, that your office can apply to determine whether such disagreement is rationally sustainable or signals a correctable failure of belief alignment. Structure it as ordered stages: first, a prior-registration check (do the parties hold explicit, shared baseline probabilities before encountering the joint evidence?); second, an evidence-transparency audit (is all relevant data and its interpretation genuinely common knowledge, or do private sub-models remain institutionally siloed?); third, a reasoning-exposure test (have the parties fully disclosed their update rules so that posteriors are mutually observable?). At each stage specify what counts as passing, where the audit halts with a determination that disagreement is permissible, where it mandates reconciliation, and where it must hand the case to a human chair because the dispute turns on normative weighting of evidence rather than on belief structure. Identify the precise conditions—common priors combined with common knowledge of posteriors—under which the theorem forces convergence, and flag practical misfire modes: hidden priors masked as empirical disputes, technically shareable evidence kept private by disciplinary norms, and reasoning opacity that mimics rational divergence. Then apply the protocol to three cases: (1) an ecologist and a computer scientist who disagree on harm probability solely because they entered with different ecological resilience priors; (2) two machine-learning researchers who claim shared priors but have not disclosed their adversarial robustness checks, producing divergent posteriors that should converge upon exposure; and (3) a case in which the audit confirms full common knowledge, shared priors, and transparent reasoning, yet disagreement persists—an outcome the board will find unacceptable because it implies a breach of rationality norms, but for which the protocol offers no constructive remedy beyond declaring the breach. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: it cannot manufacture common priors, it cannot adjudicate contests over which evidence is admissible when admissibility itself is disputed, and it cannot substitute for institutional authority to enforce disclosure.
