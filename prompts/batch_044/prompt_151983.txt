At a philosophy reading group discussing Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is especially bad not merely because of the deaths involved but because it undermines the value of present long-term projects oriented toward an assumed human future, two members—call them Alex and Blake—got into an extended disagreement about whether the argument succeeds and exactly what it establishes. They asked a bystander who had been silent to adjudicate point by point, rating each move and explaining who is more right overall, without simply declaring a winner.

The exchange proceeded as follows.

(1) Alex opened by saying that Scheffler's core insight is relational and diachronic: activities like raising children, building scientific institutions, or composing symphonies are not self-contained events but are partly constituted by their orientation toward future human minds—readers, students, successors—who will continue, verify, or respond to them. If humanity is going extinct, these projects are not merely disappointed; they are retroactively truncated, deprived of a constitutive dimension that made them the projects they were.

(2) Blake replied that this confuses the psychological conditions for engaging in a project with the conditions for its value. A parent might be motivated by the hope of descendants, but the value of nurturing a child resides in the care actually given, not in an unbroken chain of future generations. A symphony's aesthetic value lies in its internal structure and in the experiences of those who actually hear it, not in an indefinite succession of listeners. So Scheffler has shown only that extinction frustrates our expectations, not that it destroys value that was genuinely present.

(3) Alex countered that Blake treats projects as isolated objects, but many human activities are essentially communicative across time. Scientific research, for instance, aims at truths that outlast the researcher and are confirmed by later inquiry; if all inquiry ends, the research never achieves the confirmation that partly defines its success. The value is not merely "being correct" in isolation, but being correct within an ongoing practice of verification. Without that practice, what remains is an incomplete activity, not the same activity with bad luck.

(4) Blake conceded that some projects have this diachronic structure, but pressed a reductio. If the meaningfulness of parenting genuinely depends on the survival of the species, then a parent's love is held hostage to cosmic contingency in a way that feels wrong: we want to say that a parent in a dying civilization still loves meaningfully. Alex responded that this is not a reductio but precisely the point—knowing with certainty that all human culture will end would indeed alter the character of parenting, just as knowing that no one will ever read a manuscript alters the character of writing it, even if the manuscript's sentences remain grammatically correct.

(5) Blake then raised a scaling problem. If Scheffler's argument applies only at the species level, it needs a principled threshold: why does the extinction of all humanity retroactively diminish present projects, while the extinction of just one's own family line does not? Without such a line, the argument either collapses into triviality—applying to every childless death—or becomes arbitrary. Alex suggested the threshold might lie in whether a project is oriented toward an open-ended, indefinite future rather than toward specific known recipients, but admitted this distinction needs further defense and may not fully resolve the worry.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about the scope or limits of Scheffler's argument regarding which present activities are genuinely vulnerable to the prospect of human extinction.
