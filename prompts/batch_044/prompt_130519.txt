Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—call them Mara and Eli—debating pure tranquilism, the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, and that all positive affective states are at best instrumentally useful for sustaining that absence.

Mara should defend a hierarchical variant of tranquilism. She should distinguish first-order craving absence (simply lacking a particular desire) from second-order meta-craving absence (lacking even the desire to lack desires), and argue that genuine well-being requires the second-order condition. She should claim that standard well-being metrics erroneously treat absence as a scalar quantity, when in fact tranquility is a structural achievement: once second-order absence is secured, first-order absences become redundant, and any introduced positive state—joy, curiosity, aesthetic absorption—risks reactivating the hierarchical architecture of desire.

Eli should defend dynamic affective pluralism. He should hold that well-being is irreducibly plural and that a life of mere absence, however deep, is axiologically impoverished rather than maximally good. Eli should press Mara on whether second-order absence is genuinely distinguishable from a disguised positive state—call it “peace”—and whether her view can account for the intuition that a consciousness engineered never to crave, yet also never to feel, possesses zero well-being rather than perfect well-being.

The exchange must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the specific point just made, not deliver parallel monologues. Neither should be a strawman; Eli should concede that suffering is intrinsically bad, and Mara should concede that positive states occur—disagreeing only about their axiological status. Keep the discussion conceptually dense: address the asymmetry between craving satisfaction (temporary relief that preserves the underlying desire-structure) and craving elimination (stable absence that dissolves it), and discuss whether a being that never experienced craving at all falls inside the domain of well-being evaluation, outside it, or at its null point.

After the dialogue, include a brief reflective note stating how confident you are in the philosophical accuracy of the positions represented—particularly regarding the hierarchical distinction between first-order and second-order absence—and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
