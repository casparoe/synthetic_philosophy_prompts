The debate over whether the categories 'Middle Ages', 'Renaissance', and 'Scientific Revolution' denote real historical divisions or are retrospective historiographic constructions has cycled through the same arguments for decades: continuists stress the persistence of Aristotelian natural philosophy in university curricula well into the seventeenth century and the gradual, geographically scattered emergence of experimental practices; discontinuists emphasize institutional ruptures, the self-conscious rejection of scholastic authority, and shifting standards of evidence around 1600. Yet the literature feels like two parallel monologues—neither side concedes ground, and the empirical record is largely shared. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly about archives but about ontology: one camp treats a period boundary as requiring a discrete causal regime with identifiable boundary conditions, while the other treats it as explanatory scaffolding that organizes narrative without carving history at its joints.
