Following a lengthy argument with a friend last evening about whether Frank Ramsey’s dissolution of the meaning-of-life question can be reconciled with a normative theory of life-planning, I want a consistency audit of the package I ended up defending. Here are the commitments I take to be in the spirit of Ramsey, or at least consistent with his broader framework.

First, I hold that asking “What is the meaning of life?” involves a syntactic category mistake: the predicate “meaning” is being applied to lives as though they possessed the logical form required to bear it, comparable to asking how heavy the number seven is. The correct philosophical response is dissolution, not discovery of a hidden property.

Second, once the pseudo-question is dissolved, what remains is a genuinely practical problem: how to rank competing life-plans by the preference-satisfaction they yield over time. I want to treat this as an application of subjective expected utility theory—rational choice maximizes expected preference intensity over one’s remaining lifespan, with probabilities assigned by one’s credences.

Third, I also claim that certain goods—specifically, sustained open-ended engagement in mathematical or aesthetic inquiry—have lexicographically infinite value relative to ordinary welfare goods. Any finite cost, including the complete forfeit of alternative life-plans, is rationally acceptable to secure them.

Fourth, I accept Ramsey’s redundancy theory of truth: to call a belief true adds nothing to asserting the belief itself. Consequently, I reject any interpretation of “life has meaning X” that treats meaning as a metaphysical property we might discover.

Fifth, I think evolutionary debunking arguments undermine our intuitive weightings of life-plans in general, but I exempt judgments about lexicographically infinite goods from debunking, on the grounds that these are not evolved heuristics but rational axioms of preference ordering.

Sixth, I hold that when two life-plans both promise infinite value, rational choice is still salvageable through a second-order stability rule: select the plan that survives calm, fully informed reflection without generating a preference reversal.

My question is whether these six commitments actually hang together. In particular, does the introduction of infinite value in the third commitment break the decision-theoretic framework in the second, in the way that undefined expected values undermine standard choice rules? Does the fifth commitment’s exemption for infinite goods look like an ad hoc rescue that sits uneasily with the fourth commitment’s rejection of hidden properties? Is there a hidden tension between the first commitment’s dissolution strategy and the sixth commitment’s reliance on reflective equilibrium to resolve ties? Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish pairs that only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most problems with the least damage to the overall picture.
