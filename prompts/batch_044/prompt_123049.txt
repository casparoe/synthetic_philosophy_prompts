Consider this reconstructed line of reasoning from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), in the chapter on the ideal. Moore seems to claim that friendship, like beautiful objects, derives its value not from utility but from being an organic unity of appropriate mental states—specifically, that the pleasure taken in a friend's good qualities must be combined with the consciousness that those qualities genuinely belong to the friend, producing a value greater than the sum of its parts. At the same time, he insists that we cannot call a friendship good unless the mental states in question are actually present, which makes the value of friendship depend on a kind of epistemic access that beauty also requires, though apparently in a different modality.

I am trying to understand whether Moore is advancing a single theory of value that applies uniformly to both aesthetic and personal goods, or whether he implicitly distinguishes two kinds of organic unity—one perceptual, one interpersonal—that share a formal structure but differ in how they resist substitution or simulation. In particular, does his requirement that the mental states be genuinely present mean that friendship is more vulnerable to value-degradation through misrepresentation than beauty is, or is the vulnerability identical in both cases? I do not need a survey of Moore scholarship; I want help working through the logic of the passage itself, especially whether the same account of presence and combination can cover both domains without collapsing them into one.
