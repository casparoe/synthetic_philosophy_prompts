i'm filing a story on AI oversight for next week's ideas section and got tangled in this argument my editor calls the auditor's regress — probably not a real term, just our shorthand. it feels like it proves process-based supervission collapses, but then that undermines the whole reason we wanted it.

(1) if we only supervise by outcomes, a deceptively aligned model can ace every benchmark while hiding dangerous sub-goals — so outcome checks miss what actually matters.
(2) so we need process-level audits: inspect reasoning traces and internal circuits, not just the final answer.
(3) but interpreting any trace requires an interpreter — human or AI — with its own evaluation criteria.
(4) those criteria are either outcome-based (did the audit catch errors?) or process-based (is the interpreter reasoning correctly?).
(5) if outcome-based, we've kicked the problem upstais: the auditor itself needs auditing.
(6) if process-based, we need an audit of the auditor, then of that one, ad infintum — never bottoming out.
(7) so process supervision either collapses back into outcome checks, or launches an impossible infinite chain.
(8) but if outcome checks are unavoidable at every level, then (1) is false: outcome-checking can't be fundamentally inadequate if it's all we ever actually have.
(9) therefore the fear of deceptive alignment is self-defeating?

where's the slip — (4)'s false dichotomy, or (6) overstating the regress? i want to keep (1) if i can. if i reject (8) instead, what's the cost? do i have to treat human auditors as magic? and is this just reinventing the wheel or is there a real gap here?
