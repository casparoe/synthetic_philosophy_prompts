I am grading exams for our graduate seminar in metaphysics and epistemology of mathematics. The exam question was: "State Benacerraf's dilemma for mathematical Platonism and evaluate whether a modal-causal theory of epistemic access—according to which knowledge of an abstract object requires only that there be a metaphysically possible world in which a concrete instance of the relevant mathematical structure stands in a causal relation to a counterpart of the knower—can resolve the access problem without collapsing into physicalism or eliminative structuralism. Be precise about which horn of the dilemma is affected."

Below is one student's answer.

Benacerraf's dilemma demonstrates that mathematical Platonism is incompatible with a causal theory of knowledge. The first horn holds that if numbers are abstract, causally inert objects outside spacetime, then human knowers cannot enter into causal relations with them, leaving mathematical knowledge unexplained. The second horn holds that if numbers are concrete spatiotemporal entities, then standard arithmetic is false, since the physical world does not contain infinitely many objects and cannot realize the large cardinalities required by set theory.

The modal-causal accessibility thesis proposes to resolve this by maintaining that knowledge of an abstract object requires only that there exist a metaphysically possible world in which a concrete instance of the relevant structure is causally linked to a counterpart of the knower. This allows the Platonist to retain abstract numbers in the actual world while explaining epistemic access through modal mediation.

This thesis addresses both horns of Benacerraf's dilemma. Against the epistemic horn, it replaces direct causation with modal causation, so the absence of actual causal contact with abstract objects is no longer fatal. Against the ontological horn, it avoids the scarcity objection because concrete numerical instances exist only across modal space rather than in the actual spatiotemporal universe, leaving arithmetic unconstrained by physical resources.

That said, the view risks collapsing into eliminative structuralism. Because our knowledge depends on structural instantiations in other worlds, it appears that what we know are not the abstract numbers themselves but merely the positions they occupy within structures. A Platonist might respond that structures depend for their existence on the abstract objects that realize them, but this reverses the dependency relation that structuralists defend. Furthermore, as Field's formulation of Benacerraf's problem emphasizes, the proposal still owes an account of how we access the modal facts that identify the correct instantiating worlds; without such an account, the explanation is circular, since identifying those worlds already requires mathematical knowledge. Therefore, although the modal-causal approach significantly weakens the epistemic challenge, it does not fully dissolve Benacerraf's dilemma.
