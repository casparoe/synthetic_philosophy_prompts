I am writing a novel in which a future society governs itself through an explicit ethical calculus, and in constructing its rules I have become fixated on a particular structural feature for which I lack a name. The calculus treats pleasure and suffering as generally comparable quantities—citizens accept that a great deal of joy might justify a moderate amount of pain, and the system employs continuous weights to adjudicate ordinary trade-offs. Yet there exists a sharply delineated class of extreme suffering—let us call it abyssal anguish, though in the story it carries no special technical label—such that once its intensity crosses a precise threshold, the calculus no longer permits any amount of pleasure, however vast or widely distributed, to offset it. Crucially, the very same kind of anguish, if it remains just below that threshold, is treated like any other negative value and can be balanced against happiness without restriction. The boundary is not gradual; it functions like an absolute barrier that activates only above a certain magnitude, whereas below that magnitude the two kinds of experience are weighed together on a single scale.

I have encountered philosophical arguments about whether intense suffering can ever be compensated by happiness, and about whether pleasure and pain share a common measure at all, but the specific configuration—continuous weighing below a sharp line, followed by an abrupt, non-compensable prohibition above it—seems more particular than those broad debates. Does this structure possess an established name in moral philosophy? If so, how precisely is it distinguished from a simple unconditional prohibition regardless of intensity, and from the view that all experiences of pleasure and suffering can always be traded against one another? Should no settled term exist, I would welcome a precise coinage together with its boundary conditions—particularly whether the threshold applies to individual experiences, to aggregate totals across a population, or to both—so that I may render the calculus in my narrative with the appropriate conceptual rigor.
