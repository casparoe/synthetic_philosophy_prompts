I hold a modified Keynesian view of the good life, drawn from his 1930 essay on economic possibilities but updated with a structural claim I term the "status-leisure bind". Keynes predicted that within a century the economic problem would be solved, leaving people free to work roughly fifteen hours a week and to devote themselves to leisure, friendship, and aesthetic cultivation. I accept that advanced economies have technically reached this abundance: universal basic comfort could be sustained with far less labour than is currently performed. Yet the predicted leisure society has not arrived, and I claim this failure is not economic but social-coordinative. In competitive labour markets, occupational prestige functions as a positional good; reducing work hours signals low productivity and invites status loss, so leisure is stigmatised rather than cultivated. Contemplative leisure therefore remains socially inaccessible except to the independently wealthy, and the obstacle to Keynes's vision is not insufficient growth but the absence of credible non-positional status markers.

What would count against this position? I want an honest assessment of its exposure—not a catalogue of familiar objections to Keynes, but a map of which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would genuinely damage the "status-leisure bind" account, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability.

Please begin with a one-sentence bottom line summarising how exposed the position is overall. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate what the evidence or the world would have to look like, and distinguish clearly between what a committed defender of the bind would concede as counting against them and what they would dismiss by reinterpretation or insulation. Include whether there are predictions this view makes that rival explanations—such as that people genuinely prefer income and consumption to leisure, or that regulatory capture alone explains long hours—do not share, and whether those predictions have been tested. Be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated and that this is, or is not, a problem for a thesis of this kind. I am particularly interested in whether evidence from societies with strong work-time regulations, where leisure is legally protected but status competition may migrate to consumption or visible side-projects, counts as decisive against the bind, or whether the thesis can absorb it without damage.
