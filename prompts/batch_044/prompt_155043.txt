I was sitting with two friends from my book club who got into a surprisingly heated argument about whether things stay themselves when their parts change. The first friend, Mira, started with her grandmother's patchwork quilt: over forty years every single square had been replaced with new fabric, yet she insisted it was still the very same quilt because the pattern, the family stories tied to it, and its continuous presence in the house made it identical to the original. Her friend Leo pushed back hard—he said that's just emotional attachment talking, and if every fiber is physically different then what remains is at best a faithful reconstruction, not the original object, no matter how similar it looks or how beloved it is.

Mira then mentioned a clay statue she had seen at a local museum that had suffered water damage, and conservators had replaced about half of the original clay with a synthetic compound that looked identical. The museum still labeled it as the original work, not a replica. Leo said that was just institutional politeness; if you replaced half the substance, you have a restored hybrid, and calling it identical is like calling a ship with all new planks the same ship. Mira countered that Leo was ignoring the form and the history—the statue never disappeared, it was continuously maintained, and its shape and location in the world never broke, so of course it's the original, just with some repairs.

Then Leo raised what he called his knockout case: imagine the replaced clay from the statue had been saved, stored in a box, and years later an artist reshaped that exact original material into a completely different sculpture. Now you have two objects—the museum statue with half new material, and the new sculpture made of the actual original clay. Leo said this proves the statue was never identical to its clay, because if it were, you'd have the same thing existing in two places at once, which is impossible. Mira replied that this actually proves her point: the museum statue is the real one because it maintained continuous form and social recognition, while the reshaped clay is just raw material that used to belong to something else, like cutting hair off a person and saying the hair is still them. They then turned to a case Mira invented: a violin that had every wooden part replaced over centuries, but the remaining original wood had also been chemically treated so that its molecular structure was altered but technically derived from the first tree. She argued this means both the original form and the original material are present, so it's doubly the original; Leo argued that chemical transformation severs material continuity, so it's neither the original wood nor a simple replacement, but something in between that breaks both theories.

I don't have philosophical training, but I want to understand who is right about which specific claim here—the quilt, the museum statue, the split-clay scenario, and the chemically treated violin. Is there a consistent way to decide when an object survives part-replacement, or do Mira and Leo both have to give up something? And is there a hidden assumption they're both missing about what makes something the same thing over time?
