I've been trying to map my own gut reactions about education onto something like Locke's framework — not the broad strokes, but the actual mechanics of teaching — and I keep ending up with a pile of verdicts that don't obviously fit together.

Case by case: letting a kid sit with a wrong math answer for a whole day before you correct them feels right, like the confusion is doing real cognitive work. Hiding every book about death from an anxious child feels wrong — it stunts the reasoining faculty by shielding them from hard concepts. Making them practice writing left-handed just to build discipline feels wrong too, like arbitrary hardship with no educational purpose. But letting them go outside without a coat, feel cold, and then talk through why — that feels right, classic expreiential learning. Rewarding honesty with candy feels corrupting, like it replaces an internal motive with an external one. But praising them specifically for catching a contradiction in their own argument feels exactly right.

Then there's the case I keep flipping on: a tutor makes a student defend a position they clearly don't hold, purely to exercise logical form. Is that good mental gymnastics, or does it teach that reasoning is just performance?

Is there a coherent principle — maybe Lockean, maybe not — that captures the clear verdicts? What does it predict for the logic-defense case? And if I have to drop one of my gut reactions to keep the principle clean, which one should go? I'm not looking for a bibliography or quotes from Locke's education writing, just whether there's an actual principle here or I'm just being inconsistent.
