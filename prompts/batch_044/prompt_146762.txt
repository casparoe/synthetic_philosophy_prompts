I have been thinking, without any formal training, about Douglas Hofstadter’s suggestion that an “I” is not confined to a single brain but can persist as a pattern inside other people who knew it well—something like a melody continuing in instruments after the first player has stopped. I want to explain this idea to a book-club group of curious adults with no philosophy background in about forty minutes. Do not explain the concept to me; instead, plan exactly how I should teach it. First, set out the dependency order: which distinction—perhaps between a physical brain and a replicable pattern—must be established before the claim about a distributed self can even be heard as plausible rather than mystical, and what should come immediately after. Second, I want to carry the explanation with a scenario I have invented: two elderly twins who have kept a single handwritten journal for fifty years, writing alternating entries in one shared voice so thoroughly that neither can identify which sentences are theirs, and then one twin dies. Tell me what this example must include and what it must deliberately lack if it is to illuminate Hofstadter’s view rather than reduce it to mere shared memory or grief. Third, map the misconceptions my audience is likely to form—my guesses are that they will think this is only a poetic way of saying we remember dead people, or that consciousness literally moves like electricity between skulls—and specify the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Fourth, propose one concrete question I can ask at the end to test whether they have grasped the conceptual claim rather than just been moved by the imagery. Fifth, identify which part of Hofstadter’s broader argument—perhaps the technical machinery about symbols and loops—I should explicitly set aside at this level and openly say I am leaving out. The plan should be precise enough that I can walk into the room with it.
