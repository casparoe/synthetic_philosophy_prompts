As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been reflecting on a design principle that seems to govern how I feel about distributing costs and benefits across users in distributed systems. I want to know whether there is a coherent moral principle behind my intuitions, or whether some of them have to be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, when a security patch requires all users to accept a brief, non-destructive service delay—approximately two minutes each—to prevent one specific user from suffering permanent data corruption, I think applying the patch is clearly permissible. The cost is spread so thinly that it feels like a shared maintenance burden rather than a sacrifice of one party for others.

Second, if the same vulnerability could be fixed by permanently revoking access for one uninvolved user, leaving the rest entirely unaffected, that strikes me as impermissible. Even if the total disutility is lower—one person loses access rather than five hundred lose two minutes each—I feel we are not permitted to solve our problem by concentrating all harm on a single party who gains nothing from the trade.

Third, during overload, our load-balancer randomly drops one connection in every thousand, preventing total cluster failure. This is standard practice and feels acceptable.

Fourth, an engineer proposed an alternative protocol that identifies the connections with the lowest account tenure and deterministically drops one hundred percent of those connections, achieving exactly the same statistical reliability. Even though the aggregate numbers are identical, targeting users by a specific attribute feels wrong in a way that random dropping does not.

Fifth, there is a case that troubles me. Suppose we apply the distributed-delay patch from the first case, but we learn in advance that, due to an unforeseen interaction with a legacy client, one particular user will suffer not two minutes but ten hours of outage, while the other four hundred ninety-nine suffer only the brief delay. If we do not know in advance which user it will be, applying the patch still seems permissible—the harm is an unfortunate side-effect of a fair distribution. But if we do know in advance that it will be this specific user, applying the patch feels morally different, closer to deliberately assigning them the ten-hour burden. Yet the aggregate outcome—four hundred ninety-nine brief delays plus one ten-hour outage—is numerically identical to a policy that deliberately assigns the ten-hour outage to that user and spares everyone else. I cannot reconcile why foreseeability should change the verdict, but my intuition insists that it does.

Is there a single principle—perhaps related to what philosophers call the separateness of persons—that captures all five verdicts? Or must I drop one? Specifically, I want to know whether the principle turns on the distribution of harm, on whether victims are identifiable in advance, on whether harm is assigned rather than risked, or on something else entirely. And what does it say about a case I have not considered: if all five hundred users consent in advance to a protocol that randomly assigns one of them a ten-hour maintenance window each quarter, does consent override the objection?

Please state the principle or competing principles precisely, indicate which of my verdicts each can accommodate, and be honest about which verdict must be sacrificed for the sake of simplicity.
