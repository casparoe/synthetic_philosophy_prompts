I cover philosophy and the sciences for a general-interest publication, and I am presently researching how advanced students negotiate classic problems in epistemology. For a forthcoming feature, I have been permitted to review anonymised examination scripts from an upper-level university module on knowledge and justification. One particular answer has left me uncertain whether its coherence masks a subtle technical error, and I should value a rigorous external audit before I characterise student reasoning in my article.

The examination asked candidates to explain the bootstrapping problem for reliabilist accounts of perceptual justification, with explicit reference to Stewart Cohen's discussion of 'easy knowledge', and then to evaluate whether Cohen's distinction between basic and non-basic knowledge genuinely blocks the circular inference that allows a subject to derive knowledge of her own non-envatted status from ordinary perceptual beliefs alone.

The script reads as follows. 'The bootstrapping problem for reliabilism is that a belief-forming process cannot justify its own reliability without circularity. If my perceptual faculties are reliable, then my belief that I have hands is justified; but I can then observe that these beliefs consistently cohere with my other experiences, and from this infer that my faculties are indeed reliable, and hence that I am not a brain in a vat. This uses the outputs of perception to validate perception itself. Cohen's concept of easy knowledge shows why this is troubling. He argues that basic perceptual knowledge—such as knowing one has hands—requires no prior justification. If we combine this with a closure principle, we can easily derive knowledge that sceptical hypotheses are false. Because this makes defeating scepticism far too simple, Cohen proposes that basic knowledge must be restricted: it cannot be combined with self-knowledge or closure principles to generate inferences about reliability. In my view, however, Cohen's restriction merely relocates the circularity. By deciding which beliefs count as basic, the reliabilist must already possess a prior grasp of which processes are trustworthy; otherwise the classification would be arbitrary. Consequently, the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge are essentially identical—both express the same circularity in different terms—and Cohen's distinction between basic and non-basic knowledge fails to solve it, instead burying the circular step inside the conditions for basicness itself.'

Could you please assign a specific grade—say, out of twenty, with an appropriate classification such as first-class, upper-second, or lower-second—and provide a point-by-point assessment? I need to know precisely which argumentative moves are sound, where there is a subtle misrepresentation of Cohen's position, whether the student conflates two related but distinct difficulties, and whether the final conclusion actually follows from the premises given. I am not seeking a general impression but a detailed justification that an examiner could cite in a marking report.
