I serve on a three-person municipal ethics advisory panel for a mid-sized city (population approximately 400,000) that is revising its substance-use policy framework. Our mandate is evaluative rather than legislative: we must assess concrete proposals—maintaining prohibition with expanded pre-arrest diversion, adopting a Portugal-style administrative decriminalization model for personal-use possession, or legalizing cannabis through licensed supply with regulatory caps—before they advance to the city council. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Philip Pettit's republican theory of freedom as non-domination and its distinction between arbitrary and non-arbitrary interference, that my panel can apply in roughly ninety minutes. The procedure should proceed in ordered stages: first, identifying whether a proposal creates, removes, or restructures powers of arbitrary interference over users, suppliers, and affected communities; second, testing whether institutional safeguards (transparency, contestability, proportionality, and reversibility) render any remaining interference non-arbitrary; third, assessing outcomes through a capabilities lens—specifically, which real freedoms are expanded or contracted—without reducing this step to a utilitarian aggregation. For each stage, specify what evidence would count as sufficient, where the procedure must halt with a negative recommendation, and where it necessarily yields to judgment because the issue is one of normative values rather than institutional design. Include a compact set of test scenarios for classifying borderline cases, and flag any point where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) retaining criminal prohibition for possession but instituting mandatory diversion-to-treatment for first offenses; (2) decriminalizing possession for personal use and substituting administrative referral for criminal sanctions, modeled on Portugal's framework; and (3) legalizing cannabis through state-licensed private retailers with age restrictions and potency limits. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields an unwelcome result—specifically, that a prohibition regime with robust judicial oversight and clearly defined statutory limits may qualify as non-arbitrary interference, whereas a legalization regime with discretionary licensing and weak mechanisms for public contestation may not. Finally, please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on republican freedom and non-domination from what is genuinely contested—particularly whether commercial supply under legalization can avoid arbitrary domination by market actors, and whether applying the capabilities approach to drug policy inevitably imports contested comprehensive moral doctrines—and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
