I'm in a reading group working through Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, and we got stuck on their claim that analogy-making is the core of cognition rather than one tool among others. One member kept reaching for the analogy of jazz improvisation: a musician takes a familiar harmonic structure — the source — and maps it onto an unfamiliar melodic situation — the target — with meaning arising from the tension between recognition and novelty. She argued this captures exactly what Hofstadter and Sander mean, that even apparently rule-bound deduction is just high-level analogical transposition with the mapping made explicit. Another member said that romanticizes pattern-matching and countered with the Lego-brick analogy: cognition assembles discrete conceptual units via fixed combinatorial rules, with no essential blending or transposition, just explicit construction from parts with predetermined connection points.

Can you audit both analogies? For the jazz comparison, lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the chord progression, the melody, the listener's recognition, the error-correction of a wrong note, and the improviser's agency — then identify where it breaks, especially regarding explicit rule-following, falsifiability, and whether surface similarity ever drives the mapping rather than deep structure. For the Lego comparison, map the bricks, the instruction manual, the builder, and the completed model onto cognitive elements, and show where it fails to capture the fluidity and context-sensitivity that Hofstadter and Sander emphasize. Finally, tell me whether either analogy actually supports the conclusion that there is no non-analogical core to reasoning, or whether both analogies are doing hidden work — importing features like creativity or determinism that the target domain of cognition may not share — and if neither survives intact, suggest which disanalogies matter most for the philosophical claim.
