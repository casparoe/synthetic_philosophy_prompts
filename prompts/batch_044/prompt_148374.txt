In a reading group on practical rationality that I have been convening for over a year, we have reached a strange kind of stalemate about the inside view versus the outside view that feels different from ordinary disagreement. The two positions are articulated with precision every session: one camp insists that reasoning from the specific details of your own case—your particular competencies, the idiosyncratic obstacles, the local institutional context—is not merely permissible but rationally required, and that treating yourself as an interchangeable draw from a reference class amounts to a kind of self-alienation in which you ignore the very features that distinguish your decision problem; the opposing camp holds that without an outside view anchored in base-rate frequencies any “adjustment” you make is almost certainly post-hoc rationalization, and that epistemic modesty demands you begin with the statistical record and only deviate under extraordinary evidential conditions. We have all read the standard forecasting and planning-fallacy literature, we share the same examples—career changes, research-project timelines, predictions about organizational success—yet no one has shifted position, not because anyone lacks counterarguments but because the arguments seem to travel on parallel tracks without collision. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about predictive methodology at all, but about two incompatible conceptions of epistemic modesty: one treating modesty as a first-order attitude toward the content of one’s beliefs (you should weaken confidence because you know you are biased), the other treating it as a second-order procedural constraint (you should outsource judgment to an external algorithm or base rate because your reasoning apparatus is unreliable regardless of what you think about this case). Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about the actual calibration of experts in specific domains, or about whether inside-view adjustments systematically improve or degrade accuracy—that we have failed to isolate? I want an honest structural diagnosis: is this a substantive normative disagreement about the authority of first-person reasoning, a verbal dispute in which “modesty” and “inside view” are being used for different conceptual jobs, or are the sides answering different questions—one about justification, one about expected accuracy—under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result specifying when inside-view adjustments are rationally licensed, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, or should we conclude that the disagreement is bedrock and ask instead how to hold both norms in tension without resolving them?
