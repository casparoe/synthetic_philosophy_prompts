For a philosophy-of-mind seminar on temporal consciousness, write a taxonomy in tree form that sorts positions on the color phi phenomenon and on whether conscious experience is ever retrospectively revised. Begin with a branch asking whether the phenomenon reveals a genuine temporal anomaly in experience itself, as opposed to an artifact of report, memory, or post-hoc inference. From there, separate Orwellian revisions (the phenomenal content of the prior moment is altered after the fact) from Stalinesque revisions (a false representation is inserted into access or memory without changing the prior experience). Add a third branch for predictive-binding views, which hold that intermediate qualitative representations are generated prospectively within a narrow integration window rather than inserted retrospectively—this hybrid is rarely included in standard two-way taxonomies. At deeper nodes, use concrete diagnostic claims: whether revision applies to phenomenal quality or only to temporal ordering; whether there is a minimal integration window (approximately 100–200 ms) within which retrospective alteration is impossible; and whether the subject retains first-person authority over the pre-revised state. Each leaf should carry a position name—retrospective phenomenal revisionism, access-level Stalinesque confabulation, predictive-binding minimalism, deflationary no-anomaly, or “unoccupied”—plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and include at least one logically coherent combination that appears to be defended by nobody, with a brief diagnosis of why the space is empty. Phrase every branching question so that a respondent can answer it without already knowing the philosophical labels.
