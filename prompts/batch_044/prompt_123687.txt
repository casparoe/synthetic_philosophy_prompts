I have noticed a recurring ambiguity in mechanistic interpretability studies of anomaly detection, and I am unsure whether it has an established name. Consider two neural networks with different architectures—say, a transformer and a linear recurrent state-space model—both trained on the same distribution. Both develop an internal process that suppresses activation spikes caused by out-of-distribution inputs. In the transformer, this process is implemented by a specific attention head that computes a divergence metric in key-query space and applies a scaling factor. In the state-space model, the identical behavioral suppression is produced by a contraction along a particular eigenvector in the linear state-transition matrix. The two implementations share no structural homology: different layer types, incomparable parameter shapes, no corresponding nodes in the computation graph, and no visible algorithmic sub-routine in common. Nevertheless, they are causally interchangeable in the same functional role—ablating either eliminates the suppression, and both respond to the same class of anomalous inputs with quantitatively similar effects.

I have observed analogous pairings elsewhere: one architecture uses an MLP gate, another uses a modified normalization layer, yet both perform the same anomaly-rejection job with no structural correspondence. My question concerns the precise conceptual status of this relationship. Is there an established term—perhaps in philosophy of science, cognitive science, or interpretability—for two mechanisms that are functionally and causally equivalent in performing a specific anomaly-detection task, yet structurally and parametrically dissimilar across architecture families? I know that multiple realizability and functional equivalence are nearby, but those concepts seem either too broad or anchored in debates about mental states rather than in engineered circuits with matched ablation profiles and input-output signatures. There is also mechanistic equivalence in neuroscience, but that usually presupposes some shared organizational principle, which appears absent here.

If no precise term exists, I would like a clear distinction: how exactly does this differ from mere behavioral convergence, where two systems reject anomalies for entirely unrelated internal reasons? And what boundary conditions should govern whether we call two such anomaly-detection implementations the same mechanism rather than different mechanisms that happen to do the same job?
