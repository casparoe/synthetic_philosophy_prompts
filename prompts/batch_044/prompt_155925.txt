Following a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about occupational segregation, I find myself stuck on a conceptual problem rather than an empirical one. We were discussing the well-known pattern that societies with the strongest gender-equality infrastructures—universal childcare, long shared parental leave, robust pay-transparency laws—often exhibit larger gender differences in career clustering than societies where economic pressure forces both sexes into similar occupational tracks. My friend insisted this must reveal innate preference distributions that only emerge once coercion is removed; I countered that it might instead show gender being performed more intensely through vocational identity when material survival is no longer at stake. At some point we realized we were not really disagreeing about the data but about which interpretive framework should govern it, and that the same correlation was serving as the central exhibit for three incompatible stories: a biological-release narrative, a cultural-expression narrative, and a justice-as-procedure narrative that treats any remaining disparity under equality of opportunity as voluntary and therefore beyond remedy.

What I want to name, if it exists, is not the gender-equality paradox itself, but the specific argumentative configuration we kept falling into: a single cross-national regularity functioning as an unavoidable but conceptually indeterminate pivot among theories of nature, culture, and distributive justice. In this configuration, the correlation between policy equality and occupational divergence does not merely underdetermine theory in the ordinary sense, because each framework assigns opposite causal directions—nature revealed versus culture amplified versus autonomy exercised—and opposite normative implications for whether further intervention is justified, unnecessary, or even patronizing. Is there an established term for this kind of evidentiary structure, in which one empirical pattern becomes a shared premise for mutually exclusive causal and normative interpretations? If there is no specific name, how should one delineate it from standard underdetermination, from the non-identity problem in policy evaluation, and from simple equivocation on the word "choice"? I am less interested in which interpretation is correct than in whether the pattern of interpretation itself has been mapped, and where exactly its edges lie.
