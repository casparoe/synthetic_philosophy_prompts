I have been working through a version of David Lewis’s modal realism for a graduate seminar, and I have constructed a chain of reasoning that seems to show far more than I intended—namely, that counterpart theory eliminates every essential property of every actual object, including self-identity. Here is the argument as I have it. (1) On modal realism, for me to have been taller is for there to exist a world containing a counterpart of me who is taller. (2) Counterparthood is governed by appropriate similarity: one object is a counterpart of another just in case they share enough of the properties that matter for cross-world identification. (3) Now suppose that similarity is graded and context-sensitive; in some contexts intrinsic physical features weigh heavily, in others relational or causal ones do, and the weighting is not uniquely fixed in advance. (4) Take any property P that I actually possess. Because the weighting is graded and context-sensitive, for any world w there will be some legitimate weighting of properties such that an object in w that lacks P is still similar enough to me to count as my counterpart. (5) So for every property P, there exists a world—under some permissible weighting—where my counterpart lacks P. (6) Therefore I have no essential properties whatsoever: for each property, there is a possible world in which I, via my counterpart, lack it. (7) Apply (4)–(6) to the property of being identical to me. There will be some weighting and some world in which my counterpart is not identical to me but is only highly similar; hence even self-identity fails to be essential to me. (8) But if nothing is essential to me, then there is no constraint on which objects in other worlds count as me; counterpart theory collapses into arbitrary assignment, and modal realism cannot distinguish the claim that I could have been a musician from the claim that some numerically distinct person with my body could have existed. (9) Thus modal realism either eliminates all de re modality or renders it vacuous, undermining the very phenomenon it was designed to explain. (10) Therefore we must reject at least one premise.

I find step (4) particularly suspicious—it looks like a slide from “similarity is graded” to “any property can be discounted in some context”—but I am unsure whether the real damage is done there, in (2), or in the move to (7).
