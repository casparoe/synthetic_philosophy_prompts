i run a philosophy seminar, about 12 grad students, 90 minutes. i want them to feel why Williams and Appiah think norms like dueling die from honor and coordination, not from people just being talked into it. design a structured role-play/game.

set it in a fictional "Guild of Translators" with a harmful loyalty ritual: whenever a junior mistranslates in public, every member must perform a loud ritual correction that humiliates the junior but proves guild solidarity. leaving the guild means career death. split roles: 3 elders (high status, control norms), 6 members (can act but risk expulsion), 3 juniors (affected, speak but dont vote).

timeline: round 1, 20 min open debate. anyone can argue the ritual is wrong. but the hidden rule is arguments alone never change anything. round 2, 25 min, members and elders secretly choose "break" on index cards: refuse the ritual publicly. breaking costs 2 reputation points; breaking alone costs 4 and the norm survives. if 4+ break together, norm collapses, everyone gets +5 collective points and breakers only lose 1. juniors can speak in round 1 but are silent in round 2.

round 3, 15 min: reveal cards, tally payoffs, sit in silence for 2 min with the result.

debrief, 20 min: map round 1 to rational persuasion (ask why nobody changed anyone's mind despite good arguments?), round 2 to honor dynamics (who broke? did elders go first? did anyone hide their card to avoid being alone?). ask what had to be true for the norm to actually fall. honest note: the activity cant reproduce the years of social death or the real economic stakes, so you need to supply that feeling verbally in the debrief. also say what will go wrong: people will try to negotiate in round 2 instead of choosing secretly, so use sealed envelopes. also juniors will dominate round 1 with moral outrage, which is realistic but can derail the mechanics—give them a 2-minute speaking cap.

what they should come away seeing: that moral change looks less like an essay winning and more like a risky coordination problem where status and visibility do all the work. keep it casual, maybe one typo is fine.
