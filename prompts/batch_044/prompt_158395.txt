I just came away from a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend holding a specific view about Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. The view is not just the familiar one that moral responsibility can survive without alternative possibilities, but something more structural: that Frankfurt-style scenarios never actually eliminate the kind of modal openness that matters for responsibility in the first place. On this account, the relevant alternatives are not the agent's own unexercised options, but a counterfactual stability in the decision architecture—specifically, the robustness of the agent's volitional pathway relative to the intervener's monitoring conditions. So PAP fails not because responsibility is compatible with causal closure, but because the "could have done otherwise" condition was always mislocated: it tracks whether the agent's psychology is counterfactually sensitive in the right way, and that sensitivity is preserved even when an external controller stands ready to intervene, since the controller's presence itself introduces a modal dependency rather than removing one.
