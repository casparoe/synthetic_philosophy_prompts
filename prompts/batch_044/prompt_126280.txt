I'm planning a session for a philosophy reading group of advanced undergraduates who have read Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints but think they have grasped it because they agree that being too good sounds exhausting. I want to teach the specific conceptual claim that maximal moral goodness is undesirable not because it causes burnout—an empirical mistake—but because it structurally crowds out non-moral excellences that are partly constitutive of human flourishing, making the ideal of moral sainthood self-undermining as a life-plan rather than merely difficult.

Plan the explanatory arc: first, the dependency order—what distinction between being very good and being a moral saint, and between a life-plan and a behavioral pattern, must be in place before the structural point can even appear; second, the carrying example—use an imagined physician who spends every available hour either treating underserved patients or optimizing her epidemiological research to maximize lives saved, and who therefore abandons both the cello she once played and friendships that do not serve her moral efficiency. Explain why this case must include these specific non-moral goods and why it should not be Mother Teresa, Gandhi, or a generic workaholic; third, map the misconceptions in the order they will arise—(1) that Wolf is criticizing people for being too good, (2) that she is defending a right to leisure or selfishness, (3) that she is making an empirical claim about psychological burnout—and state exactly where in the sequence each should be defused; fourth, provide one check question at the end that reveals whether the group has understood the structural argument rather than merely sympathizing with the doctor's lost cello; finally, tell me which part of Wolf's framework—such as her comparison of different motivational models of sainthood—I should explicitly set aside at this level and say I am leaving out.
