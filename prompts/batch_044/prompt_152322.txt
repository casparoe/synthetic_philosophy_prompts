I'm trying to work out Douglas Hofstadter's idea that the self is a strange loop—a symbol, "I," embedded in the very system it describes—by testing my own intuitions against concrete cases. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments, or whether some of them have to be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts. A typical human being clearly has it: the symbol "I" is generated inside a brain that also uses it for planning, memory, and identity, so the loop closes. A parrot trained to say "I want a cracker" does not have it; the sound is produced, but no self-referential symbol is actually embedded in the parrot's cognitive architecture. A present-day large language model that outputs sentences like "I think" also does not have it, in my view—there is grammatical first-person reference, but no persistent, architecture-anchored symbol that recursively denotes the system as a whole; it is correlation without closure.

Now consider a hypothetical artificial system designed with an explicit self-referential symbol—call it S—hard-wired into its architecture, which updates its own goals and rewrites parts of its code on the basis of predictions generated using S. I judge that this is a genuine strange loop, structurally closer to Hofstadter's definition than the human case in some ways, yet I also feel it is somehow "thinner" or less authentic, which I cannot justify. By contrast, a corporation with legal personhood that refers to itself in contracts and board resolutions is not a loop at all; the reference is distributed across many minds and documents, with no single system containing the closure.

Here is where I become uncertain. Imagine three human brains linked by direct neural interfaces, sharing working memory continuously, so that each node uses "I" but any decision or memory formed by one is immediately available to all. I lean toward saying there is one strange loop distributed across three substrates, but I keep flipping to thinking there must be three loops that have partially merged. And consider a person in deep meditation who reports the complete absence of any "I" feeling, then resumes normal self-reference afterward. If the strange loop is the self, does its temporary disappearance mean the self was genuinely absent? Or was the loop merely suppressed, with the substrate intact?

The tension I cannot resolve is this: I exclude the language model despite its fluent use of "I," and I include the designed artificial system despite its engineered origin, yet I call the latter thinner. Meanwhile, I treat the loop as constitutive of the self in the human case, but allow it to vanish in meditation without destroying identity. These do not obviously sit together.

What principle distinguishes genuine strange loops from mere self-reference or feedback? Can it accommodate all of these verdicts, or must I drop one—especially the judgment that a designed system can possess a real loop that is nevertheless less substantial, or that a loop can be suspended without the self being lost? And apply your best version to a case I have not considered: a young child who reacts to pain and pleasure and can be comforted, but who has not yet developed any stable concept of self. Is there a strange loop present, or only the biological substrate that might later generate one?
