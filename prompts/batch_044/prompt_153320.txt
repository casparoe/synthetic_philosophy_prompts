I have been thinking through some ethical questions about food choices on my own, partly after a long conversation with friends about whether it is worse to eat beef or chicken. One argument I keep returning to compares the direct suffering of the animals raised for food: a cow lives for years, is physically large, and seems capable of more complex experience, so beef production appears to involve prolonged harm to a more developed being. The other argument looks past the farmed animals themselves and focuses on the feed required—chicken production depends on vast quantities of grain and soy, and the machinery, pesticides, and land conversion involved in growing those crops kill enormous numbers of wild rodents, birds, and insects. By that accounting, chicken might be responsible for far more total death and suffering per calorie, despite each chicken's shorter life.

What I am struggling to integrate is a distinction I have tried to formulate for myself, which I rarely see discussed in these debates. It concerns the moral difference between harming a being that already exists—such as the farmed cow or chicken, or a wild mouse killed during harvest—and altering an ecosystem so that certain wild animals are never born at all. Pasture for beef and fields for chicken feed both displace wild habitats, but I am unsure whether displacing a potential population counts the same as actively killing an existing one, or whether this distinction collapses when we think about future generations. Could you compare the direct-suffering framework and the crop-death framework, specifically addressing how each treats this boundary between actual harm to existing beings and the prevention of future wild lives? Do they rely on incompatible moral premises about what counts as harm, or can they be combined into a single evaluation of beef versus chicken?
