Take the Challenger launch call in January '86 and run it through the Principal Principle — the idea that your credence in an outcome should match the objective chance you know about. The engineers had cold-weather O-ring failure data pointing to a real physical risk, but managment went with a subjective "go" feeling. Map it carefully: who counts as the rational agent whose credence should defer, what exactly was the objective chance they either knew or ignored, and whether PP says the launch should have been scrubbed automatically once that chance was known. Then push on the institutional side — does PP apply to committees, or only to individual minds? And here's the twist: if the true failure rate was genuinely epistemicly inaccessible (not just suppressed), does PP stay silent, or does it still constrain belief? Don't just recount the disaster; use it to test whether deference to chance works when human organizations are the ones holding the beliefs.
