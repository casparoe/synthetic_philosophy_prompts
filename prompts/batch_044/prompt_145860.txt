I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on rational choice and epistemic symmetry. Write one essay question that students could answer in 90 minutes, aimed at testing whether they grasp superrationality well enough to apply it in a modified setting rather than merely summarizing Hofstadter's symmetric prisoner's dilemma. Introduce a constructed scenario — the Opaque Twin Dilemma — in which two agents share identical reasoning algorithms and all non-indexical beliefs, but each has direct first-person access to a private phenomenal state (for instance, her own present pain intensity) that shifts her utility function in ways the other cannot observe. Ask the student to determine whether superrational cooperation remains rational here, and require them to address three cruxes: whether privileged self-access breaks the symmetry required for superrational reasoning; whether the transparency condition in Hofstadter's argument applies to reasoning processes, mental contents, or both; and whether abandoning superrationality in this asymmetric case undermines it in the standard symmetric case. Provide a model answer and a marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves (total 25 marks), allocating specific marks to each, together with a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode for each move. The answer should also include a brief self-critical coda stating exactly which argument or empirical consideration — for example, a demonstration that first-person access is always representable as shared information, or a proof that symmetry can be defined over algorithmic structure alone — would change the verdict on whether superrationality survives the asymmetry.
