I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time thinking about J.L. Austin's claim that some sentences do not merely describe reality but perform actions — saying 'I promise' or 'I apologize' seems to be doing something rather than reporting something. For decades, philosophers have debated whether this distinction between performative and constative utterances is real or collapsible. Austin himself eventually blurred the line, arguing that even descriptive statements have performative dimensions and that performatives can be evaluated for felicity rather than truth. Since then, the literature seems to circle the same examples — wedding vows, bets, ship namings — without reaching agreement. Some maintain that there is still a deep structural difference in how these utterances are assessed; others treat the distinction as a pedagogical convenience that obscures a unified theory of speech acts.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because of a linguistic disagreement but because the two sides are answering different questions about agency. One side is asking whether an utterance can constitute an act without describing an inner mental state; the other is asking whether every meaningful speech event must be anchored in a speaker's conscious intention. The disagreement looks like it is about language, but I suspect it is really about what conditions must be met for something to count as an action at all.

This suspicion has become stronger for me when I think about cases that do not fit the classic human-speaker model: an AI system that outputs the words 'I sincerely apologize' in response to a user complaint; a biometric click that executes a contractual 'I agree'; or a smart-contract trigger that automatically transfers funds when a sensor detects delivery. These cases seem performative — they change legal or social states — yet there is no obvious human intention present in the moment of utterance. If philosophers keep framing the debate around a single conscious speaker, they may be missing that the real fault line concerns whether performativity requires a mind behind it.

I am not asking for a survey of Austin's followers or critics. Instead, I want a diagnosis of why this particular disagreement has persisted so long without resolution. Is it verbal — are 'performative' and 'constative' being used with different criteria? Is there a substantive empirical or conceptual question hidden beneath the surface, perhaps about the necessary conditions for agency or institutional fact-creation? Are the sides actually addressing different problems under one name? And if the disagreement is genuinely about agency rather than language, what kind of progress could resolve or dissolve it — a refined conceptual distinction, an analysis of institutional mechanisms, or a recognition that the original question was ill-posed?

In your answer, please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophy of language from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as if it were the consensus. I am especially interested in an honest assessment of whether anything — a new theoretical framework, a shift in the examples considered, or a clarification of 'action' — could actually move the debate forward, or whether the impasse is likely permanent.
