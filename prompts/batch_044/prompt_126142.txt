Two friends in my study group got stuck arguing about whether feeling cold counts as what philosophers call luminous — the idea that if you're genuinely in a mental state you're always in a position to know it — and whether the KK princple (knowing p entails knowing you know p) ends up saving that idea or destroying it.

Mara kicked things off by saying yes, it's luminous: feeling cold is direct first-person access, there's no hidden internal thermometer you could misread. Leo pushed back with the gradual-change problme: your core temperature drops slowly, so there's a borderline zone where you're sort of chilly but can't tell whether you've actually crossed into being cold; at that margin you lack knowledge, so luminosity fails right there. Mara replied that this only works if we smuggle in an empirical safety condition that doesn't apply to phenomenal states, and she added that applying KK here just doubles the direct access — knowing you're cold means knowing you know it, which for self-knowledge is just the same transparency repeated. Leo said that's backwards: near the boundary, KK creates an infinite regress, because knowing you're cold requires knowing you know, which requires knowing you know you know, and since you fail the first link at the margin the whole chain collapses, so KK undermines luminosity rather than protecting it. Mara then tried splitting the differnce by saying there's phenomenal luminosity (you always feel it) versus epistemic luminosity (you don't always know that you know it), meaning KK only hits the second layer without touching the first. Leo said that's just relabeling and doesn't answer whether the original mental state was luminous in any philosophically interesting sense.
