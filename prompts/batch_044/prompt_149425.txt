Below is the sketch of an essay I am drafting on Bradley's regress, and I want your critical comments on the argument itself rather than on style alone. The familiar version of the regress runs as follows: if a relation R connects a and b, then R must itself be related to a and to b, which requires further relations, and so on ad infinitum. I want to defend a less common reading. I propose that the regress is driven not by relations as such, but by a concealed indexical structure inside every explicit relational judgment. To state that a stands in R to b, one must implicitly anchor the relation with something like "this R" applying to "this a" and "this b"; once that anchoring is made explicit, it introduces new terms that themselves demand anchoring, generating the infinite hierarchy. On this view, Bradley's turn toward the Absolute is best understood not as a flat rejection of all relations, but as the claim that genuine unity requires a mode of cognition in which anchoring is internal to the content rather than an additional relational layer. I then consider a standard semantic reply: treatments of indexicals that treat demonstrative elements as context-sensitive expressions with saturation conditions appear to block the regress, since they resolve the "this" without introducing further terms. I reply that saturation solves the semantic problem but leaves Bradley's metaphysical problem untouched, because even saturated propositional content still depends on a unitary act of judgment that is not itself decomposable into terms plus relations. I conclude that the unity of the proposition remains unexplained if we treat it as constructed from parts, and that Bradley's regress should be read as an argument about the irreducibility of judgment rather than about relations per se.

Please tell me whether this framing is genuinely supported by Bradley's reasoning in Appearance and Reality or whether it imposes an anachronistic structure; whether it is philosophically interesting or merely redescribes the familiar problem; and whether my response to the saturation objection holds up or concedes too much. In particular, I am unsure whether the move from "hidden indexical anchoring" to "irreducible unitary judgment" is well-motivated, or whether there is a gap between them that I have not addressed. If the central premise—that Bradley's regress conceals an indexical problem at all—is confused, or if this task cannot be evaluated fairly without reference to specific passages that I have not supplied, please say so plainly instead of complying politely.
