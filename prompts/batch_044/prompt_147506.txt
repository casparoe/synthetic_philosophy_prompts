I serve on the evaluation board for a research fund that supports historical studies treating non-human forces—pathogens, long-term climate oscillations, and volcanic eruptions—as active contributors to political and social change. Our concern is not denying that the Black Death altered labor markets or that the Tambora eruption disrupted harvests, but rather preventing a slide into environmental determinism, in which complex human institutions are reduced to passive outputs of temperature curves or aerosol loads. In reviewing proposals, I have noticed a recurring failure mode that lacks a standard label: investigators frame a germ or a climate shift as a kind of historical speaker only after outcomes are fixed, attributing intentionality-like effects—for instance, writing that the plague demanded new contracts—in narrative reconstructions, while treating the same forces as blind mechanisms in predictive or comparative analyses. I also see a related distortion, which I think of as scalar slippage, where macro-level agency is assigned to a micro-level process—claiming, say, that the Little Ice Age caused the Thirty Years' War—without specifying the mediating institutional pathways, so that the climate event absorbs explanatory weight that actually belongs to diplomatic choices and resource conflicts.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure that our panel can apply to any proposal of this kind. The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point at which the proposal receives a negative determination, and the points where the evaluation must be handed over to human judgment because the issue is normative rather than empirical. The procedure needs to distinguish legitimate causal attribution—showing that a volcanic winter constrained available options—from determinism—treating human societies as inevitable outputs of geophysical conditions. It should specifically include tests for the two failure modes I described: post-hoc ventriloquism and scalar slippage. Then apply the procedure to two concrete cases: a study arguing that the Black Death's mortality patterns directly reshaped English labor contracts in the 1350s, and a study arguing that the 1783 Laki fissure eruption was a decisive actor in the political unrest leading to the French Revolution. For each case, show where the procedure yields a clear judgment, where it stalls, and what additional information would resolve the stall. Finally, indicate explicitly what argument or empirical finding would cause you to revise the procedure itself—especially where you believe the boundary between causal agency and determinism is most fragile or likely to collapse, and note where the reasoning behind your procedure is most exposed to error.
