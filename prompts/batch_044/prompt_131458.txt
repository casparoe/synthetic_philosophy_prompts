I'm taking a graduate seminar on epistemology, and a classmate and I have been stuck in a loop about pragmatic encroachment versus contextualism. We kept returning to a modified version of the classic bank case, but we added a layer involving scientific risk that I think exposes the real fault line. I want you to adjudicate our exchange move by move—rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating—then tell me who is closer to being right overall and whether the final distinction she introduces saves her position or collapses it. Here is exactly what we said.

(1) Jonas said: Picture a researcher who has carefully reviewed peer-reviewed studies showing that compound X is safe at dosage Y; she tells a colleague, "We know X is safe at Y," and they proceed. In version A, they run a low-cost pilot study; in version B, they administer it to human subjects with a fatal risk if the dosage is wrong. The evidence hasn't changed. Jonas argued that in B she does not genuinely know, because knowledge requires evidence proportionate to what's at stake—this is pure pragmatic encroachment, not just a shift in how we talk.

(2) Mira replied that Jonas is mislabeling his own view. On her contextualist account, the researcher bears the exact same mental relation to the fact in both versions; what changes is the conversational standard for correctly applying the word "knows." In B, error possibilities that are pragmatically irrelevant in A become salient, so attributing knowledge is false, even though her underlying epistemic position is identical.

(3) Jonas came back: If the difference is purely linguistic, then in version B she should still be rational to act on her belief, since her belief remains true and well-supported. But intuitively she ought to gather more data before exposing people to fatal risk. The fact that she should seek further evidence shows her epistemic standing is genuinely weaker in B, not that we are merely speaking more strictly. Contextualism cannot explain this normative push without smuggling stake-sensitivity back into knowledge itself.

(4) Mira answered by distinguishing epistemic rationality from practical rationality. In B, she should check more not because she lacks knowledge, but because the practical cost of error is catastrophic; her factive epistemic status is unchanged, but her all-things-considered reasons for action shift. She then added a two-tier twist: stakes affect the norms governing justified belief—how strong one's evidence must be to form a belief rationally—while leaving the conditions for knowledge proper untouched. Jonas immediately said this is ad hoc: if belief-formation norms tighten with stakes but knowledge conditions stay flat, then in B she could rationally withhold belief while still knowing, which is incoherent.

Please rate each of the four moves, explain who is more right overall, and address whether Mira's two-tier distinction holds up or collapses back into Jonas's encroachment view.
