As a novelist drafting a narrative in which economic rationality governs every social institution, I have become fixated on the practice of revealed preference analysis in welfare economics: specifically, the institutional convention by which an observer records that bundle A is strictly preferred to bundle B whenever a consumer selects A from an affordable set containing both, then aggregates these pairwise records into a social welfare ordering without ever interrogating whether the chooser possessed any mental state worthy of being called "utility." What philosophical commitments regarding the ontology of preference, the completeness and transitivity of mental states, and the legitimacy of interpersonal comparison does this practice silently presuppose? Can it survive if we treat revealed choices not as windows onto an underlying representational content but as context-dependent behavioral dispositions that are strategic, semantically empty, and potentially intransitive—much as some critics of rational-choice theory have suggested? If we replaced the standard revealed-preference framework with one that denied all introspective access to cardinal utility and treated every observed choice as a local, unstable reaction to framing, which components of modern policy evaluation—cost-benefit analysis, demand forecasting, algorithmic recommendation—would necessarily collapse, and which could be reconstructed on purely behavioral grounds without invoking any concept of welfare?
