I'm preparing the Q&A defense for a workshop presentation I'll deliver next month on practical ethics and normative decision theory. The room will hold about fifteen scholars: mostly moral philosophers with broadly consequentialist sympathies, but also two Kantians who specialize in moral status and intergenerational obligations, a virtue ethicist who has published on moral expertise and practical wisdom, and three formal decision theorists—one of whom is openly skeptical that "moral uncertainty" denotes anything more than ordinary empirical uncertainty about the effects of actions. Several attendees work on climate ethics and long-term governance, but none are AI ethicists, and the group generally treats bibliographic appeals with suspicion rather than deference.

The position I intend to defend is the following. When we face what I call structural moral uncertainty—uncertainty not about parameter values within a shared normative framework, but about which framework (consequentialist, deontological, contractualist, or virtue-theoretic) correctly governs obligations across generations—I claim that standard expected moral value maximization is the wrong resolution strategy. Instead, I propose a form of framework-sensitive deference: decision weights should be assigned not by flat prior probabilities over frameworks, but by assessing each framework's internal coherence with its own account of moral learning and revision. More specifically, I argue that frameworks which treat moral knowledge as progressively refined through institutional feedback loops—certain contractualist and virtue-theoretic accounts—should carry greater weight in long-horizon policy domains such as climate mitigation, because their internal dynamics allow for self-correction over timescales that match the problem. Frameworks that treat moral principles as fixed axioms updated only by empirical discovery, by contrast, should be weighted downward in these contexts. Applied to intergenerational climate obligations, this yields a demanding but non-absolute duty: stronger than standard expected-value analyses recommend, yet weaker than pure deontological prohibitions on causing harm.

Please draft my prep sheet: approximately eight objections, ordered by how likely they are to arise and how damaging each would be if left unanswered, each phrased in the precise vocabulary and tone that this particular audience—epistemologists of normativity, climate ethicists, and skeptical decision theorists—would actually use in the room. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for roughly two minutes of speaking time, together with a candid note on where that reply is weakest or most concessive. Be sure to include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely something that charges me with smuggling in a hidden consequentialist metric under the guise of "internal coherence," or with treating framework comparison as possible only after I have already assumed a meta-normative stance I have not defended. I do not want generic catalogues of decision-theoretic paradoxes; I want the objections that target the specific move from moral-learning coherence to policy weight, especially as it applies to obligations we hold toward people who do not yet exist and cannot participate in the feedback loops I treat as normatively salient. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; this is for oral defense, not a syllabus.
