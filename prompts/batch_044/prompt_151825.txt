You are delivering the eight-minute opening address at the inauguration of the Levinson Institute for Historical Ethics and Animal Welfare. The room is divided: one half is made up of scholars of medieval philosophy and comparative religion, who will immediately recognize references to Maimonides' treatment of sacrificial worship in the Guide for the Perplexed; the other half consists of veterinary ethicists, sanctuary directors, and benefactors with no background in that tradition. Frame the Institute's purpose—examining how societies gradually reform practices they cannot instantly perfect—around Maimonides' claim that animal sacrifice was never the divine ideal but a pedagogical concession: a transitional institution that preserved reverence for life within rituals a people was not yet ready to abandon. This claim must shape the speech structurally, not ornamentally. It should bar you from presenting any current animal-use practice as morally complete; it should allow you to praise incremental reform without requiring instantaneous abolition; and it should let you honor the scholars present for studying texts whose practical implications remain contested, without turning the ceremony into a seminar. Do not provide footnotes, citations, or reading lists. Do not quote any source verbatim; paraphrase freely without quotation marks. Write so that a listener unfamiliar with the Guide understands the philosophical move, while a specialist recognizes it precisely and finds no distortion.
