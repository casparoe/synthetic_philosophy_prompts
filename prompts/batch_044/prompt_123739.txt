I'm writing this for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on consciousness and introspection, and I want to understand why one particular exchange has become so immobile. For years, the debate between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness—the line running from Dennett through Frankish and Kammerer, who treat phenomenal properties as products of introspective misrepresentation, confabulation, or predictive-processing error—and the realist camp, including Chalmers, Block, and defenders of the phenomenal-concept strategy, has struck me as curiously static. Each side has well-rehearsed moves: the illusionist appeals to the unreliability of introspection, the explanatory sufficiency of access-consciousness, and the absence of phenomenal properties in functional architectures; the realist replies with the epistemic gap, the directness of phenomenal acquaintance, and the failure of any functional analysis to capture what-it-is-likeness. Yet in my reading group, no one has moved anyone in months, and the literature feels like a script performed from memory.

My working hypothesis is that this is neither a hidden empirical disagreement—there is no experiment both sides would accept as decisive—nor a purely verbal dispute about the word "phenomenal," since both camps agree on the target: the subjective, qualitative character of experience. Instead, I suspect the stall arises because the two sides operate with incompatible models of introspective architecture. The illusionist seems to assume a unitary representational system that can systematically misrepresent its own states, so that "phenomenal reports" are outputs of a single mechanism that can be calibrated, corrected, or suppressed. The realist seems to assume a direct, non-representational acquaintance relation that is not decomposable into sub-personal mechanisms and therefore cannot be "turned off" without destroying the very state it reveals. If this is right, they may be answering different questions—one about the etiology and reliability of reports, the other about the ontology of experience—while using the same vocabulary.

To avoid a generic "they talk past each other" diagnosis, I want to introduce a specific conceptual pressure point. Consider a hypothetical scenario—call it gradual phenomenal erosion—in which a subject undergoes targeted neurofeedback that slowly suppresses the neural correlates typically associated with phenomenal reports, while leaving all behavioral, cognitive, and access-level functions fully intact. The subject eventually reports, sincerely and consistently, that they no longer have any phenomenal experience whatsoever: no colors, no pains, no subjective presence. Yet they navigate the world, respond to stimuli, and discuss philosophy with perfect fluency. For the illusionist, this is precisely what the framework predicts: the misrepresentational mechanism has been calibrated away, leaving only accurate access-consciousness. For the realist, the case is either incoherent—if direct acquaintance is present, the report must be false—or it reveals a radical dissociation between phenomenal consciousness and its reportability, in which acquaintance persists but has become inexpressible.

Given this framing, I want a diagnosis of the debate's structure rather than an adjudication. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether such erosion is metaphysically possible—or is it a disagreement about what counts as a successful explanation of consciousness? Are the sides divided over a hidden empirical claim about the architecture of introspection, or over bedrock intuitions about whether direct acquaintance can be eliminated without behavioral change? More specifically, does the erosion case show that the question is ill-posed, asking whether phenomenal properties exist within a framework that already assumes either representationalism or direct realism? Or does it reveal a genuine formal or conceptual distinction—perhaps between phenomenal absence and phenomenal silence—that could restructure the debate?

Finally, what would count as progress? Would it require a new conceptual distinction about the conditions under which introspective reports can be decoupled from first-order states? A formal result about the architecture of self-monitoring? Or should we conclude that no progress is expected, because the disagreement tracks a choice between explanatory paradigms that are empirically equivalent and differ only in which costs—ontological or explanatory—we are willing to pay?
