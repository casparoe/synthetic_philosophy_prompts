I just finished a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether archaeology can genuinely reconstruct past beliefs or whether it only projects present categories onto silent objects. She pressed the point that inference is always overdetermined: a chipped blade might indicate butchery, ritual breaking, or accidental damage, and selecting one reading usually reveals the interpreter's institutional commitments more than any past intention. I tried to defend the reflexive rigor of linking physical traces to social claims, but I left feeling that the circularity—we need the belief to identify the object, and the object to establish the belief—is not a bug to solve but the actual condition of the work. Rather than settling this, I want a poem that performs the tension.

Write an eighteen-line poem divided into three six-line stanzas. Use the refrain "The shard holds what we have taught it to mean" exactly once in each stanza, altering its grammatical subject or tense so that in the first stanza the speaker is the object, in the second the archaeologist, and in the third neither or both, leaving the voice undecidable. The first stanza should work through functional and use-wear inference—residues, edges, microscopic traces—without deciding between utilitarian and ritual causation. The second should move to symbolic interpretation—orientation, deliberate breakage, burial context—showing how the same physical feature becomes evidence for a belief only after the belief is already assumed. The third should expose the institutional frame—the museum storage protocol, the funding narrative, the peer-reviewed caption—that makes either prior reading legible at all. Do not resolve the circularity; let the poem end with the refrain in its ambiguous form, so the reader cannot tell whether the object, the scientist, or the gap between them is speaking.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone in archaeological theory would recognize the specific problems—the overdetermination of functional traces, the hermeneutic circularity of belief reconstruction from material culture, and the reflexive role of institutional practice—rather than treating archaeology as generic mystery. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific scholars by name. Write in American English.
