Two members of my philosophy of physics reading group have been deadlocked over whether the Rietdijk-Putnam argument actually establishes eternalism, and they asked me to relay the exchange for an outside adjudication. The first, Mara, laid out the standard move: because special relativity makes simultaneity frame-dependent, there is no uniquely correct plane of simultaneity, and therefore no observer-independent "present" that could serve as the ontological boundary required by presentism; if event E is simultaneous with my "now" in one inertial frame but lies in my past or future in another, presentism must either arbitrarily privilege one frame or deny reality to events that other frames treat as present, both of which are unacceptable. The second, Leo, replied that this confuses epistemic accessibility with ontological structure: the conventionality thesis already shows that simultaneity is partly a matter of coordinative definition, so presentists can consistently adopt a conventional global hyperplane of simultaneity and declare that plane alone ontologically privileged, without claiming any empirical discovery about which frame is "right." Mara pushed back that such conventional presentism collapses into a merely linguistic stipulation—if the present is just whatever we label with our coordinate choice, then presentism loses its metaphysical bite and becomes indistinguishable from eternalism coupled with an indexical preference for one slice.

Leo then introduced a more concrete wrinkle that neither of them could resolve. He asked what happens if we apply the argument not to point-observers but to spatially extended objects—say, a kilometer-long spaceship accelerating through different reference frames—so that different physical parts of the very same entity fall on different planes of simultaneity depending on frame choice. If the Rietdijk-Putnam reasoning holds, then in one frame the ship's bow is "present" while its stern is already past, and in another frame the reverse holds; but if presentism requires a single global present, this seems to generate an intra-object ontological contradiction rather than merely inter-frame disagreement. Mara conceded the tension but argued it actually strengthens the block view: the fact that a single extended object can be "stretched" across ontologically distinct temporal regions shows that treating any slice as uniquely real is incoherent for macroscopic bodies, so presentism must retreat to point-sized present moments, which is physically implausible. Leo countered that this only follows if one assumes presentism must assign a single temporal property to every part of an extended object simultaneously—which begs the question against a relational presentism on which "being present" is a local, part-relative relation rather than a global predicate.

Please go through their exchange move by move. For each stage—Mara's initial argument, Leo's conventionalist reply, Mara's response about stipulation, Leo's extended-object challenge, and Mara's counter—rate whether the move is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or genuinely ambiguous, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether the Rietdijk-Putnam argument succeeds, and identify what both are missing: in particular, whether there is a principled distinction between "frame-relative simultaneity" and "frame-relative ontology" that neither has fully articulated, and whether the extended-object case introduces a genuinely new difficulty or merely dramatizes the old Andromeda-paradox intuition.
