I'm running a 90-minute workshop on consciousness for about twelve postgraduates — mostly philosophy, a couple from cognitive science — and I want them to feel the force of panpsychism's combination problem rather than just talking about it. The idea is that if every tiny part of matter has its own micro-experience, we still need an explanation of how those add up to my single big experience, and I think a game might make that gap visceral. So I'd like a structured classroom activity with rules, roles, and a debrief, but I need you to build in a specific constraint I'm calling the "transparency rule" and a "binding narrative" task that I've sketched below.

Split the group into four "micro-subject" pods of three. Each pod receives one minimal phenomenal quality — not an emotion, just something like "a faint reddish warmth at the left boundary" or "a downward pressure without spatial location". Their job is to merge into one macro-subject that genuinely accomodates all four qualities as a unified field, but they can only communicate verbally, with two hard rules. First, no analogies: they can't say "it's like heat" or "like being pushed"; they must report the quality in its own terms. Second, the transparency rule: once a pod writes down its initial description, they may only change one word during the negotiation phase; if they alter more, the "phenomenal bond" is broken and that quality is excluded from the macro-subject. Meanwhile, the macro-group must produce a single binding narrative that explains why these four belong to one subject rather than four seperate subjects sitting near each other. I'd like specific roles within each pod — say, one "skeptic" whose job is to argue that merging is metaphysically impossible, and one "binder" who defends the narrative — plus a clear timeline: maybe twenty minutes for private description, thirty for negotiation, ten for presentation, and the rest for debrief.

The debrief should map the breakdown moments onto the philosophy: the failure to translate private qualities onto the problem of phenomenal concepts, the skeptic's resistance onto the "subject-summing" objection, and the transparency rule onto the thought that phenomenal properties don't combine like physical parts. Please also be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — likely the actual ontological mystery, since here the failure is communicative and social rather than metaphysical — and say how you'd handle predictable chaos, like pods refusing to compromise or the macro-group producing a story that's obviously just a list. Don't include any reading lists, citations, or bibliographic references; the group won't have texts to hand and I don't want them guessing at names. Could you lay out the full activity, timeline, roles, rules, debrief questions, and the honest limitation?
