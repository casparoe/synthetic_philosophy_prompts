People in the Anthropocene discussion keep using this analogy: picking a start date is like deciding the "official" birthday of a social movement. The ideas were around earlier, but you need one public, measurable event to put on the birth certificate — so Crutzen and company point to the 1950 radionuclide spike as the certificate, while the Orbis-spike folks say 1610 is the real conception and 1950 is just late paperwork. The analogy gets used to argue that the ICS vote on 1950 is arbitary, like picking the day a movement incorporated instead of when it actually started. Can you audit how far this holds? Map out exactly what corresponds to the "ideas circulating," the "public event," the "certificate," and the "certifying body" — then say where it breaks, especially since rock layers don't have intentions or awareness. Does it smuggle in the assumption that there's one true origin we're merely documenting, when actually stratigraphers might be constructing a practical boundary (a GSSP) rather than discovering a pre-existing fact? And does that matter for the conclusion that the 1950 date is "just politics"? If the analogy collapses, what's a better one for thinking about how the ICS actually negotiates these lines — maybe something that captures the institutional, somewhat political nature without treating it like a court ruling on history? Also, does the analogy hide anything about who gets excluded when we center radionuclides over, say, colonial land-use signatures?
