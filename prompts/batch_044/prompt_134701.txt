For an upper-level seminar or philosophy workshop of about 16 participants (graduate students or advanced undergraduates), with 90 minutes available and no expectation of specialized historiographic training, design a structured in-class deliberation with explicit game mechanics that makes participants feel, through their own negotiations and choices, the philosophical difficulty of holding a nation-state collectively responsible for the outbreak of the First World War in light of the Fischer controversy.

The central tension they should run into themselves, before it is named in debrief: attributing robust blame to Germany requires constructing and defending a long counterfactual dependency chain—each link claiming that if not for a specific German decision or institutional feature, then a necessary condition for the war's outbreak would have failed—while simultaneously accepting that every such link competes with structural alliance dynamics, contingent events, and the independent actions of other states. The rules should make it mechanically impossible to maintain both a high national-blame score and full acknowledgment of these competing factors, so that participants experience the suppression of contingency as a cost they must actively pay, not as an abstract theoretical move.

Structure the session in three phases with tangible materials (cards, tokens, or a shared board). Phase one (15 minutes): silent individual allocation. Each participant receives 20 responsibility tokens to distribute across five categories—intentional state policy, institutional military structure, systemic alliance dynamics, contingent diplomatic failures, and actions of other nations—plus a separate national attribution score from 0 to 100 that they must justify with reference to their token distribution.

Phase two (45 minutes): collaborative chain-building in assigned roles. Assign roles: a German chancellor's adviser, an Austrian foreign minister, a Russian general staff officer, a British cabinet secretary, a French president, a Serbian nationalist representative, and two independent historians with veto powers. The group must collectively construct a linear counterfactual chain of no more than six causal links connecting a specific German institutional or intentional factor to the outbreak of war in August 1914. Each proposed link requires spending one epistemic confidence point from a collective pool of exactly eight points; the pool is deliberately insufficient to cover all proposed links if structural and contingent categories are also represented. Once a link is bought, it is placed on the board; links that are not bought remain visible as unsecured alternatives. At the midpoint (after three links), the facilitator introduces a hidden contingency card—for example, an unanticipated diplomatic note or a local commander's unauthorized mobilization order—that breaks any chain lacking redundant pathways, forcing the group to rebuild or abandon their attribution.

Phase three (20 minutes): binding vote and justification. The group must vote on a single national attribution score and write a two-sentence collective justification. The two historians may exercise veto power over any score above 60, but exercising the veto costs them three reputation points from a private pool; they must decide whether the epistemic risk is worth the cost.

Use non-monetary stakes: the collective justification will be read aloud in the debrief; each participant receives three reputation points that determine speaking order in the debrief, and these can be gained by successful negotiation or lost by veto overuse.

In the debrief (10 minutes), map each mechanic onto the philosophical problem: token allocation onto distributive versus aggregative blame; the confidence-point scarcity onto the epistemic burden of counterfactual historical judgment; the contingency-card interruption onto the structuralist critique central to the Fischer debate; the historians' veto onto the observer's problem of judging past collective agents; and the forced trade-off between high national scores and acknowledged contingency onto the conditions for collective moral agency.

State explicitly what will go wrong on the first run: participants will likely collapse all tokens onto intentional state policy and rush to a high national score to avoid negotiation failure, ignoring structural categories; or the historians will hoard veto power and deadlock the vote. Give precise fixes—e.g., begin with a private commitment round before role assignment to prevent groupthink, and cap historian veto use at one per session.

Finally, include an honest limitation: the activity can reproduce the epistemic and structural pressures on collective blame, but it cannot reproduce the temporal asymmetry of judging dead agents who cannot respond, nor the normative weight of retrospective national identity formation. The facilitator must supply these verbally, perhaps by asking participants to imagine the vote being published as an official historical judgment rather than a classroom exercise.

Do not request bibliographies, citation lists, or specific page references. Do not ask participants to quote verbatim from Fischer or other historians. You may assume familiarity with the general shape of the Fischer controversy—intentionalist explanations emphasizing German war aims versus structuralist explanations emphasizing systemic forces—but do not depend on untranslated titles or precise publication dates in the activity design.
