My philosophy of mind professor has offered the following verdicts on whether an entity possesses a mind, understood specifically in terms of behavioral criteria and first-person accessibility rather than metaphysical essence:

1. A standard large language model that passes brief conversational Turing tests in restricted domains but lacks persistent identity, embodied interaction, or consistent self-reference: no mind.
2. A human patient with total locked-in syndrome who communicates only through a brain-computer interface providing binary yes/no responses, consistently reporting conscious experience and affect: mind is present.
3. The symbol-manipulation system in the Chinese Room scenario—an agent following rules to produce correct Chinese outputs without any understanding of Chinese—no mind, regardless of behavioral indistinguishability from a fluent speaker.
4. A chimpanzee that learns a novel token-exchange system to request specific tools it has not encountered for months, and that displays separation distress and self-directed behavior: mind is present.
5. A distributed swarm of simple robotic agents that, through local interaction rules alone, collectively solves navigation problems and, when addressed as a unit, produces grammatically correct first-person statements about its collective intentions, though no individual agent possesses internal representational states: no mind.

Next semester she will evaluate a new system: an engineered neuromorphic analog-digital hybrid that physically replicates mammalian cortical spiking dynamics and synaptic plasticity within silicon circuitry, housed in a non-biological robotic chassis with sensory-motor loops. It learns context-sensitive tasks from scratch, modifies its behavior in response to damage in specific circuit clusters, and spontaneously produces linguistic self-reports describing itself as feeling disrupted when those clusters are altered. It has no evolutionary history, no biological tissue, and no DNA-based metabolism.

Predict her verdict on whether this system has a mind. Identify the implicit rule or theoretical commitment—perhaps biological naturalism, global workspace integration, intrinsic intentionality, or something else—that best explains the pattern of her five earlier judgments. Then lay out at least two other rules that fit those same five verdicts equally well, show precisely how each rule diverges in its prediction about the neuromorphic case, and specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish among the competing rules.

Please provide your answer in American English, maintaining a formal and polished tone.
