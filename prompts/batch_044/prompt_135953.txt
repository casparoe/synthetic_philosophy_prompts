You are delivering the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop, "Where Legend Ends and History Begins," convened jointly by a university history department and a museum of archaeology. The audience is split evenly: academic historians and archaeologists on one side, and on the other clergy, museum donors, and community educators for whom at least one of the cases you will discuss carries living religious or cultural weight. You have been asked not to review the literature but to frame the day's inquiry, and to do so with conceptual precision rather than with a popular summary.

The philosophical idea that should structure your speech—not decorate it—is what you will call an "evidentiary phase shift": the claim that a narrative does not gradually dissolve from history into legend, but crosses a discrete threshold when the governing question changes from "Does this cohere with other received narratives?" to "What material trace would this leave, and what do the presence, absence, or ambiguity of that trace actually license us to claim?" Use this framework structurally for three specific touchstones—the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. For each case, identify the particular condition under which the evidentiary frame shifts, and show how that shift constrains what can be said: not by resolving whether the event "really happened," but by clarifying that the rules for asking the question are different on either side of the threshold, and that crossing it imposes obligations that narrative coherence alone cannot satisfy.

Your remarks—approximately six to seven minutes spoken—must land for both halves of the room. The specialists should recognize that you are advancing a precise point about the ontology of historical knowledge and the asymmetry between textual transmission and material discontinuity. The non-specialists should be able to follow the argument without encountering disciplinary jargon, and they should feel that their traditions are being engaged seriously rather than being dismissed as pre-critical. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to specific scholars; the speech must stand entirely on its own conceptual architecture. End by stating what the workshop can and cannot hope to settle, given where each of the three cases currently sits with respect to that threshold.
