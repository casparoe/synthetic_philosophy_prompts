I'm preparing interview questions for a philosophy podcast episode with a researcher who defends a revisionist reading of the Knobe effect. She argues that the standard harm/help asymmetry in judgments of intentional action is not driven by moral evaluation per se, but by what she terms "normative contamination"—a specific interaction between norm-violation detection and whether the side-effect figures in the agent's plan as a means rather than as a mere byproduct. She claims that conventional vignettes confound these dimensions because they always present the harmful outcome as a foreseen but unintended byproduct; she predicts that if the harmful outcome were redesigned as a necessary means to the agent's stated end, while the beneficial counterpart remained a byproduct, the asymmetry would reverse rather than merely weaken.

Draft eight to ten questions that go beyond asking her to restate the view. I want an opening question that forces her to specify the mechanism by which means-status interacts with norm-violation detection, with a follow-up pressing whether the interaction is multiplicative or threshold-based. Include a question about the control conditions required to isolate this effect from generic affective priming or salience asymmetries, keyed to her most likely reply that matched-valence vignettes already control for affect. Add at least one question on how her account handles the original Knobe vignettes where both harm and help are byproducts—does she deny the asymmetry exists there, explain it away, or treat it as a separate phenomenon? Include a follow-up anticipating her defense of a modular moral-psychology architecture, asking whether that modularity distinguishes her view from standard moral-evaluation accounts or collapses her "normative contamination" into them. End with a closing question about what specific experimental result—perhaps a null reversal, or a reversal that tracks means-status regardless of moral valence—would genuinely disconfirm her thesis rather than prompting only a parameter adjustment. Keep the sequence tight and suitable for a 45-minute interview.
