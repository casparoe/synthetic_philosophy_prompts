In my philosophy of mind seminar we have been working through the symbol-grounding literature—Harnad’s original problem, the computationalist reply that functional role-relations are sufficient, the embodied-cognition insistence on sensorimotor coupling, and the Searle-style intuition that syntax never yields semantics—and the discussion has stalled in exactly the way the literature has: everyone repeats the same moves without concession. I want a diagnosis rather than another survey. Are the sides answering one question about whether formal systems can have intrinsic intentionality, or two different questions—one semantic, one about phenomenal understanding—under the single heading 'grounding'? Or is the disagreement substantive but hidden behind different standards for what counts as 'direct' causal contact with the world?

To make this concrete, consider a hypothetical case my instructor raised: a silicon-based receptor array that correlates chemical compounds with non-linguistic internal states, then binds symbolic tokens to those states, so that the symbol 'benzene' is causally tethered to the compound without biological mediation or linguistic definition. One camp treats this as solved grounding; the other treats it as relocated symbol-manipulation, arguing that the receptor-state is just another uninterpreted symbol unless an interpretive consciousness is present. Is this stalemate a verbal dispute about 'directness,' a substantive disagreement about the necessary conditions for intentionality, or an unargued bedrock intuition about consciousness?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about causal-informational chains, an empirical behavioral difference, a conceptual distinction that splits the problem, or an argument that no resolution is possible because the question is ill-posed.
