I've come to accept that counterfactual history is conceptually parasitic on a linear model of inevitability that path-dependent systems don't support. If history is genuinely branching—more like a tree in which no trajectory is modally privileged—then "the actual world" is one realized node among structurally equivalent branches, and "closeness to actual history" collapses as a criterion for counterfactual validity. This dissolves the usual defense that counterfactual reasoning works when it stays near the facts, since there is no privileged line to stay near. What would genuinely count against this position—not just the standard causal-inference reply, but which borderline near-miss events, which formal results about robustness in branching models, and which intuitions about explanation or responsibility depend on there being a single actual path? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender should concede as damaging rather than dismiss as insulation.
