I am designing a session for a graduate seminar in moral psychology and institutional design—twelve students, ninety minutes—after a recent experience facilitating restorative justice mediation left me unsettled about whether guilt and shame are culturally selectable instruments or structurally determined outcomes of deeper metaphysical commitments. I want an in-class simulation, which we might call the Moral Repair Protocol, in which participants must experience the constraint rather than merely discuss it. Divide the group into two factions representing communities recovering from collective harm: one faction may design repair mechanisms using only guilt-structured resources—private moral accounting, contractual restitution, and agent-centered forgiveness—while the other may use only shame-structured resources—public recognition of status injury, ritual reintegration, and collective witnessing. The critical design feature should be a hidden Ontological Framework card distributed to each faction that specifies whether the self in that community is treated as a stable moral ledger or as a performatively reconstructed social position; these cards remain concealed until after both factions have finalized their protocols, at which point they are revealed and the factions must negotiate whether their emotional architecture actually coheres with the metaphysics of personhood they implicitly adopted. Include explicit rules for cross-faction negotiation, a payoff scheme based on internal coherence and mutual recognizability rather than money, and materials simple enough to run without technology. The debrief should map each phase—the initial design, the hidden-card revelation, and the negotiation failure or success—onto the philosophical question of whether a culture can genuinely choose its dominant moral emotion, or whether that choice is already fixed by prior commitments about what a person essentially is. Please also predict what will go wrong the first time I run it, particularly the predictable confusion between emotional vocabulary and institutional form, and be frank about which feature of strong cultural constructivism about emotion this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce without direct facilitator supplementation.
