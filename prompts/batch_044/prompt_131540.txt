Question: The Axiom of Choice (AC) is frequently characterised as a logical convenience with no ontological weight, yet it is also criticised for violating a principle of ‘constructive accountability’—the demand that asserting the existence of a mathematical object requires an explicit rule of construction. Meanwhile, Cohen’s forcing results establish that AC is independent of ZF. Evaluate whether independence genuinely addresses the constructivist objection, or merely sidesteps it. In your answer, explain the relationship between AC, the well-ordering theorem, and the Banach-Tarski paradox, and say whether AC should be regarded as philosophically optional.

Student answer:
“The Axiom of Choice states that for every collection of non-empty sets, there exists a choice function selecting one element from each. Cohen’s forcing shows that AC is independent of ZF, meaning it can be consistently added to or removed from the axioms without generating contradictions. Consequently, adopting or rejecting AC is a purely technical decision with no deeper philosophical significance.

AC entails the well-ordering theorem, which asserts that every set can be arranged in a well-order. This is important because it extends transfinite induction to all sets, not merely the countable ones.

The Banach-Tarski paradox demonstrates one counterintuitive consequence: a solid sphere can be decomposed into a finite number of pieces and, using rigid motions alone, reassembled into two spheres of the original radius. This result depends on AC, specifically in selecting representatives from equivalence classes of points under rotation. In fact, the well-ordering theorem and Banach-Tarski rely on essentially the same mechanism—both apply choice to arbitrary sets in order to construct objects that cannot be explicitly defined.

The claim that AC violates constructive accountability is therefore answered by independence. Because AC introduces no logical inconsistencies, there is no formal defect in using it; the constructivist preference for explicit rules is merely an aesthetic or methodological commitment to one axiom system over another. Since well-ordering and Banach-Tarski both flow from the same source, anyone who accepts AC for the former must also accept the latter. Thus AC is philosophically optional: mathematicians may work in ZFC or ZF according to practical taste, and the paradoxes it produces are only paradoxical if one has already assumed that every object must be constructively specified.”
