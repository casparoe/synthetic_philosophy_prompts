I work as a monitoring officer for a mid-sized international development fund that supports community infrastructure in regions where formal contracts coexist with kinship obligations and ceremonial exchange. Over the past year I have kept running into the same problem: our compliance manual treats corruption as a deviation from explicit, universal rules—bribes, kickbacks, conflicts of interest—yet in the partner communities I visit, resources and authority flow through reciprocal gift-giving, clientelist networks, and labor obligations that sustain social life but look suspiciously like favoritism to our auditors. I am trying to build a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with clear stop points and hand-offs—that my small review team could run on any field report within an hour, without legal training and without having to resolve deep philosophical questions from scratch each time, since we review about two dozen reports a quarter and board sign-off is required within 48 hours. The procedure should draw on the conceptual distinctions that actually matter in this space: whether a practice violates an explicit institutional mandate or only an implicit universal norm; whether gift exchange creates exploitative dependency or expresses legitimate reciprocal care; and whether clientelism undermines impartial allocation of public goods or substitutes for state functions that are absent. At each step it should specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure halts with a definitive finding that intervention is not warranted, and where it must pass the decision to human judgment about values rather than facts. Please include explicit notes on where the procedure will misfire, what information it demands that we rarely have, and which questions it cannot settle on its own.

Apply the procedure to three concrete field reports. First, a contractor who delivers a ceremonial livestock gift to the extended family of a local water committee chair shortly after being awarded an irrigation contract. Second, a village elder who distributes drought-relief supplies primarily within his kin network, leaving no formal record but creating an expectation of future support in local disputes—here the procedure should reach a clear finding, even if it is one that feels uncomfortable because it treats traditional authority as corruption. Third, a system of communal labor for maintaining shared canals organized through reciprocal obligations that looks like clientelist favoritism to our auditors but should not be interrupted—show me precisely where the procedure protects it, and where it breaks down and requires a values judgment. Be honest about the hard cases, especially where Western assumptions about impartiality may be driving the analysis more than local practices of obligation and reciprocity do.
