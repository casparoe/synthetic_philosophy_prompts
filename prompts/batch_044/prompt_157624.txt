Over the past year I have been navigating treatment for a chronic autoimmune disorder that, despite persistently abnormal biomarkers, has settled into a remarkably stable functional equilibrium—what clinicians might call well-controlled, yet what remains objectively pathological. This personal encounter with a body that is simultaneously impaired and adapted has made the philosophy of medicine unexpectedly urgent. I am drafting an essay that asks whether the familiar opposition between naturalism and normativism about health and disease can survive close examination of such cases—conditions that I tentatively describe as exhibiting stabilized dysfunction, in which a biological system maintains a statistically deviant but durable baseline, and in which the patient's subjective welfare is ambiguous because genuine adaptation and residual harm appear inseparable.

The essay's working thesis is that naturalist accounts, which identify disease with deviation from species-typical biological function, cannot easily classify a sustained abnormal equilibrium that does not progress toward failure; meanwhile, normativist accounts, which tie disease to a reduction in welfare or in the capacity for vital goals, risk collapsing the distinction between an objectively harmful state and a subjectively accepted one, particularly when technology or time allows the patient to flourish within the abnormal range. I want to argue that these cases expose a hidden temporal and technological dependency in both frameworks: what counts as restored function versus compensated dysfunction seems to depend on the duration of the state and on the availability of prosthetic or pharmacological mediation, neither of which is internal to the concept of health as traditionally analyzed.

The essay remains in outline form, and I am writing to request focused philosophical commentary rather than clinical or bibliographic guidance. Specifically: first, does the category of stabilized dysfunction genuinely fracture the naturalism/normativism dichotomy, or does it merely relocate the dispute into questions about the appropriate reference class for statistical normality and about the weight we should assign to temporal persistence? Second, is there a principled conceptual distinction between physiological adaptation to a new baseline and normative resignation—that is, between genuinely integrating an abnormal state and merely lowering one's expectations to match it? Third, might my argument depend on an unexamined assumption about what counts as a unified biological system, particularly in conditions where technological intervention becomes functionally indistinguishable from endogenous regulation?

Please respond with substantive criticisms, suggestions for sharpening the conceptual distinctions, and indications of where the reasoning seems weakest.
