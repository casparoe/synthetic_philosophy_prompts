At our study session yesterday, Maya and Jake got into it over whether knowing something automatically puts you in position to know you know it — the KK principle, I think? — and this thing called luminosity that I think Timothy Williamson went after.

Maya opened with: if I genuinely know my coffee is hot, then I should be able to tell I'm in a state where I know it's hot. Otherwise what's the point of calling it knowledge? She said denying that is like saying you can be fully rational but have zero access to your own rationality, which feels wrong.

Jake came back with the fading-light case: imagine you're in a room and the lights dim exactly one lumen at a time. At some point you stop knowing it's bright, but there's no specific tick where you notice the change. So right next to a case where you don't know, there's one that's practically identical and you do know. If luminosity held, you'd have to discriminate those neighbors, but you can't. So knowledge isn't luminous, period.

Maya said that's just about perceptual boundaries, not knowledge in general. She proposed a restricted KK principle: it only applies when your evidence is "discretely packaged" — like knowing you just read a sentence — not in continuous-gradient cases like brightness. She said Jake was overgeneralizing from sorites-style vagueness.

Jake replied that the restriction is totally ad hoc. Once you admit knowledge without self-access in boundary cases, you've already broken KK. Then he dropped something I hadn't heard before, he called it "epistemic shadowing": the idea that second-order beliefs can lag behind first-order ones by a small margin without being irrational, like a shadow trailing the object. He said this is why we feel shaky about whether we "really know" something even when the first-order belief is rock solid.

Maya countered that "shadowing" just renames the gap. If there's a principled lag, then KK fails for those cases; if there isn't, Jake hasn't shown anything. She also said he was conflating "being able to discriminate adjacent cases" with "being in a position to know" — they're not the same thing.
