I am a high school student working on a rational-choice essay. Write a formal dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—call them A and B—debating whether backward induction genuinely dissolves the paradox in the centipede game. A should defend the standard view applied to a variant I call the “hesitation-cost centipede”: past the second node, continuing carries a small but publicly observable social penalty if the game eventually ends in defection, so rational agents must weigh not only monetary payoffs but also the cost of being seen as naively cooperative; she should argue that even with this added layer, backward induction still forces an immediate stop at node one. B should resist, claiming that this very variant exposes a hidden premise in the inductive argument—what he calls “epistemic closure” about a player’s knowledge of her own future rationality—because once you allow that a player can rationally revise her beliefs about her own future reasoning (for instance, by recognizing the signaling value of an early stop), the chain of common-knowledge reasoning fractures and continuation becomes defensible. They must respond directly to each other’s actual points: B should answer A’s signaling argument, and A should answer B’s claim about self-updating belief, rather than talking past one another. If my framing of backward induction as requiring “epistemic closure,” or my “hesitation-cost” variant itself, rests on a confused premise—say, if backward induction makes no such assumption about self-knowledge or if the signaling mechanism is incoherent—say so plainly instead of constructing the dialogue as though the confusion were valid.
