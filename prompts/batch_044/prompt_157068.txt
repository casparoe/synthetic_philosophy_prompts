Question: Cross-cultural moral psychology—particularly the WEIRD thesis—documents that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations are psychological outliers, weighting harm and fairness more heavily and loyalty, authority, and purity less heavily than many other societies. Some philosophers take this descriptive variation to threaten moral objectivism: if intuitive moral judgments diverge systematically across cultures, they argue, there is no culture-independent fact of the matter about which weights or domains are correct. Evaluate this anti-objectivist argument. In your answer, clearly distinguish (i) descriptive variation in moral weights from (ii) normative disagreement about which weights are correct, and address whether a thin universal core—say, widespread aversion to direct physical harm—can ground a robust objectivism for the remaining domains. Additionally, apply the distinction between axiological fragmentation (variation in which values are recognized as moral at all) and deontic divergence (variation in how recognized values are ranked when they conflict). Does introducing this distinction weaken the anti-objectivist argument, or does it merely relocate the problem?

Student answer:

Cross-cultural studies show that WEIRD populations are outliers in emphasizing individualizing foundations like harm and fairness over binding foundations like loyalty and authority. This is a descriptive claim, not a normative one, so it doesn't by itself prove that moral relativism is true—people in different cultures can be wrong about morality just as they can be wrong about physics. That said, moral objectivism requires that there be a single correct ranking of moral considerations; since cultures disagree not only on weights but on whether loyalty and purity are moral domains at all, there is no convergence on the correct view. The thin universal core of harm-avoidance does appear everywhere, which supports a limited Kantian objectivism for harm and fairness, since Kant's categorical imperative is essentially about treating harm-avoidance as a universal rational requirement. For the other domains, axiological fragmentation shows that some cultures don't even recognize loyalty as a moral value, while deontic divergence shows that those who do recognize it rank it differently. Both patterns indicate that loyalty and purity are culturally constructed rather than objective. So objectivism survives only in a narrow, harm-based form, and the distinction between fragmentation and divergence doesn't save it—it just shows that the cultural construction happens at different levels.
