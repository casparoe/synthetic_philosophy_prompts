After a long argument with a friend about whether Nozick's entitlement theory survives the Wilt Chamberlain example, I want a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—that sorts people by their actual commitments rather than by the labels they prefer. Build the tree around concrete variants of the Chamberlain sequence: begin with a node asking whether a voluntary exchange, taken in isolation, can ever justify a macro-level distributive shift regardless of background conditions; if yes, branch on whether historical entitlement is preserved through arbitrary-length exchange chains or whether cumulative effects can eventually erode it, with sub-branches for inherited talent and intergenerational transfer; if no, branch on whether the decisive objection concerns an unjust baseline (left-libertarian), a lexically prior pattern (Rawlsian/patternist), or an incommensurability between micro-justice and macro-justice (call this the incoherence view). For intermediate logical spaces—such as dynamic patterning, where distributive patterns are evaluated only over moving temporal windows rather than at endpoints, and threshold entitlement, where exchange remains legitimate only until inequality crosses a domain-specific boundary—include them as named or unnamed leaves, each carrying a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Every node should be phrased as a concrete question answerable without prior knowledge of philosophical taxonomy, and the tree should map out the full logical space of answers: flag any combination that remains unoccupied (for instance, accepting both full historical preservation and lexical pattern priority) and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere lack of exploration. The deliverable should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on obscure historical details; it should function for someone reasoning about the case from common knowledge alone. Aim for eight to ten leaves.
