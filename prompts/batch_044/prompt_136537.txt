I cover philosophy of mind and the history of ideas for a long-form magazine, and I'm drafting a proposal for a one-semester interdisciplinary fellowship. The project addresses Avicenna's flying-man argument, but I want to move past the standard dualism-versus-embodiment stalemate. Instead, frame the suspended figure as an early attempt to isolate minimal phenomenal selfhood—the bare first-person presence that survives when sensory content, bodily boundary, and spatial orientation are all suspended. State the precise question: does Avicenna establish a form of self-knowledge structurally prior to embodiment, and can that claim be reconciled with predictive-processing models without reducing the self to a prediction-error minimization routine? Explain why existing scholarship leaves this open: readings either recruit Avicenna as a Cartesian precursor or dismiss the argument as an intuition pump for substance dualism, both missing the intermediate possibility that self-awareness is non-inferential yet still tacitly anchored in a bodily schema that is suspended rather than absent. Propose a method—close conceptual analysis of the argument's logical structure paired with comparison to modern neurophenomenological accounts of minimal selfhood and sensory-deprivation studies—rather than pure historical exegesis or armchair analysis alone, because only the pairing lets the argument constrain current theory without anachronism. Define a successful result: a defensible interpretation in which Avicenna's claim survives not as proof of an immaterial substance but as a constraint on any view that treats the body as constitutive rather than merely causal for selfhood. Specify what counts as failure: either attributing to Avicenna a fully formed predictive-processing framework, or allowing minimal selfhood to collapse into bodily schema without remainder. Assess the scope honestly for a single semester and identify the precise point at which the project becomes unmanageable. Take a committed position: argue that reconciliation is achievable only if we abandon the either/or framing, say exactly what each side must concede, and defend that cost as worth paying. Write formally, do not include bibliographies or citations, and assume an audience familiar with philosophy of mind but not necessarily with medieval Arabic philosophy.
