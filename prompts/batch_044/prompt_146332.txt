I am currently drafting an essay for an advanced seminar in philosophy of psychiatry, and over the past few weeks I have become genuinely preoccupied with Ian Hacking's thesis that psychiatric classifications can generate looping effects—processes by which being labeled in a particular way alters the self-understanding and behavior of the classified, thereby feeding back into the classification system itself. While I am familiar with the standard illustrations involving dissociative identity disorder and the historical pathologization of homosexuality, I want to move beyond those well-worn examples. Specifically, I am interested in what Hacking's framework implies for our evaluation of the recent formalization of prolonged grief disorder in the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. In particular, I am trying to understand how the medicalization of a specific temporal boundary for mourning—roughly twelve months—might not merely describe but actively reshape the phenomenology of bereavement, influence clinicians' diagnostic thresholds, and interact with institutional pressures such as insurance reimbursement and pharmaceutical research priorities. Could you please describe the most important implications of Hacking's looping effects for how philosophers and clinicians ought to assess the stability and ethical legitimacy of this particular diagnostic category? I am especially interested in implications that concern the feedback loops between patient self-interpretation, family expectations, and the refinement of diagnostic criteria over successive editions of classification manuals, rather than in general historical surveys or bibliographic recommendations.
