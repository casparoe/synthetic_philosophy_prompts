I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level history and philosophy of science seminar, aimed at advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students. Over the term we have worked through the Scientific Revolution with sustained attention to the fact that the boundary between astronomy, natural philosophy, and what we now call pseudoscience was not yet fixed in the seventeenth century. I want one essay question that prevents students from treating Johannes Kepler as either a modern rationalist who quietly rejected astrology or as an unreflective practitioner who simply inherited medieval superstition. Instead, the question should force them to reconstruct the specific conceptual architecture of Kepler's position.

The crux should be the tension between two commitments that appear across his corpus: first, his physical-cosmological conviction that celestial bodies exert real, mathematically describable influences on terrestrial and human affairs—above all through solar and lunar forces tied to his new celestial physics; and second, his explicit rejection of deterministic, horoscope-based predictions about individual human destinies. The prompt should ask students to articulate the distinction Kepler draws between legitimate study of celestial sympathy and deterministic horoscopic practice, and then to evaluate whether that distinction remains coherent in light of his own astronomical discoveries—specifically, whether the replacement of perfect circular orbits with elliptical motion undermines the geometric harmony that originally grounded his belief in cosmic sympathy, or whether his physicalist account of planetary motion can actually be made to support it. The question should also ask them to address the role of his theological commitment to an intelligible divine plan, without allowing them to reduce his astrology either to pure empirical science or to mere religious mysticism.

Please draft the exam question itself, suitable for a 75-minute essay response; a model answer of roughly 600–700 words that makes the required conceptual moves explicit; and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer. The marking scheme should identify the specific analytical steps students must take—such as isolating the physical-influence versus determinist-prediction distinction, engaging the ellipse-versus-circle problem, and integrating the theological dimension without collapsing the argument into it—while specifying the common failures that should cost marks, for instance treating Kepler as a straightforward empiricist or conflating his celestial physics with vulgar determinism. The question must be answerable by a student who has studied Kepler's major astronomical and philosophical writings and the standard secondary literature, but it should not be answerable by someone who only knows that Kepler cast horoscopes and also discovered elliptical orbits. Do not require bibliographies, page citations, or reading lists; write the prompt so that a well-prepared student can answer from memory and conceptual understanding alone.
