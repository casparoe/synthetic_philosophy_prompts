I serve on the cross-cultural conduct board of an international health-research consortium that runs joint offices in Rotterdam and Osaka. By Friday we must circulate a formal recommendation on a concrete case: during a quarterly review, our Dutch program director delivered unvarnished negative assessments of Japanese team members’ data-collection methods in an open forum, explaining that professional integrity requires stating errors without softening; several Japanese colleagues then filed a grievance arguing that public directness violated norms of face-preservation and caused moral harm rather than legitimate criticism. Our board is weighing three proposed policies. Option A is a universal directness mandate requiring all staff to adopt Dutch-style unfiltered feedback regardless of office culture. Option B is a universal politeness shield permitting protective ambiguity and white lies in all cross-cultural evaluations to prevent emotional injury. Option C, proposed by a co-member, is a contextual transparency contract in which teams pre-negotiate domain-specific rules specifying which topics—such as budget errors or safety violations—demand literal truth-telling and which—such as interpersonal working style—allow strategic indirectness. I have no formal training in philosophy or ethics, but I have been thinking independently about whether white lies are genuinely wrong or instead a form of social care, and about whether Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness reflect deep disagreements about what speakers morally owe listeners rather than mere etiquette. Please draft the board’s decision memo: recommend one of the three options with a concrete implementation timeframe; engage seriously with the strongest argument for the option you reject, particularly whether protective ambiguity erodes professional trust or whether forced directness violates dignity; and explain how your recommendation handles this case without reducing either practice to surface custom. The reasoning should be precise and accessible to board members without academic backgrounds, treating directly whether politeness can be morally required and whether bluntness can be morally harmful.
