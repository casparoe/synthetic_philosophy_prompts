I have been reading Hannah Arendt's reflections on the trial of Adolf Eichmann largely on my own, without any formal training in philosophy or political theory, and after a long conversation with friends about whether her concept of the banality of evil is usually misunderstood, I tried to put my thoughts into a short essay. I would be grateful for your comments and suggestions, particularly regarding the accuracy of my interpretation, whether the argument sounds genuinely interesting or merely repeats familiar points, and whether one specific connection I make feels forced.

The essay, which I am tentatively calling Cliché as Shield, argues that Arendt's observation was not primarily that Eichmann was an unremarkable bureaucrat who happened to commit atrocious acts, but that his wrongdoing was anchored in a particular cognitive failure: an inability to imagine reality from any standpoint outside his own administrative vocabulary. I focus on Arendt's attention to Eichmann's constant reliance on stock phrases and official jargon—his habit of speaking in clichés—to propose that the banality she identified concerned less the mediocrity of the individual and more the mediocrity of the conceptual language he inhabited. From there, I suggest that the deeper danger is not that ordinary people become monstrous, but that ordinary institutional language can become so self-enclosed that it accommodates extreme harm without producing internal moral friction. I then ask whether this framing alters how we should understand responsibility within large modern organizations, where harmful outcomes are often justified through appeals to procedural norms or quantitative metrics rather than through personal hatred or ideological conviction. I conclude by wondering whether Arendt's ultimate point was about the fragility of human judgment whenever it is replaced by a vocabulary that leaves no room for the particular experience of other people.

I am especially uncertain about the middle section, where I extend her analysis of Eichmann's speech patterns to contemporary bureaucratic behavior. I worry that I may be projecting modern concerns onto her historical argument, or that I am neglecting important context about the Jerusalem trial and the specific legal and political circumstances she was addressing. I also wonder whether my distinction between the mediocrity of the person and the mediocrity of language is genuinely supported by her writing, or whether I have invented a dichotomy that she would reject. Could you comment on these concerns, evaluate whether the essay's trajectory feels coherent, and suggest how I might sharpen the conclusion, which currently feels somewhat vague to me?
