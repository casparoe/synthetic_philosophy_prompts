I'm a retired physicist who only started taking philosophy seriously about eighteen months ago, and now I've talked a few other retired lab people into a fortnightly discussion group — engineers, a chemist, one biologist — all clever but with zero background in analytical philosophy. Next session I want to explain Wittgenstien's hinge epistomology from On Certainty, but I need a proper teaching plan rather than just talking at them. They understand calibration, null measurements, and why you can't check every instrument against every other one at once, so I want to build around that.

Plan the arc for about forty-five minutes: which idea has to land first before hinge propositions even make sense (and why the usual Cartesian doubt-everything opening will backfire with this crowd), the one concrete analogy — probably calibration null-points — that should carry the session, exactly what that analogy needs to include and where it breaks down, and the misconseptions they'll form because of their scientific training (I'm guessing they'll treat hinges as just untested axioms we keep for convenience). Lay out the dependency order: component by component, where in the sequence to head off each misconception, what to openly leave out — definitely don't drag in the private language argument — and one check-question at the end that shows they've understood hinges aren't a kind of evidence but conditions for evidence, rather than just repeating the phrase. Keep it practical; no bibliography or reading list, please.
