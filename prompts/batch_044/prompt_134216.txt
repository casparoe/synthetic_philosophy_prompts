Consider the institutional divergence between Scandinavian administrative practice—where individual tax returns and income data are made publicly accessible, and compliance is maintained largely through social visibility and peer-level transparency rather than through adversarial audit and punitive sanction—and the American system, where tax confidentiality is legally protected and enforcement relies on centralized audits, penalties, and a structural presumption of privacy against government intrusion. What philosophical commitments about trust, legitimacy, and the moral psychology of citizens does each design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory in each case: does the Scandinavian model presuppose that horizontal accountability among citizens can substitute for vertical suspicion of the state, and does that depend on viewing citizens as cooperative moral agents rather than strategic maximizers? Does the American model, conversely, presuppose that suspicion is an irreducible structural check, and does that commit it to a liberal conception of the individual as ontologically prior to the collective? Examine whether each practice is internally consistent with other commitments of its legal culture—for instance, whether Scandinavian openness is compatible with the extensive welfare-state redistribution it accompanies, or whether American privacy is compatible with the surveillance capacities the same state deploys in other domains. Finally, assess which attitude—Scandinavian trust or American suspicion—is more philosophically defensible if we reject the empirical premise that transparent data will be used fairly rather than punitively, and specify what would have to change in institutional design if the presupposed view of human motivation or of state-citizen relations were abandoned.
