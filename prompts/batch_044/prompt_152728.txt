Consider Wittgenstein's discussion in Philosophical Investigations §§65–67, where he examines whether there is anything common to all games—board-games, card-games, ball-games, Olympic games—and rejects the search for a single essential feature shared by every instance. He proposes instead that games form a family linked by a network of overlapping similarities, analogous to the fibers in a rope: no one fiber runs through the entire length, yet the rope holds together because of the way many fibers overlap and reinforce one another. I am trying to understand whether this family-resemblance structure can accommodate graded centrality without collapsing back into the essentialism Wittgenstein wants to avoid. If some games, such as chess or soccer, strike us as more paradigmatic or central examples than others, such as ring-a-rosie or a child throwing a ball against a wall, does that psychological salience imply a hidden necessary condition, or can Wittgenstein allow that some members are more central purely in virtue of denser networks of resemblance? Moreover, how should the rope-fiber analogy be understood in relation to Eleanor Rosch's prototype theory of categorization, which explicitly posits graded membership and central exemplars? Does Wittgenstein's view entail a flat, non-hierarchical web of similarities in which no instance is genuinely more central, or can it be read as consistent with the claim that category members vary in typicality? Please help me understand the passage and whether family resemblance can consistently incorporate graded membership, or whether doing so necessarily reintroduces the kind of definitional essentialism that the argument was designed to reject.
