I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up the study of moral philosophy and formal decision theory. I now serve on the clinical-ethics advisory panel of a large teaching hospital, where we evaluate proposals for high-risk, non-standard therapeutic interventions — mostly compassionate-use requests and early-phase trials. Our deliberations too often reduce to an unstructured weighing of benefit against risk, and I wish to replace this with a disciplined, reusable procedure.

My interest lies specifically in risk-weighted expected utility theory, which I understand as allowing the decision weight of an outcome to vary with its risk — the probability of occurrence — independently of any curvature in the utility function itself. I need this principle translated into an operational checklist that a panel of clinicians and lay members, none of whom are decision theorists, can run in a single one-hour session, with explicit stopping rules and with the points where human judgement is unavoidable clearly flagged.

Please design such a procedure. It should proceed as an ordered sequence of questions or tests. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a recommendation to reject, where it terminates with approval subject to conditions, and where it must halt and declare that the issue is one of values rather than calculation. The procedure must distinguish three dimensions that are often conflated in clinical discussion: first, the risk-weighted valuation of therapeutic outcomes, with benefits and harms weighted according to the framework's risk-dependent weights; second, the epistemic assessment of the probabilities themselves — that is, the confidence in the estimates and the cost of being wrong about them; third, the moral status and vulnerability of the patient population, which may affect how risk weights are applied without altering the underlying utility assessments. Specify how conflicts among these dimensions are to be handled within the framework, and identify the precise inputs — probability estimates, risk-weight parameters, utility values — that must be supplied by the panel.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. A: a gene-editing trial for a progressive neurodegenerative disease, with an estimated sixty per cent probability of halting progression and a fifteen per cent probability of an irreversible systemic inflammatory response; the evidence comprises three preclinical studies and one unpublished human case report. B: a palliative sedation protocol for terminal respiratory failure, offering near-certain relief of suffering but carrying a non-trivial risk of hastening death by several days; the patient is fully competent and has explicitly requested it. C: an off-label immunosuppressant regimen for an autoimmune disorder in which standard therapy has failed. Here the risk-weighted calculation yields a strongly positive recommendation, but the risk weights have been calibrated on a genetically distinct population. Applying them to this patient requires assuming biological uniformity across groups — an assumption I, given my scientific background, consider epistemically unwarranted. Nevertheless, if the procedure is followed mechanically, it mandates approval.

For each case, show the step-by-step application, note any ambiguities, and for case C explain exactly why the framework produces a result I am inclined to reject, and whether the theory contains any internal mechanism — for instance, through the epistemic dimension you have built in — that permits or requires overriding the mechanical result.

Finally, be explicit about the procedure's limitations: which disagreements it cannot settle, which ethical premises are smuggled in as apparently technical inputs, and how the panel should record a deliberate departure from the procedure when it chooses to take one.

Please present the answer in British English, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
