I need a taxonomy — like a decision tree or sorting quiz — for positions on self-amending legal rules and the paradoxes that come with them. I'm running a philosophy of law study group and everyone already knows the standard Ross paradox, so I want something that sorts people by their actual commitments on concrete cases rather than by which philosopher they read last week.

The tree should start with whether a legal system can contain a rule that coherently authorizes it's own change without any external authority. From there branch on: is the problem logical (a statement referring to its own conditions of validity) or practical (who actually holds the power after the change)? Then split on whether mediating the reference through a separate meta-rule solves anything, or whether that just pushes the self-reference up one level.

To keep this from being a straight encyclopedia recite, I want the whole thing built around a specific constructed scenario: a constitution with only three articles. Article 1 creates a council. Article 2 lists basic rights. Article 3 says it can be amended by simple majority, but any amendment to Article 3 automatically rewrites Article 1 to give the council veto power over all future amendments. The taxonomy should classify positions based on what you think happens if Article 3 gets amended twice — does the system stabilize, collapse, or enter an infinite loop of authority shifts?

Each leaf needs to be either a named position (Ross-paradoxist, hierarchicalist, delegationist, whatever) or an unnamed logical space with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it's empty or weird. Label every branch by the claim that actually divides it, not by theorist names. And flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate — for example, maybe the hierarchical solution and the meta-rule delegation come out identical in this three-article case.

This is for a handout, so keep it informal. No bibliographies, no "see Kelsen" type references — the students won't have library access during the session anyway. There's probably a typo or two in this prompt but you get the idea.
