In Mill's unfinished late writings on socialism—drafted in the 1870s and published after his death—he examines whether collective ownership of capital necessarily extinguishes individual liberty. In one particularly dense passage, he imagines a cooperative system in which all instruments of production are held in common, yet no central authority assigns individuals to particular jobs. Workers are free to choose their occupation and to leave one cooperative for another. Mill argues that under these conditions socialism does not, in itself, threaten freedom. But he then introduces a reservation that seems to undercut the reassurance: inside any given cooperative, decisions about wages, working conditions, and the division of surplus will be made by majority vote. He asks whether a worker who is consistently outvoted is not, in practice, just as subordinate as one who takes orders from a capitalist employer—except that the authority is now the collective will of his peers rather than an individual owner. To prevent this internal domination, Mill suggests that the cooperative must guarantee not merely formal membership rights but a meaningful right of exit, including the ability to withdraw one's share of capital and find comparable employment without destitution.

I am struggling to understand the precise logical structure of this move. Mill appears to distinguish collective ownership of capital from collective authority over labor, but he never explicitly states the principle that separates legitimate cooperative discipline from oppressive majoritarian rule within the workshop. Is he relying on an implicit separation between property rights and managerial authority—treating capital as jointly owned but labor as individually controlled—and if so, does that separation collapse once the cooperative must decide collectively how to deploy both? Furthermore, he treats economic exit as the safeguard of liberty, yet in a fully socialized system there may be no independent capital pool outside the cooperatives into which one can exit. Does Mill's argument therefore depend on preserving a residual private market that his socialism was meant to transcend?

More specifically, I wonder whether the failure mode he describes—democratic ownership reproducing hierarchical control over dissenting minorities—corresponds to a recognized concept in later discussions of worker self-management or cooperative governance. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I want to know whether there is an established term for this internal-majoritarian domination, how Mill's framing of exit rights compares to modern treatments of workplace democracy, and whether the tension he identifies is genuinely resolvable within his own framework or reveals an unresolved contradiction between common ownership and individual autonomy.
