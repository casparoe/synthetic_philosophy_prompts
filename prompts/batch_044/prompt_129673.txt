So epistemicists like Williamson say there's actually a sharp cutoff for being bald, we just can't know where it is. Supervaluationists say there's no one true cutoff, just lots of admissible precisifications, and 'bald' is true only if your bald on all of them.

But then I started thinking about this concept my roommate invented - she calls it 'ambient presence' - like when someone has there phone on and notifications are technically reaching them but they're not mentally engaged. There's clearly vagueness here: is she present? I tried to apply both theories. The epistemicist view would say there's a precise neural or temporal threshold for ambient presence, but we'll never pin it down. The supervaluationist says there are multiple legitimate ways to draw the line depending on context.

Here's where I think I found a problem. If epistemicism already admits we can never access the sharp boundary, then for all practical and linguistic purposes were treating it like there isn't one - we're just picking a precisification that works for the situation. So doesn't that mean epistemicism, once you actually try to use it in real life, becomes supervaluationism? Like, the sharp boundary is there but it's causally inert, so metaphysically it's redundant. Am I wrong that this makes epistemicism collapse? Or is there some hidden difference I'm missing?
