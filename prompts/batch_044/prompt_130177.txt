I am a strategic-studies graduate student who recently observed a policy briefing in which advisors successively invoked the Munich analogy, the Vietnam quagmire, and the Thucydides Trap to support mutually exclusive recommendations. The experience convinced me that the deeper problem is not selecting the wrong historical parallel, but that analogies covertly import entire causal narratives and constrain the decision space without explicit argument. I am closing a seminar on historical reasoning in foreign policy and would like a poem—specifically a sestina using the end-words Munich, Saigon, Athens, Sparta, echo, and shadow—to capture this dynamic. The compulsive repetition of the sestina should mirror the cyclical return of these analogies in strategic discourse, despite repeated debunking, while the envoi should collapse that repetition by exposing that the true danger lies not in repeating history but in smuggling an unexamined causal model into present judgment. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in international relations or philosophy of history would recognize the conceptual structure—particularly the distinction between analogical comparison and analogical framing—without resolving the tension into either a defense of historical learning or a dismissal of it.
