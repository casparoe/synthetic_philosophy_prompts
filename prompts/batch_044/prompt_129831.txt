(1) Translation is genuinely indeterminate at the ontological level: "gavagai" underdetermines not merely our epistemic access but the fact of the matter—there is no hidden state that fixes whether the speaker tracks rabbits, undetached parts, or temporal stages.

(2) This indeterminacy is not confined to exotic field cases; it extends to core natural-language vocabulary, though pragmatic pressures can mask it.

(3) Scientific terms such as "electron" or "gene" achieve determinacy not through hidden essences but through rigid integration into predictive theoretical networks, so mature scientific vocabulary translates determinately in a way "gavagai" does not.

(4) The principle of charity is constitutive, not heuristic: without presupposing rationality, there is no interpretation at all, not merely an inferior one.

(5) All intentional content is relational and interpretive; there are no intrinsic mental representations that carry meaning independently of external assignment.

(6) Therefore, cross-linguistic ontological disputes—say, over whether enduring objects exist—are largely verbal, since rival ontologies are equally compatible with all behavioral and theoretical data.

(7) Yet recent convergence results in multilingual representation learning suggest that algorithmic cross-lingual alignment tends toward unique embedding structures, which implies Quinean underdetermination may be an artifact of human cognitive limitation rather than a deep feature of language.

Please audit this package. Which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity, specify the cheapest fix for each, and identify the single commitment whose abandonment would resolve the greatest number of conflicts. In particular, address whether (3) and (7) jointly undermine (1) and (2); whether (4) and (5) conceal a circularity or dissolve on inspection; and whether (6) remains compatible with the determinacy claimed in (3).
