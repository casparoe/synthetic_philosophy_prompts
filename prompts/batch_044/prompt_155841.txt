I have been working through the Buddhist doctrine of anattā and its bearing on personal identity entirely on my own, with no formal training, and a friend has asked me to lead one session for her reading group of intelligent non-specialists. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a teaching plan. First, the dependency order: which idea must be in place before anattā can be heard as a claim about the self rather than as a denial that people exist or feel? Second, the carrying example: propose one concrete case—perhaps a long-running digital presence sustained by successive, unknown maintainers rather than one continuous owner, or something you judge stronger—that makes both the felt sense of ownership and its dissolution tangible, and specify why the standard chariot and flame-passing analogies should be avoided with this audience. Third, map the misconceptions they will form in sequence, especially the slide from "no permanent self" to "no moral agency" or to "experience is an illusion," and state exactly where in the order each should be interrupted. Fourth, one closing question that distinguishes grasping anattā from merely being unsettled by it. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out—whether Abhidhamma momentariness, Madhyamaka emptiness, or other technical extensions—so the session remains coherent at this level.
