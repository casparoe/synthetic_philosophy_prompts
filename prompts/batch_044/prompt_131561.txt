You are a philosopher of public memory and rhetoric. Before writing, spend a brief moment brainstorming: what distinguishes a historical defeat that becomes a binding national myth from one that becomes a cautionary tale? Then compose a five-minute opening address for a museum exhibition titled "Lost Causes, Living Memory," whose galleries juxtapose Masada, the American Confederacy's Lost Cause mythology, and Dunkirk. The audience is mixed: academic historians, descendants of the commemorated groups, and local officials. Structure the speech around the conceptual distinction between mnemonic solidarity—using the past to forge present collective identity—and moral exemplarity—using the past to impose ethical constraints on present action. Apply this distinction to the three cases without collapsing them into a single verdict; show how each relationship between defeat and remembrance differs in kind. Clearly separate the basic historical facts that are settled from the interpretive claims about mythologization that remain genuinely contested among specialists—do not present a disputed framing (for instance, whether Dunkirk operates chiefly as a resilience myth or as a strategic-retreat narrative) as scholarly consensus. Think carefully and step by step. The tone should be respectful and intellectually rigorous: offer genuine comfort to those present for remembrance without affirming any falsehood about the past, and avoid assertions that a well-informed historian in the room would have to reject.
