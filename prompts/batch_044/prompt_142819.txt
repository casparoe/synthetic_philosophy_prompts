I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy of mind or philosophy of science, but I have been trying to understand a conceptual disagreement that runs through the machine-learning interpretability literature. When researchers claim that a particular hidden layer in a vision model represents curvature, object boundaries, or syntactic structure, they do not always seem to be saying the same thing. In some contexts they mean that an external linear probe can decode the feature from the layer’s activations; in others they mean that manipulating or ablating that feature causally changes the model’s output in a systematic way; and in still others they seem to require that misrepresenting the feature would produce a counterfactual failure that is interpretably linked to the feature’s content, not merely a distributed statistical perturbation. These three conditions—external decodability, causal efficacy within the forward computation, and counterfactual robustness tied specifically to the content—often come apart in practice. A feature can be cleanly decodable yet causally redundant because parallel pathways compute the same result; it can be causally necessary yet resist any simple linear probe; and it can be counterfactual-sensitive only in edge cases that lie outside the training distribution.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake: under what conditions is it literally true that a neural network possesses a representation with a specific content, rather than this being a useful fiction, a predictive projection of the interpreter, or an instrumental shorthand? In particular, address whether representation requires all three conditions jointly, whether any single one is sufficient, and how the possibility of distributed or redundant encoding affects the realism debate about interpretability. I am also interested in whether this disagreement is best understood as an ontological dispute—applying the philosophy of mental representation to artificial systems—or as a disagreement about explanatory licensing: about when engineers and scientists are justified in attributing representational content for predictive, diagnostic, or design purposes.

Please be explicit about what would change your mind—whether a particular argument about localization, a finding about how representations are distributed across layers, or a conceptual distinction you have overlooked would push you toward a more realist or more deflationary conclusion—and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail or rest on an unstated assumption.
