I've been working through a puzzle about recursive higher-order calibration and have arrived at an argument that seems to show rational agents should end up in total doxastic suspension, but I cannot locate the precise error. Here are the steps as I have them formulated:

(1) If you possess higher-order evidence that your first-order reasoning in domain D has reliability r < 1, then rationality requires adjusting your confidence in judgments within D downward by a factor proportional to (1 − r).

(2) Your assessment that the reliability is r is itself a judgment produced by some meta-reasoning process, call it M. So you can apply (1) to M: if you have evidence that M is unreliable, you must adjust your confidence in r downward.

(3) Applying (1) recursively generates an infinite hierarchy: confidence in p is modified by confidence in the reliability assessment, which is modified by confidence in the meta-assessment, and so on.

(4) For any initial confidence c in p and any sequence of reliability estimates bounded away from 1, this infinite adjustment converges to 0. Thus the rationally required confidence in any substantive belief is 0.

(5) But an agent with universal confidence 0 cannot form intentions, make predictions, or even coherently endorse (4) itself.

(6) Therefore one of the premises must fail. The slide from (3) to (4) looks suspicious—perhaps the recursion misapplies the calibration rule, or there is a hidden base-level exemption—but I cannot articulate the mistake precisely.

Which step should be rejected? I am especially inclined to preserve (1) in some qualified form, since it captures the intuitive force of higher-order defeat in clear cases of epistemic akrasia (for instance, the sleep-deprived mathematician who continues to believe her proof is correct). If we weaken (1), do we lose the standard explanation of why akrasia is rationally defective? Or is the real problem in (4)'s claim about convergence?

Please identify the weak step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives. In particular: if we keep (1) but block the regress at some base level, what does that commit us to about rational self-trust? And if we reject (1) outright, can we still make sense of rational higher-order defeat without collapsing into unconditional first-order dogmatism?
