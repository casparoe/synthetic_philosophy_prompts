For a 90-minute upper-level philosophy-of-mind exam, draft an essay question on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment that cannot be answered by summarizing Jackson’s knowledge argument or Chalmers’s zombie argument. The question should require students to explain precisely why the causal inertness of phenomenal properties undermines the reliability of first-person phenomenal judgments, and then ask them to assess whether the paradox can be dissolved without abandoning epiphenomenalism—specifically by appealing to the access-consciousness/phenomenal-consciousness distinction and by confronting a hypothetical “phenomenal-zombie reporter”: a physically identical system that lacks qualia yet produces indistinguishable introspective reports. Include a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks: list five must-hit argumentative moves (with marks allocated to each), and for each move identify the specific misunderstanding or omission that should cost points. Ensure the crux is the epistemic circularity of using physically caused judgments to justify claims about causally isolated properties, so that students who have only memorized the labels “epiphenomenalism” and “phenomenal judgment” cannot earn high marks.
