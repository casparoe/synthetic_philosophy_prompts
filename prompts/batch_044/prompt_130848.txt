I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether "the will of the people" names a real collective attitude or is merely a statistical artifact we project onto election results. She insisted that a 52/48 split plainly reveals the people's will, and I kept countering that different aggregation rules change the winner, but neither of us could articulate why that ambiguity actually undermines democratic authority rather than being a mere technical curiosity.

Design a 60-minute classroom activity for about 18 undergraduates in a political philosophy seminar. The participants should come away feeling the force of what we might call the "manufactured will" problem: that constructing a unitary popular will from conflicting preferences requires suppressing or transforming individual inputs in ways that become visible only when people must govern themselves in real time.

Structure it as follows. Divide the class into four mechanism-groups of 4-5 students each, representing plurality rule, Borda count, Condorcet pairwise comparison, and a deliberative "transformative" model. Give them a fictional polity of 12 citizens with fixed, conflicting preference orders over three policies—taxation, migration, and energy supply. In each of three rounds, the groups apply their rule to derive "the will of the people," after which two neutral student-governors must implement the winning policy. Crucially, in round 3 all four mechanisms yield different winners, and each group must defend why their result counts as the authentic popular will despite the fact that no actual citizen holds that aggregate preference. No lecture up front; the tension should emerge from the rules themselves.

Then provide a debrief mapping each round onto the distinction between aggregative and transformative conceptions of popular will, with an explicit admission of which philosophical claim—the idea that a genuine collective will can never err—cannot be reproduced by rules and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also tell me what will predictably fail the first time and how to fix it.
