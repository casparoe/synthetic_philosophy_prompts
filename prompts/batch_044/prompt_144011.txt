I am a third-year undergraduate reading Philosophy, Politics and Economics, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop on collective reasoning in democratic institutions, hosted jointly by my university’s philosophy and politics departments. The audience will be roughly thirty people: about half are academics in philosophy of science and political theory who will recognise any reference to the Condorcet jury theorem, and the other half are non-specialist guests—including family members of the retiring convener—who will not. I need a speech of about seven minutes that treats the Condorcet jury theorem not as a decorative reference but as structural scaffolding: the conditions under which collective competence improves with group size should determine which claims I can legitimately make about the workshop’s own deliberative format, and which I must explicitly withhold.

Specifically, I want the speech to turn on a somewhat niche extension of the theorem that is not usually mentioned in introductory treatments: the problem of dimensional competence, where members of a group may be individually competent on different axes of a problem—for instance, empirical, normative, and conceptual dimensions—but where competence is neither binary nor independent across those axes. The speech should introduce this tension without requiring me to cite specific papers, and it should shape the rhetoric so that I acknowledge that the workshop’s interdisciplinary design risks violating the independence condition in precisely this multidimensional way—while also arguing that this violation is not necessarily fatal if we restructure how we pool judgments.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific authors beyond what is common knowledge (Condorcet himself). Do not ask the audience to vote on anything, and avoid framing the idea as a simple celebration of democracy. Instead, the philosophical point should constrain what I can promise about the day’s outcomes: I should be able to say that collective wisdom is possible here, but only under conditions that the workshop’s format must actively maintain, and I should be explicit about what those conditions are and where they are likely to fail. The tone should be formal, respectful of the occasion, and accessible to the non-specialists without becoming patronising to the experts. Write in British English.
