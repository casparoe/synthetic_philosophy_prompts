I am preparing a feature for an ideas-and-science audience on an under-discussed thread in philosophy of history: the hinge-of-history hypothesis, which borrows the structure of Wittgensteinian hinge propositions—those commitments that stand fast within a framework not because they are evidentially grounded, but because they serve as conditions for all subsequent questioning—and applies it to macro-historical interpretation. For this piece, I need a formal, intellectually demanding dialogue between two equally capable scholars who disagree sharply on whether history genuinely contains such hinge events.

One interlocutor should defend the hypothesis in its strongest form: that certain moments do not function as ordinary evidential nodes within a narrative but rather as ungrounded pivot points, conditions of intelligibility for everything that follows—much as hinge propositions operate in epistemology. The other should resist, arguing that the analogy collapses because historical narratives are structurally revisable in ways that belief-forming frameworks are not, and that labeling any event a hinge smuggles in a normative theory of historical significance rather than describing an objective feature of the past.

The exchange must contain real back-and-forth. Have them directly address whether a hinge event can be falsified or revised without undermining the entire interpretive edifice built upon it; whether identifying a hinge requires prior commitment to a particular historiographical scale—state-level institutional change versus micro-social practice; and whether treating the Westphalian settlement not as an archival occurrence but as a conceptual candidate for modern sovereignty's hinge actually bears the analogical weight demanded. They should respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past one another. Neither should be a strawman.

Please write this as a polished dialogue, free of bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to obscure primary sources that a reader could not verify without specialist access. The aim is conceptual depth accessible to an informed non-specialist, but rigorous enough that someone familiar with both late Wittgensteinian epistemology and philosophy of history would recognize the arguments as correctly formed rather than generic textbook restatements. Keep the tone formal, precise, and journalistic.
