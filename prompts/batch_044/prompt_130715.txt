I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been working through epistemic logic puzzles with a colleague who thinks carefully about rationality and common knowledge in distributed systems. Here are her verdicts so far: in the classic Muddy Children puzzle with a perfect public announcement, she says the children eventually reach common knowledge of who is muddy; in the email game where messages may fail with an arbitrarily small but positive probability, she says common knowledge of rationality is never achieved, so cooperative play is unjustified; in a variant where messages are guaranteed but delayed by an unknown yet finite amount, she says common knowledge is eventually achieved but not at any specific round; in a scenario where two agents share identical beliefs about each other's rationality but formed them through private, unobservable reasoning, she denies they have common knowledge; and in a model with a single trusted broadcaster who sends one confirmed receipt to all parties, she accepts immediate common knowledge.

Now consider a protocol I'm analyzing: nodes share a common prior, have perfect reasoning, and exchange cryptographically signed messages over a network with bounded but unknown latency. Every delivered message carries proof of receipt, but nodes cannot observe each other's internal computation states. Crucially, there is a silent failure mode—occurring with prior probability exactly 0.01—where a node stops updating beliefs correctly; all nodes observe that this particular run is not in failure mode, though they do not observe others' internal states. They all know the protocol rules and each other's rationality.

Would my colleague say these nodes have common knowledge of rationality sufficient to justify cooperative consensus? Lay out the candidate rules that fit her earlier judgments—strict infinite-hierarchy requirements, approximate common knowledge within epsilon, structural observability conditions, or something else—say what each predicts about this protocol, give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and propose the minimal variant case that would best distinguish the leading candidates.
