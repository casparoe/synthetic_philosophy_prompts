While reporting from a recent workshop on the philosophy of perception, I witnessed an extended exchange between Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen about whether disjunctivism genuinely answers the argument from illusion, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty. I am preparing a feature on conceptual disputes in contemporary epistemology and want a rigorous, point-by-point adjudication of their arguments as presented; I have no settled view of my own and am reporting the disagreement from the sidelines.

Vasquez opened by arguing that disjunctivism fails to address the core force of the argument from illusion. She said that if a veridical perception of a red cube and an introspectively indistinguishable hallucination of one are genuinely different mental kinds, the disjunctivist still owes an explanation of why the subject cannot discriminate them from the inside. Without such an explanation, she claimed, the view simply concedes that there is a shared phenomenal core—exactly what the argument asserts—and then arbitrarily denies that this core constitutes a common mental state.

Chen responded that this mischaracterizes the disjunctivist commitment. He granted subjective indistinguishability but insisted it does not entail shared mental ontology. The causal-functional profile of genuine perception, he said, involves direct environmental relation and appropriate counterfactual dependencies, whereas hallucination lacks these entirely. This is not representationalism, he emphasized, because representationalism individuates mental states by content or functional role within the subject; disjunctivism makes an ontological claim about the kind of event that occurs.

Vasquez countered that if the internal phenomenal profile is identical and only external relations differ, then Chen has conceded the argument's central premise: there is a common internal state. The disjunctive distinction, she said, then applies only to an external relation, not to the mental event itself, which collapses disjunctivism into either representationalism or a sense-datum theory with redundant metaphysical scaffolding.

Chen then introduced a boundary case designed to test this objection: adversarial perceptual engineering in which a subject's visual cortex is stimulated to reproduce precisely the neural signature of seeing a red cube, despite no cube being present. The resulting state, he argued, should not be classified as a "bad case" of seeing but as a different mental kind altogether—a quasi-perceptual state that lacks the relational structure definitive of genuine perception. On his view, disjunctivism resolves the illusion argument not by denying phenomenal similarity but by refusing to group these states under a single mental taxonomy.

Vasquez replied that this move is ontologically extravagant and ad hoc. Without a principled criterion—other than the mere presence or absence of an appropriate causal chain—for when a state counts as veridical perception versus quasi-perceptual state, she argued, Chen is simply labeling cases to preserve his theory. Moreover, she added, if the criterion is purely causal and external, then he has again made mental-kind individuation depend on factors outside the subject's experience, which is precisely what critics of the illusion argument challenge when they ask what the two experiences share from the inside.

Chen conceded that the boundary problem is real but maintained that Vasquez's demand for an internal, introspective criterion begs the question against disjunctivism. The argument from illusion, he said, presupposes that mental kinds must be individuated by what is accessible to the subject. If that presupposition is false—if mental ontology legitimately includes relational properties—then there is no need for an internal boundary line. He also noted that both of them had so far neglected a further question: how a subject should revise her self-attributions upon discovering that she has been in a quasi-perceptual state rather than genuinely perceiving, and whether disjunctivism offers any normative guidance for that revision.

Then say who is more right overall, identify what substantive issue—if any—both parties are missing, and specify what further case or conceptual distinction would best determine whether Chen's quasi-perceptual category is genuinely explanatory or merely taxonomic.
