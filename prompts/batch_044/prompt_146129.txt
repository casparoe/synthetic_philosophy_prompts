Please describe the most important implications of the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness for institutional decision procedures that must assign precise legal or regulatory categories to entities falling in genuinely borderline regions. Concentrate on the case of classifying synthetically modified organisms that display an intermediate and unstable set of phenotypic traits—some characteristic of a protected native species, others of an invasive one—under statutory predicates such as "native species" or "invasive organism," where the vagueness is metaphysical rather than epistemic. Specifically, address the following: (1) whether an epistemicist view, which holds that there is a sharp but unknown boundary, commits regulators to treating every borderline case as determinately belonging to one category or the other, and what practical or ethical costs that hidden determinacy might carry; (2) whether supervaluationism, which allows for truth-value gaps, can justify a principled default rule—such as provisional exclusion from protected status—without presupposing an ontological fact of the matter that the framework explicitly denies; and (3) how each position should respond to cumulative generational drift, in which successive modifications produce organisms that gradually shift across an undefined threshold, raising the question of whether there is a discrete point of category change or merely an extended zone of indeterminacy. Reason conceptually from the core commitments of each view; do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
