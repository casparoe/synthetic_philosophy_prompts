I've come around to this reconstructed Moorean position: friendship is the supreme intrinsic good not just because it combines pleasure and affection, but because it's the purest instance of organic unity—the pleasure, the friend themself, and the emotional attitude form a whole whose value excedes the parts summed. But I think Moore only gets this result if he treats the emotional component as non-cognitive, just a bare feeling directed at a person rather than involving any belief about their worth. So my view is that friendship's top ranking depends on the claim that we can genuinely love someone without implicitly judging them worthy of love. What would count against it? I don't want the standard instrumentalist objection—that's too cheap. I want to know: if moral psychology shows affection is always partly evaluative, does the organic-unity defense collapse, or can Moore just absorb it? What about loving someone genuinely awful—does that prove the attitude can't be value-neutral, or do we just recieve a counterexample to the purity claim? And is there some result about aesthetic experience—maybe that beauty judgements are always partly cognitive—that breaks the analogy Moore needs between friendship and beauty? Rank the defeaters from fatal to patchable, and say honestly whether this view is well-insulated or just unfalsifiable in a bad way. Oh, and if you think I've misread Moore on whether affection involves judgement, correct me, but don't just cite pages—work with the idea. I don't want a reading list, just the damage assessment.
