I'm working through some problems in the philosophy of historical explanation, and I'm struck by how the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management is always retold as a morality play: a hedge fund staffed by Nobel laureates in economics, running convergence trades at leverage often cited near twenty-five to one, is destroyed when Russia defaults and devalues the ruble in August, global liquidity dries up, and the firm faces insolvency within days. The standard post-hoc account treats the disaster as retrospectively inevitable—of course a highly leveraged book dependent on correlation stability could not survive a sovereign shock paired with a flight to quality—yet I want to examine the episode through the joint lens of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, with particular attention to a feature that standard treatments smooth over: the contingency of the bailout itself. Before the Federal Reserve arranged the private consortium rescue, there was a genuine branching point—LTCM could have filed for bankruptcy, forcing fire-sales across global fixed-income markets—yet historical narratives almost always treat the rescue as the necessary endpoint, folding a policy decision into the causal chain and erasing the contingency.

Can you analyze the episode in both directions? First, use hindsight bias to identify which elements of LTCM's risk framework—its reliance on historical correlation matrices, its modeling of liquidity in U.S. Treasuries and Russian sovereign debt as largely independent, its treatment of mean reversion as a structural law rather than a statistical tendency—appear as obvious errors now but were epistemically defensible given the information available to traders in early 1998. Then apply the narrative fallacy: map the specific causal plot that was imposed after the fact, noting which actors and structural conditions it foregrounds (the "Russian shock" as inciting incident, "genius hubris" as character flaw, the Fed rescue as inevitable resolution) and which it suppresses, particularly the role of repo-market haircuts and the fact that several counterparties had already begun reducing exposure in the days before the default. Finally, tell me what the episode reveals about the limits of these concepts—does the fact that regulators later used the LTCM story to justify capital-requirement rules show that narrative fallacy can be instrumentally valuable despite being epistemically distorting? And please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: for instance, would evidence that LTCM's partners privately anticipated a Russian crisis weaken the hindsight-bias claim, or would it merely shift the narrative from collective ignorance to individual recklessness?
