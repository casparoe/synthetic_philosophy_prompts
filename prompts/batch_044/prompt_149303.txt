Public discourse and policy planning increasingly treat demographic cohorts—Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, Gen Alpha—as if they were natural kinds with stable psychological and economic profiles. Behind this lies an ontological tangle: they may be pure birth-year bins, clusters unified by shared formative events, institutional artifacts produced by census categories and marketing feedback loops, or life-stage effects mislabeled as cohort properties. I am advising a small research fund that can support about four interdisciplinary projects over three years, combining philosophy of science with quantitative sociology but without massive new panel data. We do not want a literature survey; we want a defended agenda for which open conceptual and empirical questions should be settled first, because resolving them determines what follows.

Please propose and rank the two or three most important lines of work. For each, specify: whether it is conceptually upstream of the others; how tractable it is for our kind of team; and what practical difference it makes for intergenerational policy—for instance, whether climate-adaptation or pension budgets should be targeted by cohort rather than by income or geography. Also address this specific tension: the more a cohort label is used to design targeted services, the more institutional investment creates the very behavioral homogeneity the label claimed merely to discover. Is this best understood as a self-fulfilling prophecy, a performative kind, or something distinct? And does settling its ontological status belong near the top or bottom of our agenda?

Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—dependency, tractability, and downstream policy stakes—and say what empirical or conceptual result over the next two years would cause you to reverse the ranking.
