I'm an undergraduate simultaneously enrolled in a political philosophy course on freedom and an upper-division seminar in mechanism design and institutional economics. I want you to restate the standard tripartite taxonomy of liberty — negative liberty as the absence of external impediments, positive liberty as self-mastery or the presence of conditions for autonomous action, and republican liberty as non-domination — entirely within the vocabulary of mechanism design: principal-agent relationships, incentive compatibility constraints, information asymmetry, credible commitment devices, ex ante constitutional rules versus ex post discretionary authority, and the cost structures of monitoring versus interference. For each conception, map it onto a specific institutional architecture (for instance, negative liberty as a minimal contracting regime with low-cost exit options; positive liberty as a planner with preference-alignment mechanisms; republican liberty as an architecture with distributed veto points and transparent audit trails). Be explicit about what gets translated cleanly, what must be distorted to fit — particularly whether republican non-domination's focus on the mere capacity for arbitrary interference can be expressed without importing a normative or psychological claim that mechanism design treats as exogenous — and what has no counterpart whatsoever in your vocabulary. Then deliver a residue report: identify whether the untranslatable elements indicate a deficiency in the mechanism-design framework (for example, its inability to register modal or counterfactual capacities) or whether they reveal that some philosophical claims about liberty depend on distinctions that collapse once we restrict attention to realized contractual outcomes and institutional equilibria. Do not smuggle normative conclusions back in by labeling them as inefficiencies or market failures; keep the translation honest, and treat the residue as the philosophically interesting part.
