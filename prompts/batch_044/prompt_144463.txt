I'm working to stabilize a hybrid view of emotion before drafting a longer piece, and I want a consistency audit rather than a general defense. Here is my current package:

(1) Emotions are not propositional judgments—one can fear a harmless spider despite believing it poses no danger—so pure cognitivism is false.
(2) They are perceptual representations of core relational themes (danger, offense, loss, and so on) delivered in a non-conceptual, analog format; they are closer to color perception than to belief.
(3) The bodily feeling—the sinking chest in grief, the heat in anger—is not merely a causal byproduct but partly constitutive: without that phenomenological component you have an appraisal or evaluation, not an emotion proper.
(4) Basic emotions are shared with non-linguistic mammals, but self-conscious emotions (shame, pride, regret) require a narrative self-model; an animal without autobiographical continuity can feel fear but not shame.
(5) Rational assessment applies only to the representational content: an emotion is rational when its core relational theme fits the situation and irrational when it does not. The bodily feeling is arational—neither correct nor incorrect, only present or absent.
(6) I want to defend a principle—call it interoceptive transparency—according to which, when emotional representation is systematically miscalibrated (e.g., chronic anxiety in objectively safe environments), the bodily feeling remains veridical about the subject's internal arousal state rather than about external value, and should be read as interoceptive evidence rather than as world-directed evaluation.
(7) The distinction between emotion and mood is drawn by intentional object, not by intensity or duration: a diffuse, objectless unease counts as a mood even if it feels phenomenologically like fear.

These commitments feel like they form a perceptual-feeling hybrid with a narrative threshold for higher-order cases, but I suspect I'm helping myself to incompatible motivations. Does (3)'s claim that bodily feeling is constitutive conflict with (2)'s perceptual analogy, since seeing red doesn't require a distinctive bodily feeling to be a perception? Does (5)'s arational-feeling claim sit badly with (6), which treats the feeling as carrying veridical information about internal states—if it bears information, why is it not assessable for accuracy in some broader sense? Does (4)'s narrative-language threshold for shame imply that the representational content of self-conscious emotions is conceptually structured, which pulls against (2)'s insistence that emotional representation is non-conceptual across the board? And is there a hidden tension between (1)'s rejection of judgment and (5)'s language of rational fit, which sounds like assessing the correctness of a judgment?

Please audit the set pair by pair. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say exactly what commitment would have to be revised and at what theoretical cost. Identify which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection—particularly whether I'm misreading (1) against (5) or (6) against (5). Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most conflicts, and whether that revision costs me the view I actually care about. Do not treat every pair as problematic; some should come out consistent.
