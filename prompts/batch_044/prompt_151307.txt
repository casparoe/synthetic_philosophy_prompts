Since my mother's recent diagnosis with a progressive neurological condition, the question of whether life retains meaning when its narrative arc is visibly truncated has become personally urgent. In the literature, the long-standing disagreement between absurdist accounts—rooted in the claim that human projects lack cosmic significance—and contemporary hybrid theories, which hold that meaning consists in subjective attraction to objectively valuable activities, appears stalled: both sides accept that finitude is inescapable, that objective value exists, and that we long for transcendence, yet neither concedes whether local projects survive the absence of eternal justification. My hypothesis is that this is not a substantive dispute about value or mortality but a verbal disagreement masked by shared vocabulary: one side treats meaning in life as an evaluative predicate akin to good, the other as a structural predicate akin to narrative coherence, with each answering a different question under a single heading. Is that right? Or is the impasse driven by bedrock intuitions about naturalness, by a hidden disagreement about what counts as adequate subjective engagement, or by an unacknowledged empirical claim about human psychology under conditions of narrative rupture? Please diagnose the kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, substantive, or a misaligned pair of questions—using what each position says about truncated lives and cosmic scale. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction between evaluative and structural meaning, a phenomenological account of coherence failure, a formal condition on narrative integration, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed.
