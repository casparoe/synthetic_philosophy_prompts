I'm trying to sharpen my understanding of material constitution by testing two frameworks against a somewhat contrived but revealing case. On one side is Lynne Rudder Baker's constitution view, according to which a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct entities that happen to occupy exactly the same region of space at a given time; the statue possesses properties like being representational or aesthetically valuable that the clay qua clay does not, and the relation is one of asymmetric dependence rather than strict identity. On the other side is a dominance-based hylomorphic account, which denies that there are two objects in one place and instead holds that the clay, when organized and functioning as a statue, simply is the statue—the clay's ordinary sortal properties are suppressed or backgrounded by the dominant form, and there is only one entity with a layered, context-dependent identity.

Now apply both to the following scenario. Imagine a statue carved not from homogeneous marble but from a sedimentary clay composite with chemically distinct internal strata—some hard and calcified, others porous and friable. The sculptor removes material only from the outer layers, leaving an untouched core intact. Over time, water seeps through microscopic fissures and erodes those untouched internal strata, so that eventually the statue consists only of the carved exterior shell surrounding an empty cavity or a region filled with conservation resin and new mineral deposits, though the external form, representational content, and artistic properties remain fully intact. Under Baker's constitution view, does the original statue survive if the very clay that originally constituted it has largely vanished? Or does the statue disappear, replaced by a new one constituted by whatever matter now occupies the shell? By contrast, on the dominance view, does persistence depend on the continuity of the dominant form rather than on the persistence of any particular underlying matter, allowing the statue to survive as long as the carved exterior maintains its functional and aesthetic profile regardless of internal substitution?

Evaluate which framework gives the more coherent account of persistence through partial internal replacement, and consider whether Baker's view is forced either to accept arbitrary cutoffs for how much original matter must remain or to proliferate successive statues each constituted by a slightly different remaining portion of clay. Finally, ask whether the dominance view avoids these problems only by smuggling in a criterion of form-continuity that is itself vague, or whether it genuinely offers a leaner ontology without excessive multiplication of coincident objects.
