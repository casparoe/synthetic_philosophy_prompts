Consider the gender-equality paradox, in which countries with stronger legal and institutional gender equality often show greater occupational segregation by sex rather than less. Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises in interpreting this pattern, specifically one that contrasts a revealed-preference explanation—where reduced social constraint allows intrinsic differences in interests or risk preferences to shape career choice—with a structural-feedback explanation, in which egalitarian institutions generate new gendered signaling mechanisms through competitive credentialing or status-seeking within ostensibly neutral meritocratic structures. Center your example on a concrete occupational divergence, such as the gender composition of engineering versus nursing or of primary-school teaching versus corporate management, and show how the same empirical distribution can be read either as evidence that culture previously suppressed natural differences or as evidence that culture is actively reshaping itself around new axes of distinction. Do not resolve the tension; instead, articulate what is conceptually at stake for theories of justice, particularly whether treating free occupational choice as the endpoint of equality might inadvertently obscure how institutional neutrality can reproduce gendered outcomes.
