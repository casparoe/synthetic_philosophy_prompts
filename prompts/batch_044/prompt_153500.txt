I'm drafting my position for a philosophy of mind seminar and want a consistency check before I present. I hold the following commitments, not necessarily ordered by importance. First, phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness are genuinely distinct kinds of mental state, not just two descriptions of the same thing, and there can be subjective experience that exceeds what is globally available for reasoning or report, as suggested by overflow effects in rapid serial visual presentation tasks. Second, I accept the global neuronal workspace account—specifically the late-broadcast version in which information becomes access-conscious only after global cortical dissemination following an initial pre-conscious processing window of roughly three hundred to five hundred milliseconds—as the correct functional architecture for human access consciousness, but I deny that this model explains, or even attempts to explain, phenomenal consciousness itself. Third, because phenomenology is not functionally defined, I maintain that any attribution of phenomenal consciousness to another being, human or artificial, must be mediated by access-based indicators such as verbal report, intentional behavior, or architectural integration, none of which can guarantee the presence of subjective experience. Fourth, despite that evidential limitation, I think it is rational to attribute phenomenal consciousness to other humans by analogical inference from my own first-person case, yet I also hold that an artificial system implementing an identical global workspace architecture should be denied moral status precisely because broadcasting alone gives us no analogous first-person basis for attributing phenomenology to it. Fifth, I take there to be no conceptual contradiction in the idea of a system that fully realizes global broadcasting—meeting every functional criterion of workspace consciousness—while being a complete phenomenal zombie with respect to subjective experience, so that broadcasting is neither necessary nor sufficient for phenomenology.

Do these commitments actually hang together? I want an audit rather than a lecture: trace which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give—whether one of the listed commitments, an unstated assumption, or a qualification—and what that revision costs. I am especially unsure whether my acceptance of the workspace model for human access consciousness conflicts with my denial that it illuminates phenomenal consciousness, and whether my asymmetrical treatment of humans and artificial systems can survive the evidential gap I have accepted.
