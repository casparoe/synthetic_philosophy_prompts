I write fiction and think in terms of focalization, diegetic levels, unreliable narration, free indirect discourse, and the contract between implied author and reader; I do not think in the vocabulary of philosophy of mind. Restate Dennett's heterophenomenology—the methodological proposal that first-person reports of conscious experience be treated as texts describing a heterophenomenological world, that the investigator adopt a neutral stance regarding whether the entities and events described in those texts are real, and that the resulting data be used to study the subject's theory of consciousness rather than consciousness itself—using only the conceptual resources of narrative craft. For each of the three moves named above, identify the closest literary analog, show how the analog functions differently, and note what must be distorted for the fit to hold. Then provide the residue report: which commitments central to heterophenomenology, particularly any deflationary or eliminativist implications about phenomenal consciousness, lack any counterpart in how novels construct and interrogate interiority; and whether that gap suggests that narrative theory misses something essential about subjective report, or that heterophenomenology relies on a presupposition about text and reality that fiction does not share. No bibliography, no citation of sources, and no summary of Dennett's broader corpus are required; I want only the translation and the diagnosis of what fails to translate.
