I have been thinking about debates over welfare conditionality and the rhetoric surrounding the undeserving poor, particularly in contexts where workfare requirements, benefit fraud surveillance, and mandatory job-search reporting are defended. I keep encountering a specific argumentative structure that does not map cleanly onto the familiar fallacies and framing concepts I already know, and I am unsure whether it has an established name in social policy analysis or political philosophy.

Consider a defender of strict conditionality who first justifies sanctions and policing mechanisms by appealing to fiscal discipline and the need to prevent fraud, often invoking vivid but statistically unrepresentative anecdotes. When critics respond that fraud rates are low, that administrative policing costs frequently exceed the savings recovered, and that universal unconditional transfers often have lower overhead, the defender shifts ground: the primary justification is not fiscal but normative, namely that unconditional aid erodes the reciprocal obligation between citizen and state and that work requirements restore dignity and social membership. So far this resembles a standard move from efficiency-based reasoning to moral reasoning.

But a second, more distinctive shift then occurs. If the critic accepts the reciprocity framing and asks why the same logic is not applied consistently to large corporate subsidies, inherited-wealth tax advantages, or unemployment benefits for executives—forms of state support that also lack direct labor reciprocity—the defender does not extend the reciprocity principle to cover these cases. Instead, they revert to a different register: those other cases are economically productive, they involve different contractual relationships, or they are already regulated by other mechanisms. Crucially, they do not defend the reciprocity principle in general; they defend it only for poor recipients. Meanwhile, if the debate returns to the question of expanding unconditional cash transfers, the defender immediately reactivates the fiscal and fraud-based rationale, as though the reciprocity argument had never been presented as the core justification. The two justifications—fiscal prudence and reciprocal morality—are not integrated into a stable normative theory; they function as interchangeable defensive layers, with the boundary of who counts as undeserving being redrawn on the fly to protect whichever justification is currently under pressure.

This pattern seems more specific than simple hypocrisy, motte-and-bailey, or special pleading, because both justifications are genuinely held and publicly articulated, yet they are not coordinated into a consistent framework. It also differs from plain ideological rationalization, since the conclusion—that poor people should face conditions—remains constant, but the normative architecture supporting it is rebuilt depending on which comparison case is introduced. Is there an established concept for this kind of oscillating, comparison-resistant justification structure in welfare-state theory? If not, how should one delineate it from adjacent notions like framing effects or strategic ambiguity, and what would be the key boundary conditions—for instance, whether the oscillation requires both justifications to be sincerely believed, or whether it can occur with purely strategic deployment? I would also be interested in what further case comparison—say, between workfare sanctions and conditional subsidies for wealthy investors—would best reveal whether a given defender is running this particular structure rather than an ordinary inconsistent preference.
