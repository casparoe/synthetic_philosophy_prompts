For my senior philosophy seminar, I am examining Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is not a singular physical entity but a high-level pattern—what he terms a strange loop—capable in principle of running on more than one neural substrate. To avoid another expository essay, I want to present an original poem that performs this idea structurally rather than merely describing it. The poem should be in blank verse, divided into two stanzas of roughly seven lines each. The first stanza should speak from within a single first-person consciousness, using concrete sensory images to evoke the familiar feeling of being one continuous "I" anchored in one body. The second stanza should redeploy the same images and syntactic frames, but with pronouns and possessives rearranged so that the grammatical subject becomes ambiguous: the "I" should be readable either as the original speaker now observing from outside, or as a second brain that has internalized the same pattern, or as both simultaneously. The turn between the stanzas should occur without any change in setting or explicit announcement; the shift should be carried solely by this grammatical redistribution, so that the form itself enacts the absence of a clear boundary between one self and another. I want the poem to resist both a mystical interpretation (that the two brains form one soul) and a purely informational interpretation (that one has simply copied data into the other), leaving the ontological tension open. Please do not include citations, footnotes, or references to specific editions; the poem should be conceptually accurate for someone familiar with Hofstadter's view that an "I" is a strange loop rather than a single object, and poetically coherent for a reader who is not.
