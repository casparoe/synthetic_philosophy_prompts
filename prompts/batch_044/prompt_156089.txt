I am drafting a philosophical framework for a novel in which two characters—one recovering from systematic skepticism, the other an unyielding common-sensist—debate whether an argument can genuinely establish the existence of objects outside the mind without already assuming what the skeptic puts in doubt. Rather than a catalog of named philosophers, I need a decision tree that sorts the available stances on what is conventionally called Moore's proof, built from logical commitments rather than historical labels. The root node should ask whether the direct exhibition of a particular object—say, holding up one's hand and saying "here is a hand"—can legitimately function as a premise in an argument for an external world, or whether such an exhibition already presupposes the conclusion the skeptic denies. From there, the tree should branch according to three concrete distinctions: first, whether the skeptical challenge is best construed as a demand for justification, a demand for explanation, or a demand for the elimination of a specific deceptive hypothesis; second, whether perceptual knowledge is logically prior to any argumentative proof or can only be vindicated through argument; and third, whether a successful proof, if successful, establishes metaphysical existence, establishes only epistemic entitlement without metaphysical proof, or functions solely as a therapeutic dissolution of philosophical confusion. Each terminal leaf should name a coherent position—such as direct demonstrative realism, the entitlement view that denies proof is necessary, the circularity objection requiring independent premises, the pragmatic-therapeutic interpretation, and the grammatical-certainty critique that distinguishes knowing from being able to doubt—located in the logical space of answers. I want every intermediate node phrased as a question that a reader could answer without knowing which philosopher advocates which view, and I want you to flag any combination of answers that produces an unoccupied branch, briefly diagnosing whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The instrument should contain approximately six to eight leaves, must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be constructed so that its taxonomy emerges from the structure of the questions rather than from a predetermined list of authorities.
