In my independent study of Wittgenstein's On Certainty, I keep returning to a disagreement that feels circular. One group of readers takes hinge propositions — for instance, that the earth existed long before my birth, or that I have hands — to be a peculiar kind of groundless belief that still carries epistemic weight; another group insists they are not beliefs at all but background conditions, like rules of grammar, that make belief and doubt possible in the first place. Both sides appeal to the same sections of the text, yet neither seems to concede anything. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of knowledge, a verbal dispute about what counts as a belief, or a case of two groups asking different questions — one epistemological, one therapeutic or grammatical — under a single heading? Please use what each side actually says about the status of hinges, and consider whether a hidden formal issue — such as whether the word proposition is being used uniformly — is doing the hidden work. I also want to know what, given your diagnosis, would count as real progress: a new conceptual distinction, a reinterpretation of Wittgenstein's remarks on certainty and grammar, or a demonstration that the question itself is ill-posed. Finally, I am curious whether framing hinges as analogous to Kuhnian paradigm commitments — framework principles that are not tested individually — illuminates the debate or merely imports an alien model; does that comparison change your diagnosis?
