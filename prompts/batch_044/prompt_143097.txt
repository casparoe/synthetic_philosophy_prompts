I’ve been turning this over since a long thread on a rationalist forum about whether prediction markets actually aggregate anything meaningful when every participant shares roughly the same world-model, calibration curve, and background evidence. The discussion kept returning to the classic no-trade result—if agents begin with common priors, are risk-neutral, and update rationally on shared information, then in competitive equilibrium no party should wish to trade with another unless there is genuinely private information, because the market price itself reveals all commonly known data. Yet institutions routinely design internal prediction markets—corporate boards using them to weight R&D allocations, forecasting tournaments in policy labs, even some governance experiments—treating the resulting price as an epistemic summary of collective belief, even though the traders are usually drawn from the same expert pool with largely overlapping models. I am trying to understand what philosophical commitments that institutional practice actually presupposes about belief, value, and rational interaction. Does running such a market implicitly commit the organization to the view that participants do not share common priors, or that common knowledge of rationality systematically fails in practice? Or does it presuppose something quite different—that prices encode not subjective credence but a revealed-preference or social-coordination structure that need not be interpreted through Bayesian updating at all? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival epistemologies of markets could license it without contradiction, or does it genuinely require one specific account—perhaps a heterodox view of belief aggregation or a rejection of the standard common-knowledge framework—to remain coherent? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge and value that makes the design intelligible, to distinguish strictly necessary commitments from those that are merely compatible, and to say whether replacing that underlying view would force concrete changes in how these markets are structured or interpreted. Please present your answer as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, written as a single careful letter.
