I am drafting a proposal for a semester-long independent study in moral philosophy (twelve weeks, deliverable: a 25-page paper and a brief workshop presentation). The topic is Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and the problem of one thought too many. Williams argues that utilitarian moral reasoning can require an agent to treat her deepest commitments—what he calls ground projects—as merely one factor in an impartial calculus, producing the characteristic failure mode of having one thought too many, as when a person saves a stranger rather than his spouse only because the calculus permits it rather than because she is his spouse. Existing responses largely divide between utilitarian defenses that absorb ground projects into preference-satisfaction or weighted utility, and Williamsian defenses that insist on the incommensurability of ground projects with moral demands. Both sides leave largely unexplored whether the critical force of the objection depends specifically on a first-personal phenomenology of commitment that cannot survive translation into third-personal institutional rules. My precise question is therefore: can an institution—such as a hospital triage committee or an artistic patronage board—have integrity in Williams’s sense, or does applying the objection to institutional decision-making either collapse it into standard cost-benefit reasoning or reveal that integrity is inherently anti-institutional? The method will be conceptual analysis comparing first-personal and third-personal modes of commitment, plus examination of two structured case studies (medical triage protocols and long-term artistic patronage obligations) without empirical data collection; I will not pursue a historical-textual exegesis of Williams’s corpus, since that risks substituting interpretation for structural argument. A successful result would be a principled distinction between role-integrity and personal-integrity that either saves or undermines the institutional application, together with criteria identifying when institutional reasoning crosses into the one-thought-too-many failure mode. The project fails if the distinction collapses into preference-based reasoning or if the institutional extension turns out to be trivially resolvable by existing utilitarian frameworks. Please separate what is settled about Williams’s original argument from what is genuinely contested regarding its extension to institutional agents, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as whether ground projects are incommensurable with impartial moral demands—as if it were the consensus view.
