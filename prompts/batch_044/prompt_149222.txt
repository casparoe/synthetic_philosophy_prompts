As a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, I've been trying to understand John Taurek's argument that in a rescue case—choosing between saving one stranger and saving five—the numbers should not count, because each individual's claim to be saved is equally strong and these claims do not aggregate into a collective weight. I tried to formalize the reasoning so I could apply it to algorithmic resource-allocation logic, and I ended up with an argument that seems to prove rational system design is impossible if pairwise equality holds. Here are the steps:

(1) In a direct pairwise choice between saving person A and saving person B, both have an identical moral claim, so saving either is permissible.
(2) This equality is strictly pairwise and non-aggregating: B's claim relative to A does not change in strength merely because C, D, E, and F are also in danger.
(3) Consequently, in a choice between saving A alone and saving B, C, D, E, and F together, there exist only separate pairwise comparisons—A versus B, A versus C, and so on—each yielding parity, with no mechanism by which the five claims sum into a stronger collective reason.
(4) Therefore the agent should be indifferent between saving A and saving the five.
(5) Now apply the same structure to a load-shedding decision in distributed computing: the system must either terminate one critical job or terminate five critical jobs belonging to the same priority class.
(6) By the same logic, each of the five jobs bears a claim equal to that of the single job, and these claims remain non-aggregating.
(7) So the system should be indifferent between terminating one job and terminating five.
(8) Yet a scheduler built on this principle would ignore queue depth and trigger cascading failures; any engineer would reject it as irrational.
(9) Thus, if we insist that rational allocation requires counting the jobs in (7), consistency demands that we reject the non-aggregation premise in (2) or (3) for moral rescue as well, which means numbers do count after all.

This conclusion feels suspicious, but I cannot pinpoint the slip. Is the error an equivocation in (5) between moral claims and engineering priorities? A hidden modal shift between the permission in (4) and the design requirement in (7)? Or is the argument actually valid, meaning that accepting pairwise equality in ethics really does commit us to abandoning it everywhere—including in system design? I want to know which step to reject, why that step initially looks sound, and what the standard philosophical responses pay to reject it—especially whether doing so forces us to treat individuals as fungible units, or whether it merely separates moral reasoning from practical optimization.
