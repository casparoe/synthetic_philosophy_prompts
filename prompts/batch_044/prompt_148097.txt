I am preparing this for an assignment in my graduate seminar on moral philosophy and the institutional sociology of philanthropy. Over the past semester, two fellow students—one working from a critical-institutional framework and one embedded in the effective-altruism research community—have been debating whether the organizational architecture of modern effective altruism is structurally sound or systematically self-undermining. Rather than continue exchanging objections that miss each other’s premises, they have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement they should both be able to sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than obscured by rhetorical framing.

Treat the two parties as follows. The Institutional-Critique Party maintains that effective altruism’s dependence on ultra-high-net-worth donors creates path dependencies that privilege quantifiable, short-to-medium-term interventions; that the “earning to give” career model functions as moral licensing that separates wealth accumulation from structural redistribution; and that the movement’s measurability standards conflate epistemic tractability with moral significance, thereby systematically excluding relational, democratic, and long-run institutional goods. The Optimization-Defense Party maintains that large-scale philanthropic impact requires precisely the professionalized fundraising and career-path integration that critics mistake for corruption; that earning to give is ethically permissible and often obligatory when an individual’s comparative advantage genuinely lies in high-income labor; and that measurability functions as an epistemic corrective to unreliable intuitive reasoning, not as an arbitrary filter.

Please produce the collaboration document in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Jointly Endorsed Claims (approximately 350 words): Numbered statements both parties accept about the empirical state of effective-altruism institutions, the logic of cause prioritization, and the conceptual distinctions required—specifically including an agreed distinction between epistemic measurability (the confidence with which an effect can be estimated) and ontological measurability (whether the effect is constituted by properties that admit of quantitative representation).

2. Residual Disagreements: A table with three columns—Disagreement Topic, Institutional-Critique Formulation (phrased so that party accepts it), and Optimization-Defense Formulation (phrased so that party accepts it). Cover at least: (a) the moral status of earning to give when labor markets are non-competitive or structurally coercive; (b) whether measurability bias can be corrected by institutional design or is inherent to utilitarian aggregation; and (c) whether donor dependence necessarily distorts cause selection or can be structurally insulated through governance mechanisms.

3. Crux Identification (approximately 300 words): Identify three cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party closer to the other. One must concern whether institutional autonomy and local democratic legitimacy possess independent moral weight in cause evaluation, separate from aggregate welfare outcomes. Another must concern the comparative predictive reliability of long-run institutional-impact forecasting versus direct welfare metrics over decadal timescales. The third should be constructed in the weeds of decision theory and institutional design—specifically, whether a portfolio framework that includes causes scoring poorly on quantifiable welfare metrics but highly on autonomy-preservation can remain coherent without collapsing into ad hoc exception-making.

4. Joint Tests and Divergent Predictions (approximately 350 words): For each crux, specify one concrete hypothetical case or piece of reasoning both parties agree is relevant, and state in advance what each party predicts. Include: a 25-year comparison between direct cash-transfer scale-up and democratic-institution-strengthening in a specific hypothetical low-income context evaluated under conditions of radical uncertainty about institutional decay rates; and a conceptual test regarding whether autonomy-preservation metrics can be incorporated into cause-ranking frameworks without undermining the epistemic function those frameworks serve.

Keep all formulations neutral: neither party should feel that its view is caricatured. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific unpublished papers or named scholars beyond the general positions described above. Write in formal academic prose appropriate for a philosophy seminar.
