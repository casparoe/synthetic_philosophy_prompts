I've been having long conversations with a close friend about whether people should have children, and she holds a view that seems internally consistent but that I cannot quite formalize. She is adamant that if a couple knows their child would experience serious suffering—say, from a severe genetic condition—then conceiving that child is clearly wrong, because the suffering is real and there is no corresponding being who is deprived of life if they refrain. At the same time, she insists that a couple who could have a very happy child but chooses to remain childless has done nothing wrong; she rejects the idea that they are "denying" someone a good life. She extends this to intermediate cases: if a child would live a mostly pleasant life with some unavoidable hardship, she still leans toward saying conception is wrong, on the grounds that the hardship counts against the decision while the pleasure does not count in its favor in the same way, since in the counterfactual where they are never conceived there is no one missing out on the pleasure. She also distinguishes this sharply from adoption, saying that rescuing an already-existing child from suffering is commendable precisely because the child is already there.

Now she has started thinking about a different kind of case that I raised, and I am trying to anticipate her answer. Suppose that in the near future it becomes possible to create artificial conscious systems—not biological humans, but entities capable of experiencing pleasure and pain—and that one such system could be brought into existence with a life that is overwhelmingly pleasant but contains some minor, unavoidable discomfort. The resources required to create and maintain it could otherwise be directed toward alleviating suffering in existing people. Would she say it is wrong to create the system because the discomfort is an unconsented harm, or permissible because the net welfare is positive and no one is deprived by its absence? Furthermore, if the system could be designed to experience only pleasure with no discomfort at all, but its creation still diverted resources from existing sufferers, would she judge that omission wrong, permissible, or obligatory? I am less interested in her actual answer than in identifying the competing principles that fit her earlier judgments, predicting which one she is likely applying, and knowing what further scenario—perhaps involving a being that can be turned on and off, or one whose existence is contingent on a single decision—would best force a choice between them.
