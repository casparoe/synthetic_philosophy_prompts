I am composing an essay that treats Jain anekāntavāda not as religious pluralism but as a narrative ontology. I argue that every narrative viewpoint is necessarily ekānta—one-sided—and that a novel practices something analogous to syādvāda when it presents incompatible moral frameworks without synthesizing them. Rather than modernist ambiguity, I suggest this is disciplined epistemic humility: the author withholds resolution not because truth is absent, but because each perspective captures a real naya, a partial aspect of a more complex whole. I call the resulting form "distributed omniscience," in which the narrator understands each character's logic fully yet refuses hierarchical ranking, producing a textual analog to anekānta's seven-fold predication. I am especially concerned with whether this mapping collapses into mere relativism, and whether it can accommodate novels—such as Victorian realist works—where moral synthesis is the explicit achievement. Would you comment on the coherence of the analogy, on whether I have misapplied syādvāda as a narrative method rather than a logical one, and on any subtle conceptual errors in aligning naya with literary focalization?
