I am redesigning an upper-level philosophy seminar for next term—twelve students, seventy-five minutes, focused on comparative ethics of personhood—because the subject has become personally urgent since I recently moved back into my parents’ multigenerational household to assist with elder care. I need a runnable in-class simulation, not a lecture or debate, that makes students feel the divergence between a bounded, rights-bearing self (the dominant Western liberal framing) and a relational, role-constituted self (the Confucian and broader East Asian framing). The point is not that the two traditions weigh individual against collective goods differently, but that they disagree about what counts as damage to a person in the first place: specifically, I want students to encounter the force of what I will call, for the debrief, the “non-fungible relational position”—the idea that disrupting a network of roles does not merely sum harm across replaceable nodes, but destroys a pattern in which certain individuals’ very identities are constituted.

Please design the session with the following elements.

Setting and materials. Divide the twelve students into six paired “households,” each holding an envelope with two card-types: an “Individual Integrity Card” listing private preferences, bodily needs, and personal projects, and a “Relational Position Card” assigning each student an irreplaceable network role (e.g., elder mediator, filial child, peripheral ally, institutional memory-keeper). Provide each group with twenty “Protection Tokens” representing collective resources—time, authority, physical safety, narrative continuity—along with a hidden “Crisis Scenario” card describing an irreversible event (the collapse of the shared institution, the death of the central figure, or forced dispersal). The rules should require unanimous or supermajority votes on token allocation every ten minutes across three rounds, but the scoring must be asymmetric: for students holding Bounded roles, points are awarded for preserving individual integrity cards intact; for students holding Relational roles, points are awarded only if the specific relational pattern (not merely the individuals within it) survives, even if that requires sacrificing the integrity cards of the role-holders themselves.

What should happen. The mechanics should be arranged so that preserving every bounded individual’s autonomy systematically fractures the relational network—tokens spent on bodily safety and private preference pull resources away from maintaining role-interdependence—while preserving the network forces some students to vote against their own individual integrity cards in ways that feel, from within the bounded framework, like self-erasure rather than self-preservation. Students should discover this tension through strategic alliance and betrayal, not through facilitator explanation: for example, a bounded-role student should realize that a relational-role student who “gives up” protection tokens is not being altruistic but is rationally preserving the only structure in which her self is intelligible. Use only symbolic stakes—no real money—measured in “Narrative Continuity Points” awarded after each round according to the hidden role-type scoring rules.

What participants should come away seeing. By the end, students should have experienced that the Western and East Asian frameworks are not competing utility functions but competing ontologies of harm. They should leave with the concrete intuition that, in the relational view, protecting a replaceable biological individual while letting the role-network dissolve is not a partial victory but a form of damage that the bounded framework lacks the vocabulary to register.

The debrief. Map specific simulation moments—votes, broken alliances, the point where bounded-role students interpret relational sacrifices as false consciousness or coercion, and the moment relational-role students realize they have “won” by metrics they do not personally value—onto the philosophical distinction. Predict what will go wrong the first time: bounded-role students will likely try to “rescue” relational-role students by offering them individual protection, thereby destroying the very network those students are trying to preserve, and the facilitator must resist intervening too quickly. Be explicit about which feature of the relational view the activity cannot reproduce: it can stage structural damage and strategic interdependence, but it cannot reproduce the phenomenological experience of having one’s self literally extend into another person’s role, so the facilitator must supply that conceptual bridge verbally after the mechanics have done their work. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid referencing niche theories that require specialist knowledge; build everything around the “non-fungible relational position” framing injected above.
