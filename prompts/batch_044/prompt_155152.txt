I work as a senior reliability engineer for a regional automated-rail authority, and every quarter I must review clusters of anomalous braking events across our fleet. The safety board asks whether two failures are "the same mechanism," but they slide between three distinct demands: whether the events share a root causal pathway in the hardware, whether they would respond identically to a targeted intervention, and whether they realize the same functional failure mode regardless of implementation. I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in about forty-five minutes per cluster, with only diagnostic logs, thermal readouts, and partial teardown reports available—that takes a pair of anomaly descriptions and determines, in sequence, which of these three registers of mechanistic sameness holds, which are absent, and where the evidence is genuinely silent. The procedure should list the ordered tests or questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to stop and declare sameness or difference at each stage, and explicitly flag the points where the call requires an engineering-judgment sign-off rather than being settled by the data. It must also specify a terminal "indeterminate—escalate" branch for cases where the sameness question collapses into a liability or design-ethics dispute rather than a repair decision. Apply the procedure to three cases: first, two trains showing identical sensor-lag deceleration signatures but operating under markedly different ambient temperatures and load weights; second, a braking delay traceable to a firmware timing-loop error versus one traceable to a mechanical friction increase that produces the exact same deceleration profile; and third, a cluster where two subsystems that have never previously interacted fail jointly in a way that makes causal-pathway attribution ambiguous, so the procedure should return its "indeterminate—escalate" verdict and explain exactly why structural resolution is impossible here. Finally, report what this procedure is structurally incapable of resolving, particularly regarding emergent cross-subsystem interactions and counterfactual predictions about interventions that have never been tested on the actual hardware.
