A colleague in a medieval political philosophy reading group summarized Aquinas's view on tyrannicide as follows: Aquinas holds that private citizens are generally forbidden to kill a ruler who has become tyrannical, not mainly because of the risk of social disorder, but because a legitimately constituted ruler possesses an ontological dignity tied to his office—something like a sacramental status—that makes direct private violence intrinsically disordered regardless of how severe his abuses become; only a public authority, such as a senate or the Church, may depose him. By contrast, if the ruler is a pure usurper with no legitimate title, private individuals may resist and even kill him, since no higher authority exists to judge the case and the usurper entirely lacks this metaphysical dignity of office. The colleague added that Aquinas therefore treats the permissibility of tyrannicide as turning almost entirely on the ceremonial and metaphysical legitimacy of the ruler's original ascent, rather than on the practical severity of the tyranny or on whether the common good can be protected by other means. From this, he concluded that Aquinas's framework makes the moral evaluation of killing a ruler depend more on ritual legitimacy than on measurable harm to subjects, and that this renders Aquinas's position essentially incompatible with modern human-rights-based objections to tyranny.
