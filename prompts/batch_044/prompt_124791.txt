As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of history, I am struck by the structural resemblance between Polybius’s anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun’s decay of asabiyya across generations, and the morphological life-cycles proposed by Spengler and Toynbee, yet I remain skeptical that these amount to more than retrospective narrative patterns imposed on linear or stochastic processes. Give me your actual credence—between 0 and 1—that cyclical theories of large-scale historical dynamics identify a genuine structural feature rather than an analogical projection. Decompose it into: (a) the probability that institutional and civilizational decay exhibits predictable, repeatable patterns largely independent of cumulative technological change; (b) the probability that the cycles described by Polybius, Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee are genuinely isomorphic mechanisms rather than superficially similar narratives; (c) the probability that linear-progressive or random-walk models cannot accommodate the same historical data without ad hoc adjustments; and (d) the penalty you assign to the absence of successful out-of-sample predictions by cyclical theorists. Assign a rough number to each term, show how they combine into your overall credence, identify the term that most moves your number, and specify what empirical or conceptual finding would shift that term. End with a single-line verdict in the form Verdict: [number between 0 and 1].
