I'm drafting an argument for a workshop on decision-theoretic paradoxes and their epistemic extensions, and I want to see whether the structure I'm using collapses into a familiar skeptical argument or whether it genuinely reveals a tension in superrationality theory. The idea is to take the Platonia dilemma—in which two isolated agents with identical reasoning capacities must converge on a single symmetric choice because each can model the other as a perfect mirror—and combine it with the structure of the Luring Lottery, where rationality seems to require avoiding an option precisely when the payoff conditions depend on one's own desire for them. From these I derive a general principle about rational acceptance, then apply it to belief.

Specifically, the argument runs as follows. (1) In the Platonia dilemma, superrational agents in symmetric choice situations must select the same option because each runs the other as a subroutine of her own reasoning. (2) In the Luring Lottery, an agent should reject a ticket whose value is conditional on her not valuing it, since desiring the payoff undermines the conditions for receiving it. (3) Taken together, these support a broader principle: rational acceptance of an option is legitimate only when the conditions for that option's success are not self-defeating with respect to the agent's acceptance. (4) Extending this from action to belief, a doxastic state B is rationally maintained only if B's being true, or B's being justified, is not undermined by the agent's holding B, and remains stable under reflective endorsement. (5) For any contingent empirical belief—say, that there is liquid water on Mars—one can construct a skeptical scenario in which the agent holds B but her cognitive mechanisms are unreliable; in that scenario, holding B does not contribute to B's truth and the justification for B collapses under reflection. (6) Therefore, for every contingent empirical belief there exists a reflective, self-undermining condition that violates the principle in (3). (7) Consequently, rationality requires suspending judgment on all contingent empirical claims, retaining only logical truths and the structural conditions of rationality itself. (8) Yet total suspension is practically self-defeating, since action requires empirical guidance. (9) So the argument seems to show that either the principle in (3) fails, or rationality is incompatible with empirical belief.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, but each step looks defensible in isolation. I am particularly attached to (3) because it captures what I take to be the unifying insight behind both Platonia-style convergence and Luring-style avoidance. At the same time, I do not want to abandon (8), since the practical self-defeat of global skepticism seems like a genuine constraint on any theory of rationality. If I have to choose, I would rather preserve (3) and (8) and reject something in the middle—perhaps the move from decision-theoretic structure to doxastic norms in (4), or the modal slide in (5) that treats the existence of a skeptical counterfactual as a violation of reflective stability. Could you diagnose which step actually carries the illegitimate weight? I am especially interested in whether the flaw is an equivocation between causal or predictive dependence (as in the lottery) and epistemic dependence (as in belief), or whether it is a scope error in applying a principle designed for symmetric strategic interaction to the solitary case of justification. What would rejecting my preferred step cost for the analogy between superrational choice and rational belief?
