Consider a bronze statue subjected to a decade-long, volumetrically uniform atomic replacement: each original bronze atom is substituted by a synthetically engineered alloy atom with an identical crystalline lattice, arranged so that at no point is there a discrete spatiotemporal boundary separating original matter from replacement matter, while the statue's macroscopic form, weight distribution, and causal capacities remain continuously invariant. Please construct a formal argument for the following position: the entity that exists at the conclusion of this process is neither identical to the original statue—since the spatiotemporal continuity of its original constituent matter has been disrupted—nor identical to the original lump of bronze—since that lump's identity conditions require the persistence of its specific material parts—nor merely the original statue repaired; rather, it is a distinct, successor-constituted object that instantiates the statue's form without inheriting its material history. Furthermore, argue that this case undermines the dominance argument—by which the statue and the lump are said to differ in modal and temporal properties—by demonstrating that constitution is not a two-place relation between form and matter, but a triadic relation mediated by a continuity-preserving transformation rule that can be satisfied even when both the original form-bearer and the original matter-lump have ceased to exist in their primitive states. Number your premises, address at least one objection (specifically, the claim that distributed replacement is indistinguishable from gradual repair and therefore preserves the original statue), and explain why the absence of any discrete replacement boundary blocks that reply. Keep the argument self-contained: do not invoke specific named theorists or texts by citation, and do not include a bibliography.
