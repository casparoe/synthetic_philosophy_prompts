I have recently joined the board of a small charitable trust that stewards a Victorian public garden, and at our last meeting we debated whether to remove a low stone wall that separates the herb garden from the orchard. The wall obstructs accessibility and serves no visible purpose, yet I have been reading about the principle often called Chesterton’s fence — that one ought not tear down a barrier until one understands why it was erected — and about the Burkean suspicion that rationalist reconstruction often destroys practical knowledge embedded in tradition and habit. I should like to close our next meeting with a poem that holds this tension open, since I genuinely do not know whether the wall conceals a lost drainage function, a social boundary meaningful to the garden’s original users, or nothing at all.

Please write a poem of exactly nineteen lines, in British English, with the first and last lines identical. Lines 2, 6, 10, 14 and 18 must each begin with the word “Because”, constructing an ascending chain of possible reasons for the wall — beginning with practical explanations and growing more speculative and less tangible — so that the form itself enacts the accumulating uncertainty of reform. The tenth line must serve as the volta: it should turn from practical justifications to the epistemic humility central to Burkean conservatism, acknowledging that the wall may preserve a form of knowledge that its removers do not possess. Include an internal rhyme linking “wall” and “fall” in the opening lines, and linking “know” and “go” in the closing lines, with an otherwise irregular rhyme scheme. The poem should treat Chesterton’s point not as a slogan but as a lived problem: the wall must still be standing at the end, the question unresolved, and the tone contemplative rather than didactic. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with conservative political philosophy recognises the argument, yet accessible to board members who have no specialist background. Do not resolve the tension; leave the reader, like the board, with the wall intact and the reason for it still unknown.
