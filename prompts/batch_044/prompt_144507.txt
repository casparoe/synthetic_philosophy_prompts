I am a novelist preparing a manuscript in which a protagonist faces a choice between a deeply held personal loyalty and an act that would maximize welfare for those she loves, and in researching the philosophical dimensions of this dilemma I have returned repeatedly to Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarian reasoning, especially his observation that certain moral demands require a form of calculative reflection that introduces one thought too many into relationships that should be sustained by direct attachment rather than by justification. I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that supports philosophical work conducted in dialogue with artistic practice, and I need a two-page research proposal that treats the following precise question: does Williams's objection identify a corruption that is specific to utilitarian moral reasoning, or does it reveal a more general structural flaw in any normative framework that demands transparent, calculative justification for commitments that are constitutive of identity, and can this be tested by constructing parallel cases in narrative fiction where an author's calculation of overall narrative utility threatens to violate a character's integrity? Existing philosophical treatments of Williams remain largely within moral philosophy and rarely investigate whether the integrity objection has analogues in aesthetic or dramatic domains, which leaves open whether the problem is genuinely about morality or about any system that subordinates constitutive attachments to external optimization; meanwhile, literary discussions of character integrity rarely engage with the normative structure Williams describes, so neither field has settled whether there is a meaningful parallel. My method would be to reconstruct Williams's central argument in its strongest form, then to develop a set of parallel narrative cases in which a character's loyalty or personal project is overridden by an authorial calculation of thematic coherence, plot resolution, or reader satisfaction, analyzing whether the intuitive wrongness in these fictional cases tracks the same phenomenological feature Williams identifies in moral cases or differs in kind. A useful result would be a clear demarcation criterion that distinguishes legitimate artistic manipulation of character from a kind of aesthetic utilitarianism that violates narrative integrity, together with an assessment of whether this criterion strengthens, weakens, or transforms Williams's original moral claim; failure would occur if the parallel between moral and narrative integrity collapses upon examination, or if the distinction proves too vague to guide either philosophical analysis or my novel's construction. Given the six-month scope, I will limit the philosophical reconstruction to Williams's principal essays on integrity and moral luck, restrict the narrative analysis to three canonical dramatic structures rather than attempting an empirical study of audience response, and present the proposal entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, writing as if in a single sustained letter.
