I spent last night arguing with a friend about Galen Strawson's basic argument that true moral responsibility is impossible. She thinks the infinite regress can be blocked if we allow that an agent might constitute her own mental nature in a single, atemporal act—something like a self-creating loop that doesn't depend on any prior mental state explaining it. I tried to show her why Strawson rejects this, and I kept coming back to a passage where he says, in effect, that any change you make to your own mental nature must be made by the mental nature you already possess, so you can never be the ultimate source of the nature that explains the change; and if one imagines this self-creation happening all at once, without temporal stages, the problem merely shifts, because there is still the question of why this particular nature was instantiated rather than any other, which demands an explanation outside the act itself. Can you help me understand whether Strawson's block on this loop depends essentially on a temporal notion of explanatory priority—earlier states explaining later ones—or whether it rests on a more general principle that every determination requires a prior determiner? And if we granted, purely for the sake of argument, that a being could genuinely instantiate its own nature ex nihilo in this atemporal way, would Strawson concede that such a being could be morally responsible, or would he still deny it on other grounds?
