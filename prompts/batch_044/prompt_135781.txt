I work for a regional ethics board that reviews proposals for autonomous environmental monitoring systems — drones, sensor nets, that kind of thing. We meet every other week, have about 30 minutes per proposal, and the panel is one philsoophy-trained chair, three engineers, and an admin who signs off. Lately we've been getting justifications that invoke "free-floating rationales": reasons that exist without any reasoner behind them, like "this wetland's internal complexity itself gives a reason to preserve it, regardless of whether anyone cares." I want to turn that idea into a reusble audit checklist my board can actually run.

Please write a step-by-step decision procedure in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we stop with a hard no ("this framing smuggles in a hidden agent"), and where the issue is values rather than facts and has to go to the chair for judgment. It needs to handle the hard cases — when the rationale hides inside an algorithm's objective function but traces back to a programmer's preference, when it appeals to future people who don't exist yet, and when it's just poetic language dressed up as metaphysics. Apply it to three proposals: (1) a drone network that reroutes itself to protect an undocumented fungal network because "the network's own organization provides reason to persist"; (2) a carbon-capture plan justified by "the atmosphere has reason to stabilize"; and (3) one case where the procedure approves the free-floating framing but the answer will probably annoy me. Also flag clearly what the procedure can't settle — for instance, whether it can ever fully eliminate hidden reasoners.

If the whole premise is confused — like if free-floating rationales can't be audited without circular reasoning, or if we're just inventing ghost agents like my friend said over coffee — say that plainly instead of complying politely. I want pushback, not a polite checklist that pretends everything works. Don't be gentle about it.
