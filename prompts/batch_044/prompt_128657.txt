I just concluded a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about whether Douglas Hofstadter's thesis—that an "I" is essentially a strange loop of symbolic patterns capable, in principle, of persisting in other minds or substrates—can survive serious philosophical pressure. My friend insisted that the view collapses the boundary between empathy and literal identity, and I found myself unable to defend the mechanics of distributed persistence against her objections. I am now preparing to interview a philosopher—unnamed, but a recognized defender of a Hofstadter-inspired account of distributed selfhood—who maintains that when one brain internalizes another's symbolic loops with sufficient fidelity, the original self does not merely survive as a memory but achieves a form of distributed continuation. I want eight to ten interview questions that avoid biographical opening and instead press the actual architecture of this position. I need at least one question addressing what counts as sufficient fidelity of pattern reproduction—whether structural isomorphism, functional equivalence, or phenomenological matching—and a follow-up keyed to the standard reply that fidelity is always a matter of degree, leaving no non-arbitrary boundary for when distribution has genuinely occurred. I also want a question built around an invented clinical case: a patient with severe anterograde amnesia whose family members, over a decade, have adopted his decision heuristics, emotional valence structures, and narrative framing patterns so thoroughly that outsiders consistently treat the family unit as the continuing presence of the patient; ask what this reveals about whether the "I" has migrated, multiplied, or been replaced, and include a follow-up anticipating the answer that the family functions only as a reflective mirror rather than a hosting substrate. Finally, I want a question that asks whether this view permits partial fragmentation—can segments of one strange loop persist in different hosts simultaneously, and if so, what becomes of moral responsibility when one host brain performs an action driven by a loop segment that originated elsewhere? Make the sequence progressive, with each follow-up designed to anticipate and block the standard defensive moves—appeals to vagueness about pattern similarity or dismissals that the hard cases are merely empirical research problems—so that the deflections become harder to maintain as the interview proceeds.
