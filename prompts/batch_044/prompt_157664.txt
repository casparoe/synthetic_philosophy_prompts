I retired from marine biology about five years ago—mostly tidal ecosystem stuff—and now I'm trying to actually think about philosophy instead of just skimming articles. I was in an arguement with my neighbor about Chesterton's fence and Burkean conservatism, specifcally that idea that you shouldn't tear down an institution unless you know why it was built. I think I have gut verdicts on when that applies, but they feel inconsistent, and I'm wondering if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just rationalizing my politics.

So here are my cases. There's an old town ordinance requiring every new building to have a front porch of at least six feet; the reason was some 1920s social-engineering idea about neighborliness that's now forgotten. People want to scrap it for modern apartment blocks. I think tearing it down is mostly fine—no hidden ecological or social function I can see, and the state shouldn't enforce porches. Then there's this bizarre ritual at my old university where grant proposals have to be printed in triplicate and hand-stamped by a secretary who's been there forty years. They want to digitize it. I think keep the stamp—there's friction that slows bad proposals, even if nobody designed it for that, a bit like how we don't remove a predator until we map its trophic cascades. But then there's a law in some state that requires doctors to ask family-history questions in a fixed, weird order designed for 1970s data entry; doctors now say the sequence actually catches missed diagnoses because it forces a systematic check. So tearing that up is bad. Meanwhile there's a social club that requires members to wear a specific tie to vote; the tie was originally a class marker, and now it's just exclusionary. I think tear that fence down, even though members say "maybe there's a hidden reason we don't understand."

My verdicts: porch law (fine to drop), grant stamp (bad to drop), medical questionnaire (bad to drop), club tie (fine to drop). Is there a coherent Burkean principle that gets all four? It feels like I'm using "hidden beneficial function" to save the ones I like and "unjust origin" to drop the ones I don't, which sounds like I'm smuggling in values. Is the real principle epistemic humility about unintended consequences, or does the moral status of the origin matter too? And what about the case I'm torn on—my local library still keeps an old card-catalog system next to the digital one, not because it's efficient (it isn't), but because the librarian claims it teaches kids how information was organized before algorithms. Some people want to scrap it for space. I keep flipping. If there's a principle here, does it tell me to keep the catalog or let it go? Also, which of my four verdicts does it force me to overturn, if any?
