At a recent symposium on comparative moral philosophy, two panelists fell into a sharp disagreement over whether the Mahayana practice of merit transfer—where a practitioner dedicates accumulated positive karma to benefit another being, often a deceased relative—reveals a hidden consequentialist structure in Buddhist ethics. The first argued that because merit is treated as quantifiable, directional, and capable of being redirected across lives like a transferable resource, the system implicitly treats well-being as an aggregable quantity summed across persons and rebirths; on this reading, the bodhisattva vow to liberate all sentient beings is structurally equivalent to a commitment to maximize collective welfare over an indefinite temporal span. The second countered that merit is not a unit of welfare but a purificatory status that operates relationally: its transfer alters karmic conditions rather than adding happiness to a total, and the universal vow expresses the elimination of partiality rather than an optimization target. The first then pressed that if outcomes do not aggregate in a linear fashion, there is no principled reason to prefer saving "all beings" to saving "some beings," which makes the universal vow arbitrary unless it masks a maximization principle. The second replied that in Abhidharmic analysis the moral weight of any act depends on the intensity of the mental states involved and on the "field" or quality of the recipient—factors that resist simple summation—so that even if outcomes matter, they cannot be totaled like utility, and that the vow's scope is deontological in binding the agent regardless of whether aggregate suffering actually declines. Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move: identify which premises hold up, where each speaker conflates institutional form with ethical theory, and whether Buddhist ethics can consistently maintain both its non-aggregative account of moral weight and its universal scope of concern?
