I am constructing a scene for a historical novel set in the 1780s, in which a moral philosopher—clearly modelled on Jeremy Bentham—attempts to distil his private argument against the legal punishment of same-sex intimacy into a single formal poem. For this manuscript-within-the-manuscript, I need a villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and finally stand side by side in the closing four lines. The first refrain should state the core utilitarian observation—that these acts produce no measurable harm and only innocent pleasure—while the second should expose the circular logic of the prevailing moral condemnation, in which an act is called unnatural solely because society has already resolved to punish it. The poem's relentless return of these lines should perform the very circularity being criticised: each refrain comes back unchanged, like a prejudice that will not be examined, until the final stanza forces them into proximity and reveals that they are not opposing truths but complementary descriptions of the same institutional error. The voice must remain analytical, restrained, and appropriate to the period—no modern identity terminology, no sermonising, no direct quotation from the philosopher's unpublished writings—while being philosophically precise enough that a reader acquainted with Bentham's reasoning on the subject recognises it immediately. Please treat the form as part of the argument: the villanelle's repetition should feel inevitable and almost suffocating, then break, in the last lines, into something like clarity.
