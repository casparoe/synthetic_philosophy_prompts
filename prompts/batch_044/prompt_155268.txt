Here is an argument about individual contributions to collective harms that my study group has been debating. Each premise seems defensible in isolation, yet together they seem to erase private moral agency altogether. Please diagnose where the chain breaks, which step is least costly to reject, and what each repair option commits us to.

(1) A direct moral duty not to perform action A on account of harm H requires that A be a cause of H.

(2) In large-scale collective problems—climate change, industrial animal agriculture, democratic elections—any single token action (one car trip, one meat purchase, one ballot) makes no measurable or perceptible difference to whether H occurs.

(3) If an action makes no measurable difference to H, then it is not part of the causal chain that produces H.

(4) So driving alone, eating meat in private, or casting one vote in a large election are not causes of climate damage, animal suffering, or the electoral outcome, respectively.

(5) Therefore, there is no direct moral duty to refrain from these actions on the grounds of preventing the corresponding harm.

(6) One might reply that indirect duties—expressing values, setting an example, sustaining collective norms—still apply.

(7) But indirect duties depend on being observed or influencing others; in genuinely private, unobservable circumstances they vanish.

(8) Hence, in private contexts there is no moral reason whatsoever—direct or indirect—to reduce one’s carbon footprint, abstain from meat, or vote.

(9) This applies universally: whenever an individual’s contribution is imperceptible within a collective structure, all moral reasons to act differently disappear.

(10) Therefore, the inefficacy objection, if sound, eliminates not just specific obligations but the very idea of individual moral agency within large-scale social systems.

We are especially drawn to keeping (6) and (9), since we think moral life cannot be purely public. Is the flaw an equivocation between token-causal efficacy and contribution to a collective pattern in the move from (2) to (3)? Or is the argument valid, forcing us to abandon (1) and accept a theory of structural complicity or moral risk that applies even when token effects are nil? If we save direct duties by rejecting (3), must we accept that moral reasons can track imperceptible causal contributions? If we instead reject (7), must we deny that privacy can ever extinguish expressive obligations? Please name the precise step, explain why it looked plausible, and price each alternative—particularly whether repairing the argument requires treating collective harms as linear aggregates rather than threshold-governed systems.
