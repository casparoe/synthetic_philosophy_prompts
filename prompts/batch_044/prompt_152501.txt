I have just come out of a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether strategic reasoning can ever sanitize the deliberate killing of noncombatants, and we kept circling back to G. E. M. Anscombe’s protest against Harry Truman’s honorary degree—specifically her claim that intentionally killing the innocent as a means to an end is murder rather than an act of war, however politically expedient. The conversation left me thinking about Stanley Kubrick’s Dr. Strangelove, which I assume you know well, but I want to press the comparison further than the obvious parallel between nuclear command and presidential decision-making.

Read the film through Anscombe’s framework, especially the distinction she draws in her philosophy of action between what is directly intended and what is merely foreseen. Does the satire turn on that very distinction? I am particularly interested in how her analysis of intention might classify the Doomsday Machine’s automatic retaliation and General Ripper’s unauthorized launch order: are these, in her terms, cases of intentional killing of civilians, or does bureaucratic and technological distance place them in a different moral category? Point me to specific scenes—the War Room casualty estimates, the accidental bombing report, the final montage—where Anscombe’s moral absolutism either illuminates Kubrick’s comedy or where the film’s absurdist framing genuinely resists being captured by her taxonomy. I want a reading that takes both the narrative logic of the film and the philosophical rigor of her position seriously, without reducing either to a mere illustration of the other, and that ends by saying what the work adds to the debate that philosophical argument alone does not.
