I've been trying to analyse my actual views on the regress problem — foundationalism versus coherentism versus infinitism — by checking my gut reactions to concrete belief situations. Here they are.

If I believe my hand is in front of me because I see it, that feels like a proper stopping point. I don't think I need a further belief to justify the seeing itself; its okay for the chain to end there. So I'm sympathetic to foundationalism in that spot.

Then there's climate change: I believe it because every expert I check agrees, and tracing citations back eventually reaches 19th-century measurements I can't verify personally. The chain doesn't bottom out, but it doesn't loop either — it just stretches back futher than I can follow. That still feels fine, so infinitism seems okay here.

But consider my flatmate: I think they're trustworthy because they've kept promises, and I interpret their actions as promise-keeping because I already trust them. That's circular, and it feels clearly rotten — like cheating. So a tight coherentist circle of personal belief feels wrong.

Here's the tension. I also think my belief that contradictions are impossible is partly supported by the fact that it works everywhere I check, but checking those domains already uses logic. So there's circular self-support going on. Yet that doesn't feel rotten at all; it feels unavoidable and actually okay. So circularity can't be the problem per se, but I can't see what distinguishes the flatmate loop from the logic loop.

Another case: there's a claim about an obscure 1923 tax rule I found in a blog. The blog cites Wikipedia, which cites the same blog. There's also an infinite regress of "who read the original statute?" and apparently nobody in the chain has. This feels doubly bad — both circular and infinitely shallow. I'd reject it.

So my verdicts are: direct perceptual belief is okay to stop; long expert chains with no ground floor are okay; tight circular personal trust is not okay; circular self-support for basic logic is okay; and mirrored citation loops with no original check are definitely not okay.

Is there one principle — maybe involving what I want to call "regress viscosity" — that captures all five? Or do I have to drop one? What's the real difference between the flatmate case and the logic case? And what would the principle say about a scientific theory that predicts the accuracy of its own measuring instruments, so the theory and the instruments justify each other in a wide loop?

Please separate what's settled in epistemology from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus.
