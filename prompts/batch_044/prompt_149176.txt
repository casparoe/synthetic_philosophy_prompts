I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of biology, and I wish to plan a ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group—mostly fellow scientists and engineers with no formal philosophical training—on why the concept of life resists sharp definition. Rather than presenting the familiar checklist of metabolism, reproduction, and homeostasis, I want to teach the idea that every proposed necessary condition is actually a purpose-relative proxy for some deeper explanatory goal, and that borderline cases therefore expose disagreements about what we want the concept to do rather than gaps in empirical knowledge. Plan the explanatory arc: the precise order in which the components must be introduced so that the audience sees the dependency—why one must establish what a definition is for before evaluating metabolism, reproduction, or autonomous boundary maintenance as candidates; the single carrying comparison that should run through the entire session, drawn from a synthetic minimal cell engineered with the smallest genome capable of independent replication, a large virus that possesses genetic material but performs no metabolism of its own, and a self-sustaining flame that propagates and adapts to fuel—specify which of these three should serve as the central case and exactly what the other two must contribute and lack in relation to it; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring, particularly the belief that science will eventually discover the correct boundary, the conflation of complexity with being alive, and the assumption that viruses are unambiguously non-living because they lack metabolism; the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off; one question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped that the dispute is about explanatory purposes rather than about missing facts; and what I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level—such as the technical details of synthetic genome engineering or evolutionary taxonomy—and state plainly that I am leaving it out. Do not provide readings, citations, or bibliographies.
