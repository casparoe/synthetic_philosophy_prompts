I have recently taken on a coordinating role for an interdisciplinary project on evolutionary dynamics in fragmented ecosystems, and a practical disagreement within my team has made me realize that the long-standing philosophical debate about the units of natural selection—genes, individual organisms, or groups—may be far more consequential for applied science than I had assumed. For decades, proponents of gene-level selection (working within a replicator-centered framework), defenders of organism-level selection (emphasizing functional integration and phenotypic coherence), and advocates of multilevel or group selection (stressing emergent fitness effects and between-group variance) have talked past one another despite operating with largely shared population-genetic formalisms. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily empirical, since all sides generally accept the Price equation and the conditions for evolutionary change, but rather a disagreement about what counts as a genuine causal explanation versus a convenient statistical partition. Some treat the unit of selection as an ontological claim about which entities literally reproduce, vary, and are causally privileged; others treat it as an epistemic or modeling choice about which organizational level yields the most predictive or intervention-relevant account. Meanwhile, a quieter but increasingly visible strand—drawing on causal specificity arguments and developmental niche construction—suggests that both mainstream sides conflate heritability with causal control, and that a rigorous separation of causal democracy from causal privilege across scales might dissolve the apparent conflict. Given this, I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: is the gene-versus-individual-versus-group debate a verbal dispute about the term selection, a substantive disagreement masked by shared mathematics, or two different questions—one about evolutionary ontology, one about modeling strategy—collapsed under a single heading? Please support your diagnosis with reference to what each major position actually asserts about replication, environmental interaction, and the conditions under which lower-level variation is screened off, and tell me whether any conceptual distinction, formal result, or empirical finding—perhaps a principled account of when causal specificity genuinely shifts between organizational levels—could genuinely move the discussion forward, or whether the impasse instead signals that the question is ill-posed for scientific purposes.
