Next Thursday I present the core argument of my paper, tentatively titled "Verisimilitude Without Convergence," in a philosophy-of-science colloquium whose regular members include structural realists committed to the no-miracles argument, instrumentalists skeptical of any metaphysics of approximate truth, and two philosophers of physics who specialize in quantum-gravity unification and worry constantly about theory choice in regimes where every auxiliary assumption is under experimental pressure. My thesis is that verisimilitude should be reconceived not as monotonic proximity to a single, uniquely true theory, but as modal robustness: a false theory ranks highly on this metric to the extent that its false components are modally inert—that is, they do not affect the predictive law-structure across physically accessible counterfactual perturbations—whereas a true but brittle theory, whose successful predictions depend sensitively on auxiliary conditions that break under small perturbations, ranks lower. I concede that this abandons any straightforward Popperian quantitative measure of verisimilitude, but I maintain that it better explains why superseded frameworks like Newtonian gravitation remain predictively stable within their domains despite being globally false.
