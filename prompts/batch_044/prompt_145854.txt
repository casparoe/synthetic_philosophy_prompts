In our philosophy-of-history reading group we've spent the better part of a year circling the Browning–Goldhagen dispute over Reserve Police Battalion 101 without anyone conceding ground. Browning reads the documented refusal rates and peer-pressure dynamics as evidence that situational forces can overwhelm ordinary moral character, producing atrocities without requiring pathological ideology; Goldhagen treats those same refusal statistics as negligible noise and insists that a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism was the necessary and sufficient causal driver. We all accept the same archival figures, we agree that suffering occurred, and yet the interventionists in our group—those who think structural explanation should dominate—have not moved the non-interventionists, who treat any situational account as an exculpatory dilution of individual responsibility.

I am beginning to suspect that the impasse is not empirical at all, but rather a hidden disagreement about the ontology of moral agency under institutional load. Specifically, Browning's picture seems to treat agency as a distributed system in which peer norms function like a control parameter that can shift individual outputs without altering underlying character, whereas Goldhagen's picture treats agency as a classical belief-desire engine whose behavioral outputs are only masked, never genuinely overridden, by social pressure. Given this framing, is the stalemate best diagnosed as a clash over which explanatory level deserves priority, as a bedrock intuition about whether character can be genuinely situation-dependent, or as a disguised normative dispute about whether structural explanations carry exculpatory weight? And if the disagreement is indeed conceptual rather than evidential, is there any kind of argument—perhaps from formal models of collective action or from experimental social psychology—that could actually move it, or should we treat this as a permanently incommensurable split?
