I run a 90-minute workshop for twelve advanced undergraduates in moral philosophy. They understand the standard duty/supererogation distinction but tend to treat supererogation as simply “very good but optional” acts whose status is intrinsic to the act or agent. I want them to feel the instability of that boundary under a specific framing: whether an act counts as going beyond duty depends partly on a distributed, partly hidden comparison class of peer behavior — a social baseline that is not transparent to any individual agent and can shift as group behavior changes.

Design a structured simulation or deliberation game with explicit rules, assigned roles, hidden information cards, and small real stakes (for example, pooled points that determine a shared reward or a public prestige ranking with minor consequences). The mechanics should unfold in three phases: first, participants classify a set of actions as obligatory or supererogatory using only individual judgment; second, hidden peer-baseline cards are revealed, showing that the same actions are reclassified by the group once the comparison class is visible; third, participants can alter group behavior in a second round, which in turn shifts the baseline and forces further reclassification. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants run into the reversal themselves before the facilitator names it.

Then provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the conceptual claim about the relational, baseline-dependent nature of supererogation; predicts what will predictably go wrong the first time the workshop is run (for instance, participants refusing to update classifications or treating the hidden information as irrelevant); and is explicit about which feature of the view — likely the normative authority of the hidden baseline rather than its descriptive presence — the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by direct discussion. Keep everything runnable without technology beyond paper and cards, and do not include any bibliography or citation request.
