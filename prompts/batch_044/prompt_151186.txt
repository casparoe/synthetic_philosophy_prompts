A friend summarized an argument she found in an unnamed essay on insect ethics as follows: "The scale argument begins with evolutionary continuity—there is no sharp boundary excluding insects from sentience. Given that roughly ten quintillion insects have existed across evolutionary history, even a per-individual sentience probability of 0.001 percent yields an expected population of conscious subjects that dwarfs humanity. So unless the probability is literally zero, insect suffering should dominate moral concern. Critics reply that this treats sentience as a stochastically distributed micro-property, like mutation rates, rather than as a kind-level biological capacity. If consciousness requires integrated information processing above a taxon-typical threshold, then either the species meets it or it does not, and multiplying by population size is a category error. Hence the scale argument is probabilistic confusion masked as moral urgency." I am unsure where the distortion lies. Does the scale argument actually treat sentience as randomly distributed within a taxon? Is the critics' "kind-level capacity" response faithful to how philosophers and biologists actually resist the argument, or does it misrepresent both sides? And once the summary is corrected, does the critics' dismissal still stand?
