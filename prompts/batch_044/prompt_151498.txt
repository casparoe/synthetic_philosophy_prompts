I was grading essays for the bioethics course I'm TAing and one student wrote about Pearce's hedonistic imperative. The exam question was: "David Pearce proposes something he calls the hedonistic imperative—the idea that we have an ethical duty to use biotechnolgy to eliminate suffering in all sentient beings. In about 300 words, explain (a) what the thesis actually claims, (b) how Pearce thinks biotechnology could implement it (focus on his actual proposed mechanisms), and (c) one philosophical objection that isn't just 'this is impossible.'

Here's what the student turned in:

Pearce's hedonistic imperative says that suffering is bad and we should use biotech to get rid of it for everyone, not just humans but animals too. He wants to edit genes so future generations have gradients of well-being instead of pain, and redesign ecosystems so predators don't cause suffering. It's different from regular utilitarianism because instead of changing society to maximize happiness, it targets the biological root—rewiring the reward circuitry through germline engineering and direct neural implants.

One big objection is the hedonic treadmill: psychologists showed that people adapt back to a baseline happiness, so if you genetically raise the baseline, you'll just adapt to that and need even more tweaks. This means the project might never reach a stable endpoint, always chasing a moving target.
