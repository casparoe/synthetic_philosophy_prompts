I'm working through a seminar on decision theory and keep running into the same stalemate: revealed-preference theorists treat utility as nothing but the pattern in choice, mentalists treat it as an inner state that choice reveals, and constructivists treat it as a normative artifact we impose on behavior. Before I commit to any defense, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory connecting revealed preference to the meaning—or reference, or content—of utility ascriptions actually has to deliver. Write the complete desiderata list.

The theory must handle the standard cases—complete, transitive preferences—but also the hard ones I keep constructing: cyclical choices that the agent reflectively endorses rather than disowns; framing reversals where the agent knows the frames are equivalent yet consistently picks differently; genuinely incomplete preferences where the agent lacks a comparison rather than being indifferent; and intertemporal shifts where the later self treats the earlier choice as mistaken without treating it as irrational. For each case, say why it functions as a constraint rather than an anomaly to be explained away, and flag any distinction the theory must preserve—such as between having no preference and being indifferent, between a revealed preference and a revealed judgment, or between behavioral stability and normative endorsement—that popular accounts tend to collapse.

Then specify the non-negotiable consistency conditions: empirical equivalence with standard choice axioms where they apply, compatibility with neuroscience that finds no single localized "utility signal," and preservation of first-person authority in at least some domains. Mark which of these are camp-specific preferences (for example, determinism about choice, or a prohibition on reference to unobservable mental states) and which are genuine adequacy conditions. Crucially, identify any subset of desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the combination of full first-person authority, complete behavioral determinacy, and zero normative construction—and explain whether the tension is structural or merely contingent.

Briefly score how a strict behaviorist reconstruction, a mental-state causal theory, and a normative-constructivist account fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard; I especially want to know which single requirement does the most damage to the standard positions. End by stating exactly what argument, empirical finding, or revised constructed case would lead you to drop one of the non-negotiables or reorder the list, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail—for instance, if cyclical reflective endorsement is incoherent, or if the neuroscience constraint is being misinterpreted.
