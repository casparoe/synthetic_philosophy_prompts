I am working through the metaphysics of personal origin—specifically, the origin-essentialist claim that one could not have come from different gametes—by collecting my intuitive verdicts on a range of counterfactual and technologically mediated cases. I want to determine whether these judgments cohere around a single principle, or whether I am implicitly relying on incompatible commitments that must be revised.

Consider the following seven cases and my judgments about them.

Case 1 (the standard counterfactual). Had a different sperm fertilized my mother’s egg, or had my father’s sperm fertilized a different egg, the resulting person would not have been me. This judgment is firm.

Case 2 (gestational relocation). The same sperm and egg unite, but gestation occurs in a different uterus via surrogacy. The child is still me; the gestational environment is metaphysically irrelevant.

Case 3 (xenocytoplasmic reconstruction). The nuclear genomes of my parents’ sperm and egg combine, but the egg’s cytoplasm, mitochondria, and associated molecular machinery are entirely replaced by synthetic or donor equivalents that function identically but have no causal-historical connection to my mother’s actual egg. Here my intuition splits: part of me holds that nuclear continuity suffices, so the result is still me; part of me holds that disrupting the cytoplasmic lineage breaks the origin relation in a way that surrogacy does not.

Case 4 (embryo fission). A single zygote formed from my parents’ gametes undergoes monozygotic twinning. I am inclined to say that the pre-fission embryo was me, but I am genuinely uncertain whether both twins are me, whether only one is me, or whether fission destroys the original person and creates two new ones. I keep returning to this case without resolution.

Case 5 (synthetic gametogenesis). Induced pluripotent stem cells are differentiated into gametes that carry genomes exactly matching those of my parents’ actual sperm and egg, yet no biological gametes from my parents are ever involved. These synthetic gametes fuse to produce a genome identical to mine. Despite the perfect nuclear match, I judge that the resulting child is not me; the absence of the actual causal chain through my parents’ reproductive cells seems decisive.

Case 6 (pre-cleavage editing). The same sperm and egg unite, but before the first division precise genome editing alters approximately five percent of the nuclear sequence. The result is substantially genetically divergent from me, yet I lean toward saying it is still me. This creates a direct tension with Case 5: there, perfect genetic identity is insufficient for identity, whereas here substantial genetic divergence is apparently tolerated.

Case 7 (nucleic transfer). My father’s sperm fertilizes an enucleated donor egg; all nuclear material derives from my parents, but all cytoplasmic and mitochondrial material is foreign. This feels analogous to Case 2—probably still me—yet I cannot clearly articulate why it should differ in verdict from Case 3.

My request is twofold. First, construct the most coherent principle—whether it concerns causal-historical continuity of specific cells, preservation of nuclear genome, preservation of cytoplasmic lineage, or some other factor—that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible without ad hoc adjustments. State the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and specify its predictions both for Case 4 (the fission case I keep revisiting) and for an unconsidered variant: suppose synthetic gametes are engineered not to match my genome perfectly, but to produce a genome that differs from mine by exactly the same five percent introduced in Case 6. Second, be explicit about which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, rank the necessary revisions by philosophical cost, and say whether the simplest consistent package requires overturning my judgment in Case 5, in Case 6, or in my wavering on Case 3. I want an honest reflective equilibrium, not a principle preserved at any price.
