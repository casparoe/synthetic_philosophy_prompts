A colleague recently circulated a summary of tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering bears intrinsic value for well-being—which I would like you to audit for fairness. According to her account, tranquilism treats welfare as a purely negative magnitude: every increment of well-being corresponds exactly to a reduction in craving or suffering, with no independent positive component whatsoever. She further claims that, on this view, even apparently fulfilled first-order desires are instrumentally pernicious, since satisfaction typically generates second-order cravings for repetition, preservation, or intensification, thereby planting the seeds of future suffering; consequently, the tranquilist must regard all desire-fulfillment as covertly dangerous rather than genuinely good for the agent. From these premises she draws two conclusions: first, that a life of complete affective flatness—devoid not only of pain but also of excitement, curiosity, love, or any other positive affect—would be maximally excellent, provided only that no craving remains; and second, that tranquilism therefore collapses into negative hedonism expressed in Buddhist vocabulary, with a practical upshot of ascetic withdrawal from worldly engagement rather than any form of active flourishing.

Please examine this summary claim by claim. Which parts would a careful defender of tranquilism accept as accurate, which involve subtle conflations or overstatements—particularly regarding the relation between absence of craving and absence of all positive mental states, the status of fulfilled desire, and the quantitative framing of welfare—and which conclusions simply do not follow from the accurate premises? In particular, address whether the inference to negative hedonism survives once the distinction between craving-cessation and pleasure-maximization is kept clear, and whether the characterization of the view’s practical recommendation as necessarily ascetic rests on a hidden equivocation between equanimity and emotional blankness. I am less interested in whether tranquilism is true than in whether this is a fair map of its terrain.
