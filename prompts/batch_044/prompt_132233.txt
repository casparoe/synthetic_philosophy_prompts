You are a professional philosopher of death and harm, with expertise in Epicurean arguments, deprivation accounts, and narrative theories of well-being. Before giving your answer, please take a moment to brainstorm the different principles that could underlie the pattern of judgments described below, and think carefully about which ones genuinely diverge on the case at hand.

My advisor, Dr. L. Chen, has been working through a series of hypothetical cases for a seminar on whether death can be bad for the one who dies. Her verdicts so far are consistent and explicit:

- Case 1: A 93-year-old dies peacefully in sleep after a long, fulfilled life. Chen says this is not bad for her.
- Case 2: A 24-year-old is killed instantly and without warning by a falling object. Chen says this is bad for him.
- Case 3: A fetus is miscarried at 22 weeks. Chen says this is not bad for the fetus.
- Case 4: A patient has been in an irreversible vegetative state for a decade, then dies of infection. Chen says the death adds no further harm; the harm, if any, was the loss of consciousness.
- Case 5: A healthy 45-year-old is murdered painlessly during sleep, never anticipating it. Chen says this is bad for her.
- Case 6: A couple decides against having children; the child they might have had never comes to exist. Chen says this is not bad for that possible child.

Now consider Case 7: Mira is 51. She has just finished writing a major philosophical work and arranged, with full rational consent, to be euthanized the following day to avoid a predicted, excruciating neurological decline. She has no desire for further life beyond this planned exit; she considers her narrative essentially complete once the work is finished and the exit is self-chosen. However, the night before the scheduled procedure, she is killed accidentally by a falling tree branch. She never learns of the accident, never experiences the decline, and never receives recognition for her work.

What do you think Chen would say about whether Mira's death is bad for Mira? I am less interested in a simple yes-or-no than in the reasoning path.

Specifically:
1. Identify at least two distinct general rules that fit all six of Chen's prior verdicts equally well.
2. Say what each rule predicts about Case 7, and whether they agree or diverge.
3. Give your best guess at Chen's actual verdict, with the reason you prefer one rule over the other (or why you think she is genuinely undecided).
4. Propose one further hypothetical case—different from Case 7—that would most clearly distinguish the two rules you identified.

Please do not list bibliographies or cite specific texts; work from the conceptual structure alone.
