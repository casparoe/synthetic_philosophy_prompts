You are acting as a senior philosophical advisor specializing in moral aggregation, distributive justice, and the ethics of collective choice. Please think carefully and step-by-step; begin by briefly brainstorming the conceptual landscape—specifically whether quantitative differences in affected individuals can generate a qualitatively stronger moral claim, or whether treating headcount as decisive involves a category mistake about how individual entitlements combine.

Here is the concrete situation: I serve as the independent ethics auditor for a remote, self-governing research collective operating in an isolated environment. The collective has adopted a strict charter stating that no resident may receive preferential protection based on personal connections, professional rank, or perceived utility; every member possesses an identically weighted entitlement to collective safety. Recently, an unprecedented infrastructure failure has created an emergency in which a single mobile repair system can be deployed to exactly one of two distant modules before backup systems collapse entirely. Module A currently houses fourteen residents; Module B houses four residents. The threat is uniform and imminent for all occupants. There is no hidden asymmetry—no difference in vulnerability, no prior agreement, and no special obligation linking me to either group.

One faction of the governance board argues that the charter’s equality clause applies only within each module, not across them, and that sending the system to Module A is obligatory because saving fourteen lives is self-evidently better than saving four. Another faction contends that this reasoning illicitly transforms a statistical fact into a moral weight: if each resident’s claim is genuinely equal, then multiplying identical claims inside a group does not produce a super-claim capable of overriding the equal claims of those in the smaller group. They worry that adopting numerical weighting as a principle would violate the charter’s core commitment to treating persons as non-fungible. I am not asking for generic risk-management advice or a simple vote tally. Rather, advise me on the philosophical structure of this disagreement: does the mere fact that more lives hang in the balance constitute a legitimate reason to prioritize Module A, or does such aggregation misunderstand how individual moral claims combine? In particular, please address whether there is any coherent sense in which “the numbers count” without abandoning the premise that each person’s entitlement is of equal and non-aggregable strength.
