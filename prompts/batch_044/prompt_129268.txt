I've been puzzling over a strange split in how we treat Einstein's remains versus his writings. His actual brain was preserved after death, kept in formalin, studied, and handled with an almost relic-like care—damaging it feels like a serious loss to science and to his legacy. Meanwhile, his books and papers are copied by the millions; destroying one physical copy is treated as ordinary property damage, not as harming Einstein in any way. Yet in everyday language we constantly treat books as if they contain living minds—people say they are "in conversation" with a dead author, that a text "speaks" to them, or that reading is a meeting of minds.

This tension reminds me of a thought experiment I encountered, from a book called The Mind's I, in which someone asks whether a perfect book—one that held all of Einstein's knowledge and responded exactly as he would—could literally be conscious. Our real-world behavior seems to assume consciousness is tied to the biological tissue, since we protect the brain but feel nothing special about destroying the text. But our metaphors about books suggest we think the patterns, responses, and organization matter more than the material.

What theory of mind or consciousness does this combination of practices actually commit us to? Does preserving the brain but freely copying and discarding books presuppose that being conscious requires being made of neurons, or does it just reflect practical limits? And if the organization is what really matters, shouldn't the destruction of a unique, irreplaceable manuscript feel closer to harming a person than destroying a mass-produced paperback? Or is there a hidden reason—perhaps about memory, identity, or tractability—that lets us treat brains and books differently without taking a stand on consciousness at all?

I'm not looking for citations or a reading list; I want to understand what philosophical view our everyday behavior quietly assumes, whether that view is consistent across both cases, and what would have to change in how we treat books or brains if the opposite view turned out to be right.
