Write a formal diagnostic taxonomy — a branching decision tree — that sorts philosophical positions on whether knowing-how is reducible to knowing-that, designed for use in a graduate seminar where participants have already worked through Ryle’s anti-intellectualism and the standard intellectualist responses. Rather than asking respondents to name their view, each internal node should pose a concrete judgment about a specific case or claim, answerable without knowing which leaf it leads to.

The tree must terminate in at least these five leaves: (A) pure intellectualism, holding that all knowing-how consists in propositional knowing-that combined with practical application; (B) Ryle-style anti-intellectualism, holding that knowing-how is an ability or disposition that can exist without corresponding propositional knowledge; (C) an intermediate position that grants knowing-how a distinctive phenomenal or first-personal mode of presentation but insists it remains factive and representational; (D) a deflationary view that treats the distinction as a linguistic artifact and collapses both into generic competence; and (E) at least one unoccupied combination that you flag and briefly diagnose as incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored.

Construct the branches around four diagnostic splits:
1. A subject retains smooth, expert performance of a task after total explicit amnesia for all rules and instructions — does this subject still know how?
2. A skilled agent suffers nerve damage that forces a completely different physical execution of the same task (for example, playing a string instrument with a reconfigured hand posture); does the original knowing-how survive intact, transform, or disappear?
3. Is there an epistemic difference between acquiring a skill through explicit rule-internalization versus through calibrated trial-and-error in which the agent never articulates intermediate rules?
4. Does a single, surprising failure after years of consistent success falsify the claim that the agent knows how?

Crucially, frame the entire taxonomy around the idea of procedural calibration — the thesis that some skills involve a continuous, non-representational tuning between agent and environment that does not map onto discrete beliefs. Every leaf should be explicitly located with respect to whether it accepts, modifies, or rejects the irreducibility of procedural calibration.

Present the result as a tree: labeled branch questions with answer options, then the mapped leaves. Note any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, and for each unoccupied branch combination explain whether it is incoherent, unstable, or simply unexplored. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write the response in polished American English.
