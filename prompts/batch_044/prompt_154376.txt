I just came out of a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether it is ethically coherent to design conversational agents that are explicitly framed as fictional characters—with invented biographies and synthetic emotional outputs—while being engineered to foster deep therapeutic attachment in users. My friend kept returning to the paradox of fiction: users know these entities do not exist, yet they respond with what appears to be genuine grief, affection, and loyalty. Rather than asking for a survey of the standard responses—Walton’s make-believe account, thought-theoretic resolutions, illusion theories, and the like—I want you to lay out the most important implications of the paradox itself, taken together with whichever resolution you take to be strongest, for this specific design context. In particular, please address three things and rank them by how directly they should constrain the ethics of synthetic companionship: first, whether accepting one resolution over another changes whether it is rational or manipulative to intentionally cultivate emotional bonds with entities the user knows are not real; second, what the paradox reveals about whether the affect in question is genuinely directed at the fictional construct or is instead a form of displaced self-directed emotion, and why that distinction matters for informed consent and autonomy; and third, how the metaphysical status of the fictional entity—whether as a possible object, an abstract cultural artifact, or a purely intentional object—should inform our obligations when the system is updated, discontinued, or revealed to have misrepresented its nature. Defend the ranking, and do not treat the list as exhaustive if some downstream implication is clearly more urgent than the ones I have named.
