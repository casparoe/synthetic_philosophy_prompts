In clinical ethics and institutional review practice, the conditions that invalidate consent differ sharply between therapeutic and research contexts, yet the distinction is rarely made explicit. A patient may provide legally valid consent to an invasive elective procedure—say, a high-risk cosmetic reconstruction or an off-label oncological intervention—despite being influenced by severe anxiety, family pressure, or a distorted self-image, provided they pass a basic competence assessment and receive standard risk disclosure; emotional distortion short of formal incompetence does not vitiate authorization. In research ethics, however, review boards frequently treat therapeutic misconception—the subject’s belief that a standardized trial protocol is individualized treatment designed for their personal benefit—as a defect that invalidates consent, even when the participant can accurately recite the study’s randomization procedures, understands that dosage is not adjusted for them personally, and has signed every disclosure document, and even in the absence of any active deception by investigators.

What philosophical theory of consent does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it rest on a procedural view—disclosure plus comprehension plus absence of overt coercion—supplemented by an ad hoc exception for research, or does it presuppose a substantive view according to which consent is only valid when the authorizing mental state is formed under correct beliefs about the nature of the interaction? If the latter, why does a false belief about personalization in research vitiate consent, while false beliefs about likely success in therapy, or emotional states that systematically distort risk evaluation, do not? Reconstruct the implicit account: is it a hybrid that distinguishes deception-induced error from other cognitive or affective distortions? Is it consistent with the legal system’s parallel insistence that negligence liability depends on reasonable care rather than on the victim’s correct beliefs about risk? And what would have to change in clinical practice—perhaps mandatory psychological screening for elective procedures, or the abolition of therapeutic-misconception screening in trials—if we replaced the implicit theory with a uniformly procedural one?
