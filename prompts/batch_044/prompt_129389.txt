Analyze the February 1944 Gestapo search of the ten Boom residence in Haarlem—specifically Corrie ten Boom’s direct verbal denial that hidden Jews were present in the secret room—through the lens of Augustine’s absolute prohibition on lying as developed in De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. I want a disciplined two-way mapping: first, apply Augustine’s definitional criteria (intentional falsehood ordered to deception, the rejection of mendacia officiosa, and the permitted distinction between active utterance and mere silence or ambiguous speech) to the concrete interaction—identify the agents, the available options (truthful disclosure, strategic silence, equivocal response, explicit false denial), and whether ten Boom’s denial counts as mendacium under his framework rather than as permissible concealment. Second, examine what the episode reveals about the theory’s reach: does the extreme asymmetry between just aggressor and innocent victim expose a gap in Augustine’s account of language’s teleology, or does it instead confirm that the corruption of the speaker’s will is independent of the listener’s moral standing? In particular, consider whether a later scholastic concept sometimes anachronistically read back into Augustine—mental reservation—could reconstruct her utterance as non-deceptive, and whether Augustine’s explicit arguments against such strategies in Contra Mendacium foreclose that reconstruction. Be precise about where the theory is silent—does it distinguish lying to an unjust authority from lying in ordinary social cooperation?—and do not force Augustine to speak where he does not. Finally, assess whether the case functions as a practical reductio of his view, or whether it demonstrates a cost he was prepared to accept, and say what that implies for the coherence of an exceptionless moral prohibition on false speech.
