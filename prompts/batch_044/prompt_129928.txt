I have been working through some literature in democratic theory and find myself puzzling over the institutional architecture that has become standard in contemporary evidence-based governance, particularly the division of labor between democratic goal-setting and technical evaluation. In this widespread practice, legislative bodies or administrative agencies identify a desired social outcome—such as reducing recidivism, improving early literacy, or lowering sectoral emissions—and then delegate the design and assessment of interventions to specialists who run pilot programs through randomized controlled trials. If the trials yield a statistically significant effect of sufficient magnitude, the program is scaled nationally, with democratic approval often functioning as little more than ex ante authorization and ex post ratification. Citizen participation is largely restricted to selecting the initial targets and holding representatives accountable at election time, while the interpretation of null results, the revision of measurement instruments, the diagnosis of implementation failure, and any reconsideration of the normative framing remain the exclusive province of researchers and bureaucrats. Unexpected outcomes are typically treated as technical errors—poor fidelity, insufficient dosage, confounding variables—rather than as occasions for collective democratic judgment to reconstruct its own ends in light of experience.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of inquiry, the authority of democratic participation, and the relationship between knowledge and collective decision-making does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a sharp separation between a political context of value-formation and a scientific context of empirical justification—a separation that Dewey’s experimentalist conception of democracy, in which social inquiry is itself a cooperative, participatory process of hypothesis-testing and end-revision, would reject? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology and democratic theory embedded in the practice: is democracy here conceived merely as a mechanism for aggregating preferences that experts then optimize, or could the same RCT-based architecture be reinterpreted within an experimentalist framework in which citizens are co-inquirers rather than passive sources of goals? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether its allocation of interpretive authority necessarily presupposes an instrumentalist view of democratic legitimacy. Finally, if one took seriously the view that democracy itself ought to function as a form of social inquiry, what concrete institutional changes would be required—particularly regarding who frames the research questions, who possesses standing to challenge the interpretation of a null result, and how normative objectives are permitted to shift in response to empirical surprises?
