Please write the opening remarks for a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Quietism in Practice: Tranquilism, Care Design, and the Value of Absence." The audience is split evenly: on one side, palliative-care clinicians and hospice architects whose daily work is shaped by the removal of pain and distress; on the other, academic philosophers of well-being and value theory, many of whom defend positive-state or pluralist accounts of the good life and will be instinctively skeptical of the workshop's premise. You are the convener, and you want to welcome both groups genuinely while imposing a strict "craving-neutral" framing on the proceedings—a framing you have been developing that treats every proposed theory of well-being not as a competing good to be achieved or synthesized, but as a potential source of intellectual craving whose reduction, rather than satisfaction, is the real purpose of the gathering.

Your philosophical commitment is tranquilist: you hold that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive mental states—insight, fulfillment, intellectual excitement, aesthetic enrichment—are at best the absence of disturbance, not goods in themselves. This commitment must do real structural work in the address. You must not promise the philosophers that they will leave with a richer theory, a breakthrough synthesis, or an expanded conceptual framework. You must not tell the clinicians that the workshop will "enhance" their practice with new positive goals or aspirational designs. Instead, frame the two days as a collective exercise in reducing conceptual suffering: the particular discomfort of holding incompatible theories about what makes life go well. For the clinicians, draw the parallel to analgesic spatial design—environments and practices valued for what they take away rather than what they add. For the philosophers, acknowledge that this framing sounds like a denial of their usual subject matter, but ask them to treat that denial as a temporary methodological experiment rather than a settled conclusion.

Write the remarks as a polished, approximately five-minute address (around 650–800 words). Avoid citations, bibliographies, named texts, or appeals to specific authorities. Do not use quotation marks for any passage drawn from a real source. The tone should be hospitable but conceptually rigorous, and both halves of the room should feel precisely addressed—neither spoken down to nor allowed to mistake the welcome for a conventional call to intellectual enrichment.
