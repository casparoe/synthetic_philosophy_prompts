I'm organizing a seminar on philosophy of mind, and our final session will center on eliminative materialism—the thesis that everyday mental states such as beliefs and desires are not real entities but theoretical posits of a folk psychology destined to be replaced by mature neuroscience. Rather than ending with another analytical summary, I want to offer the group a short story—roughly 800 to 1,000 words—that dramatizes this replacement from the inside, without relying on named philosophers or explicit doctrinal labels.

The setting should be a forensic evaluation unit in a near-future jurisdiction where criminal law has abolished "intent," "belief," and "desire" as legal categories, substituting mandatory connectome mapping and structural brain-state reports. The protagonist is a senior evaluator whose job is to translate defendants' first-person narratives—"I believed he meant harm," "I wanted to escape"—into purely neural descriptions for the court. Over the course of the story, she encounters a recurring anomaly: a small subset of defendants generate rich, coherent folk-psychological accounts that resist translation into any stable neural profile, yet they show no detectable neuropathology. The narrative should not resolve whether these cases vindicate eliminativism (our neuroscience is simply too crude) or undermine it (the mental states are genuinely real but currently invisible). Instead, it should leave the tension open, perhaps closing on a moment when the evaluator herself utters an untranslatable mental-state verb about her own experience and finds she cannot map that utterance onto her own readout. Please avoid bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists; the philosophical work should emerge entirely from character, setting, and situation.
