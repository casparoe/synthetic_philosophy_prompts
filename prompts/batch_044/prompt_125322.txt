Write a deadpan parody of an institutional art-insurance policy—approximately 600 words, formatted as numbered clauses—for a museum that loans a classical marble statue while its original clay armature is gradually replaced during conservation. The policy’s fine print should resolve, in bureaucratic language, the standard puzzles of material constitution: a “no-branching” clause addressing what happens if the discarded clay fragments are shipped to a second museum and reassembled into a rival statue; a definition of “the Insured Object” that distinguishes the statue from its co-located lump of matter; provisions governing “temporary parts” versus “permanent parts”; and a dispute-resolution mechanism for continuity claims. The satire should exaggerate the real metaphysical practice of treating intuitive identity conditions as if they were enforceable regulations, and the humor should come from accurate deployment of concepts—dominant sortals, endurantism, perdurantism, four-dimensional preservation, and co-located entities—rather than from misunderstanding them. Include at least one invented quotation from a “Senior Conservator” who insists on common-sense realism about the statue, voiced deadpan. The piece should be legible to a non-specialist but should reward a reader familiar with the statue-and-clay literature; do not cite named philosophers, texts, or dates. Inject one non-standard twist: the policy must cover a “shadow statue” formed from the negative space left by each removed fragment, raising a three-way identity dispute. Conclude with a single line, beginning exactly “Summary:”, that states in one sentence which metaphysical position the parody ultimately favors.
