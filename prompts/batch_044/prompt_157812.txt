I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest. At my residential community's governing board, I have observed a negotiation pattern that puzzles me. Two members, both otherwise reasonable, are deadlocked over a minor budget allocation. Each has announced that he will take an extreme, self-damaging action—resigning and opposing all future proposals—unless the other concedes. They do not actually wish to carry out these threats, but they seem to believe that making themselves appear unable to back down, or even allowing the outcome to depend partly on emotional reactions they claim not to fully control, strengthens their bargaining position. I understand that strategic interactions involve credible commitments, but I am unsure whether deliberately introducing an element of risk or seeming irrationality is philosophically defensible as rational strategy, or whether it collapses into a contradiction—using reason to engineer unreason. If I were advising them, should I treat this escalation as a legitimate technique of negotiation, or as a failure of practical rationality? What conceptual framework best captures the logic of a situation in which rational agents cultivate the appearance of being beyond rational control?
