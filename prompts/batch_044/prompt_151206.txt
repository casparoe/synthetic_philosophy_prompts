I've been revisiting the ontology of patterns in my graduate seminar on metaphysics and scientific explanation, and a disagreement with a colleague about whether design-stance patterns are genuinely emergent or merely heuristic has left me wanting a cleaner specification. Before I commit to any particular account of what makes a pattern "real" across the physical, design, and intentional stances, I want to know what such a theory is actually obligated to deliver.

Write the desiderata: what must an adequate theory of real patterns explain or preserve if it is to do justice to all three stances without collapsing them into one another? For each requirement, give the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it—whether it concerns the compressibility of physical descriptions, the normative failure conditions that distinguish design patterns from accidental regularities, or the interpretive indeterminacy that arises when an artifact's behavior can be read simultaneously as mechanical causation and as intentional action. Be explicit about which desiderata are non-negotiable (for instance, that the theory must account for why the same structural feature can support incompatible predictions across stances) and which are preferences of particular camps (such as the demand that all real patterns be ultimately reducible to physical law). In particular, address whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—say, if respecting the autonomy of intentional explanation necessarily conflicts with the completeness of physical explanation in cases of self-modifying systems that alter their own functional architecture in response to environmental noise. I want the weight on the criteria and their justifications rather than on a scorecard of existing theories, though you may briefly indicate where physicalist reductionism, functionalist design theory, and interpretivist accounts typically fall short against your list.
