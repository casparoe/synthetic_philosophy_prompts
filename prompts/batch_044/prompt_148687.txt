Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a normative-rationalist camp about weakness of will (akrasia) and an intertemporal-mechanist camp about hyperbolic discounting. Define the two sides precisely in the document itself: Camp A holds that akrasia is a distinctive failure of practical rationality in which an agent acts against her own all-things-considered judgment, and that hyperbolic discounting is at best a descriptive model of intertemporal preference reversal that fails to capture the normative and phenomenological structure of being divided against oneself; Camp B holds that what philosophers call akrasia is exhaustively explained by hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discount dynamics—including preference reversals, the value of precommitment, and associated neural correlates—and that talk of “best judgment” and “normative residue” is a dispensable folk-psychological overlay.

The deliverable must have four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign: include the formal properties of hyperbolic discount curves, the predictive successes and failures of exponential discounting, what precommitment devices mechanistically do, the existence of cases in which agents violate prior resolutions, and any empirical findings about delay of gratification or self-control technology that both accept. Second, a disagreements table that states the residual disputes in wording each side would accept for its own view. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) whether there is a “normative residue,” i.e., a sense in which a prior resolution retains authority over the present self even after present preferences have shifted, which hyperbolic models must explain rather than eliminate; (b) whether “synchronic conflict” (feeling torn at the moment of action) and “diachronic betrayal” (violating a prior plan without present subjective conflict) are both real phenomena that must be distinguished, or whether one collapses into the other; and (c) whether precommitment succeeds by altering underlying preferences or by imposing an external causal constraint, and whether that distinction bears on whether weakness of will has occurred.

For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each camp’s prediction recorded in advance. Use the “silent-precommitment case”: an agent binds herself via an automatic mechanism (e.g., a locked account or irreversible default reversal), experiences no subjective conflict at the moment the binding takes effect, and later reports she “would have done otherwise” but does not feel she acted against herself. The rationalist should predict that even without synchronic conflict there remains diachronic betrayal and a normative failure of practical reason; the mechanist should predict that without present preference reversal there is no akrasia, only a successful self-control technology, and that the agent’s retrospective report is confabulation.

In drafting, inject the following framing—defined precisely in the agreed-claims section so the collaboration does not depend on prior familiarity: “diachronic authority” (whether a prior resolution binds the present agent in virtue of rational continuity or in virtue of an external causal constraint) and the paired distinction between “binding-cost” (the effort or welfare loss incurred in maintaining a resolution) and “binding-residue” (the normative standing that remains even when the binding-cost is eliminated by technology). Treat these as live conceptual tools, not as settled terms.

Format the response as: (1) the numbered agreed-claims section; (2) the disagreements table; (3) the cruxes with predictions; (4) an explicit statement of which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive, with reasoning both sides could accept.

Finally, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic. Do not present a camp-specific claim—such as the eliminability of normative language or the irreducibility of practical reason—as the consensus. Flag clearly which claims are widely accepted in philosophy of action and behavioral economics, which belong specifically to one camp, and which remain genuinely open.
