I've been working through some philosophy of psychiatry for a seminar, and a study partner summarized Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects for me in the following way. Psychiatric classifications, she said, are "interactive kinds": once clinicians define a disorder—say, dissociative identity disorder or autism spectrum conditions—patients learn the diagnostic criteria and begin to organize their experience and behavior around them, while clinicians in turn observe these reshaped presentations and refine the manual's descriptions. This creates a feedback loop that does not occur with "indifferent kinds" like chemical elements or astronomical bodies, which do not change in response to being studied. She added that in practice this loop is asymmetric: patients alter their self-conception to fit the label, but clinicians treat the diagnostic framework itself as fixed, so taxonomies like the DSM accumulate ever more precise descriptions of behaviors that the classification system partly produced. From this, she argued that psychiatric categories therefore lack any stable essence across time, that their boundaries are entirely socially negotiated, and that this demonstrates mental disorders are constructed in a strong anti-realist sense with no biological substrate whatsoever. She concluded that Hacking regards psychiatric taxonomy as circular and scientifically unreliable, and that we ought therefore to replace categorical diagnosis with purely narrative, non-classificatory clinical practice.

Something about this feels like it turns Hacking's analytical observation into a much stronger metaphysical and normative claim, but I am not sure exactly where the slide happens. I am particularly unsure whether Hacking denies all biological contribution, or whether he allows that looping operates alongside—rather than replaces—underlying causal mechanisms, and whether his view genuinely entails abandoning diagnostic classification altogether rather than understanding it differently.
