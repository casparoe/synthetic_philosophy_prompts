As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest, I have been working through the causation literature—mostly Pearl’s interventionist framework and Lewis’s counterfactual analysis—and have formed a tentative package of views that I intend to present to a small reading group. Before doing so, I want an audit of whether these commitments actually hang together.

My current positions are as follows:

(1) In empirical science, causation should be defined interventionistically: X causes Y just in case there exists a possible intervention on X that changes the distribution of Y, with other direct causes held fixed (the do-calculus framework).

(2) For singular causal judgments—e.g., that this particular spark caused the explosion—counterfactual dependence is indispensable: the explosion would not have occurred in the nearest world where the spark was absent.

(3) Counterfactual dependence is not sufficient for causation, because standard preemption and overdetermination cases show that a cause can be present even when the effect is counterfactually independent of it.

(4) Mental causation—specifically, an agent’s decision causing an action—cannot be captured by interventionist analysis, because an external intervention on the decision destroys its status as that agent’s decision; hence mental causation must be understood through counterfactual dependence alone.

(5) There is no metaphysically deep distinction between type-level and token-level causation; they are pragmatic variants of a single underlying relation.

(6) I reject causal pluralism: scientific causation and mental causation are not genuinely different kinds, but instances of one unified concept.

(7) Recent extensions of causal modeling to quantum correlations—representing Bell-type dependencies via intervention nodes rather than counterfactual measurement outcomes—suggest that counterfactual worlds are dispensable in physics, reinforcing the idea that the interventionist approach should be universal.

Please examine these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious. I am not signaling which view I am most willing to abandon, and I genuinely do not know where the weak points lie.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your assessment overall, and explicitly single out the one point about which you are least sure.
