Consider the following argument, developed for a graduate seminar on collective rationality, which extends a familiar decision-theoretic problem into the domain of belief formation. (1) In the standard unilateralist’s curse, each agent acts on her private assessment of the optimal course without conditioning on the fact that others are doing the same, thereby producing collectively worse outcomes; rationality therefore demands some form of deference or coordination. (2) By direct structural analogy, in a scientific community each researcher updates on private evidence regarding a high-stakes empirical claim—say, the feasibility of transformative artificial intelligence within a specific horizon—without conditioning on the fact that equally informed peers have reached contrary conclusions. (3) If rational belief revision is parallel to rational action selection, then each researcher ought to defer to the community distribution rather than to her private posterior; otherwise the community suffers an epistemic analogue of the curse, namely a cascade of overconfident, mutually reinforcing extremes that distort funding, policy, and research priorities. (4) But if this reasoning holds, it applies not only to controversial forecasts but to any domain of expert disagreement, including established scientific controversies eventually resolved by dissenting minorities; indeed, it seems to entail that a researcher who privately identifies a flaw in a dominant theory should suppress that judgment unless it survives collective aggregation. (5) That conclusion is intolerable: it collapses the distinction between rational deference and epistemic authoritarianism, and it renders scientific progress conceptually impossible. (6) Therefore, the analogy between the unilateralist’s curse in action and in belief revision must fail somewhere.

The argument slides from a genuine problem—group polarization under uncoordinated private judgment—to a sweeping prohibition on dissent. I ask you to diagnose precisely where the slide occurs. Which premise or inferential step should be rejected? Is the error in (2), which assumes structural parallelism between action and belief? Or is it hidden in (3), perhaps through an equivocation between causal interference (where actions physically interact) and evidential independence (where beliefs do not causally conflict)? Or does the mistake lie in (4), which treats all disagreement as homogeneous? Please identify the weakest link, explain why it initially appears plausible—particularly to those familiar with the decision-theoretic literature—and then spell out what rejecting it costs. If you preserve (2), do you commit yourself to a radical form of epistemic individualism? If you reject (3), do you lose the normative force of the original curse? I am particularly interested in whether there is a principled distinction between domains where private judgment should be constrained (e.g., coordinated physical interventions) and domains where it should not, and whether such a distinction can be drawn without begging the question against the argument’s conclusion.
