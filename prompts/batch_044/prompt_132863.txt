I've been working through a demarcation argument for a seminar on pseudohistory, and it keeps collapsing into the conclusion that conventional chronology is methodologically indistinguishable from Fomenko's New Chronology. Here is the chain as I have it:

(1) A research program counts as pseudohistorical if it exhibits evidential asymmetry: it accommodates confirming data uncritically but deploys ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses to dismiss disconfirming data.

(2) Fomenko's New Chronology exhibits this asymmetry with respect to astronomical records and archaeological strata that conflict with its compressed timeline.

(3) Mainstream chronology of the Bronze Age collapse and classical antiquity exhibits the same asymmetry: radiocarbon outliers, anomalous stratigraphic layers, and contradictory astronomical observations are routinely classified as contamination, intrusion, or instrument error rather than being treated as potential falsifiers of the textual framework.

(4) The presence of independent cross-checks—such as dendrochronology or astronomical retrocalculation—does not dissolve the asymmetry, because any cross-check that conflicts with the textual framework is itself subjected to the same dismissive auxiliary hypotheses.

(5) Therefore, mainstream chronology exhibits the same defining evidential asymmetry as Fomenko's program.

(6) If two programs share the feature that defines pseudohistory, then either both are pseudohistorical or that feature is insufficient for demarcation.

(7) It is not acceptable to classify mainstream chronology as pseudohistorical.

(8) Hence evidential asymmetry cannot be the demarcating criterion, and "pseudohistory" reduces to a sociological label marking lack of institutional acceptance rather than an epistemological failure.

This seems to prove too much, because I want to preserve (1) as a genuine criterion and (7) as non-negotiable, yet together they seem to force (8). I am particularly unsure whether the flaw lies in (3), (4), or in an implicit slide between (5) and (6). Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to? Must I concede that Fomenko's method is legitimate, or is there a way to save the distinction without abandoning (1)? Please answer in American English.
