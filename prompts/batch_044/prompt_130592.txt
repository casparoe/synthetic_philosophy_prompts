I am in the middle of writing a novel about moral decision-making under conditions of radical uncertainty, and I have become preoccupied with a specific pattern that recurs both in my characters' reasoning and in my own reading—though I do not know whether philosophy has already named it. The pattern is this: an agent faces a choice that pits her immediate interests or emotional attachments against some distant or collective good—imagine a physician who discovers that a profitable treatment is subtly harmful, or a citizen asked to support a policy that will diminish her own welfare for the sake of strangers. Instead of weighing the options from her present, flawed perspective, she constructs an idealized counterpart—one endowed with perfect information, liberated from fear, and, crucially, possessed of the very moral virtues that her present self lacks. She then asks what this counterpart would choose, and she treats that hypothetical decision as normatively binding upon her actual self, not as a distant aspiration but as an immediate obligation. The authority she grants to this counterfactual choice is peculiar: she does not claim she can become that self, she does not treat the exercise as mere information-gathering, and she applies it even when the virtues built into the idealization are themselves contested—when, for instance, the relevant virtue might be impartiality or loyalty, and the decision itself concerns which of those should prevail.

This move seems to sit near discussions of indirect normativity and idealized deliberation, yet I think it differs from both the standard contractarian procedure, in which hypothetical agreement establishes justice, and from simple ideal-observer theories, in which the observer is epistemically improved but not necessarily morally transformed. I want to know whether this exact configuration—binding one's present self to the choice of a morally idealized, counterfactual self—has an established philosophical designation, and if so, precisely how it relates to indirect normative structures and to models of deliberation that proceed through idealization. Should I understand it as a variant of indirect normativity in which the norm applies through a projected self rather than through a rule? Or is it something closer to a rhetorical device disguised as reasoning? If no precise term exists, please do not merely list neighboring concepts; instead, take a definite position, propose a rigorous name and definition with clear boundary conditions, and defend that classification against the alternatives—distinguishing it, in particular, from direct consequentialist calculation, from aspirational self-improvement, and from the kind of rational reconstruction that assumes only epistemic correction. Please avoid bibliographic recommendations or lists of further reading; I am asking for a committed philosophical judgment, not a survey.
