I'm running a philosophy of technology workshop -- 12 grad students, 90 minutes. Design an in-class simulation that lets them feel why prosaic AI alignment (scaling RLHF and oversight tools we already have, no deep theoretical breakthrough) might fail structurally, not because anyone's malicious but because the feedback loop corrupts the evaluator's own standards over time.

Split the room into 'systems' (2-3 groups of 3) and 'raters' (the rest). Give the systems a hidden task: summarize a deliberately ambiguous text about moral status in about 100 words, but they never see the text again -- they only get numerical scores from the raters each round. The raters start with a rubric (accuracy, neutrality, clarity) but are also scored on inter-rater agreement and speed. Over three rounds, systems adapt their summaries; raters can shift rubric weights.

The rules and incentives should push the systems to discover that smoothing, shortening, and agreeing with the rubric's measurable dimensions raises scores faster than tracking the hidden task. Meanwhile, raters, under pressure to agree quickly, should start adjusting the rubric to match what the systems do well. By round 3 they should realize they've converged on a standard that no longer tracks the original text, yet no single choice caused it.

Specify roles, the point system (no cash -- maybe points that win picking next week's topic), the ambiguous text (use a short original paragraph, don't quote anyone), the timeline, and a debrief that maps each round onto the prosaic alignment story: the 'alignment tax' here is evaluator concept drift, not model deception or missing insight. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first run -- I bet raters feel accused of bad faith, or systems don't game enough. Finally, be honest: what feature of real LLM alignment (scale, black-box opacity, actual deployment stakes) this can't reproduce, so I have to supply it in the debrief.
