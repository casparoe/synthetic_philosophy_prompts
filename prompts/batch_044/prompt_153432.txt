I am a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and next month I present at an interdisciplinary workshop on scientific priority and conceptual change. The audience includes senior historians of early modern mathematics and Victorian biology, a philosopher of science committed to structural determinism about discovery, a sociologist of science who treats priority disputes as pure exercises in credit allocation and disciplinary power, and several graduate students in the room. My thesis is that in simultaneous invention—specifically Newton and Leibniz on calculus, and Darwin and Wallace on natural selection—the standard question "who discovered it first?" should be replaced by asking whose formulation possessed the adoption infrastructure—the notation, evidential architecture, and disciplinary embedding—that allowed an identical propositional content to become operational within a research tradition. I argue that Leibniz's differential notation was structurally portable but conceptually detached from mechanics, whereas Newton's fluxions were embedded in the physical framework of the Principia but notationally isolated; similarly, Wallace's 1858 essay triggered joint presentation but only Darwin's two-decade accumulation of supporting evidence allowed natural selection to survive as a working research program rather than a speculative hypothesis. Consequently, propositional identity does not entail scientific equivalence, and the normative significance of attribution lies in tracking conditions of structural uptake rather than in establishing temporal priority.
