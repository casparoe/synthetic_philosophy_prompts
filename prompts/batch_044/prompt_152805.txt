I'm attempting to clarify my own stance on when bounded rationality legitimately excuses a suboptimal choice, by testing my intuitions across concrete cases. If I am purchasing an electric kettle and stop searching once I find one that boils water reliably and costs under £30, that strikes me as proper satisficing. If I am appointing a surgeon and select the first candidate whose credentials list the required qualification, that strikes me as negligent — even though I am applying the same 'first acceptable option' rule. If I select a retirement portfolio using a simple age-based heuristic rather than modelling every asset class, that feels prudent; yet applying that same heuristic to choose a chemotherapy regimen feels reckless. Conversely, if I spend six months comparing £20 paperback editions, that feels like a failure to satisfice; but spending six months selecting a family home feels entirely warranted. There is also a case I keep revising: I am overseeing software used by millions, and I halt refinement of the error-correction protocol at 99.99% accuracy because reaching 99.999% would require years of additional labour and the residual failures are mostly harmless edge cases. Does one principle govern all of these — perhaps relating the cost of further search to the stakes of error, or the feasibility of setting a reliable aspiration level — or must I invoke separate principles for consumer goods, professional appointment, personal finance, medical treatment, and engineering safety? Please articulate the principle (or competing principles) precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, state what it predicts for an unlisted case — say, selecting a school curriculum for a district — and be explicit about which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated without abandoning simplicity.
