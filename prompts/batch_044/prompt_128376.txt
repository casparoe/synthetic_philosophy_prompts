Construct an argument that cases of pain asymbolia—where subjects retain intact sensory discrimination of noxious stimuli yet report no characteristic negative affect or avoidance motivation—demonstrate that the unpleasantness of pain is not intrinsic to its sensory quality but rather a functionally separable evaluative module. Structure your argument around a tripartite distinction usually collapsed in ordinary experience: the sensory-discriminative layer (localization, intensity, modality), the affective-evaluative layer (the registration that something is wrong or bad), and the motivational-behavioral layer (the drive to withdraw or protect). Use this separation to challenge both the essentialist claim that pain is conceptually defined by its badness and the accidentalist claim that badness is merely an external accompaniment. Specifically, address whether asymbolia should be interpreted as a localized deficit within one module or as a dissociation between fully intact modules, and whether the possibility of experiencing the complete sensory profile of pain without any negative valence undermines the idea that pain's normative force is inseparable from its phenomenology.
