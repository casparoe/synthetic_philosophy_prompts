I am working through the Athenian Stranger’s discussion of symposia and wine in the opening books of Plato’s Laws. In the passage, the Stranger proposes that drinking gatherings can function as a kind of examination or exercise for the soul, analogous to gymnastic training for the body. He observes that under the influence of wine people tend to drop their social masks: they become more candid, more prone to anger, desire, and fear, and less able to maintain the orderly conduct expected in sober company. From this he draws a legislative conclusion: rather than prohibiting symposia altogether, a wise lawgiver should regulate them, using the exposure of these hidden passions as an occasion for correction and for habituating citizens to maintain self-control even when their usual inhibitions are weakened. He adds that such gatherings are dangerous unless supervised, because unsupervised drinking merely strengthens disorder rather than testing it.

I want to understand whether the Stranger’s analogy to physical training commits him to a particular model of how intoxication relates to character. One reading is revelatory: wine removes artificial social restraints, allowing the legislator to observe the “true” configuration of a person’s appetitive and spirited parts, which he can then address in sober education. Another reading is transformative: wine does not unveil a pre-existing soul-state but temporarily reorders the soul’s hierarchy, giving appetite or thumos a dominance they do not have in sobriety. If the second reading is right, then training someone to govern themselves while drunk seems to train a different agent-configuration altogether, and the habituation might not transfer back to the sober state. Does the text give any reason to prefer one model over the other? More specifically, does the Stranger’s broader account of ethos and habituation—where repeated actions shape a stable disposition—allow for cross-state training, or does it implicitly assume that the soul being trained is the same in both conditions? I am not asking for modern psychological research or a bibliography; I want to see whether the internal logic of the passage resolves the tension, and where I might be importing assumptions about agency and identity across mental states that Plato does not share.
