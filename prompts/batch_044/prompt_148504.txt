I recently made a decisive career change after years in a specialized field, and the experience has left me genuinely uncertain whether the trajectory I was on was ever open to deviation or whether early commitments had already narrowed the space of live options. Reading about institutional path dependence has given me language for this feeling, but I remain unsure whether the "lock-in" I experienced was a matter of accumulated sunk costs or of something deeper—a shift in what I could even conceive as a viable alternative. Next week I am moderating a podcast interview with an unnamed historian of science who defends a strong path-dependence thesis about the crystallization of modern disciplinary boundaries and peer-review norms in the late nineteenth century. Her core claim is not merely that these institutions persisted because they were expensive to replace, but that they produced a normative lock-in: once established, they reshaped the conceptual framework through which knowledge-organization was evaluated, rendering alternative forms not just impractical but literally unthinkable. I need a sequence of six to eight interview questions that treat this claim with precision rather than letting her retreat to the platitude that history matters. Specifically, I want: first, an opening question that forces her to articulate the difference between normative lock-in and sunk-cost lock-in, paired with a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that normative mechanisms are primary; second, a counterfactual probe asking exactly what structural or epistemic feature an alternative institution would need to display for her to concede that the path was not locked; third, a question about apparent reversals—for instance, the recent resurgence of interdisciplinary research—with a follow-up anticipating her reply that such movements are merely internal variations within the locked-in framework; and finally, a closing question about what present-day or historical evidence could genuinely weaken her thesis, distinguishing mere contingency (things could have been different) from failed lock-in (the mechanisms she describes never actually constrained the space of alternatives). The tone should be formal and intellectually rigorous, with no biographical or generic questions, and each follow-up should be calibrated to the answer she is most likely to offer.
