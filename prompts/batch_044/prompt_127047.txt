I work as the collections registrar for a mid-size museum that handles mostly post-1960 sculpture, and lately we're drowning in edge cases about whether a damaged or restored piece is still the "same work" for our catalogue and insurance. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run—ordered questions, what counts as a passing answer at each step, where we stop and say "not the original," and where we have to punt to a human value call because metaphysics just runs out. It should be grounded in the statue-and-clay material constitution debate—parts, wholes, continuity, that kind of thing—but stripped down so an intern can use it without reading philosophy. The stakes are real: mislabel something and we face a claim, but be too rigid and we might deaccession a work that should stay.

The twist is that our holdings include a lot of process-based pieces where the artist expected decay—unfired clay that cracks, wood that warps, even ice parts that melt and get remade—so the checklist can't just be "original material present = yes." It needs to distinguish intentional replacement from accidental damage, and it needs to handle cases where the artist is alive and yelling, dead with no instructions, or represented by an estate that disagrees with our conservator.

Please build the checklist. For each step list the evidence we'd need, what answer kills the identity claim, and where we flag "judgment call—get the curator and maybe legal." Then run it on three cases:

1. A 1978 unfired clay figure that cracked in storage; we filled cracks with matching clay and fired the whole thing, so now it's ceramic, not unfired clay. The artist died in 2005 and left no instructions.

2. A 1994 "living wood" assemblage made from a specifc fallen oak. Termites destroyed about 40% of the internal structure, and we replaced those sections with oak from a different tree, shaped to match. The artist's contract says the work must remain "biologically conected to the original tree" but doesn't define that.

3. A 2010 installation with a central clay core the artist called "the heart." The heart was destroyed in shipping. A conservator rebuilt it with synthetic polymer that looks and weighs identical, but it's not clay. The artist is alive and emailed that "without the original heart it's just a copy," but our board wants to keep it displayed because it's the only intact version.

Apply the procedure to all three. I especially want case 3—I'm guessing the checklist will tell me it's not the original, and I need you to be honest about whether I'm probably going to override that for exhibition reasons anyway. Also flag any step where two reasonable people could disagree and the procedure can't force an answer. Keep it practical: we usually have 48 hours, one registrar and one conservator in the room, no lawyer unless we flag it.
