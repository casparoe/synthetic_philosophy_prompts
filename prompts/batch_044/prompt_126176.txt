In my comparative-literature seminar on Renaissance lyric translation, we have been discussing the concept of le ton beau de Marot—that distinctive lightness and social grace said to depend on both lexical choice and formal architecture. I want to compare two conceptual frameworks that my professor only sketched briefly. The first, which I will call structural mimesis, claims that for Marot's rondeaux the rhyme scheme, decasyllabic measure, and refrain structure are not mere containers but generative mechanisms of the ton beau; therefore a translation must replicate those formal constraints even if the English version requires neologism or syntactic inversion, because altering the architecture destroys the very process that produces elegance. The second framework, tonal reconstruction, holds that the ton beau is a pragmatic effect of syntactic balance, wit, and a specific conversational register, so that preserving it may justify abandoning the rondeau form in favor of a looser English stanza—perhaps blank verse with internal rhyme—if that better reproduces the original's lightness and ironic ease.

Compare these two frameworks specifically through a hypothetical case: imagine translating a Marot-style epigram in which the refrain line turns on an untranslatable pun between mer and amer, embedded in a rondeau whose rhyme scheme binds the refrain to A-rhyme positions. Structural mimesis would presumably preserve the scheme and sacrifice the pun; tonal reconstruction might preserve the pun by altering the refrain position or abandoning the scheme altogether. Which framework better preserves what is philosophically at stake in Marot's style, and where does each fail conceptually? In particular, is the disagreement about translation ultimately a disagreement about whether poetic form is constitutive of meaning or merely instrumental to it? Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am asking for a conceptual analysis that I can apply directly to my own translation exercises.
