I'm writing to ask for help thinking through a concrete institutional dilemma that seems to sit at the intersection of moral accountability and practical recovery. I serve on the oversight committee of a regional cooperative that collapsed when it was revealed that our longtime administrator had diverted emergency reserves into a private security contract he controlled, using forged signatures from three trustees who now claim they were coerced. The administrator has offered complete transparency—an unredacted ledger, identification of every accomplice, and testimony about hidden accounts—on one condition: that he and the three trustees receive formal immunity from both criminal referral and permanent expulsion from the organization. One group of members argues that justice is non-negotiable and that any information purchased with immunity is morally tainted; they want full legal and organizational sanctions regardless of what remains hidden. Another group contends that without the disclosure we cannot recover funds, identify remaining complicit parties, or repair the structural failures that enabled the fraud, and that our duty to future members may require accepting the exchange. A smaller faction proposes a middle course: accept the disclosure, but impose non-expulsive sanctions—permanent exclusion from leadership, mandatory restitution, and public acknowledgment—while explicitly reserving the right to pursue criminal referral if the disclosed information proves false or incomplete. I find myself drawn to this compromise, yet I worry it may be incoherent: either punishment is a moral requirement that cannot be partially waived, or it is a strategic tool that can be calibrated for information, and trying to split the difference may satisfy neither principle. Could you lay out the precise moral and philosophical considerations that govern institutional bargains trading knowledge of past harm for limitations on punishment? In particular, is the hybrid approach—full disclosure paired with suspended punitive sanctions and retained prosecutorial discretion—defensible as a distinct position, or does it inevitably collapse into either pure amnesty or pure retribution? Please answer in American English.
