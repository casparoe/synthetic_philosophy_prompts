I am a journalist and essayist specialising in philosophy of mind and the cognitive sciences, and next Thursday I am presenting a seminar paper at an interdisciplinary research forum that draws together analytic philosophers of mind, predictive-coding neuroscientists, and philosophers of science. The thesis I am defending is that the traditional paradox of phenomenal judgment — the concern that epiphenomenalism makes our knowledge of conscious experience causally inexplicable — can be dissolved without granting phenomenal properties causal efficacy, provided we reconceive phenomenal judgments as structural mappings within hierarchical predictive-processing architectures. On my view, second-order tracking of first-order phenomenal states operates not through direct causal transmission but through coherence constraints and cross-level error minimisation, which allows introspective reports to be both reliable and causally inert with respect to the phenomenal properties they concern; this avoids both eliminativism and the mysterious causal powers usually invoked to save self-knowledge.

The audience comprises approximately fifteen specialists: a majority of physicalists who reject dualism but are deeply wary of eliminativism; several neuroscientists who regard consciousness as essentially a predictive model and who will press me on whether coherence constraints are merely disguised neural feedback loops; and a minority of philosophers of science who specialise in causal exclusion and explanatory practice. They value conceptual clarity over metaphysical extravagance, and they will be particularly alert to whether my account smuggles in causal interaction under another name or renders phenomenal judgments epistemically groundless. I need a preparation sheet for the question period — not a general defence of epiphenomenalism, but a targeted list of the objections these particular interlocutors are likely to raise, ordered by probable damage and likelihood, each phrased in their own disciplinary vocabulary. For each objection, I require a concise reply appropriate to a two-minute exchange, together with an explicit note on where the reply is weakest or where I must concede ground. Please also include the objection I am most anxious to avoid — namely, that my structural-mapping mechanism implicitly presupposes the very causal efficacy between phenomenal states and judgment mechanisms that I deny, and that without specifying the exact neurocomputational implementation I have no defence against a reductionist reinterpretation. Present all of this in polished British English.
