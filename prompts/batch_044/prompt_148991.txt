I am drafting an application for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship—part philosophical research, part narrative development—for a novel I am writing about characters who must choose between commitments that resist comparison: artistic integrity versus familial duty, survival versus loyalty, silence versus testimony. In discussions with fellow writers, I have noticed that we tend to dramatize these moments as tragedies of loss, yet I want to understand whether there is something philosophically distinctive about the decider's position after the choice is made, not merely before it. I need a research proposal that frames this precisely, argues that the work is tractable within the fellowship period, and explains why it matters for both practical reason and narrative art.

My half-formed idea is this: the philosophical literature on incommensurable values and hard choices—whether in traditions that treat comparison failure as a rational gap, or in more recent work on transformative decision—tends to treat values as stable properties of options that exist independently of the act of choosing. I want to investigate whether a genuinely hard choice generates what I am calling post-decisional incommensurability: the value of having become the kind of agent who selected one path may itself be neither measurable against the chosen option nor against the rejected one, and may even stand in an incommensurable relation to the decider’s own prior evaluative framework. The proposal should ask whether recognizing this second-order incommensurability dissolves the paradox of rational choice, deepens it, or transforms it into a problem about narrative identity rather than practical reason alone.

Please write the proposal—approximately two pages, suitable for a committee that includes both philosophers and artists—stating the precise research question, explaining why existing accounts of hard choice leave this particular gap open, outlining the method (philosophical analysis of decision theory paired with narrative case studies drawn from draft scenarios in the novel), describing what a successful result would look like, what would count as failure, and providing an honest assessment of scope for six months. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I will supply references myself, and I want the proposal to stand entirely on its conceptual framing. The intended deliverable is a philosophical essay that can also serve as the conceptual backbone for the novel’s structure.
