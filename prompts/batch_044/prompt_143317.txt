I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when a human life counts as genuinely meaningful rather than hollow, absurd, or self-deceptive, by testing my intuitions on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

First, a marine biologist spends thirty years documenting the mating calls of a whale species she knows is going extinct and whose recordings will sit in an unvisited archive. She finds the work deeply meaningful. That strikes me as genuinely meaningful.

Second, parents raise children with full care, explicitly believing that in a billion years all traces of human life will be erased by cosmic heat death. They hold no belief in an afterlife. Still, they say the parenting itself gives their lives weight. That also strikes me as genuinely meaningful.

Third, an artist spends every morning building intricate sand structures on a beach, fully aware that the tide will destroy them within hours. She has done this for decades, never photographing them, never seeking an audience, and describes the practice as the center of her life. Meaningful.

Fourth, a person structures their entire existence around maximizing a digital "wellness score" calculated from sleep, steps, and screen time. They feel satisfied when the number is high and anxious when it drops, but they have no further goals that the score serves. To me, this is hollow—not meaningful, just addictive optimization.

Fifth, a philosopher lives in voluntary poverty and silence, claiming that by embracing total meaninglessness he achieves a "higher form of meaning." He suffers physically, refuses relationships, and says the suffering proves his authenticity. I feel this is not genuinely meaningful; it looks like theatrical self-denial or bad faith rather than real engagement.

Sixth, a woman spends her life translating an epic poem into a constructed language with no native speakers and no prospect of readers. She knows this. But she also admits, when pressed, that she continues mainly because she fears the emptiness of stopping; the work itself brings her no joy, only the avoidance of a void. Despite the surface similarity to the biologist and the artist, this strikes me as not meaningful—more like compulsion disguised as purpose.

These six feel mostly consistent, but I keep going back and forth on a seventh case: a man pushes a heavy boulder up a hill each day, knowing it will roll back down, and he does this not for health, not for spectacle, but because he has decided that the struggle itself, with no further point, is his answer to the absence of cosmic purpose. By my lights, this should be meaningful—an absurdist commitment. Yet it looks structurally identical to the philosopher in case five: both embrace pointless suffering deliberately. So either my verdict on case five is wrong and the philosopher is meaningful after all, or my verdict on the boulder-pusher is wrong and he is just as hollow.

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures my pattern—delivering the right verdicts on cases one through four and six, resolving the tension between five and seven, and explaining what distinguishes meaningful atelic engagement from hollow compulsion? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and apply it to an unlisted case: a retired engineer spends his days repairing broken toys and leaving them anonymously in public parks. He expects no thanks, knows they may be broken again, and says he does it because "things should work, even if no one notices." Meaningful or not, on your account? If the simplest principle forces me to overturn one of my verdicts, which should it be, and why? Also, state honestly which of my original verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate.
