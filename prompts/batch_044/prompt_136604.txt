I have no academic training in history, archaeology, or biblical studies, but over the past year I have been reading independently about the line between legend and recoverable history, especially in three cases that seem to be discussed together constantly: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus from Egypt. I have drafted notes for a short essay that tries to move past the usual question of "did it happen?" and instead asks what kind of mental framework makes a story feel like history rather than myth. My tentative claim is that the shift occurs not when we find physical proof, but when a community stops treating inconsistencies in the narrative as harmless variations and starts treating them as errors that must be corrected or explained away. I call this the "correction versus variation" distinction.

In the Arthurian material, contradictions between Geoffrey of Monmouth and later romances—different birth stories, different locations of Camelot, different fates for the king—have never seemed to damage the tradition, because readers and writers have treated them as creative expansions rather than as mistakes to be resolved. For the Exodus, by contrast, the absence of widespread archaeological evidence for a large-scale migration through the Sinai in the relevant period is typically experienced not as an invitation to embellish the story, but as a crisis that demands resolution: either the chronology must be adjusted, the numbers reinterpreted, or the archaeology challenged. The Trojan War seems to sit between these poles. Schliemann's excavations at Hisarlik provided a physical site that anchors the tradition, yet the mismatch between the archaeological layers and Homer's specific details is generally absorbed as poetic license rather than as a factual error—though historians certainly disagree about whether a particular Bronze Age conflict lies behind the epic at all.

I would welcome comments on these notes, especially on three points. First, does the comparison actually hold, given that Arthur lacks any archaeological anchor comparable to Hisarlik and that Exodus lacks the kind of literary multiplicity that Arthur enjoys? Second, is my "correction versus variation" idea genuinely useful, or does it merely rename distinctions that specialists already discuss in other terms? Third, I am aware that I am working outside the literature, so I may be treating genuinely contested questions—particularly about the historicity of the Exodus, the interpretation of the Hisarlik layers, and whether Arthur has any historical kernel—as more settled than they are. Please separate what is broadly accepted from what remains actively disputed among people who study these questions professionally, and avoid presenting any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. I am also interested in whether there are obvious structural weaknesses in this framing—perhaps in how I have characterized the communities involved—that I have missed precisely because I am not embedded in the academic debate.
