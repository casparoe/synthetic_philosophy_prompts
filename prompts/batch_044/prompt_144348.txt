I was arguing with my roommate about federalism after skimming some EU climate rules, and I think I built a paradox but cant spot the leak. It feels like a recursion bug in code where a function calls itself with no base case. (1) Subsidiarity says authority should sit at the lowest level thats actually competent to deal with a problem. (2) But "competent" is itself a judgment call - some local council might claim they can run carbon pricing, while a federal agency says they cant. (3) So by (1) itself, the decision about who is competent should also be made at the lowest competent level - call it recursive subsidiarity. (4) At the individual level, every voter can assess their own competence on climate economics (they have opinions, they read stuff). (5) So by (3) and (4), each person gets to decide whether they personally are competent enough to opt into or out of collective carbon policy. (6) If enough people opt out, collective climate action dissolves into a self-selected patchwork, which is exactly the coordination failure subsidiarity was meant to prevent. (7) So either subsidiarity applies recursively and destroys collective action, or it stops at some hidden top-down rule for assigning competence - but that second option seems to smuggle in a non-subsidiary authority that violates the spirit of (1). I really want to keep (1) and probably (4) since individual epistemic autonomy feels right, but then I'm stuck with (6). Which link in the chain is bogus? And if I reject the recursion step, what does that cost me - do I have to admit theres always some higher-level arbiter of competence that subsidiarity cant justify?
