Restate the thesis that empirical evidence underdetermines scientific theory — including both the Duhem-Quine claim about auxiliary hypotheses and the stronger claim that empirically equivalent but ontologically distinct rivals are always constructible — entirely within the vocabulary of statistical model selection and causal inference. Use only concepts familiar to that field: observational equivalence, structural identifiability, do-calculus, information criteria (AIC/BIC), cross-validation, and overfitting. Assume I work in causal inference and understand these tools fluently, but have not studied philosophy of science. For each element of the philosophical position — the existence of rival theories fitting the same data, the role of auxiliary assumptions in saving a theory, and the alleged threat to scientific realism — say what translates without distortion, what must be stretched or reinterpreted to fit this framework, and what has no counterpart at all. Then report the residue: does the philosophical debate contain a substantive problem that this vocabulary cannot capture, such as a normative or metaphysical claim about why we ought to prefer one equivalent theory over another? Or does the residue mainly show that philosophers are describing, in pre-formal language, technical issues that causal inference already treats as solved by identifiability conditions and model-selection criteria? Be explicit about whether identifiability and cross-validation genuinely resolve the underdetermination problem or merely rename it, and note any feature of the philosophical debate that disappears entirely in translation.
