(2) If a glyph carries that frame, then it's an 'a', no matter how stylized it gets.

(3) Now imagine a "ghost-glyph": a symbol made entirely of negative space — say, a black card with a circular hole and a thin vertical slit extending downward from it. There's no ink forming the bowl or stem; there's only absense arranged in that shape.

(4) The ghost-glyph still carries the bowl-and-stem frame, just instatiated in absence rather than presence.

(5) So by (2), the ghost-glyph is an 'a'.

(6) But here's the thing: if absence can instantiate the frame, then any blank region can be interpreted as carrying it if we imagine the right surrounding context. A blank sheet of paper with a coffee-ring stain near the margin could be read as "the absense of a bowl plus the absense of a stem."

(7) Therefore, if we accept (2) and (4), then practically any empty surface counts as an 'a' under the right interpretive conditions.

(8) That's obviously nuts — a blank page isn't the letter 'a' — so one of the premises has to go.

(9) I really don't want to give up (1), because it seems right that categorization is about relational roles, not surface geometry. And (3) feels like a legitimate test case. So i'm torn between rejecting (4) — saying absence can't carry the frame — or rejecting (6)-(7) as a slippery slope that doesn't actually follow. Which step is the weak link, and what's the cost of cutting it? Does giving up (4) force us to say that negative-space typography just isn't writing at all?
