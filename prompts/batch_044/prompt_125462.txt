As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have noticed a largely unexamined norm in the funding and peer-review practices of experimental physics and cosmology: applicants and authors are expected to describe detectors and observational protocols as instruments that reveal, detect, or measure entities and properties existing independently of the act of measurement, and reviewers regularly penalize formulations that present the same apparatus as generating, stabilizing, or constituting the phenomena it registers. This linguistic convention survives even in quantum-mechanical domains where measurement-dependence is formally acknowledged and where interpretive pluralism allows that observed values may be relational rather than pre-given. What philosophical commitments about existence, perception, and the relation between mind and matter does this institutional framing presuppose? Does it tacitly commit the scientific community to a direct realism of the kind Berkeley's master argument was intended to undermine—namely, that sensible qualities can subsist unperceived in material substrates? Or can the practice be reconstructed as presupposing only a methodological realism fully compatible with an idealist ontology of those substrates? Furthermore, if Berkeley's distinction between ideas of sense and ideas of imagination is taken seriously, does the peer-review norm implicitly depend on a criterion for separating genuine perception from imaginative construction that Berkeley himself would reject? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics and epistemology at work here, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, assess whether it is philosophically consistent with the interpretive openness quantum mechanics permits, and specify what concrete revisions to proposal language and review criteria would follow if the institution adopted an explicitly Berkeleyan framework—without collapsing into solipsism or undermining intersubjective verification.
