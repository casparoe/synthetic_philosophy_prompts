Exam question:
Pascal argues that true justice is not discernible by human reason, that societies therefore establish justice through custom and law, and that such established justice must be backed by force if it is not to be powerless. He also warns that force without justice is tyrannical. Consider a case in which a state implements a policy through a completely transparent and procedurally fair democratic process, but a significant minority considers the outcome deeply unjust. In light of Pascal’s framework, does the transparency and procedural fairness of the enforcement mechanism strengthen the legitimacy of the policy, or does it leave the relation between justice and force essentially unchanged? Discuss, paying careful attention to whether Pascal allows procedural visibility to serve as a proxy for true justice.

Student’s answer:
Pascal maintains that justice is not founded on universal reason, since human reason is too weak and variable to settle disputes about right. Instead, justice is conventional: it is whatever custom and law have established in a particular society. Because people will obey justice only if they believe it is just, and because they cannot perceive true justice with certainty, societies must back their laws with force. As Pascal sees it, justice without force is impotent, whereas force without justice is tyrannical. Applying this to the scenario, I argue that the transparency of the democratic procedure is decisive. When citizens can see that the policy was enacted through a fair and visible process, they can recognise that the coercion involved is not arbitrary tyranny but the legitimate enforcement of established justice. The minority’s claim that the outcome is unjust is therefore mistaken: they are appealing to an impossible standard of true justice that Pascal has shown to be inaccessible. For Pascal, justice simply is the established order, and where that order is visibly and fairly enforced, the tension between justice and force is resolved. Consequently, procedural transparency transforms mere coercion into legitimate authority on Pascal’s account.
