For a graduate seminar in moral philosophy I am drafting a short position paper and want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is the package I have settled on.

(1) I accept the integrity objection to utilitarianism associated with Bernard Williams: a moral framework that can require an agent to abandon her ground projects for the sake of aggregate welfare treats those projects as fungible and demands a kind of alienation from her practical identity.

(2) I take the well-known one-thought-too-many case not merely as an internal critique of utilitarian calculation, but as revealing a broader constraint on moral reasoning: in intimate or personal domains, the appropriateness of a reason depends on whether entertaining it preserves, rather than disrupts, the relational structure that makes the reason relevant in the first place.

(3) I hold that partiality toward one's ground projects and close relationships is not merely permitted but structurally necessary for practical identity. In these domains, strictly impartial reasons do not simply override personal commitments; they fail to address the agent in the correct register altogether.

(4) At the same time, I maintain that agents who occupy institutional roles—such as a public-health official distributing scarce medical resources—are required to adopt strictly impartial, aggregative reasoning. Within these roles, ground projects have no special moral weight, and symmetric treatment of all affected parties is a condition of legitimate authority.

(5) I claim that there is a single, unified moral framework underlying both domains. The transition between them is not a switch to a different theory, but is mediated by what I call integrity-neutral demands: demands generated by institutional roles that suspend, rather than violate, an agent's ground projects, thereby permitting impartial reasoning without producing the alienation described in (1).

Does this hang together? I want a precise audit: which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and what the least costly revision would be if consistency requires one. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging. In particular, does commitment (5) do hidden work that undermines (1) and (2), or does it genuinely resolve the conflict with (4)? And is there an implicit contradiction in holding that impartial reasons fail to address the agent in the right register in personal life, yet are fully authoritative in institutional life, given that the same agent moves between both contexts?
