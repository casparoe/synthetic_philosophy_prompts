I'm preparing a conceptual essay on archives and power—specifically on who gets recorded and what the silences in the sources actually signify. In seminars we've mostly discussed the standard critique: archives reflect power because institutions preserve the traces of dominant groups while allowing subaltern lives to disappear. I think this framing is incomplete, and I'd like you to argue for a more specific position. Defend the claim that archival silences should be reconceptualized not as passive omissions—mere gaps where evidence should be—but as actively produced negative spaces that perform structural work within the archive itself. To do this, draw a sharp conceptual distinction between omissive silence, in which absence results from neglect, resource scarcity, or the simple failure to document, and commissioned silence, in which institutional protocols, classification schemes, or evidentiary standards actively render certain subjects, events, or forms of knowledge unarchivable—not necessarily by destroying documents, but by defining in advance what counts as a recordable occurrence. Then make the normative case: recognizing commissioned silence should change the ethical orientation of archival practice. Rather than treating silence as a deficiency to be corrected through more inclusive collection (the representational-completeness model), the archivist should treat these negative spaces as themselves historical artifacts—structured absences that must be mapped, preserved in their form, and made legible to users without being filled or spoken over. Please develop this as a philosophical argument rather than an empirical survey; I am not looking for specific case studies, bibliographies, or historical citations. Finally, address a serious objection: that treating silence as an intentionally structured artifact risks aestheticizing or rationalizing oppression by attributing design to what is often the chaotic result of structural violence and bureaucratic indifference.
