My colleague offers this argument to show that fears about AI eroding human understanding prove too much. (1) Genuine understanding of a claim requires that one be able to reconstruct its justification from one's own cognitive resources—call this procedural epistemic autonomy. (2) When one relies on an AI, the reasoning path remains encoded in the model's weights; the user receives only the conclusion, without internalizing a recoverable justification chain. (3) Therefore sustained AI dependence in a domain replaces one's reconstructive capacity with an opaque external source, degrading understanding. (4) But all cognitive scaffolding—books, calculators, trusted experts, even language as an external storage system—similarly houses reasoning outside the user, who accesses only outputs. (5) If (1) is correct, then scaffold-dependent belief is never understanding, which is absurd: we do not think that learning geometry from a teacher leaves one in ignorance. (6) So (1) must be rejected, and AI poses no special threat.

I want to hold onto (3)—AI does feel different from reading a textbook—but I cannot locate the failure. Is the error in (2), because chain-of-thought outputs actually make AI reasoning recoverable? Or in (4), because there is a principled distinction between recoverable scaffolds (books, teachers) and non-recoverable ones (opaque weights)? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost is abandoning the autonomy condition in (1), which might force us to say that understanding is always partly distributed? Identify the weakest step, explain why it looks plausible, and say what giving it up commits us to.
