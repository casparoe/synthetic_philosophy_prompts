I'm trying to work out my own position on legal moralism—when the criminal law should enforce a moral norm beyond preventing direct harm—through concrete verdicts rather than through the standard literature. Here are my gut judgments, with one pair that keeps pulling in opposite directions.

A law criminalizing private, consensual sexual relations between adult siblings who deceive no one and harm no third party: should not exist. The state should stay out of private intimacy, even when widespread moral revulsion is justified.

A law banning a public performance art piece in which fully consenting adults desecrate symbols sacred to a minority group in a public square, with no physical violence or coercion: I think this should be prohibited, though I recognize that no individual is physically harmed and everyone involved consents.

A private membership club, operating on private property, that requires initiates to perform rituals of symbolic domination over representations of a protected class as a condition of admission: I think the state should shut this down. The harm is not to any specific victim present, but to something like the moral atmosphere that makes equal citizenship possible.

A private school that teaches, as religious doctrine, that certain groups are morally corrupt and destined for divine punishment, but that does not advocate violence, discrimination in hiring, or civic exclusion: should be permitted. Offensive belief and speech, even systematically taught, should be protected.

A regulated pharmaceutical product, sold with full disclosure to consenting adults, designed specifically to suppress the capacity for moral remorse: should be banned. This does not feel like a standard harm-prevention case; the objection is to the corruption of a capacity that makes moral agency possible.

A state program that offers financial rewards to citizens for reporting their neighbors' private, non-violent moral transgressions—say, infidelity or religious apostasy—to a government registry: should be banned. Here the reason feels closer to preventing civic tyranny than to enforcing a specific moral code.

The tension that breaks my framework is between the private incest case and the private club case: both occur in private, both involve conduct that deeply offends dominant moral norms, both involve consenting adults, yet I want the law absent in one and present in the other. Meanwhile, the desecration case and the remorse-suppressing drug both seem to call for prohibition, but apparently for different reasons—one about public symbolic order, one about internal human capacities.

Is there a coherent principle, or a small family of principles, that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on an unlisted case—specifically, a private online forum where members exchange detailed strategies for emotionally manipulating intimate partners without any physical coercion—and be explicit about which of my judgments it must reject to stay simple.

If my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the distinction I am trying to draw between "symbolic corruption of civic space" and "direct harm to individuals" collapses, or if "moral atmosphere" is too vague to do legal work—please say so plainly rather than forcing a reconciliation. How confident are you in whatever principle you offer, and which link in my pattern do you think is actually the weakest?
