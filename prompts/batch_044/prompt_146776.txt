I am writing a short essay for my philosophy club and would like to understand a conceptual issue I have encountered with the view sometimes called tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and suffering has value, whilst positive pleasures or satisfied desires have none. I can see why this differs from ordinary hedonism, but I am stuck on what seems to be a structural ambiguity. If the sole value lies in the absence of suffering, then a universe with no sentient beings—where there is no craving present to be removed—appears to be maximally good, since nothing suffers. But if tranquilism also gives us reason to help existing people achieve a state of non-craving, it seems to treat the presence of tranquil beings as itself valuable, which looks like a hidden positive commitment. Could you explain, without relying on formal proofs, whether tranquilism can consistently maintain that existence adds no value, or whether it must implicitly distinguish between "absence of craving within a subject" and "absence of subjects altogether"? I am also unsure whether framing the view around craving rather than suffering introduces a distinct conceptual problem: a state with no craving might be phenomenally empty rather than positively good. Please clarify how this position relates conceptually to negative utilitarianism, and whether the focus on craving forces tranquilism into a different account of why action is justified at all.
