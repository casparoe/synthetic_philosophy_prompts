I cover philosophy of science and metaphysics for a long-form magazine, and I am preparing a feature on whether an object can survive the complete replacement of its parts. I need you to serve as an expert metaphysician specializing in material constitution and the ontology of artifacts. Before answering, think carefully and systematically: begin with a brief brainstorming session that lists the classic puzzles—the statue and the clay, the Ship of Theseus, and the modal divergence between an artifact and its constituent matter—then extend that list with two niche cases I want included. The first is a heritage monument whose original limestone has been almost entirely replaced, over centuries, by synthetic aggregate that is chemically identical but causally and historically discontinuous with the original stone. The second is a bio-engineered installation that maintains a fixed sculptural form while metabolically turning over one hundred percent of its cellular substrate every few months.

Having done that, write the full desiderata—the specification of adequacy—for a theory of material constitution. For each requirement, state the phenomenon, case, or distinction it must handle, the consideration that makes it a requirement, and whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. The list must cover: co-location without numerical identity; different persistence conditions for object and matter; preservation of form and function across material turnover; consistency with chemistry, conservation science, and biology; and the distinctions between being made of X, being constituted by X, and being merely an aggregate of X parts. Flag any pair or subset of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—this is the part I want emphasized. Then, very briefly, indicate how the constitution view, four-dimensionalist perdurantism, mereological essentialism, and artifact-eliminativism stand against these criteria, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. Write in formal, polished prose suitable for a feature article's research brief. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning must stand on its own.
