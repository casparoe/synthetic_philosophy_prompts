I've been trying to extend the Knobe effect into a broader thesis for my seminar paper, but now I've built this chain that seems to prove mental-state attribution is just moral psychology in disguise. My adviser says there's a hidden slide or scope mistake somewhere, but I can't spot it.

(1) In standard Knobe vignettes people call the harmful side-effect intentional and the helpful one not, even when beliefs, desires, and causal control are identicle across cases. (2) The best explanation is that INTENTIONAL isn't applied on mental-state grounds alone; normative evaluation partly constitutes the concept—call this the normative-constitution thesis. (3) If one mental-state concept works this way, there's no in-principle barrier to others doing the same, unless intentionality is uniquely action-linked. Call this normative contagion. (4) Now take belief: it's usually fixed by evidence and dispositions, but belief also carries normative conditions—norms of rationality about what one ought to believe. (5) So belief, like intentionality, involves normative evaluation in its application conditions. (6) By normative contagion, whether we attribute belief p should depend partly on whether p's consequences violate moral norms, not just on evidence. (7) That means in two structurally identical cases—Dr. Reed holds a false belief that accidentally saves a patient versus one that accidentally kills—we'd have to attribute belief in one but deny it in the other, purely on moral grounds. (8) But belief attribution is supposed to track evidence, not blame; if (7) holds, epistemology collapses into ethics. (9) So either normative-constitution is false, or normative contagion is blocked.

I really want to keep (2) because it explains the data well, and (3) feels like a natural generalization. Is the mistake an equivocation between moral norms in (2) and rational norms in (4)? Or is it a modal slide from 'partly constituted' to 'fully determined'? What's the least costly premise to reject, and what does giving it up cost me?
