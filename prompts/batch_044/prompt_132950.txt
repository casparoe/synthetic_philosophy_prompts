I am designing a ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students with logic training but no prior epistemology, on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory. I want to avoid the standard Mercury-perihelion framing, since it makes the difficulty look like a historical quirk rather than a structural feature of conditionalization. Plan the teaching arc: the dependency order of the formal and conceptual pieces—specifically, why the intuitive idea of predictive success must precede any statement of Bayes's theorem, and which version of the theorem (ratio versus log-odds) should be introduced at which stage—the single carrying scenario I should build around (it must involve a model formulated without reference to an already-known dataset, then successfully accommodating that dataset, but it must not be a case where the model was explicitly parameterized to fit the data), the misconceptions students will generate in sequence (likely: that old evidence is just weak evidence; that the fix is to lower the prior for theories that post-date the data; and that the problem only arises in scientific contexts rather than in individual belief revision), and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Include a final check—one question or task—that reveals whether they understand the structural tension between prior conditioning and confirmatory value, not merely whether they recall the example. Also state explicitly which formal apparatus should be left out and acknowledged as omitted.
