ok so i've come around to a kind of defense of moore's proof — not the usual he's-just-being-stubborn reading but something like this: the proof works because there's a category of beliefs that are practically entrenched in a way that makes them immune to skeptical undermining, not because they're true by definition but because abandoning them dissolves the conditions under which doubt makes sense. call it the practical-entrenchment thesis. moore holding up his hands isn't begging the question; it's demonstrating that the skeptic demands a kind of justification that, if applied consistently, would disable the very agency required to ask for it.

what would count against this? i want actual defeaters, not just vibes. sort them: fatal vs awkward. include cases where practical entrenchment genuinely failed — maybe paradigm shifts where people did abandon obvious beliefs — and whether those are analogus or not. also, is this view already insulated? like if every failure gets reclassified as not real practical entrenchment or merely doxastic not practical, has it become unfalsifiable, and is that actually a problem for a thesis of this kind?

i'm especially interested in a distinction some philosophers draw between practical commitment (acting as if p) and doxastic commitment (actually believing p). if moore's argument only secures the first, does that gut it? or does the practical-entrenchment view predict that the distinction collapses here?

rank the evidence and arguments that should move me, include what defenders of this view would themselves concede, and be honest about whether it's well-insulated.
