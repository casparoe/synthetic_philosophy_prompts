I am a novelist and co-founder of a five-member narrative collective that publishes an annual serial volume. Each member authors one chapter within a shared world, but the group collectively decides on framing devices, thematic boundaries, and interpretive rules that bind all chapters. Over two years we have encountered recurring deadlocks: members claim sovereignty over personal spheres within their own chapters—for instance, whether a character may be depicted in addiction, whether profanity may appear, or whether a chapter may be signed pseudonymously—while others object that these choices alter the hermeneutic context for adjacent work, making no decision truly private. At the same time, we hold that if all five members genuinely prefer one framing to another, we ought not to select the worse one.

I wish to translate the tension usually called Sen's liberal paradox—where minimal individual rights over paired outcomes conflict with unanimous Pareto preference—into a reusable decision procedure that my collective can execute in forty-five minutes, without an outside mediator, with all five members required to sign off. The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: ordered questions, criteria for a sufficient answer, explicit stopping points that yield a negative or deferred result, and clear markers where judgment replaces logic. It must account for indirect interpretive effects—where one writer's personal choice rewrites the meaning of another's text—and must not suppress the paradox by redefining "personal" until the conflict disappears.

Constraints and context: we meet monthly; stakes are publication and collective reputation, not life and death; none of us are trained in social-choice theory; the procedure must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. Please include worked applications to three pending proposals.

First, Writer A asserts sovereignty over depicting addiction in her chapter; Writers B and C, whose chapters treat recovery and family, claim this creates an interpretive contamination that makes their work read as endorsement, and all five agree we would collectively prefer the volume without the depiction. I personally believe sovereignty should yield here, but I want the procedure to show honestly whether it must, even if the answer is one I will dislike.

Second, Writer D proposes a shared prologue written collectively that every member concedes improves each chapter, but Writer E refuses on the grounds that it violates her personal sphere over her chapter's entry point, and all others prefer the prologue included.

Third, a proposal allowing each writer unilateral choice over whether their chapter is published under a legal name or a pseudonym within the collective volume. This appears purely personal, yet the collective fears it will shift reader expectations for the named chapters in ways that damage their authors' intentions.

For each proposal, run the procedure step by step. Then specify exactly where the procedure breaks down or must defer to human judgment, what it cannot resolve, and whether any stable decision rule can honor both individual sovereignty and collective preference, or whether we must permanently relinquish one of the two principles. Do not provide a reading list or citation; build the procedure solely from the idea as I have outlined it.
