Here is where I have landed on the Meno problem and on what distinguishes knowledge from merely getting things right. I want a consistency check before I present this in a reading group.

(1) The superiority of knowledge over true belief is intrinsic, not instrumental. If the extra value were only practical—say, that knowledge guides action more reliably—then Meno’s question dissolves into a query about useful belief-forming mechanisms, and a lucky true belief produced by such a mechanism would be just as good.

(2) What supplies the intrinsic value is what I call epistemic anchoring: the belief is held within an accessible network of supporting considerations, so that losing it requires rational revision rather than accident. Gettier cases occur precisely when justification is present but not anchored to the truth in this way.

(3) Because anchoring is not a species of justification but a distinct epistemic relation, I think the justification condition in the classic tripartite analysis must be replaced: knowledge is anchored true belief, not justified true belief.

(4) At the same time, I want to preserve the tripartite form—belief, truth, and an appropriate epistemic condition—merely refining the third member rather than abandoning the structure altogether.

(5) Pure testimonial knowledge counts: I can know that p by accepting it from a reliable expert even without grasping the supporting reasons, because the anchoring resides in the expert’s system and is transmitted through the testimonial chain, even though I lack personal cognitive ownership of it.

(6) For the recipient’s state to carry the intrinsic value identified in (1), however, there must be at least minimal cognitive access to the source’s reliability and the domain; without this, the belief is only derivatively anchored, and its epistemic resilience depends on factors outside the recipient’s rational control.

(7) In high-stakes practical reasoning—say, deciding whether to undergo an unfamiliar medical procedure—I should prefer my own anchored knowledge to testimony in which I lack any such access, because only the former gives me adaptive flexibility; yet I also maintain that such testimony-based belief is genuinely more valuable than a lucky true belief, and that cognitive ownership is a separate epistemic good that is valuable but not necessary for knowledge.

Audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severely? Which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection? For each real tension, specify the cheapest revision and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal. Then identify which single revision—dropping or weakening one commitment—would clean up the most tension across the board. Be specific about whether I am helping myself to something in (5) or (6) that (1) and (3) should rule out, or whether the core problem lies elsewhere. Do not list every possible pairing; discriminate between serious conflicts and harmless coexistence.
