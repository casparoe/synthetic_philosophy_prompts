Read the 2016 film *Arrival* (Denis Villeneuve) through Wittgenstein's concepts of language games and family resemblance. Rather than treating Louise Banks's acquisition of Heptapod B as a straightforward case of linguistic relativism, examine where the heptapod system extends human linguistic practice through family resemblance—shared responses, embedded forms of life, use rather than representation—and where it introduces what we might call a "language rupture": a structure so alien (simultaneous, non-causal, circular) that accommodating it dissolves the conditions that make human meaning possible. Focus on three moments: Louise's initial encounter with the heptapods and the ambiguity of the "weapon/tool" translation; her breakthrough in recognizing the circular script as non-sequential; and her decision to conceive and raise Hannah despite knowing the future suffering. Does Wittgenstein's framework allow a speaker to inhabit two incommensurable language games simultaneously, or does the film rely on a representationalist picture of language—meaning as mapping reality rather than use within practice—to render Louise's choice intelligible? Where does the philosophical lens illuminate the narrative's emotional logic, where does the film resist being reduced to an illustration of linguistic determinism, and what does *Arrival* add to philosophical debates about radical otherness that conceptual argument alone cannot provide?
