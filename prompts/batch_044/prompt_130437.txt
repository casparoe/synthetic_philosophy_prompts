I've been going back and forth with a friend about sunk costs and I think I accidentally built an argument that proves honoring them is either always required or never rational, with no middle ground, and I can't see the exit. We started talking about whether quitting a grad program after three years is like quitting a bad movie — but then I realized the reasoning applies to way more than that, and now I'm stuck.

Here's the chain: (1) If rationality is forward-looking, then past unrecoverable investments shouldn't influence choice. (2) But some investments aren't just resources; they become part of your practical identity — the years you spent learning a craft or building a relationship don't just sit in the past, they shape who you are now. (3) So abandoning them isn't just inefficient, it's a kind of self-betrayal, which gives you a present reason to continue. (4) That means honoring sunk costs is rational whenever identity is at stake. (5) But identity can be at stake in harmful commitments too — say, staying in a destructive partnership because of the shared history. (6) So either honoring sunk costs is rational even there, or we have to deny that identity gives present reasons. (7) Denying that seems crazy — it would make persistance and loyalty irrational across the board. (8) But accepting (6) for harmful cases is also crazy. So where's the slip? Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? If I drop (2), do I lose the idea that loyalty and persistance have any rational weight at all?
