I'm part of a small interdisciplinary reading circle that recently turned its attention to epistemic rationality in expert communities, and we've become stuck on a phrase one member—a clinical trial statistician—keeps invoking whenever our methodological debates reach an impasse: "when two peers disagree, rationality demands they meet in the middle." She deploys it specifically to argue that deadlocks over frequentist versus Bayesian interpretation of trial data should be resolved by averaging our credences or splitting procedural differences, treating the slogan as a kind of normative closure rule for group deliberation. I feel a persistent resistance to this move, but I cannot tell whether my objection targets an obvious falsehood, a hidden ambiguity, or an illicit slide from epistemology to social pragmatics. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner might express—separating, for instance, a claim about probabilistic averaging of rational credences, a claim about equal normative weight of epistemic peers, a pragmatic decision rule for collective action under uncertainty, and any metaphysical claim about belief itself being essentially compromise-oriented? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it survives standard counterexamples involving asymmetric domain expertise or hidden evidence, and crucially whether it actually licenses her conclusion that statistical methodology disputes ought to be settled by meeting halfway. I want to bring back to the group a menu of disambiguations with verdicts, not a single sweeping refutation, so that we can see exactly which version of the slogan she needs and whether it does the work she requires.
