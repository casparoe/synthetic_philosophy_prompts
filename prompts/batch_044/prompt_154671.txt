Hey, I've been wondering about the Vatican's medical board for miracles — the one that vets cures for canonization. Since the 80s reform they require a panel with a least one non-believing doctor, and they only approve a cure if it's instanteneous, complete, lasting, and has no current scientific explanation. They never ask the doctors to identify a divine cause; they just certify a negative gap in natural explanation. What philosophical commitments about miracles, natural law, and evidence does that institutional design presuppose? Does it implicitly buy Hume's framing — that a miracle is a transgression of a law of nature that must be weighed against uniform experience — or does it smuggle in a God-of-the-gaps view? And is the practice consistent with itself, given that it treats regular medical outcomes as establishing a law but then lets one outlier override it? I'm trying to see whether the board's rules actually need a specific metaphysics of causation, or if they're philosophically underdetermined and could run on Bayesian priors or an anti-Humean theory of expert testimony just as well. What would have to shift in there procedure if we replaced the hidden view with a straightforwardly probabilistic epistemology?
