I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of action, and I'm trying to work through a conceptual knot in my reading group that standard presentations of Buridan's ass don't seem to resolve cleanly. The classic formulation presents a perfectly rational agent facing two identical bales of hay, unable to choose because there is no reason to prefer one over the other. The familiar responses fall into two camps: one holds that rationality permits an arbitrary tie-breaker, such as flipping a coin, while the other appeals to direct volitional commitment, where the will fixes on one option without prior reason. What I find underexamined is whether these responses actually address the same level of the problem, or whether they talk past each other by operating at different orders of indifference.

Specifically, I want to understand whether there is a meaningful distinction between first-order indifference, in which an agent lacks any reason to prefer option A to option B, and second-order indifference, in which an agent lacks any reason to prefer one tie-breaking procedure over another. If I resolve the first-level tie by flipping a coin, do I not immediately face a parallel paralysis about whether to flip the coin, roll a die, or count blades of grass? And if the answer is that any procedure will do, haven't I simply relocated rather than dissolved the arbitrariness? Conversely, if direct volition can break ties at the first order, why can't it also break ties at the second order, and if it can, what distinguishes such volition from mere caprice rather than rational agency?

Please explain, in conceptually precise but intuitive terms and without formal proof, how these nested levels of indifference interact, whether the randomizing-device solution genuinely resolves the paradox or only displaces it upward, and whether volitional commitment can be coherently restricted so that it saves rational choice without collapsing into arbitrariness. Please write your answer in American English.
