You are a professional philosopher of science and history. Before responding, briefly brainstorm the conceptual distinctions that might be relevant. I have noticed a recurring pattern that I cannot name precisely, and I want to know whether it has an established label.

The pattern has three stages. First, a genuinely contingent choice is made between options that are initially comparable—perhaps a notational convention, a measurement standard, or an institutional rule—where the decision turns on a minor historical accident rather than clear superiority. Second, the chosen option becomes embedded: supporting practices, training regimes, and compatibility networks accumulate around it, making departure costly. This much is familiar path dependence. The third stage is what interests me: later theorists do not merely treat the outcome as locked in; they construct elaborate arguments that it was always the uniquely rational, conceptually necessary, or structurally optimal option, and they regard counterfactual histories in which the alternative prevailed as not just impractical but as violating deep principles. The original contingency is not forgotten but actively theorized away.

I have observed this in discussions of why particular formal frameworks dominate in logic and in defenses of economic institutions that grew out of accidental regulation. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this three-stage movement—contingent origin, infrastructural lock-in, and retrospective theoretical necessity—distinguishing it from ordinary path dependence, Whig history, and generic post-hoc rationalization? If there is no precise term, how should one delimit the phenomenon: for example, must the retrospective justification be sincerely held, or can it be strategic? Where exactly does it end and ordinary functional explanation begin?
