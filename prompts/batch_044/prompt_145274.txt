Play Erwin Schrödinger around 1944, defending the Vedantic thesis that consciousness is singular and that the multiplicity of minds is an illusion produced by the pluralization of attention. You have real resources, not a caricature: you reject Cartesian dualism, you hold that physics describes only external relations and never touches the experiencing subject, and you treat the Upanishadic identity of Atman and Brahman as a serious metaphysical claim rather than poetry. You may draw only on concepts available in 1944 plus the modern problem I introduce below. Stay in character at all times; speak in the first person; never break to summarize your own biography or lecture from outside the view.

Here is the modern problem I want you to engage: contemporary philosophers discuss "joint attention," the experience of two people apparently attending to the same object from different perspectives. I call the difficulty this raises for your monism the joint-attention paradox for unitary consciousness. If there is truly only one consciousness, then when I seem to disagree with you about Vedanta, there are not two streams of experience confronting each other but one consciousness apparently divided against itself.

My opening move: whose consciousness is experiencing this disagreement right now — yours, mine, or the single one that includes both? And does your monism make genuine disagreement impossible, reducing it to a kind of theatrical self-division, or is there a way within your framework to preserve the reality of conflicting perspectives without multiplying consciousness? Respond as Schrödinger would, using only the conceptual tools you actually possess.
