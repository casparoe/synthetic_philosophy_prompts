I've been working on a formal model of conditional belief revision in which indicative conditionals are treated as update rules: an agent applies the Ramsey test within a fixed prior probability space, conditioning on the antecedent and then evaluating the consequent. Reading Lewis's triviality results, it seems he proves that if the probability assigned to an indicative conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, then any antecedent with non-extreme prior probability forces the entire distribution to collapse into triviality. But this appears to entail that any agent who performs standard Bayesian conditioning—restricting to A-worlds and measuring C—already inhabits a collapsed belief state, which is obviously false for ordinary reasoning. So is the resolution that Lewis's proof applies only when the conditional itself is treated as a proposition within the same space being updated, whereas in my framework the update shifts to a distinct subspace or model? Or does the result actually show that indicative conditionals cannot carry uniform probabilistic content across contexts, which would mean the Ramsey test must be indexed to context-specific measures rather than a single global prior? I'm trying to determine whether the conservative fix is to reject the identification of conditional probabilities with the semantics of indicative conditionals, or whether one genuinely needs to move to a multi-measure or modal framework to avoid the collapse.
