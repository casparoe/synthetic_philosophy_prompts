I am not a trained philosopher, but I have been working through ideas about possibility and reality on my own, and I keep returning to a phrase I hear in discussions: every possible world is just as real as ours, and our world is special only in being the one we happen to inhabit. I sense this bundles together very different claims — some that sound like tautologies, others like bold metaphysical commitments, and perhaps some that are simply false. Could you separate out the precise theses this statement might express? For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, or false, and indicate which version people usually invoke when they use the phrase to dismiss worries about fictional characters or purely hypothetical situations. I also want to press a specific worry I have not seen addressed. If every genuinely possible world exists, then there should be a world otherwise identical to ours except that in it modal realism itself is false; in that world, not all worlds exist. But if that world is real, then there is a real world in which the original claim fails, which seems to undermine the view from inside. Is this a genuine problem, or does it rest on a hidden mistake about how worlds relate to one another? Please work through it directly, without sending me to particular books or authors, since I am trying to reason it out from scratch.
