I am trying to avoid committing to any particular account of philosophical progress before I understand what the standard of adequacy should be. Please write the desiderata that any theory in this area must meet: the historical and conceptual phenomena it has to accommodate—such as persistent disagreement on central questions, the retrospective judgment that some problems were dissolved rather than solved, and the occasional impression that a field has moved laterally rather than forward—the distinctions it must not collapse, for instance between cumulative knowledge-gain and conceptual clarification, or between local argument-resolution and global paradigm-change; the constraints from philosophy of science and conceptual genealogy it must respect; and whether there are pairs of requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable. Please include, as a specific test case, whether the theory must make room for what I will call vertical stagnation coupled with horizontal gain—situations in which a philosophical problem remains technically unsolved in its original formulation, yet the field succeeds in demonstrating that the formulation rested on a misframed distinction—without treating such cases as either straightforward progress or mere failure. Once the list is established, briefly assess how three familiar families of view—the Kuhnian discontinuity model, the therapeutic or dissolution-oriented model, and a modest cumulative-progress model—fare against these criteria, noting which non-negotiable desiderata each violates, if any.
