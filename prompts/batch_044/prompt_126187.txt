I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against true moral responsibility. We kept circling the same loop: if you do what you do because of how you are, and you are not responsible for how you originally are, then you can never be the ultimate source of your actions. The only escape, she insisted, would be to be causa sui—to cause yourself entirely—which seems not merely unlikely but conceptually incoherent, like pulling yourself up by your own ontological bootstraps. I want to send her a poem that does not resolve the disagreement but enacts its structure precisely.

Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—in which the six rotating end-words are, in this order: cause, self, before, choice, origin, impossible. Let the rotation mirror the infinite regress of Strawson's argument: each stanza should attempt to ground moral responsibility in one of these terms, only to find that term already dependent on the next. Take the voice of an imagined agent trying to be the unmoved mover of its own character, reaching backward for an origin it never possessed. Make the envoi collapse all six end-words into a single, simultaneous recognition. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the Basic Argument recognizes the logical steps mapped onto the form—acting from nature, the demand for self-origination, the impossibility of being causa sui—yet compelling enough that a reader who knows none of this still feels the circular trap closing. No explanation of the form; let the structure speak.
