Exam question: Daniel Dennett rejects what he calls the Cartesian theater—the idea that there is a single place in the brain where neural processes are unified and presented to an inner observer. Explain (a) why Dennett believes this metaphor survives even in avowedly materialist theories of consciousness, (b) how his multiple drafts model replaces the theater without invoking a central executive or a canonical final version, and (c) how the model addresses the subjective impression of a single, continuous stream of experience. In your answer, be precise about the role of temporal anomalies (for instance, the color-phi phenomenon) and about Dennett’s distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions.

Student response: Dennett’s Cartesian theater is the lingering assumption that somewhere in the brain there is a privileged location—perhaps a neural integrating center—where information becomes conscious, as if projected onto a screen for a homuncular audience. Even materialists often preserve this structure by locating consciousness in a specific workspace that binds disparate inputs into one unified field. Dennett replaces it with the multiple drafts model, according to which the brain continuously produces many parallel streams of content—drafts—that are edited, revised, and made available for reporting. Consciousness is not the arrival of a draft at a central stage, but rather the achievement of “fame in the brain”: when a draft becomes widely accessible for verbal report and behavioral control, it counts as conscious.

With respect to temporal anomalies, Dennett uses the color-phi phenomenon to argue that the brain can construct a coherent narrative after the fact. He distinguishes Orwellian revisions, in which memory is rewritten so that a false history seems correct, from Stalinesque revisions, in which unwanted content is suppressed before it can reach awareness. Both operate within the competing drafts to determine which narrative achieves dominance, showing that there is no need for a real-time observer in a theater. The brain simply selects the most coherent draft through an unconscious executive mechanism.

As for the subjective sense of a single continuous stream, Dennett treats this as a theoretical fiction rather than an observed datum. The brain generates a virtual captain—a user-illusion—that appears to direct the drafts from a single vantage point. Because the drafts are constantly updated and none is ever finalized, there is no canonical version of experience at any given moment. Consequently, Dennett ultimately denies that there is any such thing as a continuous stream of consciousness; there are only discrete, competing representations that create the appearance of flow, and the self-model is itself just one more draft competing for fame.
