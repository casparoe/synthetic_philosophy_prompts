I'm working for this small heritage board in the Pacific Northwest and we keep recieveing applications to designate "sites of exceptional testimony" — old logging camps where survivors claim a dead coworker warned them about an avalanche, a 1920s sanitorium where patients said they were healed overnight, that kind of thing. The board doesn't care about religion per se; we just need to know whether the testimony is credible enough to justify listing the place as culturally significant. I read Hume's essay on miracles back in college and it seemed like he had a kind of formula — weigh the uniform experience of nature against the testimony, and only believe the miracle if the falsehood of the report would be more miraculous than the event itself. I want you to turn that into an actual reusable procedure my three-person review team can run in about thirty minutes per file. It needs steps in order: what evidence to gather first, how to compare the implausibility of the law-breaking against the implausibility of error or deception, the exact point where we stop and say "this doesn't support a listing," and the point where we have to hand it to a values judgment because Hume's math runs out. Please apply it to two cases: a 1912 coal-mine rescue where three survivors say they heard a guiding voice (we'd like to list it, but I worry the procedure kills it), and a 1970s drought commune where dozens report simultaneous visions. Flag where the procedure misfires — like when the claim is technically a statistical outlier, not a law-violation — and be honest about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as rules. Write the answer in American English, and don't just summarize Hume; build the checklist my coworker can actually use without being a philosopher.
