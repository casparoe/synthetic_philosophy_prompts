I have been trying to work out a coherent account of what distinguishes genuinely joint action from merely coordinated individual behavior, and I keep running into concrete cases where my intuitions seem to pull in opposite directions. Here are my verdicts. Two friends who agree to move a piano together, each knowing the other's plan and adjusting their grip in real time, are clearly acting jointly. A crowd of strangers who each independently call emergency services after seeing a car accident are not acting jointly, even though their actions are causally interdependent. But then consider a spontaneous human chain formed by strangers to pull a drowning swimmer ashore: there is no prior agreement, minimal mutual awareness of personal identities, and no shared institutional framework, yet I want to say this is joint action in a thin but real sense, because each participant treats the others as partners in a shared rescue rather than as mere environmental tools. By contrast, a multinational research consortium in which hundreds of scientists contribute data to a shared protocol they all signed, but where most do not understand the theoretical framework and have never communicated with one another, feels like mere coordination, not joint action, despite the explicit institutional agreement and the causal interdependence of their contributions. Another hard pair: two cooks preparing a meal together, but one secretly believes they are only rehearsing for a solo competition while the other believes they are hosting a dinner party for guests; they physically coordinate their movements in the kitchen, yet I judge this not joint action. Meanwhile, a corporate board with rotating membership votes on a strategic merger; the current members lack full access to the strategic reasoning of the executives who drafted the proposal, and some members joined after the deliberation began, yet I am inclined to say the corporation itself acts jointly, even if the individual board members do not fully share a we-intention.

I am tempted to propose that joint action requires three conditions: mutual responsiveness in execution, a shared representation of the goal that is at least minimally accessible to all participants, and that each participant regards the others as co-authors rather than instruments. But this framework seems to exclude the spontaneous rescue chain, which I want to keep, while including the research consortium, which I want to exclude, and it is unclear how it handles the rotating board or the deceived cooks. Can you state the best principle, or small family of competing principles, that accommodates the largest coherent subset of these judgments? For each principle, specify which clause is motivated by which case, what it predicts about further situations—such as a flash mob that self-organizes via a single social media post, or a surgical team in which the lead surgeon has been replaced mid-operation—and give an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know whether the cost of saving the rescue-chain verdict is abandoning the board-rotation verdict, or whether a more complex disjunction is unavoidable, and what that tells us about whether collective intentionality is a unified phenomenon or a family of distinct relations.
