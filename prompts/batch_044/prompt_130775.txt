Please explain the conceptual structure of the paradox of phenomenal judgment as it arises for epiphenomenalism, with particular attention to a distinction that is often collapsed in standard presentations: the difference between the causal conditions required for a mental representation to acquire its content and the recognitional conditions required for a subject to be in a position to form a true first-person judgment. The standard formulation of the paradox assumes that if phenomenal properties have no causal powers, then no mental state can stand in the appropriate relation to them to serve as a judgment about them, since judgment-formation generally requires causal interaction with the judged state of affairs. Explain whether this formulation implicitly relies on a causal-informational theory of mental content, and whether an epiphenomenalist can consistently reject that theory—perhaps by adopting a direct-acquaintance or recognitional model of phenomenal concepts—without falling into circularity. Then address the further issue: even if recognitional access does not require causal efficacy, phenomenal judgments still appear to have specific accuracy conditions (they can be correct or incorrect about intensity, quality, or temporal location). Does this accuracy impose a representational constraint that reintroduces the causal problem at the level of error-correction or calibration, or can it be accounted for entirely within the internal dynamics of access consciousness? In developing your answer, distinguish this problem sharply from the general mental-causation exclusion problem, identifying exactly what makes the phenomenal-judgment version structurally unique. Do not provide citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; treat the issue as a live conceptual problem.
