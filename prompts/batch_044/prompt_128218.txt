I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or economics, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in technology governance that I cannot quite place in the vocabulary I have encountered. In one case, a development team institutes an extensive adversarial-testing protocol for a machine-learning system that delays deployment by nearly a year; a competitor forgoes the protocol, ships first, and secures dominant market share. Then the competitor’s system fails in a highly publicized incident, regulators intervene, and the industry is required to adopt “adversarial testing”—but the mandated version is a standardized two-week checklist that satisfies compliance, eliminates the competitive disadvantage, and fails to catch the specific failure mode that caused the crisis. In a second case, a trading firm maintains a manual review layer for automated transactions that costs milliseconds of latency; a rival removes the layer, exploits arbitrage windows, and profits until a flash crash occurs. The subsequent regulation mandates a “human-in-the-loop” form that is automated, adds no latency, and serves only as documentation. In both situations, a genuine safeguard initially imposes a real competitive cost, a crisis validates the need for protection, and the institutional response transforms the safeguard into a cheap, performative ritual that neutralizes the cost without preserving the protective function. I understand that this touches on the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax—the pressure to cut safety corners and the performance penalty of careful alignment—but those concepts seem to describe the pressure itself or the cost of doing things right, not this specific post-crisis cycle in which effectiveness is traded away for competitive parity. Is there an established name for this exact dynamic? If there is, how does it differ from ordinary regulatory capture, from the alignment tax, and from a simple race to the bottom? If there is not, could you propose a precise definition with clear boundary conditions—specifically, what distinguishes it from cases where regulation genuinely improves safety despite being watered down? I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I only want to know whether the concept exists, where its edges are, and what the nearest established ideas are. Please respond entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you might write in a carefully composed letter or email.
