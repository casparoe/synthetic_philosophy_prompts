I would like you to treat the January 2021 GameStop short squeeze—when a decentralized network of retail investors, coordinating largely through anonymous Reddit forums and amplified by both algorithmic feeds and mainstream financial news coverage, collectively drove up the stock price and inflicted severe losses on institutional short sellers—as a rigorous test case for Kierkegaard’s critique of the press and of the public. The salient facts are as I have just outlined them; I am not asking for a financial narrative or for obscure market details.

First, map the episode onto the conceptual framework: identify who in this situation functions as the public in Kierkegaard’s sense—the anonymous forum users, the passive news audience, the algorithmically aggregated crowd—versus who acts as a particular individual bearing real commitment and risk. Examine whether the anonymity of the platform reproduces the leveling he associated with the print-era press, and whether the rapid circulation of sentiment and meme-driven coordination amounts to reflection without action, or whether risking actual capital introduces a form of commitment his framework can accommodate. Second, analyze the role of the financial press: did coverage by major outlets serve merely as the reflective mirror that intensified the abstraction of the crowd, or did the attachment of real names, regulatory scrutiny, and concrete losses restore the particularity that the public otherwise erases?

Finally, and most importantly, tell me precisely where the analogy strains or breaks. Does the presence of real financial exposure and subsequent legislative and regulatory response show that digital crowds are not purely abstract in the way Kierkegaard feared, or does it reveal that his distinction between reflection and action needs supplementation—perhaps by distinguishing financial exposure from existential commitment—to remain applicable today? I am especially interested in whether algorithmic virality introduces a mode of leveling that Kierkegaard could not have anticipated, and whether extending his framework to include it strengthens the theory or exposes a structural gap. For each actor or mechanism you discuss, say explicitly what it corresponds to in the theory; for each theoretical claim, say whether the episode confirms it, complicates it, or leaves it silent. Do not invent obscure Kierkegaardian sub-doctrines; stay with the core ideas of the public, anonymity, reflection versus action, and the press as mediator.
