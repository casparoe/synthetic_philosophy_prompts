I'm preparing a session for a philosophy of science reading group—twelve graduate students, ninety minutes, with mixed familiarity with formal epistemology but a shared interest in belief dynamics over time. We have been discussing rational revision, and I want an activity that makes Van Fraassen's Reflection principle feel like an active, sometimes uncomfortable constraint on reasoning rather than a formal axiom students can nod at and forget. In particular, I want them to experience the tension between holding a present credence and anticipating one's own future credence, especially when one knows in advance that the incoming evidence is designed to trigger a cognitive bias—say, a framing effect or an anchoring scenario—that one has not yet corrected in oneself.

Please design a runnable classroom simulation or structured game with explicit rules, roles, and small tangible stakes (tokens, points, or a pooled reward). The physical setting should be a seminar table with private envelopes and hidden information, organized in two rounds. In Round One, each participant privately records a current credence in a specific target claim and also writes down a predicted credence for after they open their envelope; they then place a small bet—backed by the token pool—on whether their actual Round-Two credence will fall within a narrow band of that prediction. In Round Two, they open the envelope, receive the framed evidence, update, and record the actual credence. The critical twist, which should be built into the rules rather than explained by the facilitator, is that half the envelopes contain the bias-inducing frame and that all participants were warned beforehand about their own susceptibility. The scoring and disclosure rules should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that applying reflection—aligning present belief with expected future belief—seems to require them to anticipate and discount their own anticipated irrationality, which in turn destabilizes the very prediction that the principle demands. Provide a precise timeline, the exact mechanics for scoring and for revealing predictions, and clear instructions for how the facilitator should remain silent about the philosophical framing until the debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments from Round One and Round Two onto the formal conditions of reflection, explain what predictable confusion or strategic failure will arise—such as participants who attempt to "pre-correct" for the bias and then find their actual update diverges from both their original and their corrected predictions—and state frankly which feature of the principle the activity cannot reproduce, for instance the continuous-time probabilistic coherence conditions or the idealization of perfect self-knowledge about one's rationality. Also note explicitly what the facilitator must supply verbally that the rules and payoffs alone cannot generate, so I know where my own intervention is indispensable.
