Question: Define brain death and circulatory death, explain the dead donor rule, and analyze one ethical tension that arises when veno-arterial ECMO maintains circulation and metabolism after total, irreversible brainstem failure.

Student response: Brain death is the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem; the patient is legally dead despite a heart that may continue beating with ventilatory support. Circulatory death is the irreversible cessation of heart and lung function, typically confirmed after a five-minute observation period in which spontaneous resumption is impossible. The dead donor rule requires that vital organs not be removed until death has been declared; it is especially important in circulatory death protocols, where withdrawal of support precedes declaration, whereas in brain death the patient is already deceased before procurement is discussed. In the ECMO scenario, circulation and gas exchange persist mechanically even though no brain activity remains. Critics of declaring death here often argue that a beating heart proves life continues, but this confuses mechanical perfusion with true organismal integration. Once the brainstem is destroyed, the body can no longer direct spontaneous respiration or maintain homeostasis on its own, so it is best understood as perfused tissue rather than a living organism. The main risk of conceptual error is blurring this state with a persistent vegetative condition, since both involve absent consciousness; however, vegetative patients retain brainstem reflexes and spontaneous breathing, whereas brain death eliminates these entirely. Thus, declaring death is ethically sound, though clinicians must clarify to families that the heartbeat is maintained artificially.
