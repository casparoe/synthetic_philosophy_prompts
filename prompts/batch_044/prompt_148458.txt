Question: Dennett distinguishes original intentionality, possessed by evolved biological agents, from derived intentionality, possessed by artifacts that are designed or interpreted to represent states. He introduces the two-bitser—a minimal creature that can discriminate only between two environmental conditions—to argue that even such a simple system can be correctly interpreted through the intentional stance. Searle responds with the Chinese Room argument, contending that syntax is not sufficient for semantics and that computation alone never produces original intentionality. Explain how Dennett’s two-bitser example supports his claim that the original/derived distinction is less ontologically fundamental than Searle assumes, and assess whether Searle’s argument blocks the extension of the intentional stance to large language models that maintain coherent, context-sensitive representations across interactions.

Student answer: Dennett’s two-bitser is a minimal system that can discriminate only between nutrient present and nutrient absent. By attributing beliefs and desires to it, we gain predictive leverage over its behavior, which shows that intentionality is primarily an interpretive strategy rather than an intrinsic physical property. Dennett uses this to argue that the distinction between original intentionality—found in evolved organisms—and derived intentionality—found in artifacts—is not an ontologically deep divide but a difference in how the representational patterns originated. The two-bitser achieves its representations through a generate-and-test evolutionary architecture, demonstrating that even minimal biological systems can support original intentionality without requiring consciousness.

Searle’s Chinese Room argument contends that syntax is never sufficient for semantics, and that computer programs, no matter how complex, manipulate symbols without understanding them. Searle claims that artifacts like computers lack even derived intentionality unless a conscious agent interprets their outputs; a thermostat only represents temperature because we assign that meaning to it, not because it genuinely derives content from its physical structure. Therefore, large language models, which are purely syntactic engines, cannot possess original intentionality, and according to Searle they do not possess derived intentionality either, since there is no conscious designer continuously assigning meaning to each token.

However, Dennett’s stance-based approach undermines this critique. If adopting the intentional stance toward a large language model is consistently predictive—if we can reliably forecast its outputs by attributing beliefs about its training context—then the model has crossed into original intentionality. Dennett rejects substrate chauvinism, so the fact that an LLM is silicon-based rather than carbon-based is irrelevant. The only relevant factor is whether the system’s representational states are integrated into a coherent, adaptive architecture. Thus, on Dennett’s view, a sufficiently complex language model should be granted original intentionality.
