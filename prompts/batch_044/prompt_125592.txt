I've been working through what looks like a reductio of rational-choice explanations of nuclear deterrence, and I can't locate the leak. The argument runs like this.

(1) A rational agent selects the action with the highest expected utility given its beliefs.
(2) In any actual nuclear-use scenario, the direct expected utility of launch is catastrophically negative.
(3) So if a rational agent retains full decision authority at the moment of choice, it will never launch—no matter how credible its prior threats.
(4) Credible deterrence requires that the adversary assign non-zero probability to launch.
(5) Therefore credible deterrence requires mechanisms that can produce launch without fresh rational authorization—launch-on-warning postures, delegated authority to field commanders, or automated fail-safe architectures like the Soviet Perimeter system.
(6) Installing such mechanisms deliberately lowers one's ex-ante expected utility, since they increase the risk of accidental or unauthorized escalation.
(7) So maintaining credible deterrence requires rationally adopting structures that reduce one's own rational control at the decisive moment.
(8) If that adoption is itself rational, then rationality includes second-order strategies of self-binding that suspend first-order rational evaluation.
(9) But if rationality can license its own suspension, then "rational action" no longer denotes a consistent decision procedure; it denotes whatever strategic posture serves credibility, including ones that are first-order irrational.
(10) This applies beyond nuclear strategy to any domain requiring credible commitment—burning bridges, irreversible contracts—so the collapse is general.
(11) Consequently, rational-choice theory cannot distinguish between genuinely rational conduct and strategically useful irrationality; it offers no normative guidance in strategic domains.
(12) If there is no normative guidance, then nuclear strategy reduces to performative brinkmanship with no rational basis for preferring stability to accidental catastrophe.

The conclusion feels absurd—surely rational-choice theory isn't empty—but I can't see which premise does the work. I would most like to preserve (5) and (7): that credible deterrence genuinely requires mechanisms that risk accidental escalation, and that adopting them is rational despite the risk. I'd be willing to weaken (1) or (9) if necessary, but I want to know exactly what I'm giving up.

What does rejecting (9) cost?
