I'm trying to determine what makes legal self-reference genuinely paradoxical rather than merely circular or self-defeating, by testing my intuitions against concrete institutional designs.

My verdicts: (1) A municipal charter that permits amendment only by a quorum of 100 citizens is coherent; adding a clause that lets the council lower that quorum to 50 feels like a loophole, but not incoherent—it's acceptable. (2) A club bylaw declaring "No rule here may be made irrevocable," paired with a second rule declaring "This rule is irrevocable," strikes me as not acceptable: it's a flat contradiction, not interesting self-reference. (3) A constitution allowing amendment by two-thirds vote, with no restriction on amending that amendment clause, so that a legislature replaces it with a simple-majority rule: that seems legitimate, not paradoxical. (4) A regulatory framework creates a meta-board empowered solely to revise the rules defining all other boards, including those defining the meta-board's own quorum and membership; when the meta-board eliminates its quorum and expands its membership, my intuition is that this crosses into illegitimate bootstrapping, though structurally it resembles case (3). (5) A contract that states "This contract terminates itself upon being signed by both parties," which both parties then sign: I take this to be self-defeating but not paradoxical—it does exactly what it says, so it's technically acceptable, if useless.

What principle, if any, distinguishes the acceptable cases (1, 3, 5) from the unacceptable (2) and the waffling case (4)?
