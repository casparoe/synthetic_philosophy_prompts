Hi — I'm filing a feature on why the 'how old are nations' row never actually resolves. You know the setup: Hobsbawm and Anderson frame nations as modern fabrications, stitched together from print capitalism and invented traditions, while the ethnosymbolists (Smith et al) argue there's real continuity with older ethnic cores. I've sat in seminars where both camps point at teh same Balkan census records from the 1890s and just talk past each other. My hunch is the deadlock isn't empirical — it's about whether collective memory functions as a causal mechanism or only as a post-hoc story. One camp treats 'nation' as an institutional fact that needs a charter or archive; the other treats it as a phenomenological reality that can persist without paperwork. So is this a hidden metaphysical clash over what makes a social group real? Or are they running two different enquiries — one about state formation, one about identity persistence — under one heading? And given that diagnosis, what would shift it? A fresh archival find? A clean split between 'nation-state' and 'nation-feeling'? Or should we accept the stalemate is built into the concept itself? I want a diagnosis anchored in what each side actually argues, not a literature review, plus an honest call on whether progress is even possible.
