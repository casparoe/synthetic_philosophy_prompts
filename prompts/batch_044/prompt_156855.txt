A regional environmental permitting agency operates under what it calls a “threshold-compliance” framework. Rather than ranking development proposals along a continuous scale of ecological benefit, staff sort every application into one of three bins—“fails,” “passes,” or “exceeds”—based on fixed benchmarks for habitat preservation, emissions, and water quality. The agency’s internal manual contains an explicit prohibition: even when a proposal lands in the “exceeds” bin by a wide margin, analysts may not cite that surplus ecological value as grounds to expedite review, prioritize funding, or relax secondary conditions. All “exceeds” projects are processed through the identical 30-day pipeline used for bare “passes” projects, and staff are directed to devote their analytical resources to policing the boundary between failure and adequacy rather than to comparing degrees of excellence within the adequate-or-better range. The agency publicly defends this design by appealing to hard limits on institutional cognition and computation—claiming that ranking supererogatory ecological goods against one another exceeds the rational capacity of a bureaucratic body—yet critics charge that the system treats ecological value as lexically subordinate to administrative tractability.

What philosophical commitments about practical rationality, value, and institutional design does this practice actually presuppose? Does it require a literal institutionalization of bounded-rationality satisficing, or can the same pattern be explained by rival views—say, a deontological floor theory that treats ecological benchmarks as side-constraints, or a rule-utilitarian defense that predicts better long-run outcomes from rigid thresholds than from case-by-case optimization? Then, explain what would have to change—both in the agency’s formal justification and in its day-to-day procedures—if it abandoned its bounded-rationality rationale and replaced it with an explicit value-pluralist framework that acknowledged incommensurable ecological goods without collapsing them into a single metric.
