Having retired recently from a career in atmospheric physics, I have begun auditing graduate seminars in the philosophy of history, partly to understand how scientists and humanists treat causation and scale differently. During a seminar discussion of Fernand Braudel's historiography, a fellow student—who claimed to have read the original Annales literature—summarized the doctrine of the longue durée as follows: "Braudel held that geography and climate are the sole genuine engines of historical change, while political events, individual decisions, and short-term social movements are nothing more than surface noise. Because environmental structures move on timescales of centuries or millennia, historians ought to abandon narrative and counterfactual reasoning about agents entirely, treating human societies as passive outputs of geographical constraints in the same way that statistical mechanics treats macroscopic gas properties as outputs of molecular distributions. Consequently, explaining the collapse of Rome or the course of the French Revolution by appealing to particular leaders, contingent choices, or institutional innovations is not merely incomplete but methodologically illegitimate."

The gloss sounds to me like an illegitimate importation of physical determinism into historiography, yet I lack the specialized background to diagnose exactly where it misrepresents Braudel. Would you assess whether this is a fair summary? In particular, please distinguish what is faithful to Braudel's actual three-layered temporal framework—geographical time, social time, and the time of events—from what overstates his anti-narrative or anti-agent commitments, and identify any outright errors, such as the analogy to statistical mechanics or the claim that counterfactual reasoning is barred rather than merely subordinate to structural analysis. I am especially curious whether this summary confuses Braudel with Montesquieu-style climatic determinism, or with Marxist base-superstructure models, and whether the conclusion that individual agency is epiphenomenal in a strong physicalist sense actually follows from Braudel's texts. I am not asking for a bibliography, but rather for a conceptual audit I can use to discuss the matter with my seminar group.
