I'm writing a paper for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on temporal anomalies in conscious experience, and I've become convinced that the standard dismissal of the Stalinesque model of the color-phi phenomenon is too quick. The usual objection is that if the brain constructs the intermediate color-change only after registering the second dot, then 'backdating' the experience to the interval between the two dots involves an impossible kind of temporal misplacement—either a false memory, which collapses Stalinesque into Orwellian, or a metaphysically suspect retrocausal influence on phenomenal content. But I think this objection conflates the temporal location of the representational vehicle with the temporal location of the representational content. My claim is that the Stalinesque account can be saved, and in fact strengthened, if we treat the 'backdated' phenomenal state as having a relational rather than an intrinsic temporal index: the experience is genuinely present at the later time, but its intentional object is structured as being-of-the-interval-between-the-two-stimuli, without the experience itself being located at that interval. This makes the Stalinesque model not a story about revising the past, but about constructing a present representation with a complex, relational temporal structure.

Please argue for this position in detail. You should explain how distinguishing the vehicle's time from the content's temporal index dissolves the standard collapse-objection, show why this relational reading makes the Stalinesque model empirically distinguishable from the Orwellian model despite their superficial similarity, and defend the further claim that this relational structure is actually more consistent with a direct-realist theory of perception than the Orwellian alternative, because it avoids positing an intermediate phenomenal state that is subsequently erased or overwritten. You don't need to provide citations or discuss specific experimental protocols—I'm looking for the conceptual architecture of the defense, especially how it reframes the debate about whether the 'timing of experience' is a property of individual states or of the diachronic relations between them. If you think the argument fails at some point, say so explicitly, but please begin by developing the strongest version of the relational-Stalinesque view.
