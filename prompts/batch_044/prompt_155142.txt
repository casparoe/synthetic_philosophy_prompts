I have recently become personally invested in the scope of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism because I am writing a graduate essay on whether causal denial in medieval Islamic metaphysics can be restricted to physical interaction without collapsing into broader skepticism. I find the occasionalist claim that fire does not burn by nature—rather, that God creates burning directly upon the occasion of contact—intellectually compelling, yet I have constructed the following argument to test its consistency, and now I find its conclusion intolerable.

(1) Al-Ghazālī denies that created substances possess inherent causal powers; God is the direct cause of every particular effect, with apparent causes serving merely as occasions.
(2) This denial is usually quarantined to empirical domains such as combustion or medicine, leaving logical and mathematical relations untouched.
(3) But logical entailment and arithmetic equality are also observed as constant conjunctions across instances, with no perceptible intrinsic connection linking premises to conclusions or addends to sums.
(4) If occasionalism is adopted consistently, then God must directly create each particular valid inference and correct computation, with premises and numerals serving only as occasions rather than as sources of modal necessity.
(5) Therefore, there is no metaphysical guarantee that modus ponens will preserve truth in the next instance, or that seven plus five will equal twelve in a future calculation.
(6) Yet rational deliberation and mathematical proof depend precisely on the assumption that these relations are necessarily stable, not merely habitually sustained.
(7) Consequently, consistent occasionalism undermines the very rational framework required to defend or articulate the theory.
(8) So we must either reject occasionalism, or arbitrarily restrict it to physical domains without principled justification.

Where does this argument fail, if it does? If I am forced to choose, I prefer to retain (1) and the consistency demand embedded in (4) rather than accept (8), but I want to understand exactly what cost I pay for doing so—whether the error lies in treating logical relations as empirical correlations, in an illicit modal slide, or in some other step that I have not yet identified.
