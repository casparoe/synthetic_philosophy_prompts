In a graduate seminar on intuition pumps and conceptual analysis, a fellow student keeps falling back on the line 'if you can vividly imagine it, then it's genuinely possible' whenever we evaluate a thought experiment—most recently to defend Mary's room against the charge that describing a color-blind scientist's first red experience already smuggles in the phenomenal knowledge the argument needs, and earlier to defend the Chinese Room by insisting that our ability to picture a symbol-manipulating room proves syntax is not sufficient for semantics. I think the slogan is being asked to carry far more weight than any single principle could bear, but I need help seeing exactly where the ambiguity lies. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses hidden in that sentence—distinguishing, for example, psychological imaginability from logical consistency, ideal rational conceivability from actual human visualization, metaphysical possibility from epistemic accessibility, and first-person phenomenal simulation from third-person structural modeling—and for each variant say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but controversial thesis, or plainly false? Then, for each reading, indicate whether it actually licenses using Mary's room or the Chinese Room as intuition pumps that establish substantive conclusions about consciousness or intentionality, or whether it either fails to bridge the gap from imagination to possibility or actually undermines the argument by revealing a hidden circularity in the imaginative exercise itself. I want the answer structured as a menu of readings I can bring back to the seminar, with the verdict on each and a clear statement of which reading—if any—the student is actually invoking when they use the slogan to shut down an objection.
