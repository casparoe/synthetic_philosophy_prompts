A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing over whether self-deception is genuinely possible, and I constructed the following line of reasoning to show that it collapses into contradiction—yet I now worry it eliminates far more than it should.

(1) For self-deception to occur, a single agent must simultaneously know that p and actively prevent herself from knowing that p.

(2) But knowledge is factive and doxastically transparent: if S knows p, then S knows that she knows p, and she cannot rationally withhold assent from p while retaining the underlying knowledge.

(3) Therefore the “preventing” mentioned in (1) cannot be performed by the same knowing subject upon itself; it requires a cognitive partition—something like a sub-personal module or representational compartment—that withholds p from the agent’s main belief-forming system.

(4) Once such a partition is admitted, however, we no longer have one unified agent with contradictory beliefs. Instead we have distinct sub-systems, each with its own representational content, and neither system is deceived in the relevant intentional sense.

(5) So either there is no partition—in which case self-deception reduces to ordinary ignorance, hypocrisy, or mere failure of inference—or there is a partition, in which case there is no single self that is being deceived.

(6) Hence self-deception is conceptually impossible for a unified rational agent.

(7) Yet if self-deception is impossible, then every apparent case must be re-described: for example, a senior manager who has personally compiled data showing her division is failing, yet sincerely reports to the board that all key metrics are excellent, cannot be deceiving herself about the failure. She must either be genuinely ignorant of the data (which she is not, since she compiled it) or be engaged in straightforward deception of others.

(8) This seems to prove too much. It does not merely rule out self-deception; it rules out any form of internal motivated irrationality or compartmentalised behaviour, leaving us unable to account for phenomena that look structurally like one part of the mind hiding a truth from another.

(9) I feel the slide happens somewhere between (3) and (7), but I cannot locate the precise misstep. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, I want to preserve the intuition that the manager is neither simply ignorant nor merely lying to colleagues—there is something genuinely intra-personal going on.
