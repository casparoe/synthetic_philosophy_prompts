At a seminar on pragmatist epistemology, Elena and Marcus debated whether Peirce’s convergence theory or James’s practical account of truth better handles predictive scientific models that are literally false but indispensable. They discussed “Model T,” a cognitive-science framework that treats the brain as a literal Bayesian inference engine; neuroscientists agree neurons do not compute probabilities that way, yet the model reliably predicts stress-related behavior.

(1) Elena argued that Peirce’s view handles this cleanly: Model T is literally false, which means the community of inquirers will eventually converge on the correct neurocomputational story, regardless of current predictive success. Truth is the fated final opinion, not present utility.

(2) Marcus replied that Peirce makes truth hostage to an unreachable ideal endpoint; in actual scientific practice we call Model T “true enough” precisely because it works—predicting behavior—so James’s claim that truth is what works in the way of belief captures practice better.

(3) Elena countered that James conflates “working well” with “being true,” and that Model T’s success likely tracks an approximate real structure (e.g., approximate Bayesian optimization in neural circuits) even if the literal description is wrong. Peirce’s fallibilism distinguishes useful approximations from pure fictions; James lacks resources to draw that line.

(4) Marcus said Elena smuggles in a metaphysical commitment to hidden real structures that James’s radical empiricism rejects. For James, there is no independently accessible reality behind the model—only experienced relations. If Model T’s predictions hold and no better framework replaces it, it is true in the only meaningful sense, and Elena’s approximation-versus-fiction distinction collapses without an independently verifiable reality.
