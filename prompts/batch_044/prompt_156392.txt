I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of medicine seminar, and after a long discussion with a colleague I realised that my students consistently reduce debates about psychiatric classification to a stale realism-versus-social-construction binary. I want to move them past slogans. The specific issue is Ian Hacking's concept of looping effects in psychiatric diagnosis — the process by which being placed in a category can reshape a person's behaviour and self-understanding, which then feeds back into the category itself — but I need an instrument that forces them to locate their actual commitments rather than their favourite label. Please construct a decision tree, or taxonomy arranged as a branching flowchart, that sorts positions on whether and how looping effects undermine, transform, or leave untouched the objectivity of psychiatric categories. Build the tree from the logical space of answers, not from a pre-set list of named theories. Start with the broad question of whether looping vitiates the category's claim to track a real distinction, then branch through concrete diagnostic commitments: whether the loop is driven mainly by first-person narrative adoption or by institutional and clinical incentives; whether it operates symmetrically across symptoms and identity-formation or asymmetrically between them; and whether disrupting the loop demands revising the diagnostic criteria themselves or only altering the social practices that deploy them. Each leaf should carry a standard philosophical label if one exists — for instance, weak realism, dynamic nominalism, pragmatic instrumentalism — or be marked 'unnamed', followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment (the consequence its defenders usually try to minimise). Please include at least one unoccupied branch, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The audience is graduate students who grasp Hacking's framework but tend to reach for the same two positions; phrase every node as a concrete claim or case they could assess from their own reasoning without knowing in advance which label it leads to. Limit the taxonomy to roughly six to eight leaves, present it clearly as a classroom handout, and write the response in British English.
