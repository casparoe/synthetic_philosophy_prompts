Consider the normative assessment of meat consumption not through standard carbon-footprint or direct-animal-death calculus, but through what might be called the land-use substitution cascade: the chain by which demand for beef versus chicken versus crop calories restructures not only immediate animal deaths, but the long-term ecological carrying capacity for wild sentient populations across trophic levels. Within this framing, several open conceptual questions remain unresolved: whether the relevant moral unit is the individual sentient experience, the species-level population stability, or the ecosystem-level sentience-weighted biomass; how to weigh counterfactual wild-animal suffering prevented by agricultural land-use against that caused by crop cultivation, particularly invertebrate displacement in soil systems; and whether the beef-versus-chicken comparison should be evaluated by direct deaths per calorie, by trophic-level energy efficiency, or by differential long-term habitat lock-in effects of ruminant pasture versus poultry-feed monoculture.

Assume you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group—two moral philosophers, one ecologist, and one decision theorist—with three years of funding and no capacity for original empirical fieldwork. Give a dependency-aware prioritization: which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable for this team given its composition, and which you would deprioritize because resolving them would not substantially alter normative recommendations for individual dietary choice or agricultural policy.
