A recent essay summarized Leibniz's moral philosophy like this: Leibniz held that moral truths are necessary and eternal, grounded in the rational nature of God rather than in divine will, which makes him a clear anti-voluntarist. At the same time, he believed that God selects among possible worlds without being constrained by any principle of value, since every world contains exactly the same quantity of perfection arranged differently; this means that the particular moral laws governing our world are contingent on an arbitrary divine choice. The essay also claims that, because monads are windowless and each soul accesses only its own perceptions, Leibniz must deny that we have direct moral obligations to other people—what the author calls the "monadic isolation thesis"—and that intersubjective justice is therefore only a pragmatic convention. Finally, the essay concludes that Leibniz's rationalism requires all moral knowledge to be demonstrable a priori, leaving no room for empirical moral learning. Something about this feels uneven: parts match what I remember about his rejection of the view that eternal truths depend on God's will, but other parts seem to conflict with his idea that God chooses the best world for a reason. Is this summary fair? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard oversimplification, what misrepresents Leibniz's actual positions (especially on the contingency of moral law, the principle behind world-selection, and the status of intersubjective duties), and whether the conclusion about purely a priori moral knowledge follows from the parts that are correct.
