I've been preparing a closing piece for my philosophy of mathematics reading group. We've spent the semester on the ontology of infinity—Cantor's argument for uncountably many sizes, Hilbert's hotel as an intuition pump for countable actual infinities, and the strict finitist line, running back through Kronecker, that denies any "completed" infinite totality and permits only potential, unending processes. I want a poem of about eighteen to twenty lines in the form of a villanelle that treats the hotel not as a whimsical puzzle but as a site where an ontological commitment is smuggled in through the indexing scheme itself. Specifically, the two refrains should carry the opposing voices: one, the manager's, insisting that there is always a room for one more; the other, the finitist's, insisting that there is no final room and that the numbering 1, 2, 3... already presupposes a completed sequence of ordinals—the very thing being disputed. The villanelle's alternating returns should mirror the endless shifting of guests into rooms, and the closing couplet should bring the two refrains together in a way that shows the positions passing each other rather than resolving. I want the poem to register that technical point about the numbering scheme being part of the dispute, not just background machinery. The tone should be formal and somewhat austere—suitable for a seminar closing—accurate enough that someone who knows the actual-versus-potential-infinity distinction will recognize both positions rendered fairly, but composed so that a reader unfamiliar with them still feels the structural tension working through the rhyme and refrain pattern.
