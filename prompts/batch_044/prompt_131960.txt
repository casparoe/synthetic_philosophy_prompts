Following a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend over whether a filmmaker is morally permitted to invent a deathbed conversation for a well-documented historical figure, I would like you to construct a taxonomy—delivered entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, as if writing a letter—that maps the logical space of positions on what obligations, if any, we owe to real dead people when we dramatize them in historical fiction and biopics. The taxonomy should take the form of a branching tree in which each split is labeled by a concrete claim or case on which the positions genuinely come apart, not by the names of theories or schools. Begin at the root with the question of whether dramatizations carry any obligation specific to representing real dead individuals that does not apply to purely fictional characters. If the answer is negative, that leaf should stand alone with its awkward commitment noted. If affirmative, branch according to whether the primary obligation is owed to the dead themselves—perhaps through posthumous interests in dignity or through what I will call narrative inheritance, the idea that the dead bequeath a story-identity held in trust—to living survivors and descendants who might suffer collateral harm, or to the collective historical record as such. Where the dead themselves are the creditors, split further on whether they can genuinely be wronged after death, or whether our duty is merely derivative, such as honoring a narrative inheritance even though no conscious interest remains; then split again on whether false representations alone violate that duty, or whether truthful but invasive portrayals—those that expose humiliating but accurate private details—violate it equally. Where the historical record is the creditor, split on whether its value is intrinsic, so that any fictionalization amounts to what I will call epistemic pollution of collective memory, or merely instrumental; and if instrumental, split on whether literal fidelity to documented dialogue and specific events is required, or whether structural fidelity—preserving the true causal patterns, institutional dynamics, and psychological motives—is sufficient even when individual scenes are invented. Each leaf should carry either the name of a recognized position that occupies it, or be marked unnamed if that combination of answers seems unexplored, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. You should also identify which answer-combinations appear unoccupied in the tree—for instance, holding that the record has intrinsic value while simultaneously accepting that structural fidelity alone is adequate—and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. In addition, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, such as survivor-centered obligations that may overlap with derivative narrative-inheritance duties when descendants are the sole trustees. To keep the taxonomy from being answerable by reciting standard encyclopedia entries, inject at least one somewhat niche conceptual distinction—such as narrative inheritance, epistemic pollution, or a specific conception of structural fidelity—but do not invoke ultra-obscure theories or depend on citations of particular texts. Write everything as continuous prose.
