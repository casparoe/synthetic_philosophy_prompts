I have recently been asked to design an ethics curriculum for secondary-school students, which has made the evolutionary origins of moral judgment suddenly personal: I am trying to decide what authority, if any, the norms I teach retain given where they likely come from. Here is where I stand. (1) I am a moral realist of a non-naturalist stripe: there are mind-independent moral facts that are not reducible to natural properties. (2) I take evolutionary debunking arguments seriously—our core moral intuitions are largely products of adaptive pressures for cooperation and in-group cohesion, not of any tracking relation to objective moral truth, so they are unreliable as direct guides. (3) I nonetheless hold that reflective equilibrium remains the correct method in normative ethics; we begin with considered judgments and revise them in light of principles and background theory. (4) I think the debunking pressure is asymmetrical: it bites harder on deontic judgments (what we ought to do) than on axiological judgments (what is genuinely good or bad), because selection shaped action-guiding norms more directly than value-tracking capacities. (5) I maintain that moral knowledge is still attainable, but only through highly systematized principles that survive rigorous equilibrium, not through raw intuitive deliverance. Do these commitments hang together? In particular, is there a hidden tension between (2) and (3)—if our starting intuitions are systematically debunked, does reflective equilibrium have any legitimate material to work with? Is the asymmetry in (4) defensible given (1) and (2), or does it smuggle in an unjustified distinction? And does (5) genuinely rescue knowledge, or does it merely relocate the problem? Please rank any tensions by severity, indicate which ones are only apparent, and say what the least costly revision would be.
