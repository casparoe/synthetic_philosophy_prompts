I'm preparing my position paper for a graduate metaethics seminar focused on agent-centered restrictions, and I want to stress-test a package of commitments I've assembled around Bernard Williams's integrity objection before I present them. Here is my current profile: (1) I accept the familiar critique that utilitarian reasoning demands one thought too many in the rescue case—if the husband saves his wife only after weighing her against strangers in an aggregate calculus, both his motivation and the theory guiding him have gone wrong; (2) I hold that ground projects are partly constitutive of personal identity, so that abandoning them amounts to a kind of practical self-dissolution rather than a mere preference frustration; (3) I model the weight of these projects on a scalar integrity budget rather than as binary protected spheres, which allows me to say that in genuinely catastrophic scenarios an agent may be required to deplete that budget without undergoing total identity-collapse; (4) I interpret the one-thought-too-many problem as a structural failure in moral justification—utilitarianism makes the wrong considerations decisive—rather than as a psychological claim about how motives should feel; (5) I think agent-relative permissions, such as the right to prefer one's spouse, are ultimately reducible to agent-neutral considerations about the value of autonomy and identity-continuity combined with indexical facts about whose continuity is at stake; and (6) in the standard spouse-versus-stranger case, saving one's spouse is permitted but not morally required, and failing to do so is not a breach of integrity.

My concern is that (2) and (3) may be pulling against each other—if ground projects are identity-constituting in a strong sense, it is unclear how they can be partially depleted on a budget without that dissolution being all-or-nothing—and that (5) may undermine the anti-utilitarian force of (1) and (4) by allowing a sophisticated consequentialist to accommodate agent-relative permissions after all. I also worry about (6) in relation to (2): if ground projects are so central to who one is, why does integrity not generate a positive duty to preserve them in this case rather than merely licensing one to do so? I need an audit, not a lecture: go through these pairs, rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the genuinely structural conflicts from those that only look incompatible and dissolve on closer inspection, specify what each genuine tension costs me if I keep both sides, and identify the single cheapest revision that would clean up the largest number of problems. Please do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging.
