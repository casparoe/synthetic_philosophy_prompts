Please write my opening remarks for next Friday's "Boundaries" symposium at my school. I am a high school student invited to speak for five minutes to an audience split evenly between mathematics and philosophy students—who will recognize references to Cantor's actual infinite sets and Hilbert's hotel—and art and literature students, for whom those references should illuminate rather than exclude.

The speech should take as its structural backbone the tension between actual infinity and strict finitism. I want to use Hilbert's hotel—where a fully occupied infinite hotel can still accommodate a new guest—to frame our symposium's theme of "making room for new voices." But I also want to introduce a hypothetical "finitist curator" who accepts the mathematics yet denies its physical or conceptual application: she argues that treating potential newcomers as already belonging to a completed, countable infinity smuggles in a totality no real archive or community can possess. This objection should genuinely constrain what I promise. I should not claim our program offers "infinite space" for ideas; instead, the speech must acknowledge that every addition requires a concrete displacement or restructuring, precisely because we reject the comfort of a pre-existing, fully stocked hotel.

Please shape the address so that the philosophical dispute does real work: it determines both the welcome I extend and the limits I set. The mathematics students should feel the finitist challenge is taken seriously, not as a strawman, while the other half should understand why "there is always room" can be both inspiring and misleading. Do not include citations, a reading list, or references to specific texts beyond the well-known analogies themselves.
