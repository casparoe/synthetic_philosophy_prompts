I'm running a course on comparative philosophy of religion this term — well, I'm co-running it with a historian — and yesterday in the staff common room I ended up in a bit of a row with two colleages about Jain anekāntavāda and whether it actualy does anything useful outside metaphysics. Tom, who's in ethics, reckons that if you take syādvāda seriously, with all its seven conditional predications, then saying 'euthanasia is wrong' just collapses into 'euthanasia is wrong in some respects and not wrong in others,' which means you've stopped disagreeing with anyone and dissolved the conflict into mush. He thinks anekāntavāda is therefore useless for actual moral deliberation — just epistemic cowardice dressed up in fancy Sanskrit terminology.

Sarah, from metaphysics, said Tom's mixing up not knowing which view is correct with the claim that reality itself is ontologically complex: the point of naya-vāda isn't that we're ignorant, but that the object genuinely has incompatible properties depending on standpoint (nayas), so each side captures part of the truth. Tom came back that if every standpoint is equally valid then there's no principled reason to favour one policy over another, which makes the whole thing just relativism in Sanskrit clothing. Sarah said that's wrong — syādvāda doesn't grant equal validity to every naya, it just says they're all partial, and weighing them is exactly what deliberation should involve anyway. They both turned to me but I honesty couldn't tell who was landing the blows. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? In particular, is Tom right that syādvāda dissolves disagreement, or is Sarah right that it preserves disagreement but complicates the ontology? And are they both missing something about how anekāntavāda treats contradictory predicates — do they apply simultaneously, or only perspectivley? Don't just say it depends on interpretation; I want to know whether Tom's practical objection actualy undermines the metaphysical claim, or if they're talking past each other.
