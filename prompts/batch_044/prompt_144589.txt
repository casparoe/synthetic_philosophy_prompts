I coordinate intake for an emergency shelter after a natural disaster. Our published protocol is first-come-first-served, and forty people have arrived and been waiting for hours in line based on it. We have thirty beds. A colleague insists we should abandon the queue and fill beds by medical urgency instead, arguing that need outweighs procedural fairness. I feel that switching rules after people have relied on them is a kind of institutional betrayal — but I cannot tell whether that feeling reflects a genuine moral obligation or just a psychological preference for consistency.

What makes this harder is that I am genuinely split about which moral considerations should govern the decision. One view says I should weigh overall welfare; another says I should prioritize the worst-off; a third says that once a procedure is announced and people have acted on it, abandoning it is wrong regardless of outcomes. I have heard that philosophers debate how to decide when one is uncertain in exactly this way: some recommend computing an expected value across one's beliefs about which consideration is correct; others recommend avoiding the worst possible mistake on any plausible view; and a smaller group suggests that some reasons might apply independently of any single framework, standing on their own rather than deriving from one particular moral theory. I do not know how to apply any of these approaches.

How should I allocate the thirty beds? And more fundamentally, how should I reason about the choice when I do not know which moral considerations are the right ones to apply — should I aggregate expected value, minimize worst-case risk, or look for reasons that do not depend on committing to any single framework?
