I'm planning a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy of science undergraduates who have read Popper on falsificationism but have no background in formal logic or probability theory. The concept is the problem of verisimilitude: why Popper thought science aims at truth-likeness rather than truth, and why the project of measuring it formally collapsed. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specify the exact order in which the pieces must come—why falsifiability has to be in place before the problem even arises, why the distinction between logical and empirical content must precede any talk of approximation, and where the formal difficulty belongs—together with the one carrying example I should use throughout. I intend to frame this through cartographic distortion: a world map that gets every coastline right but misplaces every city is false in one way, while a map that correctly locates every city but uses the wrong projection is false in another, and we need to see why one might be closer to the true geography despite being strictly false in more respects. State precisely what this analogy must include and what it must avoid—especially the risk that students confuse distance from truth with predictive failure. Then map the misconceptions: I expect they will conflate verisimilitude with predictive accuracy, think the formal collapse proves science cannot progress, and assume that if a theory is false it cannot be closer to truth than a trivial true statement like all swans being white. For each, identify the point in the sequence where it should be intercepted, not after it has formed. Include a final diagnostic question or task that tests whether they grasp the structural dependency—why content-based comparison requires comparing false theories—rather than merely recalling that measurement is hard. Also specify what I should openly refuse to formalize—probabilistic measures of truth-likeness, the full logical apparatus—and say so explicitly. Finally, separate what is settled here—Popper's motivation, the specific formal failure—from what is genuinely contested, such as whether the project can be rehabilitated with a different metric or whether the very idea of quantifying approximation was a category mistake. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
