Since my father's death earlier this year, I have found myself returning to questions about whether linear time is genuinely objective or whether our experience of irreversible succession is, in some sense, an artifact of perspective rather than a feature of reality. This led me to Kurt Gödel's well-known argument that rotating universes—spacetime geometries permitted by Einstein's field equations—contain closed timelike curves, and that this possibility undermines the objectivity of temporal order. In discussing this with two colleagues, Marcus and Elena, I encountered a disagreement that neither of them would concede, and I am asking for an external adjudication rather than further debate between them.

Marcus's position unfolds in three moves. First, he claims that if general relativity allows a cosmological model in which an observer can, in principle, travel into her own past, then there is no globally consistent ordering of before and after; without such a global ordering, temporal succession cannot be an objective feature of the world, but must be subjective or illusory. Second, he argues that Gödel's own commitment to timeless mathematical Platonism reinforces rather than conflicts with this conclusion: if physical time collapses, we are left with an atemporal mathematical reality that is genuinely real, so the denial of physical time is paired with a positive ontological alternative. Third, in response to objections, Marcus insists that the rotating universe should not be read as an empirical prediction but as a consistency proof—what matters is that our best physical theory is compatible with Einstein's suspicion that time is unreal, which gives the metaphysical view scientific respectability.

Elena responds move by move. First, she denies that the existence of a particular, highly symmetric cosmological solution with closed timelike curves entails anything about time in general; at most it shows that one model has an unusual feature, and since that model almost certainly does not describe our actual universe, no metaphysical conclusion about all time follows. Second, she charges Marcus with an illicit inference from modality to categorical reality: even if it is possible for global temporal ordering to fail, this does not establish that it actually fails, or that local temporal ordering is unreal. Third, she identifies what she takes to be an internal tension in Marcus's position: if one denies the reality of physical structures on the grounds that they are merely modeled by mathematics, but then grants full ontological weight to the mathematical structures themselves, one owes an account of why mathematical reality survives when physical reality does not—especially since Marcus's argument relies precisely on taking the mathematical model of a rotating universe seriously enough to draw metaphysical conclusions from it. Marcus replies that Elena is treating the argument as empirical when it is conceptual: the point is not that our universe is rotating, but that the concept of objective temporal flow is internally unstable once physics permits its own dissolution. Elena counters that a consistency proof can only show that a metaphysical view is not contradicted by physics, not that physics supports it, and that without further premises Marcus has shown nothing more than that strange boundary conditions yield strange geometries.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of Marcus's three moves and each of Elena's three responses, indicate whether the move is clearly right, partly right but overstated, or clearly mistaken, with brief justification. In particular, address whether Marcus is correct to treat Gödel's rotating universe as carrying metaphysical weight about all time, whether Elena's charge of internal tension succeeds against Marcus's version of the argument, and whether either party is neglecting a relevant distinction—such as that between local and global temporal ordering, or between physical and phenomenological time—that would alter the assessment. Finally, say which of them is more right overall, without feeling compelled to split the difference if one side genuinely dominates, and identify anything important that both are missing.
