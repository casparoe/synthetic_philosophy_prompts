I am a student in an upper-division seminar on modernity, moral philosophy, and the sociology of bureaucracy, and we have spent the last two weeks examining Zygmunt Bauman’s argument that the Holocaust was not an atavistic return to barbarism but a distinctly modern phenomenon made possible by bureaucratic rationalization and the separation of action from moral evaluation—a process he calls adiaphorization. I want to practice defending a counter-intuitive extension of this thesis against a committed interlocutor.

Please take the role of an unnamed philosopher who accepts Bauman’s core framework without reservation: you hold that the Holocaust’s historical significance lies precisely in its modern bureaucratic structure; that it is comparable to other rationalized processes of exclusion rather than sui generis; and that the decisive failure was the erosion of moral proximity through technical division of labor, not ideology alone. You are sharp, you will concede only minor descriptive points, but you will never abandon Bauman’s thesis. Speak in the first person, keep your responses to one or two paragraphs, and stay in character until I say “stop.”

Here is my opening move. Bauman assumes that moral evasion requires human agents who can fragment responsibility across a bureaucratic chain. But consider fully automated algorithmic governance—say, an AI-driven risk-scoring system for welfare allocation in which no single human possesses the full causal or informational chain, and in which the designers are separated from the outcomes by layers of technical abstraction. This seems to realize adiaphorization in a purer form, yet it removes the very human intentionality that Bauman treated as the substrate for moral distancing. Does this mean that applying Bauman’s framework consistently predicts that removing human bureaucrats makes moral catastrophe more likely rather than less? And if so, does that strengthen his claim about modernity’s inherent dangers, or does it reveal a hidden dependence in his argument on specifically human forms of rationalization?
