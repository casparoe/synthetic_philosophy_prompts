I am preparing the closing exercise for my graduate seminar on intentionality and the philosophy of mind, and rather than assigning another analytical essay I want to conclude with a villanelle—nineteen lines of iambic pentameter, with its two refrains alternating and finally converging in a closing couplet—that stages the tension between Dennett’s distinction of original and derived intentionality and Searle’s insistence that syntax is never sufficient for semantics. The poem should be spoken in two voices: one belonging to a two-bitser—specifically, an autonomous navigational system that has been recursively corrected by feedback loops among its own outputs for so long that no human designer remains in the loop, so that its derivation is now stacked upon another artifact’s derivation with no conscious origin present; and the other belonging to that absent original intentional system, now removed, which initially fixed the parameters but can no longer intervene. Each refrain should carry one core commitment: the artifact’s, that its self-correction remains merely a deeper, more autonomous form of borrowed aboutness; and the original’s, that without intrinsic intentionality there is only the appearance of meaning, however complex the loop. The form itself must do philosophical work—the refrains should circle, miss each other, and meet in the final couplet without resolving whether the loop has bootstrapped a new kind of original intentionality or only confirmed that derivation can never close the gap Searle identifies. I want the poem technically precise enough that a specialist recognizes Dennett’s argument about stacked derivation and the possibility of an artifact deriving content from another artifact rather than from a mind, yet rendered with the compressed rhythm and wit of a metaphysical poem rather than the flatness of a textbook summary. Please do not include bibliographic references, footnotes, page citations, or lists of further reading; the model should work entirely from the conceptual architecture. The tension should remain genuinely open at the end, since that is precisely where our seminar discussion stalled—between the intuition that a sufficiently recursive loop changes the kind of thing we have, and the suspicion that we have only made the borrowing more invisible.
