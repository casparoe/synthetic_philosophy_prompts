You are a senior political philosopher specialising in comparative normative theory and public institutional design. Before drafting, spend a moment reasoning through the conceptual architecture of both camps, identifying where they genuinely disagree versus where they merely talk past each other.

Draft the joint statement that could emerge from a rigorous, year-long adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on multiculturalism versus assimilation—specifically, French republican laïcité versus Anglo-American multiculturalism.

Camp A defends strict republican assimilation: public institutions must practise procedural neutrality—symbolic and practical blindness to religious and cultural particularity—to secure civic equality; they regard group-differentiated recognition as a threat to social solidarity and a vehicle for communitarian fragmentation.

Camp B defends Anglo-American multiculturalism: formal neutrality systematically privileges dominant cultural norms; substantive equality requires explicit recognition, accommodation, and sometimes group-differentiated rights; assimilationist frameworks impose an unjust burden on minorities.

Your document must address not only the standard debate but also the following injected conceptual crux: the problem of 'hermeneutic asymmetry in public reason'—the claim that requiring minority practices to be translated into majority institutional languages (legal, administrative, pedagogical) before they can be recognised imposes a non-neutral cost that assimilationist frameworks systematically obscure, and that this translation is not a pre-political given but itself a site of distributive injustice.

Structure the deliverable exactly as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—statements both camps can sign—covering the formal characteristics of both frameworks, the empirical reality of plural societies, what needs explaining (e.g. whether identically applied rules produce unequal outcomes for minority practices), and what existing comparative institutional evidence rules out.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, provide the precise formulation that Camp A would accept for its own position and the precise formulation that Camp B would accept for its own position, phrased neutrally so that neither side feels misrepresented.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. Identify at least three: (a) whether procedural neutrality is genuinely neutral or implicitly encodes majority culture; (b) whether the hermeneutic cost of cultural translation counts as an injustice demanding institutional remedy rather than private tolerance; and (c) an empirical crux concerning the long-term effects of assimilationist versus accommodationist policies on social trust, minority civic participation, and inter-group conflict.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional scenario, comparative policy case, or piece of evidence that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome or correct interpretation will be.

5. State explicitly whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—if so, identify which portion and explain why—versus genuinely normative or empirical.

Write in formal British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific academic works by title, date, or author. Do not quote any real text verbatim; if you invoke concepts associated with named thinkers, paraphrase without quotation marks.

End with a single line beginning 'Summary:' followed by a one-sentence summary of the collaboration's core finding.
