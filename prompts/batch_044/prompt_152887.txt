I am preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on comparative political philosophy, and I keep returning to the 1989 Creil headscarf affair and the 2004 French law banning conspicuous religious symbols in public schools as a case that should illuminate the theoretical divide I am examining. The familiar framing pits French republican universalism—laïcité as assimilation into an undifferentiated civic body—against Anglo-American multiculturalism, which tends to treat group visibility and recognition as goods. But that framing feels too symmetrical, and I want to press it by introducing what I will call an asymmetry of visibility demands: the republican state insists on rendering religious identity invisible in schools, while multicultural theory often demands that minority identities be made visible and institutionally acknowledged. Analyze the Creil-to-2004 sequence through both lenses.

First, map the episode carefully: identify the relevant agents—the Ministry of Education, local administrators, the students expelled in 1989 and their families, teachers, and the broader republican public—and specify, for each theoretical framework, what options and payoffs they assign to these agents and what each framework prescribes they ought to do. Second, ask whether multiculturalism actually offers a coherent account of what should happen when a minority practice clashes with an institutional neutrality norm, or whether it implicitly smuggles in assimilationist assumptions about the boundary between public and private life. Third, say precisely what the republican framework misdescribes or misses—particularly regarding gender, class dynamics, and the lived experience of the expelled students—rather than treating those omissions as minor. Fourth, and running the analysis in the opposite direction, say what the 2004 law and its enforcement reveal about both theories: is the law a vindication of assimilationist logic, an anomaly that multiculturalism can explain but not justify, or evidence that both frameworks misidentify the state's actual interest in managing symbolic boundaries rather than securing either equality or recognition? Be disciplined about where each theory is silent, and do not force either to speak where it offers no clear guidance.
