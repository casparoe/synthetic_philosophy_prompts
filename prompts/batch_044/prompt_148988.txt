As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been tracking a philosopher's verdicts on whether mental states are luminous and whether the KK principle holds. His rulings so far: in a clear case of feeling cold, he grants that one knows one is cold; in a gradual warming scenario with no sharp boundary, he denies knowledge of being cold in the intermediate region; he accepts that if one knows one is in pain then one knows that one knows it; he rejects knowledge in a stopped-clock analog applied to introspection, where the belief is true but the monitoring mechanism is unreliable in nearby worlds; and he allows knowledge of tiredness formed by a generally reliable introspective method even when a hidden confounder is present, provided the belief is stable across nearby worlds. The new case is an artificial cognitive architecture that monitors its own activation patterns through a calibrated but noisy sensor: the system is genuinely in state S, the sensor reports confidence just below the standard knowledge threshold, yet the aggregate representation is accurate and remains so in all nearby worlds, and there is no false intermediate belief. Predict his verdict—would he call this luminous knowledge, reject it due to sub-threshold monitoring, or apply a modified KK principle? Identify the competing theories—strict luminosity, Williamson-style anti-luminosity with reliability conditions, or a graded-knowledge variant—that fit his earlier rulings, state which one you think he is running and why, and specify the further case—perhaps one involving a false intermediate monitoring belief in an otherwise accurate system—that would definitively separate them.
