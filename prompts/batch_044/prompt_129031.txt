I’m putting together prompts for a metaphysics seminar I run — about 12 grad students, one 90-minute session, and we’re deep in modal realism right now. I’m tired of the same old “Sam is a surgeon in W1 and a musician in W2” examples that everyone has seen in every intro text since like 1986, so I want an in-class game that makes them actually feel the pull between counterpart theory and transworld identity, not just hear me explain it afterwards. The idea should emerge from what they do with the cards and the votes, not from my debrief lecture.

Here’s the specific framing I want to inject, and I need you to build the activity around it: instead of asking whether one person exists across worlds, I want them to wrestle with multi-dimensional counterpart relations — the idea that similarity can be weighted differently along different axes (origin story, career path, moral character, institutional role) and that there might not be one unique counterpart in another world, but several competing ones depending on what you care about. So the game should force them to adjudicate real stakes — awarding a single lifetime prize, assigning blame for harm caused in another world, honoring a promise — where different weightings of similarity yield different answers about who counts as “the same” or “the counterpart.” Design the rules, materials, roles, and a payoff scheme that uses reputation points rather than real money. Include a timeline, the debrief that maps specific moments onto parts of the theory, and an honest note on what feature of counterpart theory this can’t reproduce (probably the ontological commitment to world-bound individuals?) that I’ll have to supply myself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — like if everyone fixates on origin and ignores the moral-character axis, or if the transworld-identity defenders just dig in and refuse to split cases. Please don’t ask for a bibliography or reading list; half the group doesn’t have libary access this week anyway, and I want something they can run without looking anything up.

One more thing: if my framing of multi-dimensional counterpart relations is confused — like if I’m mixing up Lewis’s context-sensitive counterpart relations with something that doesn’t actually exist in the literature, or if the activity as designed can’t genuinely make people feel the theoretical tension — tell me straight instead of politely going along with it. I’d rather have the pushback than a mess that sounds nice but falls apart in the room.
