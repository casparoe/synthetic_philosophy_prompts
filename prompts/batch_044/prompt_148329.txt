I am trying to determine whether breaking everyday conventions is ever a moral wrong, and whether visitors are bound by local practices they do not share. Below are my verdicts on concrete cases. I would like you to propose the principle, if there is one, that best captures the pattern; if none can save all of them, identify which verdict I should abandon and why. For each clause of your principle, indicate the case that motivates it. Then apply the principle to two further situations I have not listed, and be explicit about any verdict of mine it cannot accommodate.

Case 1: In Japan, leaving a cash tip at a traditional ryokan is widely regarded as insulting; a well-meaning foreign visitor leaves one after exceptional service. Verdict: she has acted clumsily but committed no moral wrong.

Case 2: In London, queueing for public transport is strictly linear; in Naples, boarding is typically a loose cluster near the doors. A Neapolitan visitor in London pushes gently forward in a Tube queue, as he would at home. Verdict: he has done something wrong, though not as wrong as malicious queue-jumping.

Case 3: Jaywalking in Berlin at 3 a.m. with no traffic is illegal and socially disapproved of; a tourist does it. Verdict: no moral wrong, though he risks social censure.

Case 4: A US restaurant adds a mandatory 20% service charge regardless of quality; the server was openly rude. The customer refuses to pay the charge and leaves a written explanation instead of cash. Verdict: permissible, despite breaking the convention that the charge is non-negotiable.

Case 5: In a small Swedish office, an unwritten rule holds that whoever takes the last slice of communal cake must replace it by the next morning. A new employee, unaware, eats it and does not replace it. Verdict: a social mistake, not a moral failure.

Case 6: In a Moroccan souk, bargaining is expected; a British tourist pays the first price quoted, disrupting the interaction norm but paying generously. Verdict: he has not acted wrongly, even though he violated local convention.

My tension is this: in Case 2 I judge the visitor somewhat blameworthy for not adapting, whereas in Cases 1, 3, and 6 I think violating or ignoring local convention is not morally wrong. Is the difference coordination-value, foreseeable harm, or knowledge of the convention?

Now consider an injected case not listed above. In some digital coworking spaces, a silent desk claim convention has emerged: one may reserve a desk for up to thirty minutes by placing a personal item upon it, but must never speak to assert the claim. A visitor, unaware of this norm, moves the item and occupies the desk. Apply your principle: is this closer to Case 5 (mere ignorance, no moral fault) or Case 2 (failure to adapt to a coordination mechanism)?

Also apply your principle to this further case: a visitor comes from a culture where sustained direct eye contact during conversation signals respect; in her host culture, such contact is considered aggressive. She maintains intense eye contact with local colleagues. Does she do wrong?

Please state your answer in British English, with precise conditions and honest acknowledgement of which of my verdicts must be revised if your principle is to remain coherent.
