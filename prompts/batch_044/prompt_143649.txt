I'm delivering a seminar paper next Tuesday to our philosophy-of-science colloquium on a thesis about ontology and permanently sealed information. My argument is that standard accounts of ontological commitment—whether Quinean indispensability arguments or structural-realist inferences to the best explanation—treat all empirically inaccessible entities uniformly, but they should instead distinguish between entities that are merely hidden in practice and those that lie behind permanent informational barriers in principle. I focus on cosmological regions beyond the event horizon: since no causal signal or predictive embedding can ever reach us from them, I claim they should be assigned an indeterminate ontological status, neither affirmed as real nor eliminated as unreal, because the identification conditions required for ontological commitment—causal interaction, structural isomorphism, or predictive integration—are permanently unsatisfiable. A colleague in physics warned me this sounds like verificationism in cosmological clothing, and I want to be ready for the pushback.

The audience is about fourteen people: mostly structural realists and scientific realists for whom predictive success is the sole serious guide to ontology; two philosophers of physics who study cosmological horizons and are allergic to metaphysical overreach that outruns physical theory; and one metaphysician who holds a strict Quinean view that existence is nothing over and above quantification in our best-regimented theory. They care about empirical adequacy, Occam's razor, and the boundary between epistemology and metaphysics, and they will press hard on whether my notion of "permanent inaccessibility" is physically well-defined or just an epistemic gloss disguised as ontology.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific audience—not generic anti-realist talking points, but the precise objections a structural realist, a cosmological physicist, and a Quinean metaphysician would raise—ordered by how likely and damaging they are. For each, phrase it in their vocabulary without softening, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and explicitly note where that reply is weakest or where I am forced to concede ground. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks—likely something about whether my view makes ontological status depend on our contingent spatiotemporal location, or whether it collapses into epistemic relativism about distant regions—identify it precisely, and advise me on what part of it I should concede in the moment without losing the core thesis. Finally, tell me what I should concede up front in my opening remarks to defuse the worst damage before it starts.
