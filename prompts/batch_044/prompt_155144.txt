I'm putting together my profile for a moral-progress reading group and want a consistency check before I present. Roughly where I've landed:

(a) Moral realism is right — there are mind-independant facts about who has moral status, and they're knowable in principle.
(b) The expanding-circle narrative is basically correct: we've genuinely widened moral concern over time, and that's progress, not just shifting fashion.
(c) But the driver isn't empathy or rational insight into shared sentience; it's mainly instituional redesign that lowers the cost of including outsiders. When exclusion gets expensive — legally, economically, socially — the circle widens, whether or not anyone has had a moral epiphany.
(d) So progress is path-dependant and contingent: different tech or economic conditions could have left us with a smaller or differently shaped circle.
(e) Even so, I treat reflective equilibrium with our current wider judgments as my best guide to those objective moral facts.
(f) I also think care ethics (Noddings-style) gets interpersonal morality mostly right, but it doesn't scale to politics because care is non-fungable and particularist.
(g) Finally, I reject linear or inevitable progress; it's just local adaptation to cost structures.

My worry is that (c) and (d) undermine (a) and (e). If our circle is mostly shaped by contingent instituional costs, why should our current judgments track mind-independant moral truths? And is there a hidden tension between (f) and (b) — does care's particularism clash with the universalism of the expanding circle? I suspect one of these conflicts is only apparent, but I'm not sure which.

Please audit this package: go through the pairs, say which are in real tension and which only look like it, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. Don't treat them all as equally bad.

At the very end, put a line that starts with 'Verdict:' followed by the single commitment you'd tell me to weaken or drop for the cleanest overall view, in quotes.
