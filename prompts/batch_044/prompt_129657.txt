For an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students meeting for 80 minutes, design a runnable in-class simulation—call it "The Ledger of Unseen Intentions"—that makes participants feel the internal tension of karma as a theory of moral desert. The tension to stage is this: if moral desert tracks hidden mental qualities (intention, volition, or mental purity) rather than observable outcomes, but those mental states are epistemically opaque to all observers, then any institutional attempt to administer strictly proportional desert must either violate proportionality or rely on noisy observable proxies that corrupt the very standard they are meant to serve.

Specify the following. (1) Setup and roles: each participant draws one public "Observable Act" card (for example, returning lost property, withholding aid, or speaking harshly) and one secret "Mental State" card (for example, compassionate intent, malicious intent, or indifferent habit) that determines a true desert score between -10 and +10; four rotating participants serve as Administrators who must assign each agent a karmic score using only the public card and, in Round 2, one proxy question drawn from a deck with printed false-positive and false-negative rates (for example, "Did the agent appear calm?" correlates imperfectly with compassionate intent). (2) Rules for two rounds of about 15 minutes each, with a visible Ledger board tracking assigned scores versus revealed true scores, and clear instructions for how Administrators record allocations and how Agents reveal mental states only after scoring. (3) A non-monetary payoff scheme—reputation and merit tokens—that gives Administrators an incentive to minimize allocation error and gives Agents an incentive to care whether their assigned score matches their hidden true desert; include how tokens convert into a small collective reward (for example, the group with the best combined accuracy and justice-satisfaction score chooses the seminar's closing discussion question) so that stakes feel real but remain symbolic. (4) A debrief that maps Round 1 onto outcome-based desert corruption, Round 2 onto the proxy problem and predictable gaming behavior, and the overall gap onto the concept of karmic opacity; include an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first run (Administrators will conflate observable benefit with good intent; Agents will select answers that maximize token gain rather than reveal mental state) and how the facilitator should correct course in real time without breaking the simulation's framing. (5) An explicit, frank note on which feature of classical karma theory the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically the non-institutional, transpersonal causal mechanism of karmic fruition across lifetimes—and exactly what verbal framing the facilitator must supply after the activity to supply that missing element.

Make the rules, card descriptions, and token mechanics concrete enough that a facilitator could run the session without consulting outside sources. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on obscure technical terminology or specific named texts that a model could not generate from general training knowledge.
