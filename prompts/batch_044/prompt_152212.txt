I'm currently writing an essay for my political philosophy seminar on the taxonomy of liberty, and I've become stuck on a recurring pattern that doesn't map cleanly onto the standard distinctions. Negative liberty concerns the absence of external obstacles, and republican liberty concerns the absence of arbitrary domination by another agent; yet I keep encountering cases—especially in discussions of linguistic or epistemic environments—where a person's range of action is constrained not by anyone's direct interference or by the power of a master, but by a distributed background structure that renders certain alternatives literally unthinkable without anyone having intended to suppress them. For example, if a technical vocabulary is systematically absent from public education, citizens may never form preferences that depend on that vocabulary, even though no one is preventing them from learning it and no particular agent controls the curriculum. This feels different from false consciousness or ideological hegemony, since there is no intentional deception or dominant class enforcing the absence; it is closer to a structural curating of the option set itself. Is there an established concept—perhaps a subtype of positive liberty, or something like structural non-liberty—that captures this specific mechanism? If not, how should one distinguish it precisely from both negative and republican unfreedom, and does it pose a distinct normative problem of its own?
