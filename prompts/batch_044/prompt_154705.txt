I'm a theoretical physics PhD student working on quantum critical phenomena in strongly correlated lattice systems. Over the past year I have been trying to organize the parameter space of a particular Hamiltonian, and in a moment of desperation I borrowed a classification scheme from pure algebraic geometry—specifically, the resolution of singularities in a class of symplectic varieties that mathematicians developed entirely for internal consistency reasons, with no physical application in mind. I mapped my Hamiltonian onto this framework mostly because the formal structure was elegant and I needed an organizing principle for noisy experimental data. To my surprise, the geometric singularities in the classification lined up precisely with critical points in my model, including one at a parameter value that had not been experimentally resolved. A collaborator later measured the system at that point and confirmed an anomalous transition that existing physical arguments had not predicted.

My advisor argues that this proves nothing philosophically interesting: I selected the mathematical framework because it was flexible enough to accommodate my data, and I would have discarded it had the fit been poor. The correspondence, he says, is just retrofitting disguised as discovery. But I am troubled by this dismissal. The mathematicians who constructed the classification were not solving physical problems; they were resolving questions about fiber structures that arose purely within the formal system. I did not design my Hamiltonian to match their results—I imported their structure wholesale, and it predicted a physical feature I had not anticipated.

I am trying to determine whether my experience reveals a genuine philosophical puzzle about the relationship between abstract formal structures and physical reality, or whether it is fully explained by selection bias and post-hoc rationalization. Is there a meaningful distinction between mathematics developed independently of physics and then later applied, versus mathematics that a physicist deliberately imports? If the puzzle concerns why reality respects constraints that emerge purely from internal mathematical consistency, then my deliberate use of those constraints should not eliminate the strangeness—after all, I had no independent physical reason to expect that particular singular fiber to manifest in a crystal lattice.

Could you help me identify where my reasoning might be slipping? In particular, I wonder whether I am conflating predictive success with ontological commitment: does the fact that this geometric framework predicts experimental outcomes imply that those geometric structures are literally present in the material, or is there a more deflationary account that preserves the predictive power without granting mathematics a constitutive role in nature? I want to avoid the answer that mathematics is merely a descriptive language, since that seems to miss why this particular language—developed for purely formal reasons—should carry any predictive weight at all.
