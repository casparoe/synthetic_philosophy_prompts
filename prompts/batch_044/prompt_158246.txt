I'm preparing a position paper for a graduate seminar on the political ontology of famine—specifically whether mass starvation should be classified as a natural disaster or as a political crime, using Amartya Sen's entitlement theory as the central lens. Before presenting, I want an internal consistency audit of where I've landed across the canonical cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of 1845–52, and the Great Leap Forward of 1959–61. I've tried to move past the standard textbook summary and have incorporated some of the more granular recent debates about local supply data and the distinction between direct and exchange entitlements, but I'm unsure whether my judgments on explanation and culpability still hang together.

Here are the commitments I currently hold. First, I accept Sen's entitlement approach in its strongest form: famine mortality is always and only the result of collapsed direct or exchange entitlements, and explanations that invoke aggregate food availability decline (FAD) are conceptually mistaken rather than merely incomplete. Second, I apply this strictly to Bengal in 1943: rice was physically available, but war-time procurement, price inflation, and colonial administrative failure destroyed the exchange entitlements of rural laborers and fishermen, so specific British colonial administrators bear direct personal moral culpability for failing to act on reports they possessed. Third, I regard the Irish famine as fundamentally a natural biological catastrophe—the Phytophthora blight destroyed a mono-crop subsistence base—so nature was the primary cause; the impoverished Irish tenants lacked meaningful exchange entitlements from the outset, which means there was no entitlement collapse in Sen's specific sense, only the destruction of direct subsistence entitlements by biological shock. Fourth, I see the Great Leap Forward as largely confirming Sen: provincial procurement quotas, falsified harvest statistics, and the denial of relief to politically labeled regions destroyed rural entitlements despite adequate aggregate reserves in some years, making it structurally analogous to Bengal. Fifth, however, I think recent granular county-level grain-balance studies for parts of Sichuan and Anhui show per capita food availability genuinely falling below subsistence levels in 1959–60, which means the pure entitlement account is insufficient for the Leap and must be hybridized with localized supply collapse. Sixth, I hold that moral culpability requires both knowledge of risk and autonomous institutional capacity to act otherwise; therefore, while I assign individual blame to specific British officials, I do not assign comparable personal blame to local Chinese cadres, who operated under extreme coercion and information asymmetry, though I do hold the Maoist state apparatus collectively guilty. Seventh, I maintain that democratic institutions with a free press make famine impossible regardless of poverty or drought, because they protect information flow and entitlement security; India's post-1947 absence of famine-level mortality is therefore conclusive proof that democracy is famine-proof. Eighth, I believe all three cases should be analyzed through the same structural entitlement framework regardless of historical era, but that moral blame must be calibrated to the administrative and informational capacities available to the regime in question.

Please audit this package. Go through the commitments pair by pair and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which ones only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be—whether weakening one thesis, adding a qualification, or abandoning a commitment altogether—and state what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me which single revision, if I made only one, would clean up the most problems at once, and whether that revision is worth the price given what I would have to surrender.
