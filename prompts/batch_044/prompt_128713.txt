I've been trying to square the Epicurean argument—that death is not a harm because the dead do not exist to experience it, together with Lucretius's symmetry claim about prenatal non-existence—with the rationalist intuition that extending healthy lifespan is unambiguously valuable. It seems to me that if we take the no-subject condition seriously, then comparing an 80-year life to a 40-year life involves the same metaphysical mistake: in the shorter world, the missing decades are not absent for anyone, since the subject does not persist into that gap any more than the dead persist after death. So treating longevity as a good for me looks like a category error, not just an emotional bias. Yet frameworks like QALY maximization take comparative lifespan value for granted. Does the Epicurean ontology genuinely undermine deprivation-based reasoning about lifespan, forcing us to treat death and premature death as equally non-bad? Or is there a hidden premise—perhaps about identity across counterfactual worlds—that saves comparative judgments without smuggling in a persisting subject?
