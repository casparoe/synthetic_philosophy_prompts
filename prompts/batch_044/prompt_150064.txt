I was recently discussing Pascal's wager in a philosophy of religion study group, and we kept returning to the standard classroom version: a binary choice between belief in God and disbelief, driven by an infinite-utility payoff matrix. I want to push past that formulation and explore what happens when the decision framework is made continuous. Specifically, I am looking for an example of a conceptual issue that emerges only when an agent distributes credence across multiple theological hypotheses—classical theism, deism, a non-personal pantheistic framework, and naturalism—rather than treating belief as an on-off switch directed at a single deity.

Please provide a concrete illustration of the tension that arises in this gradient setting. To ground it, imagine a rational agent who assigns partial credences to each of those four positions and is considering a costly ritual practice that yields substantial utility if either classical theism or deism is true, but offers no benefit under pantheism or naturalism. Crucially, the ritual also carries an epistemic cost: it activates doxastic commitment mechanisms—habit formation, social reinforcement, and affective investment—that strengthen her confidence in the first two hypotheses in ways she values for non-instrumental reasons, yet she worries that this distorts her responsiveness to future evidence. The conceptual issue should not simply repeat familiar objections about infinite utilities being irrational or about the problem of other religions. Instead, I want it to highlight a structural problem specific to the continuous-credence version: something about how expected-utility reasoning interacts with the voluntariness of belief, or with the ethics of self-manipulation, when the payoff landscape is multi-peaked and the agent's epistemic values are in partial conflict with her practical ones. Keep the example self-contained—no bibliographic references or reading lists needed—and make it precise enough that someone unfamiliar with decision theory can still grasp the conceptual knot.
