I have been thinking about whether actual infinities can exist, though I have no formal background in mathematics or philosophy. I find Hilbert's hotel persuasive as a demonstration of strange properties, but only within an imagined system of rules; it does not convince me that an actually infinite collection of rooms or guests could exist in reality. Similarly, I understand Cantor's argument to show that, if one assumes a completed list of real numbers, a new number can always be constructed that is not on it. From this I have formed the view that both examples depend on treating an unfinished process as if it were a finished totality. I have tried to develop a more precise version of this: rather than saying infinity is impossible, I propose that every collection is either finite or can be extended by one more element, but never reaches a state of being actually infinite. On this view, Hilbert's hotel never has infinitely many rooms at once; it only has a rule for adding more, and Cantor's argument never applies because there is no completed list to diagonalise against. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Not just the standard objection that mathematics works fine with actual infinities, but which specific observations, formal results, or conceptual difficulties would show that my potentialist account collapses into contradiction or fails to capture something essential. Would a defender of this view have to concede that Cantor's proof demonstrates an unavoidable feature of any system that allows indefinite extension? Are there results in logic or physics that depend essentially on completed infinities rather than on rules for indefinite extension? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualifications to the point of being unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a weakness for a view of this kind.
