I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether “left” and “right” name anything real. I take the view that the distinction is a durable, cross-cultural cleavage rooted in contrasting moral psychologies—particularly the weighting of care and fairness against authority, loyalty, and sanctity—and in structurally opposed interests regarding hierarchy and redistribution. My friend takes the opposing view: that left and right are historically contingent framing devices, held together not by shared deep values but by strategic elite coordination and path-dependent institutional lock-in; the apparent coherence is an artefact of party systems and media narratives, and in many contexts the axis collapses or rotates beyond recognition.

We want to write the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps—call them the Realist and the Nominalist—would produce. Please draft the document both could sign, containing the following, in formal, polished British English:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: what the left-right distinction actually denotes in formal comparative-politics terms (e.g., its relation to economic equality and social traditionalism); the historical evidence about its stability and variation since the French Revolution; what phenomena any adequate account must explain (e.g., the persistence of the labels across regime changes, the occasional axis rotation in post-communist or post-colonial party systems, the clustering of policy preferences within coalitions); and the limits of current empirical methods for measuring it.

2. A disagreements table. For each remaining dispute, state the Realist’s position in wording the Realist would accept, and the Nominalist’s position in wording the Nominalist would accept. Do not water down either side.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one party. I suspect at least two are: (a) whether naturalness or tradition has intrinsic normative weight in holding coalitions together, versus being a post-hoc rationalisation of strategic interest; and (b) how far ecological and predictive models of coalition behaviour can be trusted when they assume stable preference orderings across generations. But you should identify others if they emerge.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete case, comparative pattern, or piece of argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction written down in advance. One case should be the behaviour of environmental-policy coalitions in Nordic social-democratic systems compared with Anglo-Saxon liberal democracies—specifically, whether green-left alliances hold together because of shared moral-psychological foundations or because of contingent institutional incentives. Another should address axis rotation: for instance, whether the economic-cultural alignment in Eastern European post-communist party systems tracks a universal moral-foundations structure or reflects locally contingent path dependence.

5. Explicitly flag any part of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive—e.g., disagreements about the definition of “real” that dissolve once operationalised—and say why.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works; we are interested in the conceptual architecture, not in scholarly attribution. Do not quote any real text verbatim unless you have fetched the exact passage; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Keep the prompt self-contained, so that a model without web access can engage with it fully.
