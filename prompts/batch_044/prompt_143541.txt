In philosophical discussions of the Principal Principle — the idea that rational credence should align with objective chance unless screened off by admissible evidence — people often reach for a meteorological analogy: deference to chance is like taking a weather forecaster at her word, so if she reports a thirty-percent chance of rain you should set your belief to thirty percent, updating only on private umbrella-observations she does not share. I want that analogy audited, but with a twist that the standard presentation suppresses: treat the forecaster not as a solitary expert but as a prediction-market price that aggregates traders, some of whom may hold the very private evidence you are supposed to exclude, and treat your own credence as one trader-position rather than an outside observer. Give the correspondence table — the meteorological model, the traders, the aggregated price, the actual weather, your direct observation, the screening-off condition — then show precisely where the mapping breaks. Does the market mechanism smuggle in a social-aggregation feature that has no counterpart in the original constraint? Does the conclusion that one should "just defer to the number" survive once we see that the analogy imports mechanisms for combining evidence that the principle explicitly forbids you to use? If the forecaster image collapses under that pressure, propose a cleaner substitute — say, deference to a physical randomizer whose seed is hidden from you — and say whether it repairs the disanalogy or merely relocates the problem of admissible evidence.
