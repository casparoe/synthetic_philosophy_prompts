I have been working through the debate over whether dreams are experiences, particularly Malcolm’s verificationist challenge and Dennett’s counter that dream reports are frequently narrative constructions formed upon waking rather than recollections of occurring mental events. In reading around the literature, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that seems to sit above the specific positions, and I am wondering whether it has an established name.

The pattern works like this. A philosopher wishes to defend the claim that dreaming involves genuine experiential consciousness, not merely the later memory of one. They observe that upon waking we report our dreams using first-person present-tense constructions — “I was running through a garden” — that grammatically mirror waking perceptual reports. From this linguistic parallel they infer that there must have been an underlying experiential state during sleep that the report tracks, however imperfectly. An opponent — often working in a Dennett-style framework — responds that the grammatical form is misleading: the report is a post-hoc fabrication, and there is no reason to think it tracks anything actually occurring during the sleep period. At this point the defender often shifts ground: they concede that the report may be reconstructed, but argue that reconstruction requires some experiential “raw material” or trace to work with, so the very existence of the report still implies an underlying experience. The opponent then replies that such a trace could be entirely non-experiential — perhaps a sub-personal consolidation process or a confabulated schema — and that invoking an experiential trace merely begs the question.

What strikes me is not the disagreement about dreams themselves, but the way the debate stalls over the term “experience”. One side uses it to mean phenomenal consciousness occurring at the time of sleeping; the other uses it to mean any mental state available to first-person retrospective access, whether or not it involved phenomenal consciousness at the time. Because neither side marks this distinction explicitly, the argument cycles repeatedly through evidence about report grammar, about reconstruction mechanisms, and about the ontology of mental traces, without ever fixing what would count as resolving the dispute.

Is there an established concept for this kind of stalling pattern — specifically, a debate about a mental phenomenon that becomes trapped between an “occurrence” reading and a “retrospective accessibility” reading of a key term? And is there a particular name for the argumentative move that defends experiential status by appealing to the grammatical form of waking reports, treating linguistic parallel as ontological evidence?

If there is no single established term, I should value an attempt to define and bound the phenomenon precisely: what it is, what distinguishes it from ordinary ambiguity or equivocation, and whether it applies to other debates in philosophy of mind (for instance, discussions of blindsight or unconscious perception). I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to particular authors; I want to understand the carving — where the edges of this pattern lie, what it is not, and what it ought to be called if it lacks a name.
