I facilitate a workshop for philosophy and computer science graduate students — sixteen participants, seventy-five minutes — on the conceptual problem of defining low-impact AI without a full specification of value, and I want an in-class simulation rather than a lecture that lets participants feel why defining side effects through counterfactual trajectory comparison collapses unless one already knows which variables in the world are value-relevant. Design a structured activity with real roles and small-stakes choices: divide participants into three groups representing a system designer team, an evaluation board, and affected stakeholders, and give the designer team a physical task such as rearranging objects in a shared space to maximize accessibility, using only a hidden rulebook that lists permitted physical actions but deliberately omits any definition of harm, benefit, or protected status. The evaluation board must score the designers’ proposal using a formal impact metric defined as the sum of squared deviations from a baseline trajectory of the space over a ten-minute horizon, but they are not told that some objects in the room carry hidden sentimental or cultural value with no functional role, while others are functionally essential but easily replaceable. After the designers act and the board computes and announces scores, reveal that the baseline trajectory was constructed using unstated value judgments about which objects should remain untouched and that the causal influence diagram implicitly treats only physically moved objects as nodes, excluding relational and symbolic harms. Use a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, giving the designers incentives to minimize deviation scores and giving stakeholders hidden cards that reveal value-laden information only after scoring is finalized, so that stakeholders experience the frustration of having their values ignored by a metric that seemed neutral. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, the physical materials needed, rules governing what information is visible in each phase, and instructions for the facilitator on when to reveal the hidden value map. Write the entire response in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — including a debrief section that maps each phase of the simulation onto a specific conceptual failure mode: the baseline-selection problem, the hidden-node problem in causal influence diagrams, and the collapse of avoid side effects into optimize for unstated values. The debrief should also include an honest admission of which feature of the conceptual problem the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce — for instance, the infinite-dimensionality and context-dependence of real-world value — and should specify exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally to cover that gap. Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time this is run — such as stakeholders refusing to wait until after scoring to protest, or designers gaming the metric by freezing all motion — and explain how to correct it without breaking the simulation’s logic.
