I serve, in my capacity as a novelist and as a member of the advisory panel for the National Museum of Social History, where I have been asked to draft the brief opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting of the curatorial ethics committee. The museum is finalising its permanent programme, "The Measure of Harm: Violence in the Long Twentieth Century", and the trustees require a definitive recommendation by the month's end on how we ought to present the central statistical claim. Our research office reports that intentional violent mortality—homicide, state-sanctioned execution, battle death, and targeted terror—has declined markedly on a per-capita basis since 1945, and that this trajectory represents an unambiguous historical improvement. A dissenting group of consultants, however, urges us to reject this framing. They argue that counting only direct kinetic harm obscures what they call "structural exposure mortality"—premature deaths arising from the collapse of sanitation, food distribution, and medical infrastructure in conflict zones, where no individual aggressor can be named. When these indirect deaths are included, and when the figures are presented in absolute terms rather than rates, adjusted by what the consultants term the "baseline demographic transition weighting", the downward curve either flattens or reverses across several regions. Beneath this empirical disagreement lies a philosophical dispute that the board cannot avoid. One faction holds that "violence" is essentially contested: to choose a metric—per-capita agentive harm versus absolute systemic exposure—is already to embed a normative judgement about which sufferings belong in the moral ledger, and therefore there is no single fact of the matter about whether the world is "becoming less violent". The opposing faction maintains that, although conventions are necessary, once an operational definition is fixed—say, intentional physical harm inflicted by an identifiable agent per unit of living population—there is an objective answer, and that to refuse a headline claim is not intellectual humility but epistemic evasion dressed in relativism. The committee must not deliver an academic treatise; we need a concrete display policy. Should the central gallery present the per-capita decline as established fact, present both metrics in parallel without adjudication, or withhold a headline claim entirely and frame the exhibition as an inquiry into measurement itself? I want the memo to recommend one of these courses with a clear rationale, propose a specific timeline for implementation—ideally before the trustees' vote—and to treat with complete seriousness the strongest consideration on the losing side, particularly the charge that any headline claim smuggles in a contested ontology of harm. Please write your response in American English, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write a formal letter.
