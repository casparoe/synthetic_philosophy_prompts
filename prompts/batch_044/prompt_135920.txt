I was arguing with my study group last week about the Pasadena paradox — the two-player game where every pure strategy leads to an expected payoff that isn't just infinite but genuinely undefined, so standard expected-utility theory doesn't even apply. One friend said it's just a mathematical curiosity, like dividing by zero in arithmetic: you restrict the domain of rational-choice theory and move on. Another said that misses the point, because if rationality requires maximizing expected value, and expectation isn't defined here, then the theory gives no guidance at all, which means either rationality is silent in these cases or we need a completely different normative framework. A third person kept insisting we should treat undefined expectation like ambiguity aversion in Ellsberg-style setups — that is, people should have a distinct attitude toward "not knowing the expectation" versus knowing it's bad — but nobody could agree on whether that was importing psychology into a formal problem or fixing it.

The debate's been going in circles for what feels like years in the literature, and I can't tell if people are actually disagreeing or just talking past each other. Is it a verbal dispute about what "rational" means? A hidden split between descriptive and normative aims? Or are the formalists and the philosophers literally answering different questions — one about consistency of the math, the other about practical decision-making — under the same heading? And there's this weird variant I cooked up: what if both players can pay a small cost to "exit" into a well-defined subgame, but exiting changes the payoff structure so that the undefined region becomes reachable only by accident? Does that change anything, or does it just push the undefinedness around?

Can you diagnose what kind of stall this actually is? I want to know whether the sides differ on facts, on standards of evidence, on which cases count as data for a theory of rationality, or on bedrock intuitions that can't be argued for. And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change in how we frame the question — or should we just accept that no progress is possible here and say why? Don't just survey positions; tell me what's really going on structurally.
