I'm leading a single ninety-minute seminar for a reading group of museum-curator and art-history graduate students with no formal philosophy training, though they live inside debates about digital restoration, forgery, and whether a reconstructed object retains its original identity. I want to teach them how intuition pumps actually work—not as colorful illustrations, but as rhetorical devices that covertly import modal commitments (about conceivability, possibility, and identity conditions) while presenting themselves as neutral descriptions. Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order: which component has to be in place before the next can land, and why, with special attention to why they must understand the distinction between a scenario's narrative coherence and its modal legitimacy before they can see the pump's hidden work. Second, choose the one carrying example or pump that should bear the whole session; it should come from their world—say, a pump about whether a painting reconstructed pixel-by-pixel from ashes by an AI trained on the artist's other works counts as the original work—rather than from standard philosophy-of-mind literature like Mary's room or the Chinese Room, and you must specify exactly what that example must contain and what it must lack (for instance, whether it should include emotional attachment to the original canvas) to do the job. Third, map the misconceptions: the ones they will bring from their professional context (for example, that vivid visualization equals possibility, or that the pump's conclusion is what matters rather than its smuggled premise about what can be identically preserved) and the ones they will form during the session, with the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Fourth, design one check for understanding at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the mechanism—how to detect hidden modal loading—rather than whether they can retell the example. Fifth, say explicitly what standard philosophical machinery should be refused at this level—modal logic notation, specific theories of mental representation, bibliographic debates about particular thinkers—and state why. Take a committed stance throughout: pick one sequence and defend it, choose one version of the carrying example and argue for its features, specify the misconceptions and intervention points without offering a menu of equally valid alternatives, and be direct about what you are excluding. Do not ask for readings, citations, or further texts.
