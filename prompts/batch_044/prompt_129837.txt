I am designing the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and I need a single essay prompt that forces students to do conceptual work rather than recite the standard Malthusian narrative about geometric population growth being outstripped by arithmetic food production and then falsified by industrial agricultural technology. The exam is open-notes but strictly offline—no web access—so the prompt must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and it should avoid leaning on hyper-obscure secondary literature that a well-prepared student could not be expected to recall. Instead, inject a conceptual distinction that is not part of the usual textbook summary: distinguish three distinct failure modes for a long-run predictive theory—parameter error (the quantitative ratios or constants were wrong), mechanism collapse (the causal dynamics themselves do not hold under the conditions specified), and mechanism preemption (the dynamics are structurally sound but are systematically blocked or preempted by an exogenous factor that the theory treats as contingent rather than impossible). The essay should ask students to evaluate whether Malthus’s theory is best interpreted as suffering from parameter error, mechanism collapse, or mechanism preemption, and then to use that classification to assess whether “right but early” is a coherent epistemic category or merely a post-hoc rescue strategy. They should specifically address the unfalsifiability worry: if any indefinite delay in the predicted crisis can be interpreted as preemption rather than falsification, then the “right but early” rescue risks being immune to evidence. Frame the question for a 90-minute essay of approximately 800 to 1000 words.

Please provide: (a) the exam question itself, with clear instructions and the conceptual framing embedded so students cannot answer by summary alone; (b) a model answer of roughly 650 to 750 words that executes the specific moves required for top marks—namely, articulating the three failure modes with precision, applying them to Malthus without merely recounting the Essay on the Principle of Population, arguing that mechanism preemption is the most defensible interpretation only if paired with a counterfactual stability condition and explicit domain restriction, and answering the unfalsifiability objection by showing what evidential pattern would genuinely distinguish preemption from collapse; and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A-range answer (17–20) from a B-range answer (13–16) by naming exactly which conceptual moves must be present. Allocate points approximately as follows: correct framing of the three failure modes (4 points), application to Malthus that goes beyond narrative summary (5 points), defense of “right but early” as coherent only under specific conditions (5 points), direct engagement with the unfalsifiability objection (4 points), and clarity and argumentative structure (2 points). For each point band, include a one-line note on the characteristic failure—e.g., for the B range, listing the three modes without applying them to the Malthusian mechanism, or treating mechanism preemption as obviously correct without defending it against the charge that it renders the theory immune to refutation.
