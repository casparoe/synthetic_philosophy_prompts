I am a high school student preparing an independent essay on ancient economic thought, and I have been working through the opening books of Aristotle's Politics in an older English translation. There, Aristotle distinguishes household management, which acquires goods for use, from the art of acquiring wealth through exchange. The first, he says, is natural and limited by the needs of the household; the second is unnatural and unlimited, since it treats money as the end rather than the means. He gives the example of shoes, which are made to be worn, yet if they are produced only to be sold for coin the craft is turned away from its proper purpose. He also describes interest as the most hateful form of this second art, because coin—unlike seeds or livestock—is barren and begets nothing.

Recently I encountered a comparison, unfamiliar outside niche discussions linking philosophy to decentralized finance, to staking protocols in which a digital token is locked up not to facilitate trade in consumable goods but solely to mint more of the same token, with no underlying usable product ever entering the cycle. I am trying to understand whether Aristotle's critique depends on the presence of some real, consumable thing being diverted from its natural use—such as grain or footwear—or whether an exchange loop that never touches a consumable good at all, in which token generates token, falls under the same condemnation or represents a different error altogether. Does the concept of use collapse when there is nothing intended for use, and is the barrenness of money a claim about its failure to reproduce naturally, or about its separation from the productive processes that create genuine goods?
