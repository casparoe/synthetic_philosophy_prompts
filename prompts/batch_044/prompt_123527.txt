I am preparing a small seminar on logic and narrative form, and I keep returning to a strange tension within Lewis Carroll's own work. In his dialogue featuring Achilles and the Tortoise, Carroll stages the classic regress about justifying deduction: any attempt to compel acceptance of a logical rule seems to require a further rule, leaving the application of Modus Ponens either circular or endlessly deferred. Meanwhile, in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Alice repeatedly appeals to familiar rules—of arithmetic, grammar, etiquette, and causality—only to find them suspended, inverted, or enforced by nothing more than arbitrary authority, whether from the Queen, the Mad Hatter, or the very structure of the trial. I want to read Wonderland through the lens of that logical paradox. Does the novel dramatize the tortoise's regress by showing that every rule Alice invokes is either powerless or backed only by another premise she has not endorsed? Or does the nonsense operate on a different register altogether—perhaps as a pragmatic game governed by social convention or narrative coherence rather than by formal derivation?

In particular, I am drawn to scenes where reasoning feels simultaneously necessary and futile: the Cheshire Cat's vanishing logic, the Hatter's circular questioning, and the trial's reversal of verdict and sentence. I would like a reading that takes both the philosophical problem—how deduction is justified, and whether the regress can be halted by distinguishing logical rules from norms of reasoning practice—and the literary details seriously, without reducing Carroll's fiction to a mere illustration. Where does the philosophical lens illuminate a scene's structure, and where does it distort the emotional logic of Alice's disorientation or the particular humor of the absurdity? I also wonder whether Wonderland ultimately sides with the tortoise, suggesting that deduction is unjustified yet unavoidable, or whether it hints at an alternative source of authority for rules, rooted perhaps in communal practice or in the internal consistency of a story rather than in logical form. I want an honest critical assessment: if the comparison holds, what does the novel contribute to the philosophy of logic that the abstract argument alone misses?
