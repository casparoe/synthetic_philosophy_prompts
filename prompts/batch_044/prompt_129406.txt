I serve on the governance board of a thirty-person co-housing community that has recently adopted a shared AI system to administer our collective life—energy budgets, common-space scheduling, and mediation of disagreements that touch on how we ought to live (for example, whether shared kitchens should accommodate religious dietary practices, or how aggressively to trade personal comfort for carbon reduction). We all endorsed the principle that the AI should be “aligned with our values,” yet we have fractured into three incompatible interpretations of that mandate. One group insists on direct preference aggregation: each resident’s desires count equally, and the system should optimize for whatever weighted compromise maximizes overall satisfaction across the community. A second group contends that the AI must remain strictly value-neutral; it should enforce only rules that every resident has explicitly consented to, and otherwise refrain from value-laden optimization altogether, even at the cost of collective paralysis. A third faction—led by someone with a background in participatory urban planning—proposes an experience-weighted model: on any given issue, residents who have actually lived through analogous trade-offs in prior communities should have their preferences counted more heavily, on the grounds that alignment should track not mere opinion but informed judgment.

The third view strikes many of us as pragmatically appealing, yet I feel a principled resistance to it that I cannot fully articulate, and I am unsure whether the first two positions are genuinely as distinct as they appear. Is there a hidden contradiction in treating “our values” as something that can be summed into a collective score, and does the neutrality proposal secretly smuggle in its own conception of the good life by privileging procedural consent over substantive outcomes? More specifically, does experience-weighting introduce a hierarchy of voice that undermines the egalitarian premise shared by the other camps, or can it be reconciled with them? I want to understand the philosophical structure of this disagreement—whether aggregation and neutrality are truly opposing frameworks or two sides of the same mistake—and to be told which commitments must be abandoned, and at what cost, in order to avoid incoherence in how we govern this system.
