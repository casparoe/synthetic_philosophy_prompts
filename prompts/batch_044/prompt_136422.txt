I'm writing a feature on rationality loops in AI and my editor's breathing down my neck. A philosopher friend sent me this argument that self-trust collapses into believing literally everything:

(1) A self-trusting agent should accept: if I rationally come to believe p, then p is true.
(2) Löb's theorem shows that in any system strong enough to talk about it's own proofs, proving 'if I prove p then p' lets you prove p outright.
(3) So the agent can run that construction for any proposition p and get a formal proof of p.
(4) By (1), it should then actually believe every p.
(5) But an agent that believes every p is broken, not rational. So self-trust is impossible?

I feel like step (3) sneaks in something — maybe the agent can't apply Löb to arbitrary p without already having p in it's premises? Or there's a gap between formal proof and belief? Which premise do you ditch, and what does it cost? I'd rather save (1) if I can, but I need to know the damage.
