I'm presenting next week at an interdisciplinary seminar on democratic theory and organizational epistemics, defending a synthesis of Simone Weil's critique of political parties with a framework drawn from the philosophy of attention. My thesis goes beyond Weil's familiar claim that parties corrupt judgment by substituting collective enthusiasm for individual truth-seeking: I argue that parties function as "attentional monopolies" that structurally pre-format the conceptual vocabulary through which citizens can recognize public problems, thereby making genuine deliberation impossible not by suppressing dissent but by colonizing the cognitive framework within which dissent must be articulated. Specifically, I treat party manifestos not as platforms but as "pre-processed attentional schemas" that distribute cognitive labor in ways that render citizens dependent on collective framing devices they cannot individually scrutinize, which violates Weil's requirement that justice demands direct, unmediated attention to the particular. The audience is about half democratic theorists committed to deliberative democracy—many of whom view parties as necessary aggregation mechanisms—about a quarter political scientists who study institutionalization and tend to treat normative abolitionism as historically naive, and about a quarter philosophers of mind familiar with distributed attention but largely unfamiliar with Weil's vocabulary of pesanteur and grace. Several have read Weil's essay on parties but regard it as a historical curiosity rather than a live institutional critique. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased in the vocabulary each group would use—especially the democratic theorists' worry that I'm conflating representation with epistemic closure, and the political scientists' charge that I'm ignoring the functional necessities of modern governance. For each objection, include a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a note on where that reply is softest, and a clear indication of what I should concede up front rather than defend. Most critically, identify the one question I'm hoping nobody asks—likely a challenge that forces me to defend the institutional feasibility of my proposed alternative, temporary problem-specific assemblies governed by what I call "attentional discipline" rather than party discipline—and prepare me specifically for how to answer it without sounding utopian. The sketch of my argument is that party discipline operates as a form of gravity (pesanteur) that pulls attention away from particular realities toward abstract collective identities, and that any democratic arrangement worth preserving must therefore be structured around temporary, problem-specific gatherings that enforce attentional obligations rather than loyalty obligations.
