Here is an argument about idealized-preference frameworks that now looks like it licenses universal paternalism, and I cannot locate the leak.

(1) In a coherent-extrapolated-volition (CEV) framework, an agent's rationally authoritative preferences are those they would hold under full information, logical coherence, and indefinite reflective equilibrium—not the preferences they actually hold now.

(2) If an actual agent S holds an autonomy-preference A ("my current choices should be respected unless I voluntarily revise them"), but S's idealized counterpart S* holds a general preference that unreliable, bias-ridden actual preferences should be corrected by the CEV recommendation, then A is not part of S's true preference set.

(3) For any actual agent, idealization necessarily includes knowledge that human cognition is subject to framing effects, hyperbolic discounting, and coherence failures; therefore every S* will include a preference that flawed actual preferences should not be treated as authoritative.

(4) So for every actual agent, the autonomy-preference A fails to survive extrapolation and is excluded from their CEV.

(5) By (1), only CEV-included preferences carry rational authority.

(6) Therefore no actual agent possesses a rationally authoritative preference that their actual, non-ideal choices be respected against CEV-based correction.

(7) This applies symmetrically: if I claim to know your CEV and it conflicts with your present choice, I have no reason—grounded in your own preferences—to defer to your present choice, since the deference-preference is itself excluded from your CEV.

(8) But the framework was introduced partly to avoid imposing an external standard of value; if it systematically nullifies every agent's standing to resist correction, it collapses into a universal license for paternalistic override.

(9) Hence either we abandon the framework, or we accept that knowing another's CEV rationally permits overriding their actual choices in every case of conflict.

I'd like to preserve both (1) and the anti-paternalist motivation in (8), and I also find (3) psychologically plausible. If I must reject one step, I would rather weaken (3) than (1). Where does the argument slip? Is there an ambiguity in "authoritative" between first-order and second-order preferences, a hidden quantifier shift from "S* prefers correction in general" to "S* rejects autonomy in every particular case," or a modal slide in how counterfactual preferences are mapped back onto actual agents? And if I block the exclusion of autonomy-preferences, what do I pay—must I allow that some incoherent preferences can still bind, or that idealization must be constrained by procedural conditions that are themselves not fully extrapolated?
