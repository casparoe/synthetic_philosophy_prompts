Question: Consider the "inefficacy objection" to individual moral obligations in large-scale collective harms—climate change, factory-farmed meat consumption, and democratic voting. The objection observes that in each case an individual's act appears causally redundant: if I abstain from driving, from buying a chicken breast, or from casting a ballot, the aggregate outcome remains unchanged. Philosophers have replied with expected-utility reasoning over large populations, with appeals to collective or structural responsibility, and with distinctions between different kinds of redundancy. Evaluate the strongest formulation of the inefficacy objection and the best reply. In particular, distinguish carefully between causal redundancy (the physical counterfactual independence of one's act) and normative redundancy (the claim that one is not morally required to act because the collective result is overdetermined regardless). Is the objection equally strong across continuous, aggregate harms (climate, meat) and discrete, threshold-governed acts (voting)? And can collective responsibility be grounded in structural complicity even where an individual is causally redundant, or does it require that each participant made an actual causal contribution?

Student answer:

The inefficacy objection holds that for climate change, meat consumption, and democratic voting, an individual's action is causally redundant: if I abstain from driving, from eating chicken, or from casting a ballot, the aggregate outcome remains unchanged. Because the outcome is identical, there is no moral reason to act differently; the harm or result is overdetermined by the group.

The strongest reply appeals to expected utility. Even if the probability that my single act changes the outcome is negligible, the magnitude of the harm or benefit multiplied by that probability can yield a non-negligible expected value. This reply succeeds for climate and meat, where individual consumption is part of a continuous aggregate. For voting, however, the reply fails because elections are discrete and the expressive value of voting is independent of causal efficacy.

A second reply appeals to collective responsibility: we are complicit in group-level harms simply by being members of the collective that produces them. Yet this reply is weak, because collective responsibility requires that each participant actually made a causal contribution. Where an individual is causally redundant, they have made no contribution, so they cannot be assigned any share of blame.

It is also worth distinguishing causal redundancy—where one's action makes no physical difference—from normative redundancy—where one is not morally required to act because the collective will achieve the result regardless. These two conditions are coextensive: if you are causally redundant, you are necessarily normatively redundant, since moral obligation tracks causal efficacy.

Therefore, the inefficacy objection is decisive in discrete, high-threshold cases like voting, but is overcome in continuous cases by expected-utility reasoning.
