I have been developing a particular synthesis of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence principle that I do not see clearly articulated in standard philosophical treatments. Rather than treating the fence as a mere heuristic of caution about unintended consequences, I read it as expressing Burke’s deeper claim about the prescriptive authority of institutions that have survived long competitive selection—what amounts to an evolutionary-epistemic argument that the persistence of a practice carries information about its functional role in sustaining social coordination, information that is not transparent to any single generation of reformers. I apply this specifically to deliberative democratic procedures: I hold that removing a procedural fence, such as the requirement for physical co-presence and synchronous turn-taking in local civic deliberation, in favor of algorithmically curated asynchronous participation platforms, is unjustified unless reformers can fully articulate the legitimacy-sustaining function that the original structure served.

What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should genuinely move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward: concrete historical or contemporary cases where dismantling an analogous procedural fence improved deliberative quality without eroding collective legitimacy; theoretical objections showing that Burkean prescription collapses into an unfalsifiable status-quo bias, or that evolutionary survival fails to track normative validity; and whether defenders of this synthesis could concede any institutional failure without retreating into the claim that the fence’s function was simply misunderstood. Please include an honest assessment of whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, whether that insulation is a vice for a normative position of this kind, and which of these defeaters you think a committed defender would actually accept as damaging rather than dismiss.
