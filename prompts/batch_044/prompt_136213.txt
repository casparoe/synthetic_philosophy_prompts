I've been thinking about whether an older version of me should be able to bind a future version through an advance medical directive, and I tried to work out a framework I'm calling "continuity collateral." The core idea is that the directive isn't best understood as a command from one person to another, but as a kind of secured agreement: the earlier self puts up "phenomenal collateral"—a specific, recoverable pattern of values, memories, and experiential commitments—that backs the authority of the document. If the future self's psychology has shifted so radically that none of that collateral can be reclaimed—say, after profound dementia or a personality-altering neurological change—then the agreement dissolves not because a new person has taken over, but because the security that made the obligation meaningful has disappeared. I also want to suggest an asymmetry: rejecting the directive is morally faultless when the collateral is genuinely lost, but if the collateral is still intact and the future self rejects it anyway, that looks less like autonomy and more like defaulting on a debt they still hold.
