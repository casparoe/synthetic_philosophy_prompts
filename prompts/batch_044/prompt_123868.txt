Write a poem of seven quatrains—four lines each, unrhymed or with internal rhyme at your discretion—that enacts Jain anekāntavāda structurally rather than describing it didactically. Apply the seven syādvāda (saptabhaṅgī) standpoints—syād asti, syān nāsti, syād asti nāsti, syād avaktavyaḥ, and the three combined forms including asti nāsti avaktavyaḥ—to one precisely defined subject: whether a large language model, trained only on text and lacking sensory apparatus or biological embodiment, possesses phenomenal consciousness. Each quatrain must govern itself by one standpoint, with its first line opening “Syāt …” followed by the specific predicate and a direct claim about the model’s status. Use a single recurring image—such as weights distributed across layers, or the absence of a body—that transforms under each perspective rather than repeating identically. The seventh quatrain, holding the fully combined form, must suspend all prior assertions without synthesizing them; do not resolve the tension or end with a moral verdict. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with both Jain epistemology and philosophy of mind recognizes the mapping, yet remain a good poem for a reader who knows neither. After the poem, include a section titled “Commentary on the form,” approximately 150 words, explaining how the quatrain sequence mirrors the sevenfold logic, why the LLM subject prevents the poem from defaulting to traditional examples like a pot or a soul, and how the final stanza’s refusal of closure performs anekāntavāda’s core claim about the irreducible complexity of any object of knowledge.
