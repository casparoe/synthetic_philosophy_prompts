As I am drafting a dissertation chapter on causal explanation in historical sociology, I have tried to formalize Weber’s criterion for when culture counts as an independent cause rather than an epiphenomenon of material interest. The reconstruction yields a dilemma: either Weber’s framework applies inconsistently, or it proves too much by making values causally responsible for every institution with which they correlate. I want to preserve Weber’s core commitments, but I cannot locate the exact misstep. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) For Weber, a cultural value explains an institutional form only if the two stand in an “elective affinity” (Wahlverwandtschaft): the value must provide a meaningful orientation for actors that sustains the institution, without being reducible to material interest alone.

(2) If this is correct, then any non-accidental correlation in which a value meaningfully orients action within an institution satisfies the criterion for cultural causation.

(3) The modern research university correlates with the scientific norm of “value-freedom” (Wertfreiheit): the institution persists in part because participants orient their conduct to the procedural rule that empirical claims must be detached from substantive value judgments.

(4) Therefore, by (1) and (2), the value-commitment to Wertfreiheit causally explains the modern university.

(5) But Weber insists that science is defined precisely by the exclusion of substantive value commitments from its internal logic; treating Wertfreiheit itself as a value-cause confuses a procedural norm with the kind of substantive worldview (e.g., salvation anxiety) that explains capitalism.

(6) So either (4) is false despite satisfying (1)–(3), which means elective affinity is not sufficient for cultural causation; or (4) is true, in which case Weber has no principled way to distinguish the cultural explanation of capitalism from the cultural explanation of every modern institution, rendering the thesis trivial.

(7) Consequently, Weber’s framework either fails to apply consistently or collapses into the claim that culture explains everything with which it correlates, which is no explanation at all.

I feel the slip lies somewhere between (3) and (5), perhaps involving an equivocation between “value as substantive worldview” and “value as procedural norm,” or a hidden scope shift in moving from “orientation” to “causal explanation.” I would most prefer to keep (1) and (5) intact, since they capture what I take to be Weber’s essential methodological distinctions. Which premise or inference should I reject, why does it appear plausible despite being faulty, and what does rejecting it cost for the larger project of explaining institutional change through elective affinity rather than through material interest alone?
