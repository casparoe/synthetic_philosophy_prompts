A popular science article I read recently summarizes the "consciousness as user illusion" thesis—associated with thinkers like Daniel Dennett and Thomas Metzinger, though the article never names them precisely—along the following lines: The brain does not produce consciousness in any deep metaphysical sense; instead, it constructs a transparent phenomenal self-model, a kind of virtual avatar that tracks the organism's states for action-guidance, analogous to a computer's graphical interface that hides its underlying machine code. Because the representations in this model are transparent—experienced as immediate reality rather than as representations—there is no Cartesian theater where experience "comes together"; there is only distributed predictive processing that generates the appearance of a unified subject. The article then claims that this illusion is not an accidental byproduct but an evolved design feature: natural selection specifically favored organisms capable of modeling themselves as unified agents, which means consciousness, on this view, is an adaptive interface rather than a mysterious substance. From these premises, the author draws two conclusions: first, that artificial consciousness is conceptually straightforward—any system with sufficient hierarchical predictive coding and self-modeling capacity will automatically instantiate the same user-illusion, making consciousness a reproducible information-structure; and second, that the "hard problem" of consciousness dissolves entirely, since asking why there is "something it is like" to be this model is no more meaningful than asking why a desktop folder icon "feels like" a folder. I am inclined to think the conclusion about AI is too quick, but I am not sure where the reasoning breaks down. Which parts of this summary are accurate representations of the view, which parts conflate distinct claims (for instance, transparency with absence, evolutionary adaptiveness with conceptual inevitability, or predictive processing with a specific architectural commitment), and which of the two conclusions—especially the dissolution of the hard problem and the inevitability of machine consciousness—fail to follow even if the premises are granted? Please audit the summary claim by claim, separating what a careful defender of the view would accept from what they would reject as overstatement or misattribution, and indicate whether the errors are merely rhetorical excesses or whether they undermine the inferences drawn.
