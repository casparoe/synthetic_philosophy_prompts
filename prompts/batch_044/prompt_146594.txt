I am preparing a single ninety-minute philosophy-of-economics session for second-year philosophy students who have read Hume's Treatise but have no formal economics training. The aim is to teach his argument from the essays on money and trade: that money is not itself wealth, that an increase in specie eventually raises prices proportionally, and that anxiety about a favourable balance of trade is therefore groundless. Do not explain the doctrine to me; instead, plan the explanatory sequence. First, state the order in which these three claims must be introduced so that each logically supports the next, and identify what has to be in place before the students can even understand why the balance-of-trade point is not merely a separate opinion. Second, propose one carrying example — an isolated island economy with customary fixed prices that suddenly acquires a large influx of gold — and specify exactly what this scenario must include (for instance, nominal rigidity over one production cycle) and what it must deliberately exclude (for instance, international exchange or a banking system issuing credit) if it is to illuminate Hume's mechanism without smuggling in modern macroeconomic assumptions. Third, map the misconceptions they will predictably form: I anticipate they will interpret Hume's long-run price adjustment as a proto-monetarist neutrality claim, mistake his rejection of mercantilism for a contemporary free-trade ideology, and read his allowance for temporary real effects as an anticipation of Keynesian sticky-price theory; for each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question for the end that tests whether they have grasped the dependency between the quantity of money and the price level rather than simply retained the slogans. Finally, say explicitly what should be left out at this level — any comparison with modern central-banking policy, for example — and suggest how to acknowledge the omission openly without sounding evasive.
