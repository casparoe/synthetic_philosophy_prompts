I am writing a novel whose central historian-character treats the past as a sequence of rough drafts of the present: medieval societies are flawed first editions, reform movements are editorial corrections, and modern liberal democracy is the authoritative final text that earlier ages were unconsciously attempting to compose. The analogy licenses her to dismiss alternative political theologies not as coherent rival genres but as awkward early versions, and to read dissenters as editors who finally brought the manuscript to publication. Before I settle her narrative voice, I want to audit the analogy precisely. Lay out the correspondence table: what corresponds to the author, the successive drafts, the errors corrected, the authoritative final version, the editorial criteria, and the readership that validates it? Then specify where the mapping breaks — in particular, whether it smuggles in a hidden teleology that treats historical contingency as mere noise, whether it misattributes agency by implying that modernity has a single author, and whether it can accommodate paths not taken that were not failed drafts but genuinely different literary forms. Separate the disanalogies that undermine her interpretive posture from the ones that do not. Finally, state whether the conclusion she draws — that the past should be read primarily as progress toward the present — survives once the breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If the analogy collapses, propose what a more rigorous historian-narrator might substitute for it.
