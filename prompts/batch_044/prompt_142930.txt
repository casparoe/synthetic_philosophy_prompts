I have just concluded a rather long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether physical pain is intrinsically bad, or whether its badness is only a contingent product of how our motivational and evaluative systems happen to be wired. My friend maintained that the phenomenal quality of pain—its raw, sensory hurtfulness—is self-evidently negative in itself, but I kept returning to the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia, in which patients appear to retain fully intact sensory-discriminative capacities: they can localise a noxious stimulus, describe its intensity and qualitative character with precision, and even acknowledge that tissue damage is occurring, yet they report no suffering whatsoever and display no avoidance or withdrawal behaviour. If the aversive reaction can be stripped away while the sensation remains, it seems difficult to maintain that the badness resides in the feeling itself, rather than in some further layer of affective or normative uptake.

Yet I want to push this analysis beyond the standard binary distinction between a sensory channel and an affective channel. I have been trying to articulate a more complex, triadic architecture in which there is a distinct “evaluative-binding” dimension—something like a registration of the sensation as mattering authoritatively for one’s welfare, which typically couples nociceptive signals to both affective distress and corrective motivation. On this view, ordinary pain involves not merely hurting and disliking it, but a kind of normative uptake: the body registers damage as demanding response, and the self registers that demand as binding. Pain asymbolia, then, might not be a simple dissociation between sensation and affect, but rather a specific failure of this binding layer, leaving the other two components intact but decoupled from welfare-evaluation. This raises the further conceptual possibility of a reverse dissociation—where the evaluative-binding and aversive motivation are present without any underlying nociceptive signal—suggesting that the architecture of pain and suffering is modular in ways that standard philosophical discussions rarely acknowledge.

Could you please explain, in rigorous but intuitive terms, the conceptual issue at stake here? In particular, I would like you to address whether pain asymbolia genuinely demonstrates that the badness of pain is extrinsic to its sensory character, or whether it instead reveals this more complex triadic structure in which sensory, affective, and evaluative-binding components can come apart in various combinations. Please consider the hypothetical reverse case I have sketched, and clarify what such modular dissociations imply for the claim that pain is necessarily or essentially bad.
