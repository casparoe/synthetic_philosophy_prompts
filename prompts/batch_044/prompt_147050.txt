I'm working through Simone Weil's critique of collective life for a seminar on twentieth-century political thought, and I'm trying to map the precise boundary of her hostility toward organized groups. She is famously severe on parliamentary political parties, arguing that they operate as machinery for manufacturing collective passions and demand a form of unconditional allegiance that suppresses individual judgment, replacing the pursuit of truth with organizational survival. Yet her stance is not uniformly anti-collective: she expresses deep sympathy for small, rooted communities—such as local agricultural cooperatives or medieval craft guilds—where obligation arises from direct, concrete relationships rather than from abstract ideology. At the same time, she treats large institutional bodies like the Catholic Church with pronounced ambivalence, condemning its hierarchical authority and bureaucratic discipline as structurally analogous to party machinery, while still valuing its sacramental tradition and its capacity to orient individuals toward transcendence. She also briefly engaged with revolutionary syndicalism but ultimately broke with organized labor movements once they acquired permanent bureaucratic structures, suggesting that her objection is not to collective action per se but to something more specific about institutional form.

Given these judgments, consider a new case: a "liquid deliberative assembly"—a decentralized, cryptographically mediated collective that has no formal membership roster, no permanent leadership, no enduring institutional memory, and no comprehensive ideological platform. Participants verify identity anonymously, convene only around a single, narrowly defined policy question, make a binding decision through reputation-weighted deliberation, and then automatically dissolve; each person retains the instantaneous, unilateral right to withdraw consent and nullify the collective's authority over them at any point. There is no public spectacle, no party line, and no organizational survival interest beyond the immediate decision.

How would Weil likely rule on this formation? Would she condemn it as yet another vehicle for substituting collective emotion for individual conscience, or would she approve it precisely because its revocability and lack of permanence avoid the structural vices she associates with parties? Then apply each principle to predict her verdict on the liquid assembly. Give your best guess about her actual verdict, explaining which principle you think she is actually running and why the closer analog in her record supports it. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different from those already discussed—that would best distinguish the competing principles.
