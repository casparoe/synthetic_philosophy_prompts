I was arguing with my friend last night about whether AI should be built like a rule-following robot or like a outcome-calculator, and I built this arguement that now feels like it proves to much. Here are the steps:

(1) If an AI is genuinely deontological, it needs a complete set of rules that tell it exactly what to do in every situation without comparing overall outcomes.
(2) Any rule-set that can actually be coded has to have explicit conditions—there's no room for "you know, use common sense" because the AI doesn't have common sense outside the code.
(3) But real moral rules (like don't harm people) have hidden exception spaces—times when breaking the rule is okay because the situation is weird.
(4) To cover those exception spaces in code, you'd either need an infinite list of exceptions or a meta-rule that says "break the rule when the outcomes are bad enough."
(5) If you use the meta-rule with outcomes, you've stopped being deontological and started being a threshold-utilitarian in disguise.
(6) So a working deontological AI is impossible; any AI that actually funcitons is computing outcomes somewhere.
(7) By the same token, if human deontologists also navigate weird situations, they must also be using hidden outcome-comparisons.
(8) So the whole distinction between deontological and utilitarian reasoning collapses—there's only one kind, just with different branding.
(9) But that feels wrong. People really do disagree on trolley problems in ways that don't feel like they're just arguing about math.
(10) So where's the mistake? I really want to hang onto (1) because it just captures what deontology means, but maybe I'm wrong about (4) or (5). What's the cost of giving up each?
