I've been going in circles on the paradox of fiction — like, why do we genuinely tear up when Anna Karenina dies even though we know she's not real? The literature splits into the pretend-emotion crowd (Walton's make-believe), the thought-directed camp (Lamarque and others), and the illusionists who claim we're in some partial belief state. But honestly, after reading a bunch of papers it feels like everyone is answering a different exam question. One group cares about the intentional object of the affect, another cares whether the phenomenolgy is identical to real-world grief, and a third is obsessed with whether belief-formation mechanisms get temporarily suspended.

Is this actually one stalled debate since Radford's 1975 essay, or are we running three parallel arguments under the same heading? I'm wondering if there's a hidden empirical question — maybe neuroimaging could tell us if fictional sadness activates the same circuits as real bereavement — that's doing all the work without anyone admitting it. Or maybe the stall is just that each side starts with an undefended intuition about what counts as a 'real' emotion. If it's mostly verbal, what distinction should we draw? And if it's substantive, what would actually break the impasse — a formal model of emotional directedness, an experimental result, or just admitting we're talking past each other?

I was discussing this with friends last night and we couldn't agree on whether the paradox is even a problem for philosophy of mind or just for aesthetics, so I'd love a diagnosis rather than another verdict.
