I'm presenting a 25-minute workshop paper arguing that prosperity and public order in high-growth authoritarian regimes—specifically China and Singapore—do not morally justify authoritarian rule, nor do they reduce legitimacy to mere coercion. Instead, they generate a hybrid mechanism I call intertemporal reciprocity consent: citizens implicitly trade present political liberties for anticipated intergenerational prosperity, producing a conditional legitimacy claim that structurally collapses when growth stalls because the reciprocal obligation is broken, not because democratic input is suddenly demanded. The audience is a comparative-politics seminar of institutionalist empiricists, two rational-choice modelers, and one East-Asian-development-state sociologist; they are skeptical of normative philosophy entering causal analysis, fluent in performance-legitimacy debates, but unfamiliar with analytic consent theory, and they will prioritize identification strategies, micro-foundations, and the risk of conceptual stretching.
