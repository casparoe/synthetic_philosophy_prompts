I'm preparing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on why our everyday language still treats correlation as if it were mechanical causation, and I'd like to close with a poem that enacts an alternative view structurally rather than merely describing it. Write a strict sestina—six stanzas with the classic rotating end-word sequence and a final envoi—on Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist critique: the claim that fire and burning occur together not because the first contains an essential power to produce the second, but because each pairing is a discrete event directly instantiated according to a habitual order (al-'ada) that could, in principle, be otherwise. Choose six end-words that include fire, burn, and custom, and let them rotate through the stanzas by the sestina's numerical rule. Make the form do philosophical work: the rigid, predictable rotation should stand for the rational appearance of natural law, while the poem's speaker—an observer of nature—denies that any end-word genuinely causes the next, insisting instead that at every instant the connection is freshly made and not carried over by internal necessity. The envoi should gather all six words together without resolving whether they are truly linked, leaving the occasionalist tension intact. The verse should be accurate enough that a historian of Islamic philosophy recognizes Al-Ghazālī's precise move—preserving perfect observed regularity while denying necessary causal connection—rather than treating occasionalism as generic skepticism about the external world. No citations, bibliography, or reference to specific editions are needed; the poem should stand on its own for readers unfamiliar with the debate.
