I am a doctoral student in political philosophy writing on the mechanisms of normative collapse. For my dissertation workshop—twelve advanced graduate students in philosophy and sociology, ninety minutes, one session—I want a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel a mechanism I am tentatively calling strategic-opacity collapse. The claim is that honor-bound norms such as dueling do not expire primarily through explicit moral argument, nor through a clean shift in material interests, but through a breakdown in the strategic invisibility that lets agents maintain mutual pretense. Once private defection becomes common knowledge without becoming common acknowledgment, the norm becomes performatively unsustainable: honor can no longer be enacted because everyone can observe that everyone can observe the defection, yet the social rules of the setting still forbid anyone from saying so aloud.

Please design a full activity with real but small stakes—no cash, but tangible status markers such as colored tokens that determine seating priority and speaking order during the debrief—so that participants actually experience the pressure rather than merely discussing it. The rules should be arranged so that, in Phase One, participants are assigned roles (high-status duelists, aspiring gentry, state magistrates, creditors, and pamphleteers) and paired in challenge rounds; each privately chooses whether to uphold, negotiate, or defect from a code of honor, with only public outcomes recorded on a visible board. Phase Two should reveal aggregate defection rates through an anonymous ledger while keeping individual identities hidden. Phase Three should expose individual identities through a transparency shock, but impose a social rule—backed by the token economy—that forbids anyone from verbally acknowledging what has been revealed.

By the end, the group should discover that the norm has collapsed not because anyone argued against it and not because the payoff structure changed, but because the conditions for performing honor have dissolved. Include an exact timeline with minutes allocated, the token-payoff scheme for all three phases, the role descriptions, and the materials needed. Then provide the debrief that maps each phase onto Bernard Williams’s critical-theory principle, Kwame Anthony Appiah’s analysis of honor-world dynamics, and my proposed opacity-collapse mechanism. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely a premature verbal breach of the Phase Three silence rule or a refusal by high-status players to accept token demotion—and how to handle it without breaking the simulation. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity can reproduce—probably the temporal lag between visibility and acknowledgment—and which feature it necessarily cannot reproduce and I must supply in my closing remarks. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; assume participants have not read the primary texts in detail, and let the idea emerge from what happens in the room rather than from any lecture I deliver afterward.
