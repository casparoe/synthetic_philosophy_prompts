I am enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on historical explanation that recently focused on what our instructor calls the "actor-status problem" for non-human forces. The conversation began with familiar illustrations—how the 1815 eruption of Tambora disrupted European harvest cycles for years afterward, how prolonged aridification in Mesopotamia reshaped the scale of political centralization, and how the Black Death altered labor structures across Eurasia—but quickly turned to a stronger claim: that pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic events ought to be treated not merely as background conditions or efficient causes, but as genuine "historical actors" that interact with human intentionality in structured ways. To organize the debate, the course introduced a three-part taxonomy that is not standard in the texts I have encountered: "episodic perturbations" (singular catastrophic events that rupture existing institutional trajectories), "structural selectives" (slow-moving climatic parameters that filter which social forms survive over generations), and "coevolutionary antagonists" (pathogens that adapt reciprocally to human behavior, generating sustained feedback loops). The philosophical tension is whether granting actor-status across all three categories demands a single, unified theory of agency—perhaps one that severs agency from intentionality entirely—or whether the taxonomy reveals an equivocation in the metaphor itself. There is also the risk of environmental determinism: if microbes and climate are actors, do human deliberations become merely reactive effects?

Write an essay that takes up this controversy directly. Weigh the case for treating each of the three categories as possessing a form of historical agency, examine whether such attribution can be reconciled with meaningful human autonomy or inevitably collapses into determinism, and defend a specific verdict on whether a culture or historiographical tradition can legitimately choose to employ the actor metaphor without committing to an implausible metaphysics. Your conclusion should specify whether actor-status should be granted uniformly, restricted to particular categories, or rejected altogether, and you should explain what that decision implies for the practice of writing history.
