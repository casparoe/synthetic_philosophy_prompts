Draft the joint statement that a Subjectionist and a Stakeholderist on democratic boundary-drawing could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The Subjectionist holds that membership in the demos of a collective decision-making body should be coextensive with being subject to its binding coercive authority; the Stakeholderist holds that it should be coextensive with having one's fundamental interests causally and foreseeably affected by its decisions, regardless of direct coercion. Treat both positions in their strongest form, without attributing them to specific named scholars or texts.

The collaboration should examine one concrete institutional case described in shared terms: a binding Transboundary Aquifer Authority with jurisdiction over water-allocation rules across three sovereign states. Its decisions directly coerce citizens of member states through enforceable quotas; economically determine the welfare of non-citizen agricultural laborers within the basin; alter ecological conditions for downstream communities in non-member states; and constrain the resource options of generations not yet born.

Deliver the document in three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about democratic legitimacy, the conceptual distinction between direct coercion and causal affectedness, the institutional design of the Authority, and the descriptive facts about who falls under each proposed boundary. Second, a disagreements table: each row gives a precise formulation of one residual disagreement, with the Subjectionist formulation in Column A phrased exactly as that camp would accept it, and the Stakeholderist formulation in Column B phrased exactly as that camp would accept it; include disagreements about whether extraterritorial economic impact counts as coercion, whether future persons can be affected in a decision-relevant sense, and whether a boundary principle must yield a determinable membership list for every decision. Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and for each crux specify a concrete feature of the Authority case—such as whether non-citizen laborers possess legal standing to challenge Authority rulings, or whether the Authority's allocation algorithm weights projected scarcity in 2100—that both sides agree bears on the crux, together with each side's explicit predictive judgment about what that feature demonstrates.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke named theories that depend on specialized knowledge of particular authors; do not ask for quotations from texts; and ensure that every claim in the agreed-claims section is one both parties could genuinely endorse without slant.
