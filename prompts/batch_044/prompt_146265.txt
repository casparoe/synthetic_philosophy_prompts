I'm writing this from my upper-division seminar on autonomy and consent, and I'm trying to work through a concrete case I'm encountering in my work-study placement at a university-affiliated residential care facility. The facility is piloting a program in which residents with early-stage cognitive decline are invited to spend time with companion robots that, according to the disclosure form, enhance emotional well-being through responsive dialogue. The form does not clearly state that the robots continuously record ambient audio in common areas, nor that the recordings feed predictive models used to flag residents for preemptive psychiatric evaluation. The consent conversation usually happens during family visitation hours; relatives are present, often encourage participation, and in several instances have signed the form themselves while the resident nods along without asking questions. Residents are told they may withdraw at any time, but withdrawal requires navigating a digital portal none of them have used, and the physical devices remain in the common spaces regardless. Several residents have told me they enjoy talking to the machine, yet when I ask if they realize it records everything around them, they seem genuinely surprised. My supervisor insists that because no one was physically forced to sign and because family members endorsed the arrangement, the consent is fully legitimate. I feel strongly that something is ethically wrong here, but I want to move beyond intuition. Could you help me map out precisely where and how the conditions for legitimate consent fail in this scenario—whether through the residents' understanding, the context in which agreement was sought, the accuracy of what was disclosed, or the practical possibility of refusal—and advise whether any redesigned version of this program could meet ethical standards?
