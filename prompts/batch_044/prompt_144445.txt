Write a philosophical joke that turns on the repugnant conclusion in population axiology. The premise should involve a cosmic planner defending an enormous population of beings whose lives are barely worth living against an auditor who accepts only average-welfare metrics, with the punchline turning on the structural feature of totalist aggregation: that a sufficiently large number of barely-positive lives can sum to greater total welfare than a smaller population of extremely happy lives. To avoid a generic large-number gag, frame the scene as a bureaucratic audit in which the planner keeps inventing new evaluation criteria—“integrated bliss density,” “cumulative satisfaction index,” “aggregate existential dividend”—in a futile attempt to hide that, by the arithmetic of total welfare, the barely-content multitudes technically outscore a compact paradise. The humor should depend on the listener recognizing exactly why the metric-shifting collapses back into the same comparison, not on superficial wordplay or math puns. Keep the joke concise and conceptually precise.
