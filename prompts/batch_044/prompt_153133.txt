I'm working through some decision-theoretic frameworks for evaluating large-scale ecological interventions, and I've found the standard treatments of risk-weighted expected utility somewhat unsatisfying when applied outside of anthropocentric risk domains. In particular, I'm trying to understand how the framework behaves when the outcome space involves wild animal suffering—where the number of affected individuals is enormous but the probability distribution over welfare states is shaped not only by ordinary empirical uncertainty, but by what might be called structural moral uncertainty: we are unsure not just about which ecological outcomes will occur, but about which entities possess the kind of sentience that makes welfare outcomes morally relevant at all.

Could you explain, in conceptual terms rather than formal derivation, how risk-weighted expected utility ought to handle this kind of double-layered uncertainty? I am especially interested in whether risk-weights should be applied only to the empirical probabilities of ecological outcomes—say, the likelihood that altering a predator population actually improves net welfare across prey and downstream species—or whether they should also modulate the utility weights assigned to different classes of beings, such as insects versus vertebrates, in proportion to our confidence in their sentience. Does maintaining a sharp distinction between these two applications survive scrutiny, or does it collapse once we treat the boundaries of moral consideration as variables internal to the decision problem rather than fixed parameters?

Furthermore, I want to understand whether the framework can even get off the ground in contexts where causal pathways are mediated by trophic cascades. If an intervention's effects propagate through an ecosystem in ways that make the final welfare distribution radically indeterminate—because we do not know which species will be affected, in what numbers, or with what subjective experience—is the requirement of a well-defined outcome space violated? Or can risk-weighting be extended coherently to cover ontological uncertainty about the structure of the causal graph itself, and if so, does that extension preserve the normative force that distinguishes risk-weighted expected utility from a more conservative maximin rule?
