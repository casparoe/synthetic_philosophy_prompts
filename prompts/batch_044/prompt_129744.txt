I'm writing my dissertation on the normative architecture of labor-market interventions, and I've become preoccupied by a specific institutional design that strikes me as philosophically underexamined: in several progressive jurisdictions, local minimum-wage ordinances are now indexed not to national productivity metrics or uniform living-wage formulas, but directly to hyperlocal rent-burden ratios—specifically, the median ratio of housing costs to income within a census tract. The explicit justification is economic and ethical—ensuring that full-time labor yields dignified shelter—but I want to understand what deeper commitments about value, desert, and the ontology of labor this practice silently presupposes.

Does tying the wage floor to local consumption costs necessarily commit its defenders to a consumption-based rather than production-based theory of just compensation? Must it treat housing access as a merit good or basic entitlement rather than a commodity priced by supply and demand, and if so, does that require rejecting the standard neoclassical separation between factor markets and consumption markets? I am especially struck by an apparent asymmetry: these same jurisdictions typically peg rent-control caps to capital-improvement costs or CPI rather than to the tenant's wage-earning capacity, suggesting that the implicit theory of value applies differently to labor income than to property income. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework this policy cluster requires, distinguish what it strictly entails from what merely accommodates it, and identify rival frameworks—perhaps a pure capabilities approach, or a Lockean labor-mixing view of value—that could license the wage policy without endorsing the asymmetry. Finally, tell me what institutional redesign would be necessary if we replaced the presupposed view with a strictly marginal-product theory of wages coupled with a separate, unconditional housing subsidy.
