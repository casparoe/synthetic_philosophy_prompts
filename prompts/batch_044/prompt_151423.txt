As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am writing a feature on the “old evidence” problem in Bayesian confirmation theory—the tension that arises when long-known data suddenly appear to support a newly proposed theory, even though Bayes’s rule seems to forbid any confirmatory gain from conditioning on evidence whose prior probability was already near unity. Rather than requesting a survey of the literature, I want to pressure-test five conceptual resolutions in a deliberately contrived archival scenario: in 2024, researchers propose a modified lattice-QCD framework that precisely retrodicts isotopic ratios recorded in a largely forgotten 1962 cloud-chamber archive—measurements that were never doubted, but were never structurally linked to quantum chromodynamics either.

Please rank the following five responses into tiers—label them, for example, “strong fit,” “partial fit,” and “poor fit”—according to how well each handles this specific case, in which the 1962 data were already fully credible but lacked any prior explanatory embedding within the 2024 framework. The five are: (1) the learning-versus-knowing distinction, which holds that confirmation occurs only when an agent first grasps that the new theory entails the old data; (2) counterfactual conditioning on ignorance, which evaluates confirmation by asking what credence the agent would have assigned had the evidence not yet been observed; (3) Jeffrey-style probabilistic redistribution, which permits non-rigid updates across an entire belief network rather than conditioning on a single proposition; (4) the epistemic-restructuring view, which requires that old data confirm only if they alter the explanatory dependency relations among beliefs, not merely raise one isolated credence; and (5) the predictive-compression account, which grants old data confirmatory power only if integrating them into the theory yields a lower algorithmic-complexity description of the total evidence set than rival frameworks can provide.

For each tier, list which resolutions belong there and provide exactly one precise sentence explaining why that placement follows specifically from the archival-rediscovery scenario—particularly addressing whether the resolution respects the fact that the measurements were always trusted but only now become explanatory nodes. Do not supply bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; treat these positions as conceptual instruments you can evaluate from first principles. Keep the reasoning formal, concise, and focused on the logic of confirmation rather than on historical attribution.
