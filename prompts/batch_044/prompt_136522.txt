I am preparing a graduate seminar discussion on trolley-style dilemmas and want to stress-test the doctrine with a variant that is not the standard switch-versus-push scenario. Here is my opening challenge: imagine an automated rail yard where five workers are trapped in a ventilation tunnel that is about to fill with toxic gas. The only emergency purge override is secured behind a blast door that is currently held shut by a counterweight mechanism; due to a malfunction, a sixth worker is pinned directly beneath that counterweight. The release protocol—pulling the lever—is designed solely to open the door for emergency access and to activate the purge; it is not designed to harm anyone, and the worker’s presence beneath the weight is an accidental malfunction of the safety system, not part of its intended function. If I pull the lever, I know with certainty that the counterweight will drop and kill the pinned worker, but my physical act is opening the door, and his death is caused by the malfunctioning machinery rather than by my using his body as an instrumental part of my plan. By the doctrine’s lights, is this permissible? It seems to me that if I know the mechanism requires his weight to be displaced in order for the door to open, then I am treating his death as part of the causal pathway I deliberately exploit, which makes him a means—yet if I treat it as a pure side-effect of a legitimate institutional procedure, then I could justify nearly any engineered harm simply by embedding it in complex machinery and calling the lethal part a “malfunction.” Where does the doctrine actually draw the line between a bad effect that is genuinely unintended and one that is merely laundered through institutional design, and can you defend that line without collapsing it into a merely psychological claim about what I happen to “focus” on?
