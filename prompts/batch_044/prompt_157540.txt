Design a final-exam essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science and environmental history (90 minutes, 30 marks, approximately 1,000-word response expected). The question should ask whether the Anthropocene can coherently serve as a historical period when its proposed boundary dates are fixed by geochemical and stratigraphic criteria rather than by events that human agents could have experienced as transformative. Require students to distinguish chronostratigraphic formalization, as practiced by bodies like the International Commission on Stratigraphy, from historical periodization as practiced by historians, and to assess whether authority over epochal boundaries belongs to earth-system scientists, to historians, or to some hybrid institution—without allowing a simple recitation of proposed start points (such as 1610, 1784, 1945, or 1950). To prevent summary-level answers, inject the following conceptual crux: imagine a hypothetical Interdisciplinary Council on Epochal Boundaries that rules a period counts as historically real only if its boundary coincides with an event contemporaneous agents could have recognized as world-altering, a criterion that excludes every current Anthropocene candidate except possibly the nuclear era. Ask students to evaluate whether this phenomenological-access criterion is defensible, whether it illegitimately privileges human experience over Earth-system dynamics, and whether accepting it forces us to reject the Anthropocene as a historical category entirely or merely to reconceive it as a post-historical geological interlude. Provide a model answer of about 800 words that executes three specific moves a top response must make—first, clarifying the jurisdictional conflict between stratigraphic and historiographic authority; second, analyzing the phenomenological-access criterion and its disanalogy with conventional period boundaries such as the fall of Rome or the Industrial Revolution; third, defending a precise position on whether the Anthropocene survives as a meaningful historical concept—together with a marking scheme that assigns weight to each move and lists specific errors that should cost marks, including conflating geological with historical time, attributing historiographic authority to the ICS, treating rejection of the phenomenological criterion as automatic validation of the Anthropocene in history, and failing to address whether the concept functions differently as a scientific versus a cultural category. The difficulty must arise from conceptual precision, not from recalling specific literature, and the prompt must not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
