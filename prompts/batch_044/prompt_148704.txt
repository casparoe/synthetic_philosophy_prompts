Write the opening remarks—approximately six minutes, spoken rather than read—for the retirement symposium of a historian of science whose career was built on the claim that macro-historical inevitability can only be defended through structured counterfactual testing, not through narrative coherence alone. The room contains senior historians who treat counterfactual reasoning as methodological heresy, philosophers of science who defend modal reasoning in explanation, graduate students from both disciplines, and family members who are not academics. Honor her specific contribution—what she termed modal fragility analysis, the practice of identifying which nodes in a causal chain are genuinely overdetermined versus which only appear inevitable because nearby possible worlds are suppressed—without delivering a lecture or a hagiography. Build the speech around one concrete, non-obvious example of modal fragility from the history of technology, avoiding the standard Industrial Revolution or space-race narratives. The example should show how a technological adoption that looks retrospectively unavoidable was in fact fragile, and should demonstrate that recognizing that fragility changes the moral evaluation of the actors involved, not just the causal account. The remarks must contain at least one precise conceptual distinction—between inevitability-as-overdetermination and inevitability-as-retrospective-illusion—that does real structural work: it should determine what can and cannot be said about her legacy, and it should be sharp enough that the philosophers and historians in the room can continue debating it after the event, yet clear enough that the family members can follow why the distinction matters. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or reading recommendations; do not ask the audience to consult specific texts. The tone should be respectful, intellectually serious, and accessible to a mixed audience.
