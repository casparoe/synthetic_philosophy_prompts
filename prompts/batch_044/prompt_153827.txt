I am preparing an essay for an advanced course in historical ethics, and the idea grew out of a seminar discussion in which someone defended Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus with the familiar claim that each was "a man of his time." Rather than treating that defence as a uniform excuse, I want to disaggregate it by introducing a distinction between normative inheritors—individuals who largely accepted and enforced moral and political frameworks they neither designed nor had the power to reshape—and normative architects, who actively constructed, codified, or propagated the very norms that now appear to condemn them. Jefferson strikes me as closer to an architect: he was not merely a slaveholder in a slaveholding society, but a principal author of a political philosophy that articulated universal human equality, which means he possessed the conceptual resources to recognise the contradiction in his own practice. Columbus occupies a more ambiguous position: he inherited Iberian frameworks of religious conquest and royal patronage, yet he personally designed administrative systems of extraction and enslavement in the Caribbean, suggesting he was partly an inheritor and partly an architect of colonial violence. Churchill, by contrast, seems largely an inheritor of imperial norms he did not originate, though he certainly reinforced and defended them vigorously. I also want to advance a subsidiary claim that I am calling the fallacy of moral prevalence: the observation that a wrong was statistically common in a given era is not itself a moral justification, and widespread injustice ought not to be easier to excuse than isolated injustice. I am particularly concerned with whether this framework genuinely illuminates our judgments or merely repackages the intuition that "some people knew better," and whether applying it risks anachronism of a different kind.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. I would welcome precise comments on whether my historical characterisations are accurate, whether the inheritor/architect distinction is philosophically coherent or collapses under pressure, and whether the argument as a whole is interesting or simply states the obvious in elaborate form. I do not want bibliographic suggestions or a reading list; I am looking for direct analytical feedback on the essay's structure and substance.
