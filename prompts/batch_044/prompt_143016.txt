A colleague summarized a view they read in a recent historiography essay about overdetermination and macrohistorical explanation: "Major events like World War I are overdetermined in the strict logical sense—the alliance system, imperial competition, and the Sarajevo assassination were each individually sufficient to trigger a continental war within a few years, so naming any one of them 'the cause' is not merely incomplete but positively misleading. In fact, every macrohistorical turning point possesses at least three independent sufficient conditions. Therefore historians should abandon singular causal narratives entirely and adopt 'structural field' accounts that enumerate contributing factors without ranking them. The essay also concludes that this renders counterfactual analysis impossible: if you remove one sufficient cause, the others still guarantee the outcome, so asking 'what if the archduke had not been killed' has no meaningful answer."

I am inclined to think the summary captures something real about multi-causal explanation, but I cannot tell where the reasoning slips. Which parts of this characterization would a careful philosopher of history accept, which parts conflate overdetermination with ordinary multi-causality or misrepresent the status of counterfactual reasoning, and does the conclusion that singular narratives must be abandoned actually follow from the premise about multiple sufficient conditions?
