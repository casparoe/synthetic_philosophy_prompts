I am drafting a scene in a historical novel set in the immediate post-war period, in which a fictional Municipal Commission for Rational Reconstruction—modeled partly on the logical-empiricist planning circles of that era—must rule on a concrete proposal called the Axiological Partition Protocol. The protocol applies Rudolf Carnap’s well-known distinction between cognitively meaningful statements, which are empirically verifiable through urban data, and non-cognitive value expressions, such as community preferences regarding belonging, aesthetic continuity, or social dignity, to the redesign of a bomb-damaged industrial quarter. Under the protocol, every zoning recommendation must be bifurcated into a factual branch specifying measurable outcomes—traffic flow, sanitation loads, housing density, commute duration—and a value branch recording community attitudes without assigning them cognitive claim-status. The Commission is divided. The technical planners maintain that without this partition, value disputes will contaminate empirical forecasting and render resource allocation arbitrary. The opposition, composed of local architects and former resistance organizers, contends that Carnap’s separation is itself a normative imposition: the selection of which metrics count as factual—economic efficiency versus pedestrian proximity, for instance—already embeds unacknowledged value commitments, and relegating community voices to a non-cognitive register effectively disenfranchises them by denying those voices any role in justifying policy. I need you to write the Commission’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the City Reconstruction Authority. The memo must contain: (1) a clear recommendation with actionable conditions or explicit reasons for rejection; (2) an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, specifically addressing whether the selection of empirical metrics can ever be value-neutral in the Carnapian sense; (3) a brief discussion of whether Carnap’s own position—that rational value discussion requires translating preferences into empirical predictions about human satisfaction—can salvage the protocol without collapsing the cognitive/non-cognitive distinction; and (4) a practical note on how the Commission should adjudicate disputes in which two communities express contradictory value-branch preferences that both predict equal satisfaction on the factual metrics. The tone should be that of an administrative document meant to guide action, not an academic essay, and the reasoning should be fully self-contained so that a reader without access to external sources can follow it from the memo’s own definitions and arguments alone. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works. If you describe Carnap’s positions, do so in your own words and do not use quotation marks for any passage attributed to a real text.
