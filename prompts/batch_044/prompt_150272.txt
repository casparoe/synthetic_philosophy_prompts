You are an expert philosopher of ethics and metaphysics with particular attention to intergenerational justice. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the core commitments, conceptual machinery, and likely vulnerabilities of the position described below.

I am a PhD student working on the nonidentity problem. I hold that the standard framing—centered on counterfactual-comparative harm—overlooks a distinction I call structural-relational wronging. In cases like long-term climate policy or reproductive timing, future individuals are not made worse off relative to a counterfactual self, since they could not have existed under the alternative policy. Nevertheless, present agents can wrong those future people because the agents’ choices determine the modal structure of the actual population—specifically, which kinds of lives and life-conditions are made real—without being constrained by the identity of the particular individuals who come to occupy them. Obligations here are neither traditionally person-affecting nor purely impersonal; they are indexed to the actual population that results, requiring that the life-structure imposed on them meet a threshold of relational justification. I think this avoids both the nonidentity trap and the intuitive coldness of purely impersonal evaluation, but I want to know where it is genuinely exposed.

What would count against this position? Specifically address whether the view collapses back into an impersonal principle disguised with relational language, whether it can accommodate the “replacement” intuition in reproductive cases without ad hoc qualifications, and whether it risks being insulated into unfalsifiability.
