I serve on the University Committee on Intellectual Pluralism and Curriculum Oversight, and by next Friday we must reach a recommendation on whether to approve for the general-education core a proposed required first-year seminar entitled Many-Sided Reasoning: Anekāntavāda and Contemporary Policy Debate. The philosophy department's proposal uses the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, specifically the seven-fold conditional-predication schema known as saptabhaṅgī, as a pedagogical framework for analyzing expert disagreements in which contradictory conclusions appear simultaneously defensible — for instance, competing climate-risk assessments or epidemiological forecasts that rely on incompatible modeling assumptions. The faculty senate's Committee on Scientific Literacy has formally objected, arguing that instructing undergraduates to treat contradictory empirical predictions as each partially true in the Jain sense — that is, as expressing different naya, or standpoints, rather than being straightforwardly false — undermines the university's accreditation commitment to scientific literacy and risks institutionalizing a form of epistemic relativism that makes empirical correction impossible. The philosophy department counters that anekāntavāda is not relativism but ontological pluralism: reality is manifold (anekānta), any single predication captures only one aspect, and the doctrine explicitly allows that some standpoints are false outright, so the framework actually demands more precise discrimination among partial truths rather than abandoning discrimination altogether. Our committee's charge is not to settle the metaphysical dispute but to issue an actionable recommendation — approve the course for the core, approve it only as an upper-division elective, or reject it with specific revision guidance — together with a clear time frame for implementation or response. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should present a concrete bottom line with reasoning that the philosophy department and the senate committee could both recognize as addressing their actual concerns, include an honest and serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and explain whether the pedagogical deployment of saptabhaṅgī genuinely differs from the relativism the accreditors fear or whether the distinction collapses under classroom pressure. Write the response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter or internal memorandum.
