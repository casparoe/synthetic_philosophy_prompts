Read the film as a philosophical text rather than a social satire alone: does Chaplin dramatize labor as a form of moral discipline that the Tramp instinctively resists, or does the comedy depend on treating his unemployment as personal incompetence rather than a structural feature? Focus on the factory sequences, especially the feeding-machine episode and the Tramp's cyclical return to the assembly line after breakdown and release. Where does the film illuminate the idea that leisure is not merely the absence of work but a positive right that the work ethic actively suppresses? Does Modern Times present the Tramp as an accidental precursor to thirty-five-hour-week advocates, or does it ultimately restore the work ethic by resolving his arc through romanticized, couple-based escape at the film's close? Be precise about which scenes support which interpretation, and note honestly where the film resists reduction to Weberian ethics or to modern labor-rights discourse. Finally, what does this specific cinematic representation contribute to the philosophical debate about work, leisure, and the right to rest — particularly regarding karoshi and the moral status of structured idleness — that purely conceptual argument tends to overlook?
