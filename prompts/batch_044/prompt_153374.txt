Write a joke about Good's theorem on the value of information. The joke should turn on the theorem's core claim — that a rational Bayesian agent with free choice of action should never, in expectation, be worse off for having received an additional observation — and contrast it with the familiar human tendency to avoid learning something (say, a test result or an algorithm's prediction) out of fear that the knowledge will force a costly or unpleasant decision. Make the punchline depend on the actual conceptual tension between the formal non-negativity of informational value and practical information avoidance, rather than on generic math-anxiety humor. The joke should be intelligible to someone familiar with basic decision theory without requiring external references.
