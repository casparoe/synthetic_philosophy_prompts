I'm putting together a take-home for my upper-level philosphy of action course and need one essay prompt that gets into the weeds on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting. I don't want students just reciting Davidson's essay on weakness of will or summarising Ainslie's picoeconomics. Instead, I want them to wrestle with a tension I'll call the precommitment paradox: if your present self binds your future self with a Ulysses contract to avoid akrasia, you're treating your own future agency as an external threat—like locking a stranger in a room—which seems to undermine the very diachronic autonomy that rational choice theory assumes. At the same time, if you don't bind yourself, hyperbolic discounting predicts you'll systematically betray your earlier judgments without any irrationality in the moment, since each present self is maximising its local utility.

The question should ask students to do three things: first, explain why hyperbolic discounting, on its own, might dissolve the problem of akrasia into a series of locally rational choices; second, defend or reject the claim that Ulysses-style precommitment restores diachronic rationality only by fragmenting the agent into competing temporal parts; and third, propose how a defender of unified agency might reply—maybe by appealing to something like a narrative constraint or a standing commitment—without just name-dropping Parfit or Bratman.

Please write the exam question, a model anwser of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that lists five must-hit moves, assigns marks out of 25, and notes the typical mistake that costs marks for each. Keep it meaningful for a model without web access—no citations needed, no obscure papers—but don't let it be answered by pure regurgitation either.
