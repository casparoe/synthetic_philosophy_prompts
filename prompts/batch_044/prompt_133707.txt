I've been thinking for some time about what it actually means to call a body "healthy" or "diseased," especially after watching a close friend navigate years of conflicting medical opinions about her chronic fatigue. The more I reflect on it, the more it seems there are two broad camps people fall into. One treats health as a statistical fact about the organism: your biomarkers, organ functions, and physiological parameters fall within a range that supports survival and reproduction, so disease is essentially deviation from that biological blueprint. The other treats health as a normative achievement: your body is healthy to the extent that it lets you pursue the activities and relationships you value, so disease is whatever meaningfully obstructs your life plan.

I've tried to work out my own position, and I would appreciate comments on a short essay sketch I'm drafting. My central claim is that both camps commit what I call the "thermostat error." The statistical view treats the body like a thermostat with a factory-set temperature: if you are within the programmed range, you are healthy, regardless of context or environment. The normative view treats it like a thermostat that the homeowner manually adjusts to whatever temperature feels right. I think both miss something essential about living systems. A body is not a thermostat at all; it behaves more like an adaptive climate system that constantly renegotiates its own targets through feedback with an environment it cannot fully predict. On this view, health is neither a fixed internal state nor a chosen preference, but a kind of dynamic coherence: the capacity to keep recalibrating one's stable operating conditions without losing structural integrity.

Here is the outline of my argument:

1. The statistical view cannot explain why some people feel profoundly unwell despite every biomarker reading "normal," nor why others live with clear biological deviations—such as color blindness or the absence of a limb—without any sense of being ill.
2. The normative view collapses health into mere preference. If health is just whatever aligns the body with one's goals, then there is no meaningful distinction between curing an infection and enhancing an already healthy athlete; both are simply adjustments that support a chosen project.
3. My proposed alternative is health as predictive resilience. The body does not maintain a single set point but rather a repertoire of possible stable states. Disease occurs not when one deviates from a species norm, nor when one fails a personal project, but when the system's range of adaptive responses collapses—when it can no longer generate new stable configurations in response to change.
4. I apply this to my friend's case. Her fatigue is not explained away by normal bloodwork, nor is it merely "not fitting her lifestyle." Rather, her system appears stuck in one narrow mode—unable to shift patterns of energy allocation—so her predictive resilience has narrowed in a way that feels like illness even when no specific biomarker flags it.

I would specifically welcome feedback on the following:

- Is my critique of the two standard views fair, or am I attacking simplified versions that more sophisticated defenders would reject?
- Is "predictive resilience" coherent on its own terms, or does it secretly smuggle back in one of the two views I am trying to avoid?
- Are there obvious counterexamples—conditions that involve a collapsed adaptive range but should not count as disease, or conditions that are clearly diseases yet preserve broad adaptive flexibility?

I am especially concerned about one boundary case: the healthy athlete who trains her body into extreme but stable specialization versus the sick person whose body has genuinely lost flexibility. I worry this is the weakest point in my framework, because both might appear to have "narrowed" ranges, yet only one should count as unhealthy. Any thoughts on how to draw that line, or on whether my whole approach is misguided, would be very helpful.
