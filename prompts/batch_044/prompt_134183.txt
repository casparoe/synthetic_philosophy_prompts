I'm leading a ninety-minute session for public-policy graduate students—strong quantitative backgrounds, no philosophy—on Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. Don't explain Dewey to me; design the teaching plan. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual piece must be secured before democracy can be presented as a mode of distributed inquiry rather than as a voting mechanism, and why the distinction between inquiry-as-problem-solving and truth-as-correspondence needs to come before any social dimension is introduced. Second, choose and justify the one carrying example: I'm considering a city that redesigns bus routes through iterative pilot loops with rider feedback rather than through a centralized master plan—evaluate whether this works, specify exactly what it must include (for instance, that failure is treated as data rather than error) and what it must exclude (for instance, pure market aggregation of preferences), and suggest one concrete adjustment if the example falls short. Third, map the misconceptions these students are likely to bring or form: that experimentalism licenses arbitrary trial-and-error without methodological rigor, that democratic inquiry collapses into majority opinion, and that Dewey is covertly reserving authority for a hidden expert class. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and the single move or framing that defuses it without derailing the arc. Fourth, propose a check-for-understanding question for the end that reveals whether they have grasped democracy-as-inquiry rather than democracy-as-preference-aggregation; it should be answerable without philosophical jargon and should distinguish the two interpretations clearly. Finally, state explicitly what to leave out at this level—such as Dewey's metaphysics of experience or his specific debates with Lippmann—and how to acknowledge those omissions so the session's coherence is not undermined.
