I am drafting a dissertation chapter on agent-relative reasons and have reconstructed an argument that extends Bernard Williams's integrity objection in a direction that seems to collapse all plausible moral theory. The standard objection holds that when moral reasoning requires an agent to weigh or abandon a ground project—say, saving a stranger at the cost of one's life work—it introduces one thought too many, corrupting practical identity by treating what should be constitutive of the self as fungible. I want to see whether this can be pushed further through a second-order version. Here is the reconstructed chain.

(1) Integrity requires that an agent not treat her first-order ground projects as exchangeable with other values; to do so is to suffer a form of practical alienation.
(2) But practical identity is not only first-order, defined by particular projects such as writing, parenting, or loyalty; it also includes a second-order ground project—call it the reflexive project of integrity—of being the kind of agent for whom those first-order projects are not fungible.
(3) If (2) holds, then any moral theory that permits an agent, even in extreme cases, to weigh a first-order ground project against an impartial demand implicitly requires her to ask whether she should preserve her reflexive project.
(4) Yet asking whether to preserve the reflexive project just is to treat it as fungible, subject to comparative evaluation, which violates its own content conditions.
(5) Therefore, any moral theory that allows trade-offs between ground projects and impartial demands is not merely demanding; it structurally requires the agent to violate the reflexive project in the very act of complying.
(6) All plausible moral theories—minimal deontology, agent-relative consequentialism, virtue ethics with external demands—permit such trade-offs in at least some catastrophic cases.
(7) So all plausible moral theories are structurally self-defeating for any agent who possesses a reflexive project of integrity.
(8) But moral theories are plainly not structurally self-defeating; agents navigate moral demands without practical dissolution.
(9) Hence, either we must reject all plausible moral theories, or the reflexive project lacks the status of a ground project, or there is a hidden slide in the inference.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, yet I am unsure where the damage is done. Is there an equivocation between a theory permitting evaluation and a theory requiring it? Does premise (2) illegitimately inflate a first-order condition into a second-order ground project? Or does the argument instead reveal that Williams's view, taken seriously, commits us to rejecting any comparative framework for practical reasoning? I would prefer to keep the core of the integrity objection intact, but I want to know which step is weakest, what abandoning it costs, and whether the argument exposes a genuine limit that standard presentations of agent-relative reasons miss.
