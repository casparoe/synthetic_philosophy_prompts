I'm working through a problem set for a graduate seminar on speaker meaning, and my study partner and I have constructed what looks like a reductio of the Gricean architecture itself. The worry is that the distinction between saying and implicating collapses once you take contextual saturation seriously, but I'm not sure where the escape hatch is. Here is the argument we built, step by step.

(1) Grice distinguishes saying from implicating, and implicatures are calculated against a baseline of literal content — what is said — that is prior to speaker intentions about what is being communicated.
(2) But to determine what is said, one must resolve ambiguity, fix reference, and saturate predicates (for instance, deciding whether empty applied to the bottle means empty of liquid, empty of air, or empty of its usual contents).
(3) These saturation processes are not purely semantic; they are guided by speaker intentions, contextual salience, and sometimes ad hoc concept construction. Without knowing what the speaker means to communicate, there is no principled boundary between enriching a predicate and generating an implicature.
(4) So determining what is said already requires access to speaker meaning.
(5) Therefore the Gricean framework requires speaker meaning to construct the literal baseline, but then uses that baseline to derive speaker meaning via the Cooperative Principle — a circular dependency.
(6) The only consistent way out is to abandon the idea of a minimal, context-independent proposition altogether and treat all content as speaker-meaning.
(7) But that eliminates the very distinction Grice introduced and turns every utterance into pure intentionalism, which seems to make the Cooperative Principle redundant and fails to explain how misunderstanding is even possible.
(8) So either we accept the circularity or we abandon Gricean implicature entirely. Both options seem worse than the problem they solve.

My partner thinks the flaw is in step (3) — that saturation is governed by linguistic convention, not speaker intention — but that feels like it just pushes the problem back to how conventions are applied in context. I want to keep the Gricean distinction intact if I can, but I'm willing to pay some price. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost (for example, giving up minimal propositions, giving up the priority of literal meaning, accepting a certain circularity), and how do the standard positions — minimalists, contextualists, and hidden-indexicalists — each price the rejection?
