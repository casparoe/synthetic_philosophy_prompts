The exam question was: "Distinguish direct normativity from indirect normativity, and apply the distinction to the following case. A physician who is emotionally attached to her patient must decide whether to administer a risky experimental treatment that offers the only plausible chance of survival. Some philosophers maintain that she ought to deliberate using an idealized model of herself—one that brackets her emotional attachment and applies only objective clinical criteria—while others hold that her attachment should figure directly in her reasoning. Using the framework of indirect normativity, analyze whether she has reason to cultivate a disposition to bracket such attachments in clinical deliberation, or whether she has reason to preserve them. In your answer, address at least one account of idealized deliberation and any relevant criticism of such idealization, and consider whether indirect reasons protect a cultivated disposition from being overridden by direct considerations in high-stakes token cases—a problem we might call deliberative insulation."

The student’s answer (approximately 650 words) reads as follows:

Direct normativity applies reasons immediately to actions: the physician has direct reason to administer the treatment if doing so maximizes the patient’s expected welfare given the clinical evidence. Indirect normativity, by contrast, applies reasons not to particular acts but to the cultivation of dispositions, rules, or decision procedures. An agent may have indirect reason to adopt a trait—such as clinical detachment—when direct application of that trait in a given case produces a locally suboptimal result, provided the long-run benefits of the trait outweigh the local costs.

In the present case, the physician should reject the idealized model that brackets her emotional attachment. The reason is indirect. While direct normativity might favor administering the treatment if the data support it, indirect normativity tells us that the physician should cultivate and preserve emotional engagement with her patients. Peter Railton’s account of the ideal advisor shows that we should ask what an ideally rational version of ourselves would want us to want. That advisor, being fully informed and coherent, would recommend emotional detachment in clinical contexts because attachment introduces cognitive bias. But this interpretation gets the structure of indirect normativity backwards: indirect normativity does not instruct us to become our ideal advisor, but rather to cultivate the dispositions that our ideal advisor would approve of having cultivated. Since the advisor approves of emotional presence—because it sustains trust and long-term therapeutic alliance—the physician should preserve her attachment and set aside the counterfactual deliberative model. Michael Smith’s rational-self analysis reinforces this conclusion. For Smith, the rational self is defined by perfect coherence among beliefs and desires rather than by clinical neutrality, so there is no inherent tension between rationality and emotional presence in medical judgment.

Bernard Williams’s critique of external reasons is decisive here. Williams argues that genuine reasons must connect to an agent’s existing motivational set, which includes her ground projects—those deep commitments that give her life meaning. The physician’s emotional attachment to caring for others is part of such a ground project. The idealized clinical model demands that she bracket this attachment, but that demand issues in an external reason that lacks normative force for her. Consequently, she should deliberate from her actual emotional standpoint rather than from the position of an emotionally neutral observer.

One might object that indirect normativity could equally support cultivating a disposition to bracket emotions, precisely because such bracketing improves decision quality across cases. This objection, however, confuses the level of analysis. Indirect reasons govern the formation and maintenance of dispositions, not the particular outputs of those dispositions. Once the physician has cultivated emotional presence as a stable trait, indirect normativity protects that trait from being overridden by direct considerations in any single case—this is the problem of deliberative insulation. Hence she should not suspend her attachment even when the clinical stakes are exceptionally high, because doing so would erode the very disposition that indirect reason supports.
