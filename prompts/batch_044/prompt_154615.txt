Read Bernhard Schlink's The Reader — the 1995 novel and, where relevant, Stephen Daldry's 2008 film adaptation — through the lens of the debate between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen over whether Holocaust perpetrators should be explained primarily by situation, culture, and social pressure (Browning's ordinary men) or by a deep, ideologically driven willingness to kill (Goldhagen's willing executioners). Schlink's Hanna Schmitz is a former concentration-camp guard who is simultaneously presented as an almost bureaucratically unremarkable woman — ashamed more of her illiteracy than of her crimes — and as someone who repeatedly made active choices to stay in her post, to conceal documentary evidence, and, ultimately, to take her own life on the eve of her release. The novel makes her literal illiteracy a central structural motif: she refuses to learn to read for years, then acquires literacy only in prison through Michael's taped books. I want you to treat this motif not merely as biography but as a philosophical symbol for moral knowledge and its refusal. Does Hanna's illiteracy represent the passive, culturally produced moral blindness that Browning describes — people who never developed the conceptual resources to recognize atrocity — or does it function as an active, character-based refusal to know, closer to Goldhagen's claim that executioners embraced their roles and deliberately avoided moral cognition? Consider specifically the trial scene in which she asks the judge what he would have done in her place, the years of prison correspondence in which Michael sends tapes but maintains a distant, one-sided relationship, and her suicide shortly before release. Where does the narrative resist being mapped onto either explanation? Does the novel imply that the distinction between ordinary and willing executioners collapses when the perpetrator is both genuinely unremarkable and genuinely culpable? Please write your answer in American English, treating both Schlink's narrative and the historical-philosophical debate with care, and conclude by saying what the novel adds to the question of perpetrator motivation that purely historical argument cannot capture.
