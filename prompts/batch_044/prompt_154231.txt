I have been thinking on my own about whether self-deception is genuinely possible, and I have noticed that people arguing about it seem to split into two camps that rarely address each other directly. One camp insists that self-deception is conceptually impossible: deception requires that the deceiver knows the truth and intentionally hides it from the victim, so in self-deception the same person would have to both know and not know the same proposition at once, which is contradictory. They tend to say that what we call self-deception is really just motivated ignorance, bias, or compartmentalized belief without any active hiding. The other camp holds that self-deception is real and common: the mind is not a single transparent agent but can contain subsystems that suppress or redirect information, so one part can effectively hide evidence from another part without requiring a unified subject to hold contradictory beliefs.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows:

First, a numbered list of claims both sides accept — for example, about the behavioral signs of self-deception, about the existence of motivated reasoning and selective attention, and about whether people ever sincerely deny claims for which they have strong but unwelcome evidence.

Second, the precise disagreements, each phrased in neutral language that the respective side would accept as a fair statement of its own position. I expect at least two: whether a single unified self is necessary for deception to occur, and whether motivated avoidance of evidence counts as deception or merely as non-rational bias.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that resolving them would actually move one side or the other. For each crux, describe a concrete case or line of reasoning both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each side would predict or conclude from it. One crux might be whether there is good evidence that people can simultaneously maintain contradictory beliefs in functionally separated contexts; another might concern whether strategic ignorance can serve a genuine epistemic or social function — for instance, consider a professional forecaster who systematically discounts her own calibration failures not out of simple error but because her credibility with her team depends on sustained confidence. Ask whether that functional role makes her state count as real deception, or only as an adaptive bias that serves group coordination.

Finally, say explicitly whether any portion of their disagreement is merely verbal — for example, if they agree on all the psychological mechanisms but only dispute whether the word deception should be applied to intra-mental suppression — and explain why.

Write this as a formal document both parties could put their names to, not as a dialogue or a verdict on who is right. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid invoking obscure theories or technical frameworks I am unlikely to have encountered. Instead, inject one somewhat niche framing into the discussion — such as treating the forecaster case not just as individual irrationality but as a form of strategic self-deception that might stabilize collective decision-making — so that the answer cannot be pulled entirely from standard textbook summaries.
