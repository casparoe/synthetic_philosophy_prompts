In our phil seminar we've been stuck on the paradox of fiction for months — you know, Radford's setup where we feel real sadness for characters we know aren't real, and the usual answers (Walton's make-believe, Currie's simulation, the belief-condition denialists). But it's going nowhere, noone's budged an inch. Recently I brought up a case that broke the usual framing: imagine a procedurally generated NPC in a single-player narrative whose entire arc — including the death scene that made me tear up — was synthesized uniquely for my playthrough and will never be experienced by anyone else. There's no shared cultural object to point to, no authorial intent behind that specific grief. Some people said this just makes it a different kind of fiction; others said it proves our emotions are always just responses to representations, not to people, so there's no paradox. I'm wondering if the whole debate is stalled because both sides are assuming fiction is always about a public, authored artifact, and they're missing this private, synthesized case. But maybe I'm wrong — maybe the stall is just that 'genuine emotion' is being used in two ways: one about phenomenological intensity, one about rational commitment. Or maybe it's a hidden empirical question about whether our brains distinguish representational content from reality in the same way regardless of authorship. I don't want a survey of Walton and Currie. I want a diagnosis: is this a verbal disagreement, are they answering different questions (phenomology vs rationality), or is there a hidden factual issue about mental representation? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it — a conceptual split, an experiment, or should we just drop the question?
