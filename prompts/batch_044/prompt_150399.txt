You are invited to deliver the opening address at the first session of the Form and Recognition workshop, convened jointly by a graduate school of graphic design and a department of cognitive science. The audience is split: half are practicing typographers, calligraphers, and type historians who think about letterforms every day; the other half are philosophers of mind and AI researchers who have never drawn an “a” but are steeped in debates about categorization and essence. Your talk must be polished, about eight minutes, and must do more than decorate a philosophical problem with design examples. Take the concrete problem that gives the workshop its name—what makes every lowercase “a,” whether in Helvetica, in a child’s crayon scrawl, or in a distorted neon sign, still recognizably an “a”—and let that problem do structural work in your remarks. Because of it, you should be able to promise the designers that you will not reduce their lived experience of infinite stylistic variation to a hidden Platonic form, and you should be able to promise the philosophers that you will not collapse recognition into pure convention, since that would leave unexplained why some distortions destroy legibility while others preserve it. At the same time, you must refuse to offer a false synthesis that makes both sides comfortable but explains nothing.

To keep the talk from sounding like a standard lecture on family resemblance or prototype theory, inject and develop one niche conceptual device of your own: the “negative-space contract,” the claim that a letter’s identity is partly constituted by the culturally learned but perceptually mandatory shape of the absence around its strokes, and that breaking this contract—not altering the stroke itself—is what actually causes a form to fail as an “a.” Build your remarks around this device so that it does real explanatory labor: show how it honors the designer’s intuition that style lives in relation, not in isolated features, and show how it satisfies the philosopher’s demand for a non-arbitrary boundary between correct and broken recognition, without positing an essential “a”-ness hidden inside every instance. Address each half of the room directly at least once. End not with a conclusion but with an invitation: ask everyone to look at the letter “a” printed on the cover of the workshop program, and to spend one silent minute deciding whether they perceive the black form first or the white absence first—turning the philosophical dispute into an immediate, shared perceptual task rather than an abstract thesis. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; the argument must stand on its own.
