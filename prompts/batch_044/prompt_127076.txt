I work in software engineering with no formal background in philosophy, but I keep returning to a problem about physical objects that reminds me of state persistence and version control. Below are my concrete gut-level verdicts on a series of cases involving a clay statue and various transformations. I want to know whether a single, precisely stated principle can capture the pattern, or whether I have to abandon one of my judgments to save coherence. I also want predictions for two cases I have not listed.

My working intuition is something like this: an object survives if there is an unbroken chain of causal-process continuity linking its present form back to its origin, where continuity allows gradual material replacement but is broken if the object is fully destroyed and then reconstructed from a blueprint or by coincidence. I am unsure whether this is correct, and I have one case that makes me seriously doubt it.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. A sculptor molds a statue of a horse from clay. The next day she squashes it into a formless ball. Verdict: the statue is destroyed; the ball is not the statue in altered form, but a different object.

2. Over fifty years, museum conservators replace every molecule of clay in the statue, one microscopic chip at a time, always leaving the form intact and fully documented. At the end, none of the original clay remains. Verdict: this is still the original statue.

3. The statue is accidentally shattered into fragments. A conservator collects the pieces, glues them together, and fills missing gaps with matching clay. The form is restored, though about fifteen percent is now filler. Verdict: this is the original statue, restored.

4. The statue is vaporized. A technician in another city uses a 3D scan to print an identical copy from new clay. Verdict: this is a replica, not the original statue.

5. The statue is vaporized. By sheer coincidence, an identical arrangement of atoms forms in the exact same location one millisecond later, with no causal connection to the original. Verdict: this is not the original; it is a bizarre accidental duplicate.

6. The statue is made of an engineered smart clay with embedded actuators and an internal material reservoir. Someone squashes it into a ball. Left alone in a vault, the ball’s internal mechanisms gradually reshape it back into the horse form over three weeks, drawing replacement material for about five percent of the mass that was lost during squashing. No human intervenes, and the ball never ceased to exist physically; there is continuous spatiotemporal presence of the material mass, just changing shape. Verdict: I feel this is not the original statue, even though there was never a break in physical presence and most atoms are the same. This conflicts with case 2.

7. A factory maintains a living statue in continuous production: every day workers replace worn sections, and over two decades every atom has been replaced many times, but there is an uninterrupted factory log and the form is never absent. Verdict: this is one persisting statue, not a sequence of new ones.

My questions: Is there a single principle, or a small competing set, that delivers all seven verdicts with minimal complexity? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, and explain how it distinguishes case 6 from case 2. If the simplest principle must sacrifice one verdict, say which one and why. Then apply the principle to two further cases: (a) the statue is disassembled into a labeled box of parts for exactly one day and then reassembled in the original order, and (b) the statue is gradually replaced as in case 2, but during the process the museum closes for a year and the form is absent from display, though the parts are stored together in a single container. Be explicit about which of my original seven verdicts the simplest version cannot accommodate. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am looking for the conceptual structure, not references.
