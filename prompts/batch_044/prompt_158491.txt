Construct a thought experiment in which a long-standing but harmful institutional practice collapses rapidly within a single generation, engineered so that three competing explanations of moral revolution—rational argument, material interest, and honour-based dynamics of the kind Appiah describes, together with the Williams scepticism about rationalist moral progress—deliver clearly distinguishable predictions. The setting should be a realistic professional or military institution, not a fantasy world or trolley-style abstraction, and the scenario itself should occupy no more than one paragraph. Invent a specific practice (not dueling, foot-binding, or chattel slavery themselves) that had persisted for generations despite widely circulated moral objections. Engineer the case so that: (i) the decisive arguments against the practice had been available and publicly discussed for over a century without producing institutional change; (ii) the practice remained materially advantageous to the elite group that eventually abandoned it; and (iii) the collapse is triggered by a discrete, public shift in elite self-image—an honour-based reframing—rather than by new evidence or economic pressure. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each of the three frameworks predicts and why, isolate the precise structural feature that allows honour dynamics to operate independently of argument and interest, and explain how a defender of the rational-argument view could attack your construction—specifically by contending that the honour shift was itself the delayed product of prior rational persuasion permeating elite culture. Finally, state what happens to the explanatory landscape if that honour-trigger feature is removed: does the collapse still occur, and which accounts then become viable?
