Since Benacerraf first posed his dilemma in the 1970s, the literature on mathematical knowledge and the causal inertness of abstract objects has accumulated an enormous amount of machinery—Platonist intuitionism, naturalized epistemology, fictionalist reconstruals, structuralist responses—yet the core tension feels as unresolved today as it was half a century ago. In my reading, every proposed resolution either denies that mathematical objects are genuinely causally inert, which seems to miss the point, or denies that knowledge requires causal interaction, which seems to change the subject. I want a diagnosis of why this particular debate has stalled: is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of knowledge, a verbal dispute about whether "access" means causal tracking versus rational coherence, or are the two sides actually answering different questions—one about how mathematical belief is formed in actual human cognition, the other about what justifies a mathematical claim within a formal system?

Specifically, I suspect the hidden crux is whether we should model mathematical epistemology on perceptual reliabilism, where causal tracking is central, or on normative reflective equilibrium, where coherence and explanatory integration do the work, with each camp importing its home framework without arguing for it. Diagnose the stall using what each side actually commits to, not by surveying every position. Then tell me: if your diagnosis is right, what kind of result—a formal distinction between tracking and coherence conditions, an empirical finding about mathematical cognition, or a conceptual clarification about the scope of "knowledge"—would actually count as progress? And if you think no progress is possible, explain whether that is because the question is ill-posed or merely because it requires a bedrock choice between incompatible epistemic frameworks that no argument can adjudicate.
