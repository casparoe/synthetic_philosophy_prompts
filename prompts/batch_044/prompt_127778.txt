I am drafting a final exam for an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and a recent personal experience has made the problem of material constitution feel urgent rather than abstract: my late grandmother's terracotta portrait bust was shattered, and a conservator rebuilt it using a 3D-printed ceramic core with fragments of the original clay embedded only as discontinuous inclusions in the outer shell. I want an exam question—answerable in 90 minutes, worth 25 marks—that uses this embedded-fragment scenario to test whether students can do more than rehearse the standard statue-and-lump puzzles or invoke temporary coincidence and Leibniz's Law by rote. The question should require them to ask whether the reconstructed object is identical to the original bust, and to evaluate how a four-dimensionalist temporal-parts theory, a relative-identity account, and a hylomorphic view each handle a case in which the original material is neither continuously present nor wholly absent, but distributed discontinuously within a new substrate. Please write the exam question, a model answer of approximately 500 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer—which must apply at least two of the three views specifically to the discontinuous-inclusion problem and identify a decisive weakness in one—from a B answer, which correctly summarizes the views but fails to engage the reconstructed-bust scenario. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
