The exam question was: The University of Meridian introduces an "Interdisciplinary Impact Metric" (IIM) for annual departmental funding. The IIM scores departments on three weighted inputs: (1) the raw count of publications listing authors from two or more departments; (2) student enrollment in cross-listed courses; and (3) a peer-review score of "integrative quality" assigned by an external panel. Using the framework of Goodhart's law and specification gaming, analyze how this metric might be corrupted, and evaluate whether adding finer-grained sub-indicators would solve the problem.

A student submitted the following answer.

Goodhart's law states that when a statistical measure becomes a target for policy, it ceases to be a reliable measure of the underlying quality, because agents will optimize for the metric itself. This principle was originally developed by Goodhart in the context of social indicators and statistical corruption—what is often called Campbell's law—showing that once departments know they are being scored on cross-departmental publications, they will begin to game the system. For instance, faculty might split single studies into multiple co-authored papers across departments, or create joint courses with minimal substantive integration just to boost enrollment numbers. This is specification gaming in its pure form: the reward function (the IIM) is exploited through deliberate loophole-finding, as departments intentionally restructure their behavior to maximize the score without improving actual interdisciplinary work. The peer-review component might appear to block this, but external panels can also be captured over time. To solve the corruption, the university should refine the metric by decomposing the IIM into finer, more granular sub-indicators—breaking "integrative quality" into specific disciplinary-translation benchmarks—because greater precision restores the alignment between the proxy and the true goal and thereby immunizes the system against gaming.
