You direct the curriculum review board at a museum of comparative genocide studies. Every quarter you receive proposals for new educational modules—lesson plans, exhibit texts, and short documentary scripts—that explain why ordinary people participated in mass killing. You need a reusable procedure your board (historians, educators, and one community representative without advanced historical training) can run consistently within a two-week review cycle, to decide whether a proposal's explanation of perpetrator behavior is defensible, oversimplified, or conceptually confused.

Ground the procedure in the well-known historiographical tension between Christopher Browning's argument in Ordinary Men—situational and organizational pressures, including peer dynamics, career incentives, dehumanization of victims, and incremental escalation, can explain much killing without requiring deep prior ideological hatred—and Daniel Goldhagen's argument in Hitler's Willing Executioners—that a pervasive, culturally specific eliminationist antisemitism created a reservoir of willingness making ordinary Germans eager executioners rather than reluctant conformists.

Design the procedure as an ordered protocol: the specific questions to ask at each stage, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the protocol stops with a clear "revise or reject," and where it must hand the decision to the board's values judgment rather than historical fact. The protocol must detect three hard errors: invoking peer pressure or bureaucratic compartmentalization without addressing whether victims were already dehumanized in the culture; invoking ideology without addressing whether organizational context was necessary to translate belief into action; and committing an additive-causation fallacy by listing situational and cultural factors side by side without specifying their interaction or temporal scale. Include explicit decision nodes for "explanatory flattening," where distinct levels of analysis are collapsed into interchangeable causes.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. A high-school lesson plan explaining Reserve Police Battalion 101's killings almost entirely through unit cohesion, fear of isolation, and bureaucratic routinization, mentioning antisemitic propaganda only as background noise.

2. A documentary treatment arguing that local auxiliary forces in occupied territories killed primarily because they had long internalized an eliminationist worldview specific to their region, with organizational structure mentioned only as an incidental enabling condition.

3. A museum panel text stating that "both situational pressure and cultural ideology caused the violence, and either one alone would have produced the same outcome," treating the two explanations as independent and interchangeable.

In your applications, include at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict the board is likely to resist—such as rejecting a proposal that sounds balanced but collapses explanatory layers into an additive model. Also specify precisely what the procedure cannot do: which distinctions require expert historical judgment it cannot automate, and which normative questions (for example, whether emphasizing situational factors risks morally excusing perpetrators) lie outside its scope and must be flagged for separate deliberation.

Present the deliverable as a reusable checklist with decision nodes, not as an essay about the debate. Mark every hand-off point explicitly.
