Write a blank-verse poem of about thirty lines voiced as Luis de Molina confronting the grounding objection to middle knowledge: if God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—what a free will would do in circumstance C—before any decree, what makes those counterfactuals true? Structure the poem so that its syntax and enjambment perform the "middle" status itself; lines should hang suspended between divine foreknowledge and creaturely determination, never resolving upward to a necessary ground nor downward to a causal source. Use a turn near line twenty to move from surveying feasible worlds—those God can actualize given middle knowledge—to the recognition that the counterfactuals seem to float without foundation, yet remain genuinely known. Render the threefold distinction among natural knowledge (what is possible), middle knowledge (what free creatures would choose), and free knowledge (what God wills) through concrete image rather than exposition—perhaps light passing through unmade glass, or paths branching in soil that has not yet been planted. The poem should be precise enough that a philosopher of religion recognizes Molina's scientia media and the specific problem of transworld depravity, not a generic omniscience trope, and it should leave open whether the grounding objection actually defeats middle knowledge. After the poem, add a brief note of no more than one hundred words stating where your reasoning is most vulnerable—particularly whether the formal analogy between syntactic suspension and metaphysical grounding is carrying illegitimate weight—and specify what discovery (for example, a defensible account of the ground of counterfactuals of freedom) would lead you to conclude that the poem's tension is decorative rather than diagnostic. Write in American English.
