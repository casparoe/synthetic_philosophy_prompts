For a graduate workshop on philosophy of history, I am developing a conceptual framework that treats macro-historical change as governed not merely by complex causation but by genuine dynamical chaos—sensitive dependence on initial conditions in the strict mathematical sense—rather than by mere epistemic opacity. Within this framework I draw a sharp distinction between "explanatory chaos," in which nonlinear interactions defeat our predictive models while the past remains deterministically fixed, and "ontological chaos," in which macro-level historical states are genuinely underdetermined by prior micro-conditions at critical branch points, so that identical prior states could yield different macro-outcomes without violating any physical law. I want to understand the implications of adopting ontological chaos specifically—not the weaker explanatory version—for three historiographical practices: first, counterfactual reasoning about pivotal micro-events (for example, the wrong turn taken by the Archduke's driver in Sarajevo in 1914); second, moral evaluation of agents whose minor actions cascade into catastrophic or world-altering consequences; and third, the legitimacy of "for want of a nail" explanatory narratives that trace massive outcomes back to tiny initial differences. Please describe the most important implications of this ontological-chaos thesis for each practice, indicating for each whether it undermines standard historiographical method, transforms it in a specific direction, or reinforces it; and specify whether that implication genuinely requires ontological chaos or could equally be derived from explanatory chaos alone. Then identify which of the three practices is most radically disrupted.
