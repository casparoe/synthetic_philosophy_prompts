I'm writing a philosophy-of-mind essay and recently had a personal reason to take split-brain cases seriously: my sister is being evaluated for a partial corpus callosotomy to treat intractable epilepsy, and her neurologist mentioned that cutting only some interhemispheric fibers can leave patients cognitively intact yet subtly dissociated. That made me wonder whether consciousness can be split in degrees, not just in the classic all-or-nothing way.

At a study session, two classmates argued about this. Maya said that after a full callosotomy there are genuinely two conscious minds in one body. She noted that the right hemisphere can solve spatial puzzles, match odors to pictures via left-hand selection, and display emotional reactions—such as apparent frustration—when the speaking left hemisphere denies knowing something, all while remaining silent to verbal questioning. If one hemisphere can harbor experiences and intentions that are entirely inaccessible to the other, she argued, then they are separate subjects of experience, not merely damaged modules of one person.

Leo pushed back. He said the right hemisphere's behavior is just sophisticated modular processing without a subject behind it—like an autopilot. Real consciousness, he claimed, requires a unified narrative self-model and the capacity for linguistic reportability, both of which the isolated right hemisphere lacks. He added that in intact brains we do not think there are two people just because hemispheres specialize, so the corpus callosum must be doing more than networking: it integrates modules into a single workspace. Maya replied that the callosum is essentially a data cable—connecting two computers does not make them one computer—and that the surgical split merely reveals what was always somewhat separate.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. Begin with a one-sentence bottom line stating who is closer to the truth overall. Then rate each of their four main moves—(1) Maya's interpretation of right-hemisphere task performance and emotional display as evidence for a second subject, (2) Leo's appeal to narrative self-model and linguistic reportability as necessary conditions for consciousness, (3) Leo's analogy between intact-brain specialization and post-surgical separation, and (4) Maya's data-cable rebuttal—on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each. Finally, say what both miss about partial callosotomies, where some fibers remain and dissociations appear graded rather than binary. Keep your response within about 350 words; no bibliographies or citations are needed.
