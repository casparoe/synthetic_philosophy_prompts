I am leading a single fifty-minute seminar for first-year political theory students on Amartya Sen's thesis that famines are failures of entitlement rather than natural disasters of food availability, with reference to the Bengal famine, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward. Do not explain Sen's theory to me; design the pedagogical sequence.

First, specify the exact dependency order: what distinction—between availability decline and distribution failure, or between direct, trade-based, and labor-based entitlements—must be secured before anything else can land, and which of the three historical cases should come first, second, and last, with justification for that sequence.

Second, identify the one carrying example—drawn from Bengal 1943, the Irish famine, or the Great Leap Forward—that should anchor the session, explaining precisely which feature of it illustrates the entitlement mechanism and which historical complexity must be stripped away so it does not become a history lesson rather than a conceptual one. Then construct a minimal hypothetical scenario—the Archipelago case—involving two adjacent regions with adequate aggregate harvest but divergent starvation outcomes due solely to a change in labor-market entitlement, not weather; specify how this invented case isolates the logic from the historical noise.

Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order students will form them: likely the belief that famine requires an absolute shortage of food, that Sen's view eliminates natural causes entirely, that the Great Leap Forward is the purest case because it involves state policy, and that entitlement analysis applies only to market economies. For each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the one-sentence correction or reframing.

Fourth, propose a final check—one question students must answer in writing—that distinguishes those who have grasped the entitlement structure from those who have merely adopted the slogan that famines are political.

Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as omitted: for example, the formal modeling of exchange entitlements, the comparative demographic statistics across the three cases, and any bibliographic discussion of Sen's sources. The deliverable is the teaching plan, not the lecture content.
