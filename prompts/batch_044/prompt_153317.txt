I am drafting a proposal for my school’s communications policy. The administration has recently replaced terms such as “exclusion” with “alternative pathway provision” and “funding reduction” with “budgetary optimisation”, insisting that this is only courteous language. I wish to argue that altering the vocabulary does not merely soften descriptions but changes what people are able to grasp about the decisions themselves. A classmate replies that I am confusing politeness with deception: so long as everyone understands the meaning, no harm is done. I sense there is a stronger philosophical objection here—perhaps about how the words available to us constrain what we can think about institutional actions—but I cannot formulate it precisely. Is my intuition defensible? Furthermore, I propose a “dual-register” charter requiring every official document to present both the administrative term and an unvarnished plain-language equivalent, with any term revised more than twice in one academic year triggering an automatic student review. Would adopting such a charter be a reasonable defence of clear thinking, or an impractical fixation on wording? Please answer in British English.
