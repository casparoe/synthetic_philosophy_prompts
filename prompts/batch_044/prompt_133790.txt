I'm presenting a working paper at our philosophy of science colloquium next Thursday to an audience of about fifteen: mostly philosophers of science with backgrounds in evolutionary biology and physics, two causal decision theorists who are skeptical of non-causal correlations, and no dedicated game theorists or superrationality specialists. The thesis I defend is that the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, when analyzed together, fail to establish superrationality in the usual sense and instead reveal a hidden dependency I call algorithmic co-accessibility—the claim that two separated agents can only achieve correlated rational choices if their decision procedures share a common causal ancestor or are instantiated within the same underlying computation. I argue specifically that the Luring Lottery does not break superrational correlation through its probabilistic lure, but through an epistemic partition that renders each agent's counterfactual choice-set structurally inaccessible to the other, thereby dissolving the correlation without invoking hidden causation. Please prepare me for the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered from the most damaging to the least, phrased in the vocabulary of philosophers of science and causal decision theorists rather than in the jargon of the LessWrong crowd; for each objection include a brief reply suited to a two-minute exchange and a frank note on where the reply is weakest. I also want to know which point I should concede in my opening remarks rather than defend, and I want you to identify the single objection I am most hoping nobody asks—the one that presses whether algorithmic co-accessibility secretly reintroduces causal interaction through the back door by treating shared computation as a disguised physical linkage. Present everything in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one writes a careful letter of advice.
