I am a journalist who covers science and governance for an independent, member-owned research collective—essentially a democratic newsroom with no corporate owner, direct membership votes on editorial stances, and a charter that requires every member to present our collective positions as unified, permitting dissent only in internal forums.

Last quarter the collective voted to endorse a specific solar-radiation management framework. I have since obtained credible documents showing that three members whose votes formed the decisive majority held undisclosed consulting agreements with firms positioned to profit from adoption of that framework. Our charter contains no public-whistleblower exception; if I publish an independent analysis that contradicts the endorsed stance, I breach the rules I signed, deploy shared resources to undermine a democratic decision, and risk collapsing the cooperative’s credibility with our audience. Yet remaining silent also feels like complicity in a captured process.

There is no state apparatus here—no legislature to petition, no court to appeal to—only a voluntary association whose legitimacy rests entirely on procedural fairness. I want to know whether my agreement to abide by collective votes generates a moral duty of the kind citizens are often said to owe a government, or whether it remains a merely contractual convenience that dissolves once hidden interests corrupt the democratic procedure. Specifically, if I violate our bylaws openly to expose the capture, can that constitute a principled refusal rather than self-serving freeloading—particularly if I voluntarily accept a concrete penalty, such as surrendering my membership and access to our archives? Or does the absence of any public legal framework make such open defiance inherently unfair, regardless of my willingness to bear costs?

Moreover, does the duty to honor the collective decision depend on others also playing fairly, so that the hidden consulting contracts release me from obligation? Give me a committed answer: take a definite position on whether the duty to obey survives this kind of associative corruption, on whether public breach can be morally justified in a non-state democratic body, and on the exact conditions—if any—under which such defiance preserves rather than betrays the cooperative’s democratic purpose. Do not lay out every school of thought and leave me to choose; argue for the view you think is right, and say what is at stake if you are wrong.
