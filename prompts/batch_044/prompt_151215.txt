I am working through the Molinist literature on middle knowledge for a philosophy of religion course, and I have been trying to formalize a distinction that I do not think is usually made explicit in the standard debates. On the usual account, God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—true propositions about what specific individuals would freely do in various circumstances—prior to any creative decree, and these ground divine providence without causally determining the outcomes. I want to compare this with a parallel but structurally different category: collective counterfactuals of institutional freedom, by which I mean propositions about how distributed groups—say, a legislature, a corporation, or a religious community—would freely respond as a unit to a regulatory or moral framework, where the relevant freedom is not reducible to any single member's will. In what ways does the Molinist account of middle knowledge apply, or fail to apply, to these collective cases? Specifically, does the grounding objection—that counterfactuals of freedom must be grounded in the essential properties of the agents involved—become more severe when there is no unified essence of the group, or can the Molinist respond by treating institutional responses as fully supervenient on individual-level counterfactuals? And does this comparison suggest that middle knowledge is inherently atomistic, or does extending it to collective agency actually provide the Molinist with additional resources against open-theist objections that deny God any knowledge of contingent future free actions?
