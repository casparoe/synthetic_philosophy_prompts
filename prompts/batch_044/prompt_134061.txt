I am constructing a novel about the moral architecture of the twentieth century, and in one chapter an aging jurist delivers a private lecture summarizing Hannah Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial. He puts it this way: Arendt maintained that Eichmann was not ideologically driven but an unremarkable bureaucrat who participated in genocide primarily through careerist ambition and a peculiar cognitive vacancy she called thoughtlessness—by which she meant an almost physical inability to inhabit another person's perspective, as though his inner life were a blank sheet upon which administrative procedure wrote itself. From this she inferred that evil in the modern world is banal not because it is common, but because it requires no deep malice, only the suspension of critical judgment within a hierarchical system. Consequently, she held that moral responsibility largely dissolves when individual decision-making is subsumed by bureaucratic protocol, and that the Jerusalem trial was conceptually flawed: it treated Eichmann as a uniquely wicked agent rather than recognizing him as an interchangeable functionary, which is why she believed the court should have focused on the structural machinery rather than hanging one replaceable cog.

As a writer rather than a philosopher, I am drawn to the dramatic symmetry of this account, yet I suspect my jurist has streamlined Arendt's argument for narrative effect. Before I commit this speech to the manuscript, I need an accuracy audit. Please examine the summary point by point: distinguish what Arendt genuinely argued about Eichmann's psychology, the specific meaning of thoughtlessness, and the relationship between bureaucratic routine and personal culpability, from what is a popular misreading or an overstatement inserted for dramatic coherence. In particular, address whether she truly believed that structural position negates individual moral responsibility, whether she considered the trial illegitimate on those grounds, and whether her concept of the banality of evil carries the deterministic implication that anyone in Eichmann's position would necessarily have acted as he did. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of secondary literature—only for a precise separation of the defensible core from the distortions, so that my character's voice remains intellectually credible without becoming a caricature.
