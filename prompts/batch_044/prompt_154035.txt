I am drafting a thesis chapter on collective rationality in deliberative institutions, and I have repeatedly observed a structural pattern that does not sit neatly within the standard formulation of the discursive dilemma. Imagine a faculty board split into three overlapping sub-committees, each assigned a distinct subset of premises that jointly entail a policy conclusion. Every sub-committee aggregates its premises sincerely via majority rule and reports a derived intermediate judgement back to the full board. The board then treats these reports as premises for a second-stage derivation, applying the same aggregation rule to reach a collective conclusion. Because membership overlaps, an individual’s sincere judgements are entered into multiple sub-committees, yet the second-stage rule weights each intermediate report equally, irrespective of how many unique members produced it. Consequently, the board’s final conclusion can reverse when the chair merely reassigns which premises belong to which sub-committee, even though no individual judgement and no aggregation rule has changed. This is not the classic discursive dilemma—there is no direct contradiction between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation at the full-board level—nor is it strategic voting in the usual sense, as every voter remains sincere throughout. It seems rather to be a framing-dependency generated by nested aggregation with non-independent intermediate inputs. Is there an established term in the judgement-aggregation literature for this kind of procedural reframing effect? If not, how should one conceptually delineate it from the discursive dilemma and from path-dependency in sequential voting?
