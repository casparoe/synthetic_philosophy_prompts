I am a novelist presently at work on a novel in which an economist defends her discipline's reliance on stark simplifications. In preparing, I have been reflecting on how narrative characterization operates—how a novelist isolates a single psychological vector, stripping away the noise of biography to expose a mechanism of desire or guilt, knowing full well that no actual person behaves with such consistency. This led me to Milton Friedman's well-known defense of unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling, particularly the position often called his "F-twist," articulated in his essay on the methodology of positive economics. I recently encountered a summary—delivered in the voice of a confident popularizer of philosophy of science—that runs as follows.

"Friedman argued that in positive economics the realism of a model's assumptions stands in inverse relation to its scientific merit: the more patently false an assumption—such as agents possessing infinite computational capacity or markets clearing instantaneously—the more effectively it isolates the essential causal mechanism. He therefore maintained that criticizing an economic model for unrealistic psychology or sociology is a fundamental category error, since assumptions function solely as predictive instruments and never as descriptions of the world. From this it follows that any model whose predictions are empirically sustained is fully vindicated, regardless of how fantastical its premises may appear, and that demands for behavioral realism or descriptive fidelity represent not merely wrong-headed objections but a deep misunderstanding of what scientific method requires."

Something in this strikes me as both too sweeping and suspiciously neat. Would a careful reader of Friedman accept each of these claims? Please audit the summary with precision: separate what accurately reflects his argument, what overstates or subtly distorts it, and what introduces errors he would explicitly reject—particularly regarding whether predictive success alone can vindicate arbitrary assumptions, whether he intended his defense to apply to all unrealistic premises or only to those serving as isolating idealizations, and whether he truly regarded behavioral criticisms as a category error rather than as concerns about scope or purpose. I am especially interested in whether the summary's concluding implication—that demands for realism miss the point entirely—actually follows from Friedman's view, or whether it smuggles in a stronger instrumentalism than he endorsed.
