I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, but next Thursday I am presenting to an interdisciplinary seminar—mostly historians of modern Europe and philosophers of collective memory, plus one political scientist who specializes in Zionist historiography and a few graduate students—defending a thesis I have developed outside my field. My claim is that national myths surrounding lost causes should be understood not primarily as heroic narratives or ideological cover, but as a kind of distributed consensus mechanism that allows collective identity to remain coherent when the historical record is fragmented. I compare the Dunkirk evacuation myth and the Masada narrative as structurally contrasting cases. In Dunkirk, the myth achieves long-term stability because the defeat is fully externalized: the adversary causes the retreat, the collective survives intact, and later generations can align around a single thread of national survival without internal contradiction. In Masada, however, the defeat involves irreducible internal division—rebels against Rome, but also secular versus religious interpretations of the mass suicide—so the myth cannot synchronize collective memory across its constituent groups; it fractures rather than stabilizes. I borrow analogies from distributed systems, specifically the distinction between external node failure and internal network partition, not to claim that societies literally run algorithms, but to propose that this framework explains why some lost-cause myths endure while others remain chronically contested.

The audience knows the archival details of both events, is skeptical of engineering metaphors in cultural analysis, and will watch carefully for misrepresentations of how Masada functions in contemporary Israeli identity politics. I need a prep sheet listing the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are, phrased in the disciplinary vocabulary each group uses. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, the weakest point in my reply, and flag the one question I am hoping nobody asks—probably whether my framework can accommodate myths that succeed precisely by preserving internal contradiction rather than resolving it.
