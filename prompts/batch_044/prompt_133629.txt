I facilitate a graduate workshop in philosophy of science and material culture—fourteen participants, one ninety-minute session, all familiar with basic philosophy of archaeology but not specialists. Design a runnable classroom simulation that makes participants feel the force of archaeological underdetermination: specifically, that material traces radically underdetermine reconstructions of both belief and social institution, and that accumulating more artifacts can proliferate competing interpretations rather than converge on one. The simulation should work as follows. Divide the group into two teams of seven; give each team a different, partially overlapping subset of replicated material traces—physical descriptions, spatial diagrams, wear-pattern notes, and deliberately weighted “negative spaces” (expected artifacts that are absent)—drawn from a single hidden archaeological context. Each team must reconstruct both a specific belief complex and an institutional structure, then defend it in a structured deliberation. Include explicit roles within each team (pattern-analyst, ethnographic-analogy skeptic, institutional-reconstruction advocate, negative-space interpreter), rules that prevent either team from seeing the other’s full trace-set until after initial presentations, and a non-monetary payoff scheme (consensus votes weighted by argument quality, with “publication credit” stakes that determine a final collective ranking). Choose one specific hidden “ground truth” belief-institution pair for me to reveal only after presentations—do not leave this open—and design the trace subsets so that both reconstructions are materially defensible but incompatible. Include a timeline with exact phases, cheap reproducible materials (paper cards, printed diagrams), a debrief that maps each simulation phase onto a specific inference problem in archaeology (e.g., middle-range linking arguments, analogical overreach, the interpretation of absence), and an honest note on which feature of real archaeological inference—such as stratigraphic chronology or taphonomic distortion—the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally. Take a committed position: choose whether to include an explicit “analogical-reasoning license” card mechanism that teams must play to invoke ethnographic comparison, or to leave analogical reasoning implicit and let it emerge as unexamined bias; defend your choice. Also be definite about whether negative spaces should carry equal evidential weight to present objects. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first run—particularly the tendency for teams to converge on the facilitator’s unstated expectations—and specify the correction without breaking the simulation’s integrity. Lead with your bottom-line design decision in the first sentence; then present the reasoning, rules, timeline, materials, debrief, predicted failure, and frank limitation. No preamble, no restatement of this prompt, and take definite positions throughout rather than surveying options.
