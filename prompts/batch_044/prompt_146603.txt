I'm giving the opening remarks at my mentor's retirement dinner. She taught philosophy of action for forty years; about half the guests are philosophers who know her work, and half are family and friends who don't. She always insisted that Galen Strawson's Basic Argument is correct—true moral responsibility is impossible because nothing can be causa sui—and she lived that conviction by refusing to take deep personal credit for her achievements. I want to honor her without contradicting her, which means the speech must not claim she "made herself" or "earned her legacy" in any ultimate sense, yet it must still genuinely celebrate her and comfort family members who want to praise her. To do this, structure the remarks around a framing I'll call "causal inheritance": since self-creation is impossible, every intellectual achievement is necessarily a joint product of inherited causes—teachers, books, accidents, students—and her distinctiveness lies not in being the sole author of her life but in being the unique node through which that network became visible and transformed others. Make the argument do real structural work: identify exactly which standard sentences of praise become false or misleading under Strawson's view, which ones survive, and how gratitude can be expressed without attributing basic desert. The speech should be precise enough that philosophers see the argument applied correctly, and warm and clear enough that family feel included rather than lectured. Please begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary.
