Consider the position that Jeffrey conditionalization—revising beliefs by distributing credence across a partition weighted by the posterior probabilities of the evidence propositions—is not merely a practical heuristic for uncertain observation, but the fundamental rule of belief revision, with standard Bayesian conditioning as the limiting case where the evidential partition is sharp. Further, suppose that to resolve the partition-dependence objection, one should apply Jeffrey's rule at a second-order level: weighting the first-order updates by a prior distribution over possible evidential partitions, so that the resulting credence is invariant under refinement of the evidential categories. The claim is that this second-order Jeffrey procedure eliminates the arbitrariness critics cite, and that any rival—whether strict conditioning on an artificially sharp proxy, or a radical Jeffrey update that disregards prior structural constraints—fails to respect the symmetry of evidential uncertainty.

Play this position straight: what would have to be true for it to collapse? Which formal commitments are load-bearing—must the second-order weights be uniquely defined, must the prior over partitions avoid smuggling in sharpness, or must the invariance result hold across all belief structures? What specific counterexample, formal result, or intuitive case—perhaps involving nested evidential uncertainty or the problem of old evidence—would count as decisive rather than merely awkward? What would the position's defenders themselves concede as counting against it: a failure of invariance under a particular refinement, a demonstration that the second-order prior is doing hidden work, or an argument that the procedure overgenerates in continuous observation spaces? Is there any development that would genuinely move them back toward standard conditioning, or is the view insulated by its ability to absorb anomalies into the choice of second-order prior?
