I retired recently from a long career in physical chemistry and have begun reading philosophy in earnest. An essay I came across summarizes deontological ethics in a way that puzzles me. The author describes what he calls 'the paradox of deontology': if moral rules are genuinely independent of consequences, then a physician who refuses to deceive a murderer about a patient's whereabouts—thereby allowing a killing—has acted blamelessly, which seems absurd. The author claims the paradox dissolves once we recognize that deontological rules are actually justified by their long-run welfare effects: prohibitions on lying and killing are maintained because societies that honor them flourish. Therefore, he argues, deontology is not truly non-consequentialist but rather an indirect, long-horizon form of consequentialism in which the agent applies rules rather than calculating case by case. He further claims that Kant acknowledged this in his Groundwork, stating that we treat humanity as an end precisely because doing so maximizes rational agency across society.

I suspect this framing collapses important distinctions—perhaps between justifying a moral principle and acting from it, or between Kant's own view and later rule-deontological theories—but I lack the background to identify the errors precisely. Is this a fair summary of the paradox and of deontology's relationship to consequentialist reasoning? Please examine the account carefully: which parts capture genuine debates in moral philosophy, which conflate separate positions, and does the author's conclusion—that deontology is essentially indirect consequentialism—actually follow from the accurate portions? I do not need references or a reading list; I simply want to know whether this essay's framing is trustworthy before I recommend it to a discussion group I am joining.
