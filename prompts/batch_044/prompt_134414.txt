I'm part of a small collective that maintains a shared fabrication workshop funded by a single, non-renewable grant. We are four people: one uses a motorized wheelchair and requires a custom-built workstation that occupies nearly half our floor space and consumes a disproportionate share of our maintenance budget; another has substantial private wealth and could easily rent independent studio space but prefers to work within the collective; a third is technically gifted and produces commissioned pieces whose profits currently subsidize about forty percent of our operating costs; and I fall somewhere in between, with moderate skills and no special physical needs. The grant is meant to sustain the workshop for exactly two years, and we must decide now how to divide both the cash and the access hours, because the space cannot physically accommodate all of us working at full capacity simultaneously.

The disagreement has crystallized into three incompatible framings. The wheelchair user argues that fairness requires distributing resources so that each member achieves roughly the same effective range of completed projects — she notes that her body converts standard equipment into usable output at a far lower rate, so giving everyone identical dollar shares or identical clock hours actually leaves her with far less real opportunity. The wealthy member counters that equality should be measured in raw resource shares: since none of us caused our physical conditions or our private bank balances, weighting allocations by need rewards a feature of her biology she did not choose, while penalizing him for being able-bodied and self-sufficient. The skilled producer takes a third line, insisting that without her labor the workshop would collapse, so the grant should be allocated in proportion to each person's contribution to the collective's survival, with equality understood as equal reward for equal productive input rather than equal shares regardless of output.

I am genuinely unsure which framing captures what fairness demands in a pooled-resource setting like ours, and I am particularly troubled by whether there is a principled way to accommodate different physical conversion rates without either treating bodies as morally arbitrary differences that must be neutralized, or without collapsing into a system where need alone determines entitlement. Could you work through the philosophical structure of our dispute — identifying which conception of equality each of us is implicitly invoking, whether the three positions can be reconciled without contradiction, and which argument holds up best once we stop treating "equal treatment" as a single, self-evident standard?
