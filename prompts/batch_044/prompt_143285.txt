I have just come out of a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether happiness can be pursued collectively or only cultivated in private. The dispute began with Émilie du Châtelet's treatise on happiness, which I have been studying in connection with a course on Enlightenment moral psychology. My friend, who works in urban planning, insisted that du Châtelet's vision is irreducibly aristocratic: she ties genuine happiness to disciplined study and to passionate love, conditions that depend on leisure and education, and therefore her framework offers nothing useful to people navigating shared scarcity or public obligations. I tried to defend the view that her real contribution is structural—she distinguishes a counterfeit form of happiness, the restless pursuit of distraction, from a genuine state that requires actively ordering one's passions rather than suppressing them—and that this distinction applies precisely to collective life, because a society that treats happiness as passive consumption will overuse its common resources of attention, care, and mutual trust. Now I must deliver the commencement address at a university graduation ceremony next month. The audience will be split between humanities faculty who know du Châtelet's work well and family members who do not. I need the address to do real philosophical work: it should be shaped by her framework so that I literally cannot fall back on familiar graduation formulas such as telling graduates to follow their bliss or assuring them that happiness is only a personal choice, because those formulations violate her logic of disciplined passion. At the same time, the speech must comfort and inspire people who are not philosophers, and it should acknowledge the shared intellectual and emotional obligations that graduates are entering, without becoming a policy lecture on collective resources. Please write an eight-minute address that respects both halves of the room, allows her idea of happiness as an active practice to constrain what can and cannot be said, and navigates the tension between private cultivation and public responsibility.
