I'm writing up my philosophical profile on historical objectivity for a colloquium reading group, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(1) I treat Ranke's injunction to show how things actually were as an ethical regulative ideal—historians should minimize presentist distortion—rather than as an achievable epistemic endpoint.

(2) I accept Carr's thesis that every historical fact is selected and arranged by the historian's present concerns, but I deny that this makes objectivity impossible; it merely makes objectivity procedural rather than metaphysical.

(3) I take Hayden White's tropological analysis seriously: historical narratives necessarily deploy one of the master tropes (romance, tragedy, comedy, satire), and the choice among them is not empirically determined by the archival record.

(4) Nevertheless, I believe in what I call archival friction: some narratives can maintain coherence only by suppressing or misrepresenting available primary sources, while others allow those sources to constrain the plot. This friction is real, I claim, even though the archive itself is constituted by prior institutional decisions about what to preserve.

(5) I think evolutionary psychology gives us reason to distrust our default narrative preferences—for instance, our tendency to impose tragic structures on complex events—but I do not think this debunks historical knowledge; it only means we should correct our tropological defaults against the archive.

(6) I want to maintain that historians can be wrong about the past in a robust, non-pragmatic sense—not merely that they are using a less useful vocabulary—yet I also reject representationalism about historical knowledge, since I deny that historical statements mirror a mind-independent past.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision to my set would clean up the most problems at once. If any of my commitments rest on a confused premise, or if the task as posed can't be done well because I've smuggled in an ambiguity or an incompatible pair that can't be repaired without abandoning the whole framework, say so plainly instead of complying politely—I'm asking for pushback, not flattery.
