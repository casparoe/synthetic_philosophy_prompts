As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am designing a 90-minute workshop for sixteen advanced undergraduates in philosophy of science and epistemology, and I want an in-person simulation—not a lecture or reading group—that makes them physically experience the tension between bootstrapping reliability and what philosophers call the problem of easy knowledge. The core idea is that once a group grants itself basic perceptual knowledge through an internal protocol, closure principles seem to allow it to derive immunity from radical skepticism almost without cost, yet that derivation feels suspiciously like using a faculty to vouch for itself. To prevent the exercise from feeling like a rehearsed classroom script, I want it organized around a framing I am calling recursive calibration debt: the notion that whenever a collective uses its own internal protocol to certify that protocol’s reliability, it accrues an invisible liability that must be settled either by accepting circular validation or by surrendering the closure-derived conclusions it hoped to secure.

Please design a runnable structured deliberation or game with explicit rules, assigned roles, and small non-monetary stakes—points, tokens, or social credit—so that the circularity becomes tangible rather than merely described. The participants should begin with a shared basic-belief task, such as identifying properties of objects distributed in sealed envelopes using only one agreed-upon perceptual method; they should then use that same method to validate the method itself, and finally attempt to extend their results to rule out a global error hypothesis represented by one hidden envelope. They must construct the sequence of inference steps themselves and only recognize where they have arrived at the end. Provide a debrief that maps each moment in the activity onto the corresponding epistemological move—basic knowledge, closure, the bootstrap loop, and the forfeiture option—together with an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time the exercise is run and how to correct for it. Be explicit about which feature of the bootstrapping problem—such as the first-person singular version versus the distributed collective version—the activity cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, must supply directly. The formal names of the philosophical problems should not be revealed until after the debrief.
