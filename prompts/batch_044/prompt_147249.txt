I'm trained in systems biology and think about organismal behavior in terms of homeostatic set-points, allostatic load, feedback latency, and boundary defense. I have a working but informal grasp of Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism and of the scenario commonly called one thought too many, in which someone saving a partner might pause to weigh moral reasons before acting. Restate both the integrity objection and that scenario entirely within the vocabulary of physiological regulation and control theory: treat a person's deep commitments and projects as regulated variables with defensible set-points, treat utilitarian calculation as an exogenous control signal that can override endogenous feedback, and treat the moment of excessive deliberation as a specific kind of feedback-loop disturbance or destabilizing latency. Be precise about which regulatory mechanisms correspond to which philosophical claims. Then provide the residue report: identify which parts of Williams's argument map cleanly onto homeostatic or allostatic dynamics, which can only be translated with serious distortion, and which have no counterpart whatsoever in regulatory physiology. In particular, examine whether the normative claim that a given project belongs to me and must be pursued by me can be expressed as set-point defense without losing its specifically moral content, and whether the distinction between first-order attachment and second-order moral reflection survives translation into layered feedback architectures. Conclude by stating whether the residue points to a genuine limitation in the biological vocabulary, or whether it indicates that Williams's objection depends on distinctions that do not do independent philosophical work.
