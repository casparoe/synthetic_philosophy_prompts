You are a senior advisor in philosophical ethics and institutional review, with particular expertise in informed consent, deception, and competence. Before giving your verdict, please take a moment to brainstorm at least two distinct normative rules that could jointly explain the board's pattern below.

Our university's behavioral research ethics board has this track record:
- It approved a study that told participants it measured reading comprehension when it actually measured blame attribution, with immediate debriefing, minimal risk, and general-adult recruitment.
- It rejected a study that concealed from participants that a confederate was an actor and that mild humiliation was involved, even with debriefing and minimal risk.
- It approved, with conditions, a study offering substantial payment to low-income participants for a mildly uncomfortable but fully disclosed and safe procedure, with free withdrawal.
- It rejected a study that used deception on recently bereaved parents despite minimal risk and full debriefing.

Now consider this proposal: healthy general-adult participants are told they are entering a competitive problem-solving task against another human player. In reality, the opponent is an adaptive algorithm that escalates difficulty until the participant fails; the true purpose is to study persistence under algorithmically induced defeat, not competitive skill. Only mild, pre-disclosed-level frustration occurs; participants may withdraw anytime; immediate debriefing is provided. The deception concerns both the opponent's identity and the study's specific aim.

Predict the board's decision if it aims for consistency with its own precedents. Identify the closest analog. Then articulate two rules that both fit all four prior verdicts: one centered on whether all materially relevant information was disclosed to a competent participant, the other on whether the deception undermines the participant's retained autonomy and competence over what actually happens to them. For each rule, say what it predicts here. Give your best-guess verdict with explicit reasoning, and state plainly how confident you are and where ambiguity remains. If the task rests on a confused premise—if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome—say so directly rather than forcing a conclusion. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish between your two rules.
