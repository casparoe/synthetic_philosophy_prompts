I’m running a philosophy workshop next week with about 16 grad studnets, 90 minutes, and I want them to feel the pull of Bernard Williams’ idea that moral revolutions—think the death of dueling or the end of slavery—aren’t mainly driven by people suddenly winning an argument, but by shifts in what counts as honorable, against Appiah’s emphasis on interests and social coordination. Design a structured role-play or game where participants belong to a fictional guild called the Velvet Ledger. In this guild, disputes are resolved by ritual challenge, but there is a hidden rule: every time a member chooses arbitration over challenge, they collect a private economic token, yet they only avoid being branded cowardly if at least two others have already switched. Give them cards with actual arguments for and against the challenge norm, but arrange the payoffs so that reasoning aloud changes nothing unless the status conditions shift first. They should construct the collapse of the norm themselves during play and only notice at the end that they moved with the crowd, not with the reasons. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Williams versus Appiah, predicts what will go wrong—like one vocal participant freezing the group—and is frank about which feature of real-world moral change, like the material decline of slave-owning classes, the simulation simply can’t reproduce and you’ll have to supply verbally. No lecture up front, don’t name either thesis until after they’ve played, and don’t include any bibliography or citation list since this is just for me to run.
