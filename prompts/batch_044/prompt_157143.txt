I'm a journalist who covers neuroscience and philosophy of mind for a general-educated readership, and next month I'm running a ninety-minute workshop for science editors and feature writers—people who can navigate a primary study but have no clinical background. The session is on pain asymbolia and the philosophical problem of whether pain is intrinsically bad. I don't want you to explain the concept to me; I want you to plan exactly how I should explain it to them.

The conceptual arc I want to build is this: begin with the standard two-track model of pain (sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational), then present asymbolia as a documented dissociation between those tracks, then use that dissociation to press the philosophical question—if the sensory track can run fully without the affective one, is the badness of pain essential to the experience or is it a separable layer?

For the carrying example, I want to avoid the familiar morphine-euphoria case. I'm considering a comparative trio: congenital insensitivity to pain (sensory track absent, affect absent), an asymbolic patient after insular damage (sensory track intact, affect missing), and a typical nociceptive response (both present). Tell me whether this trio can carry the whole explanation, what properties it must have and what it must lack to prevent the audience from collapsing the three cases into two, and whether a single narrative scenario would serve better.

Then map the dependency order: does the neuroscience of the two tracks have to come before the philosophical question, or can I introduce the "badness" problem first and use the neuroscience as resolution? Explain what depends on what.

Next, list the misconceptions this audience will predictably form—I'm especially worried they will conflate "not suffering" with "not feeling," will interpret asymbolia as extreme stoicism or high tolerance, and will conclude that if affect is detachable then pain is "just information" with no normative weight. For each, tell me precisely where in the sequence it will arise and exactly where I should head it off.

Also specify: the one check or question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped that the badness might be contingent rather than essential; what clinical detail (specific lesion sites, cingulotomy histories, precise insular pathways) I should explicitly leave out because it distracts from the conceptual point; and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so.

Keep the plan formal and structured. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote specific passages from texts.
