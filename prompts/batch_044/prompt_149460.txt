I'm taking a graduate seminar on epistemic logic, and we recently worked through the standard derivation of Fitch's paradox of knowability. I understand the formal steps—how the knowability principle, combined with the factivity of knowledge and distribution over conjunction, yields the collapse of all unknown truths—but I'm struggling with the conceptual gut-level issue: why should the mere possibility of coming to know a conjunction like "p and I do not currently know p" be conceptually incoherent, rather than merely practically unlikely?

To push this further, our instructor introduced a restricted variant she called "finite-memory knowability," in which an agent can only entertain epistemic claims that fit within a bounded working-memory register, and knowledge attribution requires explicit tokening within that register. Under this restriction, the derivation seems to break because the agent cannot simultaneously token both p and the negation of knowledge-of-p in the same representational state needed for the conjunction. But I'm not convinced this is more than an ad hoc technical patch. Could you explain, at an intuitive but conceptually precise level, whether Fitch's paradox reveals a genuine structural feature of the knowledge relation—specifically regarding how self-referential epistemic content interacts with logical conjunction—rather than merely an artifact of unbounded idealization? In particular, does the paradox depend essentially on treating the knowledge operator as fully transparent to logical combination, or can it be reconstructed in a framework where epistemic accessibility is resource-limited but still governed by classical logic?
