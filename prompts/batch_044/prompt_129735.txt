After a long argument last night with a friend about whether Machiavelli’s pairing of fortune and virtù collapses once you distinguish sudden, event-like misfortune from deep structural contingency—things like institutional path dependence, demographic drift, or ecological pressure that constrain the arena before any prince acts—I am drafting the final for my upper-level early modern political thought seminar. I want one essay question that students cannot answer by summarizing Chapter 25 of The Prince. They must instead take a position on whether virtù is conceptually separable from fortuna: is it a dispositional trait that exists independently of contingency, or is it constituted only in the successful navigation of it? They should also confront Machiavelli’s gendered metaphor—that fortune behaves like a woman who can be beaten into submission by bold, youthful action—and assess whether that image smuggles in a substantive theory of agency (direct domination of circumstances) that actually conflicts with a structural view of fortuna. Please write the exam question itself, designed for a 75-minute essay with explicit instructions to construct an argument rather than summarize; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 that separates an A answer (roughly 17–20) from a B answer (roughly 13–16) by naming the specific conceptual moves required—such as distinguishing evental from structural fortuna, explaining whether the gendered metaphor is decorative or carries a theory of control, and judging whether virtù risks collapsing into mere reactive improvisation if fortuna is structural. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from real texts unless you have fetched them. In the model answer and in a brief meta-note at the end, please say how confident you are in the interpretation offered and single out the specific distinction or claim you are least sure about. Keep the prompt formal and polished.
