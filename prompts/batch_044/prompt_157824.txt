Write a joke that operates inside Daniel Dennett's framework of the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, but do not merely label the framework or fall back on generic robot or artificial-intelligence tropes. The punchline should turn on a precise tension within the view: that the intentional stance does not merely offer a predictive shortcut but discloses real patterns—patterns genuinely invisible to both physical and design descriptions—so that what looks like a mechanical failure from one stance becomes, from the intentional stance, a coherent mistake born of belief and desire. Build the joke around a concrete, non-technological situation—an animal behavior, a bureaucratic ritual, or an ordinary household object—arranging it so that the listener first interprets the event through the design stance (function, purpose, breakdown) and is then compelled by the punchline to shift to the intentional stance, where the error transforms into a rational but absurd belief-desire pair. The humor should arise from the sudden visibility of that rational coherence, not from cute anthropomorphism. The joke should be fully intelligible to a reader acquainted with Dennett's distinction between stance-relative real patterns and mere projection, yet it must stand alone without citation, named texts, or any request for sources, and it should avoid relying on obscure technical details that a generally educated model might not reliably recall.
