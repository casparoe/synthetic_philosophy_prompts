I recently overheard an argument between two graphic-design graduate students about the Letter Spirit framework and whether letter recognition depends on intrinsic geometric invariants or on purely relational, statistical clustering. They used the lowercase "a" as their central case. I was a bystander and do not take a side; I reproduce their exchange below as faithfully as I can.

(1) The first student, arguing for an essentialist view, claimed that every instance of the letter "a"—whether roman, italic, or handwritten—must contain at least one enclosed negative space (a counter) bounded on at least three sides by continuous stroke. Without that enclosed region, she said, the form collapses into something like "c" or "e", so the counter is the necessary and sufficient geometric signature of the category.

(2) The second student objected that this invariant fails for numerous script and handwritten forms—particularly certain italic "a"s—where the bowl is open at the bottom and there is no fully enclosed counter, only a curved stroke with a tail. He argued that if one allows "partially open" counters, the invariant becomes so flexible that it applies to "q", "g", and malformed "o"s, which means it no longer distinguishes "a" from its neighbors.

(3) The first student revised her claim: the true invariant is topological rather than strictly geometric. Every "a" contains a loop that breaks rotational symmetry via a protruding tail or stem. Even when the counter is open, the loop-plus-tail structure remains, and that is what distinguishes an "a" from an "o" (pure loop) or a "d" (loop with vertical stem on the left).

(4) The second student replied that "loop plus asymmetric protrusion" is far too broad to be specific to "a"; it describes "b", "p", "d", "q", and several other lowercase letters. Therefore, he concluded, the recognition of an isolated form as an "a" cannot depend on an intrinsic structural invariant at all. Instead, it depends on relational opposition within the alphabet—what it is not (not "o", not "d")—combined with statistical clustering in a high-dimensional feature space. He added that this explains why we can recognize distorted or foreign-alphabet forms as "a"-like only when they fall near our learned cluster; there is no grasped essence.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the four moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both students are missing—particularly regarding how the Letter Spirit project's emphasis on style and emergent categorization might bear on their disagreement about intrinsic invariants versus statistical family resemblance.
