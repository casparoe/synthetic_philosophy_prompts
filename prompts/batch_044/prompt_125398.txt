I've been trying to formalize my position on state-sanctioned coercive interrogation for a discussion on institutional ethics, and I'd like a consistency audit before I defend it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) In the genuinely verified ticking-bomb scenario—where we have near-certainty about the detainee's identity, knowledge of the device's location, and efficacy of extraction—I hold that torture is not merely permissible but morally required, on the grounds that the perpetrator's prior culpable act forfeits their ordinary claim-right against coercive state violence, and the magnitude of lives saved dominates whatever residual deontological weight remains.

(2) At the institutional level, I endorse a strict deontological prohibition: a legitimate state must categorically refuse to authorize torture in its legal and operational architecture, not because such authorization never maximizes welfare, but because permitting the state to deliberately inflict severe suffering on detainees corrupts the normative relationship between citizen and government in a way that particular outcomes cannot redeem.

(3) I think first-order moral reasoning should operate within a moral-uncertainty framework: rather than being a pure consequentialist or pure deontologist, I weight competing normative considerations by my rational credence in each first-order theory being correct.

(4) I accept the base-rate and epistemic-humility argument: given empirical frequencies of mistaken identity, fabricated intelligence, and coercive inefficacy, no actual decision-maker in a real-world crisis could rationally reach the epistemic threshold that (1) demands; the ideal case is therefore practically empty.

(5) I reject moral-luck evaluations of institutional rules: whether an unauthorized, ad hoc torture session happens to save lives is irrelevant to the justification of the rule that prohibits it.

My question is whether this is a consistent package, or whether I'm smuggling in an exception in (1) that (2) and (3) jointly forbid, with (4) doing illegitimate masking work. Specifically: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity; for each, say what has to give and at what cost. Then tell me which single commitment—(1), (2), or (3)—would do the least damage to the rest of the set if dropped, and whether the remaining four form a stable equilibrium. I want the audit, not a general lecture on torture ethics.
