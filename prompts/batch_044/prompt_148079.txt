I'm a retired astrophysicist who got into philosophy late, so I might be framing things weird, but here's a stall I can't shake. In my reading group we keep arguing about moral worth: do you have to consciously represent the moral reason to count as acting for the right reasons, or is it enough that your motives are calibratted to produce right action without you knowing why — I started calling this hidden alignment "motivational dark matter," like gravity pulling behavior without visible mass. The explicitists say if you can't cite the reason its just habit or luck; the calibrationists say demanding conscious access is like insisting we only count forces we can observe directly. We've circled for months with the same examples — the honest cashier, the instinctive rescuer — and nobody moves. Is this a hidden empirical question about moral cognition, a disagreement about what counts as a reason, or just bedrock intuition about autonomy versus mechanism? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a verdict, and an honest answer about whether a distinction, an experiment, or just changing the question could actually break it, or if we're just talking past each other.
