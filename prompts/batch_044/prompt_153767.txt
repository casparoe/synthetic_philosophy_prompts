Take the role of an experienced philosophy educator with expertise in conceptual history and simulation-based pedagogy. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm: what is the minimal rule-set that can make a group experience the tension between describing a societal transformation as a “collapse” versus an “adaptive dissolution” — that is, an intentional unbundling of formal institutions to preserve core social networks? Now design a single 75-minute classroom activity for 14 upper-level undergraduates in a seminar room. The session should make participants feel, through their own choices, that labeling the Bronze Age transition, the Classic Maya restructuring, or Easter Island’s deforestation-era change as a “collapse” depends on a hidden normative commitment to institutional persistence, and that an alternative framing — adaptive dissolution — is not merely euphemism but describes a different kind of success. Do not begin with a lecture; the conceptual distinction should emerge only after the simulation ends. Provide: (1) specific roles (e.g., institutional stewards, network maintainers, external observers) with hidden victory conditions that conflict; (2) clear rules and a payoff or scoring scheme that rewards either structural preservation or relational continuity, with the criteria revealed only gradually; (3) a phased timeline (setup, stress event, decision round, restructuring, revelation); (4) a debrief script that maps each phase onto the historical cases mentioned and onto the conceptual issue of whether societies “fail” or “merely change,” being explicit about which feature of adaptive dissolution the rules can capture and which feature — likely the long-term intentionality behind institutional abandonment — the facilitator must supply verbally; (5) a frank prediction of what will misfire in the first run (e.g., participants defaulting to zero-sum framing, or observers dominating) and how to correct it. Keep the prompt self-contained: do not ask students to read specific texts in advance, do not request bibliographies or citations, and do not invoke highly specialized archaeological data that requires verification. Think carefully about how the mechanics, not the facilitator’s commentary, generate the philosophical pressure.
