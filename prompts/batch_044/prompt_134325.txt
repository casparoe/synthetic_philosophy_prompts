You are presenting a 25-minute paper at an interdisciplinary political-philosophy workshop defending what you call "epistemic non-subordination"—a revision to republican liberty. Your thesis: Pettit's freedom-as-non-domination fails in bureaucratic and algorithmic contexts unless we add an epistemic condition, namely that an agent is unfree when structurally deprived of the conceptual and informational resources needed to recognize interference as arbitrary. You argue this does not collapse republican liberty into Berlin-style positive liberty (self-mastery), because the remedy is distributed institutional transparency rather than internal psychological autonomy. The audience is precisely: two analytic republican theorists loyal to Pettit who think arbitrary-power tracking is sufficient; one Berlin scholar convinced any "resources" condition is just positive liberty in disguise; one critical-theorist skeptical of framing power-recognition as a cognitive-resource problem rather than a material one; a legal philosopher focused on institutional design; and one sharp graduate student who will push on historical examples. Draft your Q&A prep sheet. List the six objections you will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary that particular audience member would use. Include the concession you should make in your opening remarks to defuse the worst objection, and identify the single question you are hoping nobody asks—phrase it as they would ask it, with a one-paragraph reply and a frank note on why this reply is weakest. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure named theories that depend on memorized texts.
