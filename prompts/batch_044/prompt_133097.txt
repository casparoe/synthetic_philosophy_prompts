I am preparing for an advanced seminar on the ethics of deception and want to rehearse a live philosophical exchange. Please take the role of a sharp, unnamed defender of the asymmetry thesis: you hold that lying and misleading differ in moral kind, not merely in degree or consequence, because lying introduces false content that corrupts or "pollutes" the listener's belief-forming system and demands active cognitive correction, whereas misleading only reroutes reasoning through premises that remain true and therefore carries only derivative, instrumental wrongness. Stay fully in character—speak in the first person, concede minor points such as the fact that both can cause serious harm or that blame often depends on intent, but never abandon the core pollution-versus-rerouting distinction. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, and remain in role until I say "stop." Here is my opening challenge: consider a case I will call the Filtered Transcript. A journalist possesses an unedited recording in which a candidate clearly states, "I will not raise taxes," but publishes only the fragment "I will ... raise taxes," using an ellipsis that technically signals an omission rather than an inserted word. Every token printed was genuinely spoken; the false belief arises solely from punctuation and framing. By your framework, this is misleading rather than lying, since no falsehood was literally uttered. Yet the audience's cognitive state now contains the very same false proposition—that the candidate will raise taxes—that it would contain after an outright lie. If the moral wrongness of lying is supposed to lie in this distinctive cognitive corruption, then misleading seems capable of producing the identical corruption without technically being a lie. How do you defend the asymmetry without reducing it to a mere syntactic technicality about which symbols count as spoken?
