If Immanuel Kant were presented with the Sleeping Beauty problem—in which a subject is awakened once should a fair coin land heads and twice should it land tails, with each awakening being subjectively indistinguishable and amnesic to prior awakenings—how would his critical philosophy bear on the correct rule for updating her credence in heads? In particular, examine whether Kant’s distinction between empirical self-consciousness (given through inner sense) and the transcendental unity of apperception permits genuinely self-locating or indexical beliefs that persist even when no continuous empirical self spans the two possible tails-awakenings. Would Kant defend a halfer position, maintaining that the prior credence of one-half remains unchanged because the duplicated awakenings do not constitute numerically distinct subjects of belief under one transcendental ego? Or would he defend a thirder position, allowing that the phenomenological multiplication of empirical centers introduces additional evidential weight? Reconstruct his likely reasoning from the Paralogisms of Pure Reason, his conditions for synthetic unity of apperception, and his account of time-determination in inner sense, and state whether his framework resolves the problem or instead exposes a tension between transcendental psychology and probabilistic reasoning about centered worlds.
