I am a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine preparing a dissertation chapter on how twentieth-century dystopian fiction anticipates contemporary negative-utilitarian biotechnology. For a graduate seminar I am teaching, I want to move beyond the familiar reading of Aldous Huxley’s Brave New World as a simple warning against pleasure-through-pharmacology, and instead interpret the novel through the lens of the hedonistic imperative—the programmatic aim, associated with thinkers such as David Pearce, of using genetic and neurotechnological intervention to abolish suffering altogether rather than merely suppressing it chemically. Specifically, I am interested in whether the text genuinely distinguishes between eliminating suffering and flattening affective range, and whether Mustapha Mond’s defense of social stability implicitly depends on a hidden form of preference adaptation that renders citizens conceptually unable to desire suffering-free flourishing without also conceiving of it as affectively impoverished. Could you provide a philosophical reading that takes both the novel’s specific biotechnological mechanisms—Bokanovsky’s Process, hypnopaedic conditioning, and the distribution of soma—and the theoretical commitments of the hedonistic imperative seriously? In particular: where does the novel dramatize a tension internal to the imperative, for instance in John the Savage’s insistence on a right to unhappiness versus his eventual suicide; where does Huxley’s framing risk conflating the ethical question of suffering-abolition with a political question about caste-based equality; and where does the work resist being read as either a vindication or a straightforward caricature of the project? I want to use this for an essay, so I would value an analysis that remains faithful to the plot and major scenes without reducing the novel to an illustration, and that concludes by specifying what Brave New World contributes to the analytic debate—particularly regarding the phenomenology of engineered contentment and the possibility of heritable emotional architecture—that pure argument alone does not capture.
