I've recently begun volunteering with a local land trust that is weighing whether to restore a degraded meadow to historic woodland or leave it as passive "rewilded" scrub, and the debate has made wild animal suffering personally salient in a way it never was before. I have been considering a specific ecological-welfare thesis: that habitat destruction and fragmentation do not merely reduce populations but systematically shift ecosystems toward r-selected taxa with extreme reproductive turnover and catastrophic juvenile mortality, so that degradation might increase total suffering per hectare even as it reduces the number of long-lived, sentient individuals. Please describe the most important implications of accepting this "r-selection welfare trap" for conservation ethics—particularly how it should reshape our comparison of active restoration versus passive rewilding, whether it undermines the common intuition that intact ecosystems are welfare-optimal, and what it suggests about the relative moral urgency of preventing further habitat loss versus reducing suffering within already-degraded habitats.
