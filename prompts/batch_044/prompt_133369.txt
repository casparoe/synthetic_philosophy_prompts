I am preparing a seminar discussion on Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, and my study group has been arguing over a hypothetical case that moves well beyond the familiar gavagai examples. We imagined a field linguist working with an isolated coastal community that employs the single utterance "kree-von" only during a brief seasonal interval when bioluminescent tides coincide with a particular constellation. Observers record that speakers point simultaneously to the water, the sky, and a ritual net they cast, yet they never correct outsiders who apply the term to just one of these referents. Some members of my group read this as a straightforward extension of radical translation: the manual could equally well map "kree-von" onto "the glowing event," "the correct temporal boundary for casting," or "the net's permission-state," with all observable behavioral evidence leaving the choice radically underdetermined. Others think a Davidsonian approach—appealing to the principle of charity and holistic belief ascription—can uniquely resolve the ambiguity once we embed the utterance in the community's broader navigational, ecological, and ritual commitments.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—Mara, who defends a Quinean view of radical translation, and Elias, who defends a Davidsonian holistic interpretation—debating precisely this "kree-von" case. Mara should argue that even a richly holistic manual still permits empirically equivalent rival translations, and that attributing specific beliefs about navigation or ritual merely smuggles in the interpreter's own ontological preferences. Elias should respond that the interlocking practices of tide-reading, net-casting, and ancestral navigation create constraint enough to rule out the rival manuals Mara proposes, without collapsing into an implausible uniqueness claim that ignores Quine's behavioral baseline. They must engage with each other's actual points rather than talking past one another: for instance, Elias should address Mara's charge of smuggled ontology, and Mara should address Elias's claim about interlocking practice, not simply restate standard textbook positions. The exchange should be philosophically precise, avoid bibliographic lists or citation requests, and treat the invented scenario as a genuine test case for the underlying theories.
