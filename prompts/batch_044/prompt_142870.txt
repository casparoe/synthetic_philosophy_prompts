My supervisor in metaphysics has offered verdicts on the persistence of artefacts across the following cases. First, when every plank of a wooden ship is replaced gradually over two centuries of uninterrupted service, she judges it to remain numerically identical to the original. Second, when the original planks are removed, stored, and later reassembled into a seaworthy vessel in a different harbour—while the in-service vessel is maintained with new timber—she judges the reconstructed vessel to be the original and the continuously maintained one not to be. Third, when a ship is destroyed and an exact replica is built from its blueprints using entirely new timber, she denies that the replica is identical. Fourth, when sixty per cent of a ship's structure is replaced with carbon-fibre composites that are structurally integrated and the vessel remains in continuous operation, she affirms its identity. Fifth, when a vessel's design is realised as an autonomous, plank-less floating platform with identical hydrodynamic behaviour but no shared material parts or maintenance history, she denies its identity.

The new case is this. A ship undergoes gradual replacement of all wooden elements with laboratory-grown mycelium structures that are chemically distinct but physically continuous in shape and load-bearing function; each removed plank is immediately incinerated, so no original timber survives in storage. At the same time, DNA extracted from the original timber before replacement is used to grow a genetically identical wooden replica, which is assembled into a seaworthy vessel in dry dock elsewhere; this replica has never been in service and shares no physical parts with the original. The continuously maintained mycelium vessel remains in active operation.

Predict her verdict: is the mycelium vessel the original, is the genetically reconstructed wooden vessel the original, are both, or is neither? Identify the implicit rule she appears to be running—spatiotemporal-functional continuity, direct material-provenance inheritance, causal-historical best-claim, or design-instantiation with an origin-constraint—and explain which of her earlier verdicts most strongly support it and which create tension. Then state which further case would best distinguish the two leading candidates. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify the principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
