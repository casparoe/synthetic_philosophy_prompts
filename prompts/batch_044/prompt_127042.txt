I'm preparing a seminar paper in a course on genocide studies and moral psychology, and I'm trying to formalize a synthesis of Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Goldhagen's account of eliminationist antisemitism. The synthesis seems to collapse into an overgeneralization. Here is the argument:

(1) Browning's evidence shows that many policemen in Battalion 101 participated in mass shootings despite lacking prior personal antisemitic conviction; situational dynamics—peer pressure, authority, routinization—do the primary explanatory work.

(2) Goldhagen responds that such situational accounts miss the deeper cultural substrate: a widespread, historically specific eliminationist ideology made these dynamics effective and explains the enthusiasm with which many perpetrators acted.

(3) A synthesis I will call "structural ideological embedding" seems available: ideology need not be intensely present in each agent's private psychology; rather, it can be functionally embedded in institutional roles, so that occupying a designed role within a genocidal bureaucracy activates the ideology's practical requirements regardless of the occupant's personal belief.

(4) If this embedding thesis holds, then culpability for atrocity attaches not primarily to private mental states or to immediate coercive pressure, but to one's structural position within an organization whose functional purpose includes mass killing.

(5) Contemporary bureaucratic systems—global supply chains, administrative apparatuses—share the relevant structural properties: division of labor, epistemic opacity, routinization, and functional contribution to harmful outcomes.

(6) Therefore, by the logic of structural embedding, ordinary participants in modern harmful bureaucracies bear culpability structurally analogous to that of the Holocaust perpetrators.

(7) But this conclusion is intolerable: we do not treat modern logistics coordinators or civil administrators as perpetrators of atrocity.

(8) Hence, structural ideological embedding must be false, and we are driven back either to pure situationism—which seems to dissolve individual culpability—or to pure ideological individualism—which seems to exonerate the bureaucratic functionary who lacked personal hatred.

Which step should I reject? I am inclined to defend (1) through (3), since the synthesis captures something both standard accounts miss about institutional design. But if I keep them, I need to block the slide to (6) without simply asserting that modern bureaucracies differ in kind. What does rejecting each of the other steps cost for the situationist, the ideological, and the hybrid position? In particular, is there a principled way to restrict embedded culpability to genocidal contexts without making that restriction look ad hoc?
