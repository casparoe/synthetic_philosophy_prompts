Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two scholars who have argued for years about whether culture can be an independent cause in social explanation, using Max Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis as their central case. One party—call them the Value-Causalists—defends Weber's claim that Calvinist ascetic values possessed genuine causal efficacy in the emergence of modern capitalism, operating through what Weber termed an elective affinity between religious meaning and economic rationalization. The other party—the Structural Reductionists—maintains that values never enter causal explanations: all apparent cultural causation collapses into material conditions, institutional incentives, or retrospective rationalization, and Weber's elective affinity is at best a correlation requiring mediation by banking structures, property law, and labor markets.

The document must include:

1. A numbered Agreed Claims section. Both sides should sign onto claims about: (i) what Weber's text actually asserts about the relationship between ascetic Protestantism and capitalist accumulation; (ii) the formal conditions under which a factor counts as a cause in historical sociology; (iii) the established historical correlation between regions with dominant Calvinist populations and early development of rationalized capitalist enterprise; and (iv) the conceptual distinction between explaining an action by reference to its subjective meaning versus explaining it by reference to its structural conditions. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.

2. A Disagreements Table with two columns—Value-Causalism and Structural Reductionism—each stating in neutral, precise wording the positions each side accepts for itself. Required rows must cover: whether elective affinity denotes a causal mechanism or a narrative correlation; whether attributing causal power to a value-system commits a category mistake analogous to treating software instructions as physical forces on hardware; and whether subjective-meaning explanations can be tested independently of structural-prediction models.

3. The Cruxes—three or more sub-questions such that resolving one would move at least one party. Include: (a) whether a value can function as an efficient cause without full reducibility to material incentives; (b) whether elective affinity can be operationalized in a causal model yielding testable counterfactual predictions; and (c) whether explaining accumulation behavior by citing the subjective meaning that disciplined labor glorifies God adds explanatory power beyond citing expected returns to capital.

4. For each crux, specify one concrete comparative case or historical study both sides agree bears on it, with each side's explicit prediction recorded in advance. For example, both might accept a comparison of capital-formation rates in Calvinist and Lutheran regions that shared identical banking regulations and property-law frameworks; state what each camp predicts.

5. A final section addressing the injected analogy: both parties should state whether they accept the framing that the dispute over cultural causation is structurally parallel to the dispute over whether software can cause hardware events, explain their reasoning, and note whether accepting or rejecting this analogy resolves any part of their disagreement or is itself a verbal dispute.

Format the output as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions, then the analogy assessment. Keep the wording formal, avoid verbatim quotations from Weber's texts unless you can verify them against a source—otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks—and do not ask the model to provide citations or reading lists.
