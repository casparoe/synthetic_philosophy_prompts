Examination Question:
Consider the following variant of a classic moral dilemma, which we will call the "dual-track switch." A runaway trolley is approaching a junction. If the agent does nothing, the trolley will travel onto Track A, which immediately splits into two parallel sub-tracks, A1 and A2. One person is tied to A1, and one person is tied to A2. The trolley's impact with the person on A1 will trigger a separate mechanical device that kills the person on A2. If the agent flips a switch, the trolley is diverted onto Track B, where exactly one person is tied, and no secondary mechanism is present. Consequently, inaction results in two deaths produced by distinct causal paths, while action results in a single death.

The student is asked to apply the doctrine of double effect (DDE) to determine whether flipping the switch is permissible. Specifically, the student should address whether the presence of the secondary mechanism on Track A alters the moral evaluation of the agent's omission relative to the standard switch case, and whether the numerical comparison of lives saved or lost bears on the proportionality condition.

Student's Answer:
The agent may flip the switch. Under DDE, the act of diverting the trolley is morally neutral, the intended good effect is saving the lives that would otherwise be lost on Tracks A1 and A2, and the foreseen bad effect—the death of the individual on Track B—is not the means by which the trolley is diverted; the diversion mechanism operates independently of the victim's body. The four conditions are satisfied: (1) the act is not intrinsically wrong; (2) the agent intends only the good effect; (3) the bad effect is a side effect rather than a means; and (4) the good of saving two lives is proportionate to the bad of one death.

As for omission, if the agent does not flip the switch, the trolley proceeds to Track A, causing two deaths. Because DDE requires that the bad effect not be disproportionate to the intended good, and because inaction here produces a greater evil (two deaths) without producing any corresponding good, the omission fails the proportionality condition. In other words, the agent's failure to act allows an evil that is not offset by any intended benefit, which makes the omission morally impermissible under DDE. The secondary mechanism is significant because it shows that the two deaths on Track A arise from separate causal chains—direct impact and triggered mechanism—so the agent's inaction is causally responsible for both outcomes, not just one. Therefore, not only is flipping the switch permitted, it is morally required, since omitting it violates the proportionality requirement of DDE by allowing disproportionate harm.
