I'm preparing notes for a seminar on authenticity and artistic value, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is where I stand:

(1) The value of an original artwork is partly constituted by its direct causal-historical link to the artist's intentional production—authenticity is non-instrumentally valuable, not merely a reliable marker for other properties.

(2) If a forgery is experientially indistinguishable from an original for every competent observer, then, in the moment of perception, the two share identical aesthetic value; any divergence in overall value must be explained by non-perceptual or post-hoc factors.

(3) A work's place in an art-historical narrative is part of its value, so an undetected forgery can temporarily occupy a value-laden narrative position, but that value collapses upon discovery—not gradually, but discontinuously.

(4) I accept a principle of causal inheritance for destroyed works: a high-fidelity digital replica produced from direct molecular or structural scanning of the original before its destruction possesses derivative but genuine artistic value, not merely representational or documentary value.

(5) I reject strong artist-intentionalism: posthumous reinterpretations, accidental features, and unintended audience responses can genuinely augment a work's value, so the artist's mental states are neither necessary nor sufficient for the work's final value-profile.

(6) The primary wrong of forging is deceptive: it misleads viewers about the causal history they are encountering, rather than causing economic harm or violating a property right in the original's uniqueness.

My questions: Do these commitments form a stable package, or do they pull against each other in ways that force a revision? Specifically, does (1) conflict with (2) in a manner that requires abandoning one? Does (4) sit awkwardly with (5), given that causal inheritance seems to privilege the original production chain in a way that might smuggle intentionalist assumptions back in? Is the discontinuous collapse described in (3) compatible with (2)'s claim that perceptual identity fixes value at the time of encounter? And does (6) survive intact if (2) is true—if the deceived viewer receives exactly the same aesthetic value, what is genuinely lost by the deception beyond a bare epistemic failure?

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. Identify which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection—for instance, whether (4) and (5) can be reconciled by distinguishing causal continuity from mental-state dependence. Finally, indicate the least costly revision: whether it requires weakening (1), refining (2) with a scope restriction, or giving up (4) altogether, and explain precisely what that revision costs me conceptually.
