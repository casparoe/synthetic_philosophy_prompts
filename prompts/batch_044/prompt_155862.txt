ok so i've been trying to wrap my head around how non-human stuff shapes history without falling into that determinism trap, and here's where i've landed—does it actually hang together?

1. i think germs and volcanoes can be "historical actors" in a real sense, not just metaphors. like the black death bacteria had a kind of directional push that reshaped feudal labor markets, and tambora in 1815 wasn't just weather—it was a veto actor that blocked agricultural recovery paths in europe for years.

2. but i also think enviromental determinism is bogus when people use it to excuse human choices, like blaming the little ice age for witch trials or saying climate "made" people revolt. people still chose.

3. i think it's okay to say co2 today has "interests" in warming the planet and that forces governments to respond, but i don't think co2 has a mind or moral responsibility.

4. and i think if something doesn't have intentions, calling it an actor is just sloppy anthropomorphism—so bacteria and volcanoes don't literally have goals, they're just causal nodes.

5. finally, i think the safest way to talk about this is through "causal veto points"—non-human events that don't cause outcomes positively but just eliminate certain historical branches—so tambora vetoed recovery, plague vetoed serfdom, etc. that lets me keep agency for humans on the remaining branches.

the tension i feel is between (1) and (4): if bacteria are real actors with directional push, why am i saying in (4) that lacking intentions means they're just causal nodes? and does (5) actually solve it, or am i just renaming determinism as "branch pruning"? also is (3) consistent with (2)—why is it okay to talk about co2 interests but not okay to say climate made people do witch hunts?

am i helping myself to something? which one has to go, and what's the cheapest fix? be specific about which commitments pull against each other, and whether (5) is actually doing any work or just dressing up (1).
