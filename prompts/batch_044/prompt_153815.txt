ok so in my philosophy of mind reading group we've been going in circles on whether dreams are actual experiences, and its driving me a bit nuts. One side keeps bringing up Malcolm's verificationist-style worry — that if you're asleep and can't check or report what's happening, then calling it an 'experience' is conceptually off, like trying to apply a private-language criterion where no criterion can run. The other side is basically Dennett: dreams are confabulated narratives that get assembled on waking, not movies playing in your head during REM, and the brain-science about REM activation doesn't prove there's anyone 'home' to undergo them. Everyone in the room accepts the same sleep science — prefrontal deactivation, hippocampal replay, whatever — but the disagreement hasn't budged in like six months. My working hunch is that this isn't really about consciousness at all, but about what makes something count as being 'undergone' by a subject: do you need access, reportability, or at least some minimal self-model running for phenomenal presence? But I might be wrong — maybe its just bedrock intuition about whether sleeping people are 'there' phenomenally, or maybe there's a hidden empirical claim about minimal conditions for awareness that neither side has made explicit. Can you actually diagnose what's stalling us? Is it verbal (what 'experience' means), a disagreement about standards of evidence for mental ascription, or incompatible starting points that neither can argue for? And be honest: is there anything — a new distinction, a weird result about sleep-state self-modeling, a reframing — that could actually move this, or should we accept that its permanently stuck? Don't just survey Malcolm and Dennett; I want to know why they don't move.
