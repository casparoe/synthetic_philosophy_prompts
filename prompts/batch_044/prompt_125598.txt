Question:
David Pearce's The Hedonistic Imperative proposes that suffering ought to be abolished through biotechnology and replaced by what he terms gradients of bliss. Address the following in your answer:
(i) How does Pearce's position relate to negative utilitarianism, particularly regarding the claim that eliminating suffering is morally more urgent than promoting happiness?
(ii) What are gradients of bliss, and why does Pearce reject a uniform, maximal hedonic state?
(iii) Present one philosophical objection to paradise engineering — for instance, the concern that biotechnologically engineered well-being severs the connection between subjective reward and objective achievement — and indicate how Pearce might respond.

Student answer:
(i) Pearce's hedonistic imperative shares with negative utilitarianism the conviction that suffering is the supreme moral evil, but it departs from the classical negative-utilitarian asymmetry that assigns moral weight only to reducing suffering rather than to promoting happiness. For Pearce, the abolition of suffering is necessary but not sufficient for justice: he holds that we ought to replace suffering with robust, biologically engineered positive welfare. Thus, unlike negative utilitarians such as Karl Popper, Pearce does not treat happiness-promotion as morally optional. He further integrates preference satisfaction into the core of the imperative, arguing that engineered gradients of well-being can be calibrated to align with an organism's evolved preference structures, so that desires continue to be satisfied even without aversive experience.

(ii) Gradients of bliss denote Pearce's proposal that well-being should vary in intensity rather than being flattened into a uniform state of maximal pleasure. A uniform hedonic ceiling — sometimes likened to wireheading — would eliminate the motivational gradients required for learning, exploration, and problem-solving. Pearce argues that by retaining a dynamic range of positive affect, organisms preserve information-sensitive behaviour: reward-prediction errors can still signal useful environmental feedback, and cognitive architectures remain functionally intact. The gradients therefore serve both a hedonic and an instrumental purpose, allowing paradise engineering to avoid the stagnation associated with static euphoria.

(iii) A prominent objection holds that biotechnologically engineered well-being severs the link between subjective experience and objective value, producing a form of adaptive preference in which people are satisfied regardless of whether their lives genuinely flourish. Pearce's reply is that this objection mistakes the mechanism of intervention: paradise engineering does not manipulate beliefs or preferences directly, but rather recalibrates the hedonic set-point at the genetic level. Because the brain's belief-formation and value-judgement modules remain unaltered, subjective states continue to be formed through ordinary evidential processes, and subjective reward remains tethered to objective achievement. Consequently, the charge of adaptive preference formation does not apply; the engineered state is not false consciousness but a biologically grounded elevation of baseline well-being that preserves the functional role of reward.
