Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a legal moralist working in the tradition of Patrick Devlin and a liberal anti-moralist working in the tradition of H.L.A. Hart. The subject is whether criminal law may legitimately enforce shared moral norms in the absence of direct, individualized harm. Rather than reproducing the standard summary, the collaboration must work with two distinctions that introductory accounts often conflate: first, expressive legal moralism, in which law symbolically affirms communal moral identity, versus instrumental legal moralism, in which law is defended as a causal instrument to prevent moral decay that is predicted to generate tangible social damage; second, direct harm, understood as victimization of specific individuals, versus structural harm, understood as erosion of the normative framework that sustains cooperative social life.

Structure the document as follows. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims—propositions about the nature of legal coercion, the burden of justification for criminalization, the empirical reality of pluralism, the logical form of the harm principle, and the conditions under which a society can be said to possess shared moral sentiments—that both parties can sign without reservation. Follow this with a table of precise disagreements: for each issue (the legitimacy of expressive justification; the epistemic status of moral-ecology predictions; whether structural harm is reducible to aggregated direct harms or is sui generis; the relevance of majority sentiment to legislative justification), state the moralist position and the anti-moralist position in wording that each side accepts as an accurate rendering of its own view.

Next, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would cause the opposing party to revise its stance. For each crux, specify in advance what kind of evidence, conceptual analysis, or historical case both sides agree would be genuinely probative—for example, empirical patterns of social cohesion following decriminalization of victimless offenses; conceptual arguments showing whether indirect normative erosion can be captured within an expanded harm principle; or comparative institutional examples in which moral legislation either stabilized or destabilized cooperative norms. Record each side’s explicit prediction about what that evidence or analysis would demonstrate.

Conclude with a brief residue section assessing whether any part of the dispute is verbal: that is, whether terms such as harm, morality, or social fabric are used in ways that generate an appearance of substantive disagreement where the parties actually share the same judgment expressed in different vocabulary. State clearly which disagreements survive this scrutiny and which dissolve.

The document must be suitable for evaluation by a reader familiar with the Hart-Devlin exchange but must not depend on external references. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to obscure theoretical frameworks outside mainstream philosophy of law. Ensure that every formulation of disagreement is one that the named party would recognize as its own view, not a straw man.
