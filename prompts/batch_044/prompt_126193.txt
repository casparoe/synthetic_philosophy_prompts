Write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of tragic pleasure—why audiences voluntarily seek out and value artworks that evoke grief, fear, or pity—must meet. For each requirement, ground it in a specific boundary case or distinction: (i) the gap between enjoying Sophocles’ Oedipus Rex and being disturbed by documentary footage of comparable real-world suffering; (ii) the fact that we seek repetition of the same tragic work despite knowing the outcome, which undermines simple suspense-based explanations; (iii) the need to preserve the intuition that our pleasure is not masochistic or morally callous, since we still judge the depicted events as genuinely bad; (iv) the difference between cathartic relief and mere emotional fatigue; and (v) a further constraint: the theory must distinguish representational sorrow (directed at the fictional situation) from presentational sorrow (directed at the artistic rendering itself), and explain why only the latter—or only a specific interaction of both—can be pleasurable without requiring us to take pleasure in human suffering per se. Flag which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps (Aristotelian, control-theory, “rich experience” advocates). Then identify the pair of desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, explaining the voluntary pursuit of negative affect while denying that suffering is valued for its own sake—and explain whether that tension is apparent or real. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; briefly score catharsis, emotional-control, and meta-response accounts against the list only to illustrate how the criteria work. Conclude by stating what kind of empirical finding or conceptual counterexample would most weaken your judgment about which requirements are essential, and flag where your reasoning about joint satisfiability is most likely to fail.
