As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of science, I have become puzzled by an institutional asymmetry in how different fields treat explanation. In pharmaceutical regulation, a compound is typically approved only when reviewers can articulate a mechanistic pathway linking the molecule to the clinical endpoint, even when the statistical efficacy is overwhelming; by contrast, epidemiological warnings about environmental exposures are often issued on statistical association alone, with the underlying mechanism left entirely unspecified. What theory of inference to the best explanation does this divergence presuppose? Must the regulator treat explanatory coherence as an independent epistemic virtue, or can the asymmetry be explained solely by differences in risk tolerance, tractability, and the possibility of intervention? And does the practice quietly commit to a particular account of explanatory virtue—causal depth, unification, or the suppression of ad hoc assumptions—such that replacing that account would undermine the justification for demanding mechanism in one domain but not the other? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a consistent institutional framework could abandon mechanistic explanation in regulation without also weakening the standing of epidemiological inference.
