I am scheduled to present at an interdisciplinary philosophy of social science seminar an argument for ontologically separating money from race and gender despite their shared institutional constitution. My thesis holds that race and gender are sedimented performative kinds: they depend on historical recognition patterns embedded in perceptual and interactional schemas, so that even if explicit institutional support vanished, residual normative positioning effects would persist across generations. Money, I claim, is a pure coordination artifact with distributed consensus dependency; it lacks any sedimented performative layer, and if institutional consensus mechanisms—legal tender designations, central banking protocols, accounting standards—were fully suspended, the kind would collapse immediately without leaving residual normative traces. I therefore reject the view that these three domains are social constructions in a unified sense, and I propose a three-part taxonomy of institutional dependency modes—sedimented-performative, interactional-iterative, and consensus-coordination—to replace it.

The audience comprises about eighteen people: eight social ontologists working on race and gender construction who are broadly sympathetic to institutional-kind analyses; three philosophers of economics who regard monetary conventionality as overstated by social ontology and want to defend functional indispensability; two legal philosophers concerned with anti-discrimination doctrine who worry that separating money from identity-constituting kinds carries regulatory implications; and five graduate students in philosophy and sociology familiar with the basic debates but uncommitted. The economists tend to invoke coordination indispensability, while the ontologists defend the unity of constructionist analysis across domains.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will come. For each objection, phrase it as the specific faction or interlocutor would phrase it—particularly the committed social ontologists and the philosophers of economics. For each, specify what I should concede, what I should hold firm, and provide a concise reply deliverable in under two minutes. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I must be ready to defend rather than hope goes unasked, and include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely from the legal philosophers on anti-discrimination analogies or from the ontologists on whether my taxonomy smuggles in a nature-culture distinction. Give me a committed assessment: take definite positions on which objections are genuinely damaging versus merely noisy, rank them by likelihood of derailing the presentation, and state precisely where my argument is most vulnerable and what specific conceptual move I should have ready to deploy there. Do not present options for me to select among; take stands, defend them with reasons, and tell me exactly how to allocate my defensive energy.
