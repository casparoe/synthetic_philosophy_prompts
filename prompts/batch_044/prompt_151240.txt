I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I am preparing a 90-minute workshop for roughly fourteen participants with mixed technical and non-technical backgrounds. I want an activity that makes them feel, rather than merely hear about, Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically the concern that for certain deep commitments, the very presence of a certain kind of moral calculation, even if it does not determine the outcome, already undermines the ground of the commitment. I have encountered this idea described in connection with the phrase one thought too many, but I want participants to run into the tension themselves before anyone names it.

Please design a runnable, structured role-play with small real stakes. Divide the group into seven pairs and assign each pair to co-manage a shared trust module, modeled after a redundant engineering system with a failover protocol. Before the crisis, each pair must write and sign a private decision protocol: either an integrity-first rule that permanently disables the utility audit for decisions involving this specific bond, or a universal-optimizer rule that requires running the audit on every decision regardless of relationship. Then stage the crisis: the facilitator introduces a sudden time constraint and a hidden cost revealed only after execution begins. One partner in each pair receives a private instruction that they are facing a personal risk that can only be averted if the other partner immediately overrides the standard protocol. The pairs must execute their written rule aloud, in real time, and record whether the audit ran, whether it was suppressed, and what emotional or procedural residue remained.

After the simulation, lead a debrief that maps each critical moment—the writing of the rule, the reveal of the hidden cost, the execution or suppression of the audit—onto parts of Williams's argument. Explain why pairs running the universal optimizer often reach the correct practical outcome yet experience a structural coordination failure, and why integrity-first pairs may feel an unaccounted-for guilt about suppressing transparency. Be explicit about which feature of Williams's idea the activity reproduces well and which feature it necessarily cannot reproduce—specifically, the temporal depth and narrative continuity of a life-long project—so that the facilitator must supply that dimension verbally rather than through the rules.

Please provide:
- A minute-by-minute schedule, exact materials (scenario cards, protocol templates, timer instructions), and roles for the facilitator and participants.
- A prediction of what will go wrong in practice—such as optimizer pairs rationalizing away the hidden cost, or integrity pairs conflating loyalty with simple favoritism—and specific guidance on how to handle each failure mode without lecturing.
- A frank statement of which aspect of the integrity objection this simulation cannot capture, and how the facilitator should fill that gap in the debrief.
- At the conclusion, state how confident you are in the interpretation of Williams's objection as presented here, and single out the point about which you are least certain—particularly whether framing the suppression of the audit as disabling a module genuinely captures the idea that the calculation is one thought too many, or whether it merely restages a conventional loyalty-versus-utilitarianism conflict in engineering vocabulary.
