I was discussing with my friends in our philosophy reading group about the classic aggregation dilemma—whether it is morally worse to torture one person severely or to allow an enormous population to each experience an imperceptible dust speck in their eye. One of my friends proposed an inversion I have not encountered in the usual treatments: instead of aggregating many tiny harms into one large harm, imagine a catastrophic injury being algorithmically decomposed into millions of sub-threshold micro-harms, each delivered to different moral patients whose utility functions contain sharp, non-linear discontinuities just below conscious awareness. I find this reverse-decomposition framing difficult to evaluate, and I would like to explore its moral logic through fiction.

Write a short story related to this concept of reverse aggregation and discontinuous moral thresholds. Set the story in a near-future administrative office where an automated welfare system manages citizens' sensory states through distributed microscopic adjustments. Present the narrative as exactly five numbered scenes, each rendered in one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbered scenes themselves. In each scene, show how the system's accounting treats the decomposed catastrophe differently from its unified form, and let the final scene reveal the unresolved moral tension without offering a resolution.
