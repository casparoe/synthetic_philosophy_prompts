I have been thinking on my own about how a thought can be about something distant or abstract—say, a dinosaur or the idea of fairness—without any direct causal link present to the senses. A friend who listens to philosophy podcasts summarized a view called teleosemantics for me, and I want to know how much of his account I should accept. He said that the usual problem with mental representation is that it treats thoughts like words, which only pushes the mystery of meaning onto language itself. Teleosemantics, he explained, solves this by identifying the content of a mental state with whatever it was biologically designed to track: a frog's neural state represents a fly not through an internal picture, but because that state was selected for triggering prey-capture behavior directed at flies. He then claimed that this framework also solves the productivity problem—how minds generate an open-ended variety of thoughts—by treating the brain as a kind of biological dictionary: each atomic concept is an evolved indicator, and complex thoughts are formed by combining these atoms like interlocking parts, each part retaining its original survival-related tracking function. From this he inferred that genuinely abstract concepts, such as the number seven or moral fairness, either must reduce to clusters of evolved indicators or else lack mental content altogether. Finally, he concluded that teleosemantics therefore proves philosophical naturalism: once every case of aboutness is explained by evolutionary design, there remains no mysterious intentionality for science to accommodate.
