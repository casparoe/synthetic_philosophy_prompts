I am presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on distributive justice in emergency contexts. The audience will be a mix of moral philosophers—two committed to weighted-utilitarian frameworks for public-health allocation, one Kantian working on imperfect duties, and a decision theorist interested in risk-weighting under uncertainty—alongside applied ethicists and disaster-response coordinators who care primarily about whether any philosophical view can be operationalised in actual triage protocols without producing institutional paralysis.

My thesis is a modified, non-aggregative extension of Taurek’s classic rescue-case argument. I defend what I call indexical non-aggregation: within any given rescue pathway, the moral reason generated by a victim’s claim is indexed to the specific agent-capacity and causal structure linking that agent to that victim, and these indexed reasons do not combine into a scalar weight. Hence, when a single agent can rescue either one identifiable person or five identifiable persons through the same causal pathway, the fact that there are five does not generate a proportionally stronger reason to save the five. I further argue that this indexical structure blocks inter-pathway comparison: saving five people through intervention A and one through intervention B does not yield a comparative moral reason to prefer A, because the reasons generated in A and B belong to different indexical frames. I concede that aggregate outcomes matter for institutional planning, but I deny that they constitute direct moral reasons for the individual agent facing a rescue decision.
