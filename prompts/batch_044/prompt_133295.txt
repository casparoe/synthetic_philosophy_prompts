I am working through a problem set for a graduate seminar on democratic theory, specifically on the boundary problem of who belongs to the demos. To test consistency, my instructor presented me with the advisory rulings of a hypothetical transnational Boundary Commission — not a real court, but a conceptual body that applies a principle of democratic membership rather than formal citizenship — and asked me to extrapolate. Its prior verdicts are these: (1) long-term tax-paying residents without citizenship are included in local forums but not in constitutional ones; (2) citizens who have permanently emigrated and maintain no local economic or social ties are excluded, even if they retain formal nationality; (3) seasonal laborers present for fewer than three months are excluded regardless of economic contribution; (4) future generations, represented by appointed guardians, are included in long-range environmental planning but excluded in annual budget allocations; (5) sentient non-human animals affected by zoning are excluded, though the Commission mandates separate welfare consultation; (6) corporate property-owning entities are excluded as direct members, though worker representatives may sit; (7) adolescents aged 14–17 are included in school-curriculum assemblies but excluded from general municipal bodies.

The new case concerns a dispersed, transnational network of researchers who maintain automated digital infrastructure physically hosted in the jurisdiction. They do not reside locally; they visit only for brief maintenance windows under temporary protected status granted because of political persecution elsewhere. The infrastructure's legal status directly determines regional energy policy, employs no local staff, and is funded by foreign governments. A subset of these researchers request representation in the energy-policy assembly, arguing that the regulatory framework subjects them to coercive authority and that their physical safety and livelihood depend on the infrastructure's continued legal standing. Predict the Commission's verdict if it aims for consistency with its track record. Identify at least two rules that equally explain the prior pattern — for example, one centered on sustained territorial presence combined with capacity for reciprocal justification, and another centered on direct vulnerability to collective decisions regardless of physical presence — and show how each treats this new case differently. Give your best-guess ruling, name the closest analog among the prior verdicts, and state honestly what underlying principle the Commission would be endorsing either way. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would sharply distinguish the two rules you have reconstructed.
