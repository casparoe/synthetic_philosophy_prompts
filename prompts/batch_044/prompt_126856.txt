I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether Darwin and Turing pulled off the same philosophical inversion: producing competence—design, problem-solving, linguistic behavior—without the comprehension traditionally assumed to be its source, but in opposite directions. She kept reducing Darwin to "random mutation plus time" and Turing to "mere simulation," missing that both arguments invert the reasoning that links appearance to underlying cause. I now need to teach the actual structural parallel to a reading group of literature and history graduate students with no background in philosophy of science, in a single ninety-minute session. Do not explain the parallel to me; plan the pedagogical arc.

First, set out the dependency order: what idea must be secured before the students can see either case as an inversion rather than as a mechanistic replacement, and why reversing that sequence collapses Darwin into blind chance and Turing into trickery. Second, identify the one carrying example or paired scenario that anchors both figures: it must exhibit apparent design or intelligent behavior, it must lack both conscious intention and internal representational understanding, and it must not be reducible to pure accident or pure deception—specify the case and explain why using only Darwin's eye or only the Chinese Room fails to carry the shared structure. Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: conflating evolutionary fitness with intentionality, then conflating behavioral indistinguishability with metaphysical identity, then positing a hidden homunculus to restore comprehension—say exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted and with what counter-question. Fourth, give me the end-of-session check that distinguishes genuine grasp of the inversion from mere acceptance that "evolution and machines can be clever." Finally, state what must be explicitly left out—perhaps modern evolutionary psychology, the formal mechanics of the Chinese Room, or contemporary debates about large language models—and justify each omission for this audience and time limit. The aim is that they leave seeing the inversion as one philosophical move instantiated twice, not as two separate historical curiosities.
