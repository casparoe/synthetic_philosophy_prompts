I run a postgraduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence with 16 students, in one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes the participants feel the force of Turing's argument that teaching a learning machine ought to resemble educating a child—using rewards and punishments applied to an initially unorganised system—rather than constructing an adult brain through explicit, inspectable rules. Inject a niche but intelligible constraint drawn from that analogy: each 'child-machine' (played by one student) possesses hidden 'blank sheets' bearing random initial markings—an interference pattern from its 'manufacture'—that distort incoming reward and punishment signals in ways unknown both to the machine and to the 'educators' (the other students). The educators must attempt to train their machine to classify a simple set of stimuli correctly, but they may only communicate through binary reward or punishment tokens; they may not inspect the sheets, ask the machine to explain its reasoning, or issue direct instructions. Provide clear roles, rules, materials (paper, tokens, hidden cards representing the interference patterns), a timeline for the session, and a success condition that does not involve real money. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity—initial overconfidence, a sudden inexplicable failure, the moment the hidden patterns are revealed—onto parts of Turing's distinction between education and programming, predict what will go wrong the first time this is run and how to handle it, and be frank about which aspect of Turing's view—perhaps the genuinely open-ended, long-term development of the child-machine—this exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; open with no preamble and do not restate the question. Write in British English.
