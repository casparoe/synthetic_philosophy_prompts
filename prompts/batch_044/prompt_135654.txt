As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of action, I have become stuck on an argument that seems to undermine every long-term commitment I maintained during my career. I present it with numbered steps and ask you to diagnose it precisely.

(1) Rational choice evaluates options solely by their expected consequences from the present forward.
(2) Past expenditures, being irreversible, have no causal bearing on those future consequences.
(3) Therefore, allowing past investment to influence present choice—honoring a sunk cost—is always a deviation from rational maximization.
(4) Now apply this to diachronic agency: a scientist at year one of a decadal observation program binds her future self to continue, precisely to prevent abandonment at year six when local incentives and funding pressures shift.
(5) At year six, the accumulated labor and capital are sunk; by (3), continuing the program is irrational, and the original binding was merely an attempt to institutionalize future irrationality.
(6) The same reasoning extends to promises, institutional loyalty, and the maintenance of scientific instruments whose calibration requires uninterrupted, non-locally-optimal use.
(7) But if (3) holds, rational agents can never maintain commitments across time, which makes long-baseline science, contractual obligation, and personal integrity conceptually impossible.
(8) So (3) must be false, and honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational.

I feel the argument slips somewhere between single-moment evaluation and intertemporal agency, but I cannot name the exact step or the cost of repairing it. Which premise or inference do you reject? Give me a committed answer: take a definite position, defend it with reasons, and tell me exactly what that rejection costs—do not lay out a menu of options and leave me to choose. I do not want citations, a bibliography, or references to specific texts; I need the argument dissected on its own terms.
