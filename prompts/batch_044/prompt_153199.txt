hey — i'm drafting a chapter on Lewisian conventions and this argument is definately suspicious. here's the chain i'm stuck on:

(1) For Lewis, a convention is an arbitrary regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem in some population P, sustained by common knowledge and a conditional preference for conformity.

(2) Once R is in place, the reason any individual in P has to stick with R is that deviation breaks coordination and frustrates others' expectations.

(3) Now take a zombie convention: a regularity that survives long after the original coordination problem has vanished or changed form. Think of hyper-elaborate IRB consent rituals for minimal-risk online survey work — the original problem (protecting subjects from physical harm) isn't there anymore.

(4) Even in zombie cases, common knowledge still holds: everyone knows everyone expects R, and people generally prefer not to rock the boat.

(5) So by (1)-(2), individuals in P are still genuinely bound by R.

(6) But if R is now actively harmful — it deters useful research, creates false assurance, etc. — then an individual researcher who sees this clearly ought to be able to break R without being irrational or immoral.

(7) If Lewis denies (6), then individual moral insight is impossible whenever a zombie convention is in place.

(8) That seems false. So either Lewis's account doesn't generate real obligations, or something in the chain is wrong.

(9) Therefore conventions can't ground obligations in zombie cases.

where's the slip? i really want to keep (6) — that individual insight should matter — but i'm not sure if i'm misapplying the preference condition (maybe it has to be present-tense?), or if there's a scope shift between population-level regularity and individual-level duty. which step should i reject and what does that cost?
