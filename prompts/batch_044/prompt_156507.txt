Write a dialogue between two decision theorists—call them K and L—who are trying to settle whether the Pasadena paradox, in a specific framing, exposes a real gap in expected-utility theory or merely a mathematical artifact. Frame the game as follows: a fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads; if heads lands on toss n, the payoff is 2^n, but the player can only collect after surviving n days, with daily survival probability (1/2)^n. This makes the expectation an indeterminate form—unbounded reward multiplied by exponentially decaying survival—rather than a simple divergence to infinity. K should defend the "gap" position: because the expectation fails to converge in any standard sense, there is genuinely no fact of the matter about whether to pay the entry fee, and any decision rule that forces a choice smuggles in an arbitrary resolution. L should defend the "artifact" position: apply a lexicographic ordering that treats any branch with nonzero probability of unbounded payoff as dominant over all bounded outcomes, then condition on actually reaching that branch, which restores determinacy. The dialogue must show real back-and-forth—L should press K on why this case differs structurally from ordinary long-tail gambles with tiny probabilities of huge rewards, and K should reply by distinguishing undefined expectation (where no limit exists) from merely remote possibility. Keep the tone formal but accessible to someone familiar with rationalist discussions of decision theory; avoid citations, bibliographies, references to specific papers by name, or page numbers. Paraphrase both the paradox and Scheffler-style concerns about structural limits in your own words, and ensure neither character is a strawman.
