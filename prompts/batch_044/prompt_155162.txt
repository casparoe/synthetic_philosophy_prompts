I've been prepping a unit on Sen's entitlement theory for my grad seminar—looking specifically at Bengal in '43, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward—and i keep running into teh same conceptual snag with my students. Here's the pattern I want a name for. We start with Sen's point that in all three cases there wasn't an absolute shortage of food; people starved because their exchange entitlements collapsed—wages fell, prices spiked, or procurement policies diverted grain. So naturally my students want to call these "political famines" rather than natural disasters. But then the pushback comes: in Bengal the British administration wasn't deliberately starving anyone, in Ireland the landlords and state were negligent and ideologically blind but not genocidal, and in China the local cadres were falsifying harvest reports because of perverse incentives, not because Mao ordered mass death. So the objection is, "If nobody intended the starvation, isn't it still essentially a disaster—just one made worse by bureaucracy and bad incentives?" Then the counter comes back: "No, the point isn't intent; it's that the institutional architecture made the entitlement collapse predictable and preventable, which makes it a moral failure of the system regardless of individual blame."

I can see both sides, but I'm trying to figure out if there's an established term for this exact standoff—where the dispute isn't about food availability (Sen settled that) but about whether labeling a famine "political" requires intentional agency or just structural avoidability. Does this fault line have a name? And if not, can you carve it out precisely—how it differs from ordinary debates about negligence versus malice, and where exactly it fits inside Sen's own distinction between direct and trade entitlement failures? Don't lay out every option; I want a committed answer. Is the "no intent, so not political" move basically a misreading of Sen, or does Sen himself leave room for that distinction?
