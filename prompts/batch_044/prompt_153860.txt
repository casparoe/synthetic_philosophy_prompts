But does this challenge succeed only if moral motivation requires an audience, or does it also depend on a further, rarely examined condition—what we might call epistemic anonymity, in which the agent herself cannot integrate her hidden actions into a continuous moral identity? Your proposal should distinguish sharply between social invisibility (being unseen by others) and this second form of anonymity, then ask whether standard modern replies to Glaucon—contractarian, Kantian, or virtue-ethical—presuppose that the second condition remains intact even when the first is removed. Explain why the existing literature leaves this open: most commentators treat the ring as a simple removal of reputational cost, without asking whether moral self-knowledge survives total opacity. State your methodological approach—conceptual analysis and argument reconstruction rather than empirical study or historical exegesis—and justify it against the obvious alternative of treating the problem as a prisoner's dilemma in evolutionary game theory. Describe your deliverable: for example, a reconstructed taxonomy of invisibility conditions applied to one modern account of anonymous moral behavior, or an argument map showing where each standard reply collapses if epistemic anonymity is taken seriously. Specify what would count as a successful result and what would count as failure (e.g., showing that the distinction collapses into social invisibility after all). Finally, assess honestly whether the project is tractable in one semester, identify the single section you would drop if scope must be reduced, and avoid proposing a bibliography or literature review by citation—refer to positions and arguments by name, noting that you will supply references yourself.
