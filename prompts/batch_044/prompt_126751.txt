As a journalist covering biotechnology and moral philosophy, I regularly assess research proposals that invoke the hedonistic imperative—the view that biotechnology ought to aim at the complete abolition of suffering. I need a reusable evaluation procedure my team can run in about thirty minutes before we decide whether a proposal is conceptually coherent or merely technically ambitious. Operationalize the principle as follows: an intervention is justified only if it eliminates suffering without generating structurally equivalent suffering elsewhere, without destroying the functional or relational conditions that make experience meaningful, and without smuggling in an implicit value hierarchy that the principle itself does not supply.

Present this as an ordered checklist or decision flow in words: the specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with rejection, and where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the conflict is axiological rather than empirical. The procedure must explicitly handle three hard boundary cases: a neural implant that suppresses chronic pain but eliminates nociceptive warning; a proposal to engineer wild prey species so they do not experience predation-related distress; and a pharmacological intervention that abolishes grief but also flattens the emotional range required for deep attachment.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals: first, a viral-vector therapy targeting the neural correlates of chronic back pain in consenting adults; second, an ecosystem-management plan to genetically reduce fear and distress in wild prey populations. Ensure that one of these yields a verdict I will resist—specifically, an endorsement of an intervention that seems to violate an unarticulated boundary condition I care about. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle, especially when suffering reduction conflicts with autonomy, ecological integrity, or the conditions for meaningful choice, and flag the points where a journalist must make a call rather than apply a rule.
