Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them Dr. Lin and Dr. Reyes—who are equally sharp, neither playing strawman, and who respond directly to each other’s actual moves rather than talking past one another. The topic is the wrong-kind-reasons problem, but applied to a collective institutional decision rather than to an individual attitude like belief or desire. The specific scenario: a university tenure committee must decide on a candidate who is undeniably brilliant in research and teaching, but who has a documented record of serious harm inflicted on people outside the university. The committee’s charter lists only “research excellence, teaching effectiveness, and collegiality” as valid criteria; it says nothing about general moral character. Lin argues that the candidate’s moral record supplies reasons of the wrong kind for tenure denial—not merely weak reasons, but reasons that fail to bear on the institutional purpose of tenure, and that treating them as relevant corrupts the evaluation by importing an alien normative framework. Reyes counters that this misapplies the wrong-kind-reasons distinction, which was originally developed to separate reasons that justify an attitude from those that do not; in institutional contexts, all considerations are mediated through a mandate, so the real issue is not “wrong kind” but “outside the mandate,” and calling them “wrong kind” risks turning a design choice about institutional roles into a false metaphysical claim about reason-types. They should then push deeper: Lin claims that even within a mandate, some reasons operate at the wrong level of evaluation (they address the person as a moral subject rather than as a professional role-bearer), and that institutional purpose cannot legitimize every reason it happens to include; Reyes replies that without an independent account of institutional purpose, “wrong level” is just a rhetorical device for excluding reasons one dislikes, and that collective agents do not have the same attitude-justification structure that makes the wrong-kind-reasons problem intelligible for individuals. Require at least three full exchanges in which each philosopher addresses the specific claim the other just made.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Scenario and Stakes (about 120 words): briefly set the scene, identify Lin’s and Reyes’s core positions, and state why this case strains the standard individual-level framing of the wrong-kind-reasons problem.

2. The Dialogue (about 650 words): the actual exchange, with clear speaker labels, real back-and-forth, and direct engagement with prior points.

3. The Unresolved Crux (about 180 words): pinpoint the exact philosophical disagreement that remains—whether it is substantive, largely verbal, or turns on a hidden premise about collective agency and institutional purpose—and explain what it would take to resolve it.
