I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy working on citizenship, pluralism, and institutional design. The standard comparative framing—French republican laïcité versus Anglo-American multiculturalism—is well mapped in the literature, but I am trying to avoid producing another survey of that opposition. Instead, I want to work with what I am calling domain-specific assimilation: the idea that modern states rarely apply a uniform principle of either cultural convergence or group recognition across all institutional spheres. Rather, they seem to operate with implicit domain distinctions—demanding assimilation in some contexts (public education, labor-market entry, civic ceremony) while accommodating divergence in others (religious practice, family structure, linguistic use in private association). This framing is rarely made explicit, yet it describes how actual policy regimes function.

My dissertation will have roughly three years, and I want to build a conceptual agenda rather than an empirical study. I am asking you to help me prioritize: which open conceptual questions about domain-specific assimilation matter most for philosophy and for institutional design, and in what order should I tackle them?

Please defend an ordering—whether of the problems below or of others you think are more upstream—using criteria like tractability for philosophical rather than empirical work, dependency relations (which answers settle others), and what kind of progress over three years would actually shift policy debates. I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want a defended research agenda with honest assessments of what could go wrong.

Here are four problem-types I am considering. Rank them or replace them, and explain the reasoning:

1. Whether there is a coherent, non-circular criterion for classifying an institutional sphere as one that demands assimilation versus one that permits accommodation—especially when the "functional requirements" of an institution are themselves defined by dominant cultural norms.

2. Whether an instrumental justification for domain boundaries (e.g., common language for contract enforcement, shared norms for democratic deliberation) can avoid collapsing into disguised majoritarianism, and whether that collapse can be detected without already assuming the multiculturalist conclusion.

3. Whether a normatively robust distinction between public and private spheres can be reconstructed in this context without relying on the very liberal-republican assumptions that multicultural critics reject—particularly regarding gender, religion, and family.

4. What follows for welfare-state design if we accept that some cultural differences generate legitimate claims to differential treatment in distributive spheres (for instance, religious exemptions in healthcare funding or education subsidies) rather than merely in expressive or symbolic spheres.

For each item you include in a top-three ordering, explain why it deserves priority relative to the others, what a tractable philosophical method would look like for it, and what result—even a negative or dissolving one—would count as genuinely advancing the debate beyond the current stalemate between assimilationist and multiculturalist positions. What would change your ranking if preliminary work over the first year went differently than expected?
