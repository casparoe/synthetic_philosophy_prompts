I am a doctoral student in epistemic logic and modal metaphysics. I am treating the standard Fitch-style derivation of the knowability paradox not as a decisive refutation of the principle that all truths are in principle knowable, but as a diagnostic instrument for clarifying the scope and closure conditions of epistemic operators. For a research exercise, I ask you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps might produce.

Camp A holds that the unrestricted principle ∀p(p → ◊Kp) must be abandoned, because preserving it collapses—via the closure of knowledge over conjunction—into the implausible conclusion that all truths are actually known. Camp B maintains that the principle survives if we reject the implicit assumption that epistemic closure is distributive across all compound propositions, particularly when we distinguish 'being in a position to come to know p' from 'actually knowing p' and allow for localised knowability operators that do not aggregate across worlds.

Please produce a document containing:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—covering the formal derivation (from the knowability premise and closure to the omniscience conclusion), the explanatory target (whether genuinely unknown truths are metaphysically possible), and the limits of standard modal weakenings (for instance, that avoiding collapse in a non-normal logic carries identifiable semantic costs).

2. A disagreements table stating precisely where the camps diverge, with each side's position phrased in wording that camp would accept for itself. In particular, clarify whether their dispute is substantive—turning on modal commitments about accessibility relations—or partly verbal, arising from ambiguity in the term 'knowable'.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one camp. Examples include whether closure holds for propositions involving temporal indexicals, and whether an artificial epistemic agent with bounded memory but perfect reasoning capacities constitutes a genuine counterexample to distributive closure.

4. For each crux, identify arguments or thought experiments both sides agree are relevant, recording in advance each side's predicted outcome. For instance, both might accept that a rigorous demonstration of closure failure within a non-normal modal framework bears on the debate; they should state beforehand whether they regard such a result as decisive or as changing the subject.

Crucially, take a committed position. Do not lay out the options neutrally: argue for specific formulations of the agreed claims, defend your choice of how to phrase the closure condition so that both camps recognise it, identify which disagreements are genuine and which are verbal, and explain why. You may introduce conceptual distinctions not always foregrounded in textbook presentations—such as the difference between distributive and non-distributive epistemic closure, or the behaviour of localised knowability operators—but keep them within the reach of standard philosophical and logical training.

Write in British English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke highly specialised literature that a model without web access could not reasonably be expected to recall. Your answer should be committed, reasoned, and explicit about where the drafting forces a substantive choice.
