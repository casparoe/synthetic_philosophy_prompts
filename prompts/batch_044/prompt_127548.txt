I run an upper-level philosophy seminar of about twelve advanced students in a single ninety-minute session. Design a structured, non-lecture classroom activity—using roles, hidden cards, or forced scoring rather than discussion alone—that makes participants feel the force of the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies, specifically through the tension with deprivation-based harm and the temporal-symmetry problem. The mechanism should be arranged so that participants construct the asymmetry themselves: for example, they should be given paired profiles—one describing a person who was never conceived, the other describing an identical life cut short by death at the same age—with quantitatively identical lost goods, and be required to assign harm scores on a shared scale before they know the pairing exists. Only after scoring and negotiating should the pairing be revealed, forcing them to confront why they weight the two cases differently despite structural identity.

The deliverable must include:
- The rules, materials, and phase-by-phase timeline (silent scoring, paired negotiation, group reveal, and open deliberation) arranged so the problem emerges from their choices before any facilitator names it
- A debrief that maps each phase onto the philosophical parts: the experience-requirement claim, deprivation logic, symmetry pressure, and the "no subject" conclusion
- An honest note on which feature of the Epicurean position the activity cannot reproduce—for instance, the phenomenological absence of death or the metaphysical claim that the dead literally do not exist to be harmed—and how the facilitator must supply it verbally
- A frank prediction of what will predictably misfire the first time (e.g., students invoking "potentiality" or "future orientation" inconsistently, or refusing to score prenatal cases at all) and how to handle it without rescuing them too quickly

Please separate, in your response, what is broadly settled in the literature (that Epicurus denies death harms anyone because there is no subject present to suffer it) from what remains genuinely contested (whether deprivationism answers the symmetry objection, whether the experience requirement can be defended against counterexamples, and whether temporal location alone can ground an asymmetry in harm). Do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask participants to consult texts. If you include any verbatim quotation, verify the exact wording by fetching a source; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks.
