After a long argument with a friend last night about whether structural realism genuinely resolves the problem of theory change or merely displaces the difficulty onto relations, I am drafting the final assessment for a graduate seminar in philosophy of science. I want one essay question that students cannot answer by reciting the standard taxonomy of epistemic versus ontic structural realism, or by summarizing Worrall, Ladyman, and Ross in generic terms. Instead, the question should force them to confront what I have been calling the "individuation regress": the worry that if ontic structural realism eliminates individual objects in favor of pure relations, then either those relations secretly presuppose relata—reintroducing objects as "structural nodes"—or they become self-individuating in a way that collapses into an undefended modal-structural ontology. The prompt should ask students to evaluate whether this regress is fatal to ontic structural realism, and whether epistemic structural realism genuinely avoids it or simply abandons ontology for a cautious empiricism about predictive structures.

Please write the following, in a polished and formal register suitable for an exam committee document:

1. The essay question itself, designed for a 90-minute answer of approximately 800 words. It should make students distinguish the individuation regress from Newman's objection to Russellian structuralism, explain why epistemic structural realism's retreat to epistemology does not automatically dissolve the ontological worry, and argue for a specific resolution—or explain precisely why none is available—without appealing to bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words that directly addresses the regress, clarifies the difference between relational individuation and modal self-subsistence, assesses whether structural nodes are an ad hoc rescue or a principled component of the view, and concludes with a reasoned judgment about whether ontic structural realism survives the challenge.

3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, organized around four must-hit conceptual moves. For each move, state the point value, the specific content that earns full marks, and a one-line note on the typical error or superficial response that should cost points. The scheme should clearly separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one by identifying which moves require original argument rather than summary.
