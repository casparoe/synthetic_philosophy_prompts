I am drafting a lecture on Kierkegaard's social criticism and have encountered the following summary in an unnamed recent essay. The writer states that in Kierkegaard's analysis of modernity, newspapers do not simply report news but actively generate an imaginary collective—the public—that lacks any concrete membership yet wields a phantom authority over private judgment. Because the press must address everyone about everything, it encourages readers to hold opinions on matters outside their direct experience, replacing qualitative distinctions between expert understanding and lay curiosity with the quantitative fact of circulation and consensus. This dynamic produces leveling, whereby the passionate commitment of the single individual is dissolved into the neutral anonymity of the crowd. The writer adds that Kierkegaard treats this leveling as modernity's central spiritual pathology, structurally parallel to Marxist alienation though rooted in epistemic detachment rather than economic exploitation, and that since the public has no real existence, politics founded on public opinion rests on a category mistake. From this the essay infers that Kierkegaard's implicit remedy is hierarchical: he seeks the restoration of an authoritative ecclesiastical or aristocratic voice capable of overriding mass judgment, and it further asserts that this diagnosis applies without alteration to contemporary social media, because algorithmic aggregation reproduces the same anonymous numerical leveling that Kierkegaard described.

I am inclined to accept much of this as faithful to the essay commonly called The Present Age, but I worry that the comparison with Marx, the identification of crowd and public, and the claim about an implicit hierarchical remedy may be distortions rather than interpretations. Could you examine the summary with care? Please proceed continuously through the text, indicating which sentences or clauses a careful reader of Kierkegaard would accept, which conflate concepts he keeps separate—such as the crowd and the public, or reflection and mere indecision—which overstate the press's causal role in producing spiritual loss, and whether the inference about hierarchical restoration and the direct application to algorithmic media actually follows from Kierkegaard's premises. I would especially value clarification on whether the summary correctly distinguishes Kierkegaard's theological concern for the single individual before God from a general sociological lament about modern anonymity.
