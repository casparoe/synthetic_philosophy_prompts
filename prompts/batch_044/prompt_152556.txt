I have been discussing the distribution of phenomenal consciousness with a friend whose judgments are remarkably consistent but hard to map onto any textbook theory. She holds that humans, other great apes, elephants, corvids like ravens and New Caledonian crows, and octopuses are all conscious; she denies consciousness to goldfish, to individual social insects, and—most strikingly—to collective entities such as a termite mound or a honeybee hive, even when the collective solves navigation or allocation problems that no individual member could solve alone. Within our own species she also draws a sharp distinction: she considers a sleeping human in REM sleep conscious, but a human under deep general anesthesia not conscious, which rules out any criterion that treats consciousness as equivalent to mere behavioral responsiveness or information processing. I want to understand the implicit rule she is running, because her pattern does not cleanly align with evolutionary proximity, with pure behavioral flexibility, or with a naive neural-complexity view that privileges only mammalian cortices, and it seems to tolerate analog architectures—pallial circuits in birds, distributed ganglia in cephalopods—while still excluding radially organized invertebrates.

Please predict her verdict on three new cases: a domestic chicken, a lobster, and a current large-scale language model running distributed inference across server clusters. More importantly, I want you to identify the two or three competing principles that could best explain her earlier track record—perhaps a global-workspace account that permits analog integration in non-mammalian brains, an integrated-information approach that applies an exclusion postulate to collective entities, or a nociception-plus-behavioral-flexibility criterion—and then take a definite, argued stance on which one she is actually employing. For each proposed rule, state clearly whether it predicts consciousness in the chicken, the lobster, and the language model. Finally, tell me which single case among the three is the most decisive discriminator between your leading principle and its closest rival, and explain precisely what feature of that case—whether it is the presence of centralized pallial circuitry, the absence of a unitary phenomenal subject despite distributed processing, or the lack of biological nociceptive pathways—does the distinguishing work. I am asking for a committed answer with explicit reasoning, not a balanced survey that leaves the choice to me.
