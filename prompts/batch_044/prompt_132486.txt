I teach a philosophy seminar — about 12 people, 90 minutes — and we're on free will next week. I want an activity that makes Strawson's Basic Argument hit in the gut, not just in the head. You know the argument: you can't be truly responsible for what you do if you're not responsible for the way you are, and you can't be responsible for the way you are because of nature and nurture stretching back to conditions you never chose, so true moral responsibility is impossible. Design a structured simulation or role-play game where participants start making what feel like free choices, but round by round they discover those choices were constrained by prior conditions they didn't pick — personality tokens assigned randomly at the start, then "upbringing" cards that determine which options they even see, then group dynamics that lock them into collective outcomes. They should accumulate points for "good" collaborative decisions but then have to defend whether they deserve them. Include specific roles, rules for how the causal chain gets revealed step by step, a simple token payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps each round onto Strawson's regress (action, mental state behind it, mental state behind that, back to origin). Also tell me what's gonna go wrong the first time I run it — I definatly think people will blame me for rigging the deck — and be honest about which feature of Strawson's view the activity can't reproduce and I have to supply in discussion. Oh, and please don't make it the usual individual trolley-style free will demo; inject something weirder, like framing the nested choices around collaborative art-making or group negotiation so the regress applies to shared outcomes and collective credit/blame, not just one person's actions.
