I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background who is serving as a grader for an introductory philosophy-of-mind module offered through my university's continuing-education program. I have been asked to evaluate a student's exam response, but I want to be careful not to let my lack of philosophical training cause me to miss subtle errors or to mistake contested interpretations for settled fact.

The exam question was: "Joshua Knobe's well-known study in experimental philosophy asks participants about a CEO who implements a program for profit, knowing it will have an environmental side effect. Describe the study's structure, the central asymmetry in participants' judgments, and one philosophical interpretation of what this asymmetry reveals about the relationship between moral evaluation and the concept of intentional action. Then present one serious objection to that interpretation, and explain whether you think it succeeds."

The student wrote the following answer:

"In Knobe's 2003 experiment, participants read about a CEO who launches a program solely to increase profits. In the harm condition, the program also damages the environment; in the help condition, it improves the environment. When asked whether the CEO intentionally brought about the environmental outcome, about 82% of participants said yes for harm but only about 23% said yes for help. This is the Knobe effect, or side-effect effect.

The standard interpretation is that moral evaluation—specifically blame for harm and praise for help—directly structures our concept of intentional action. We do not first identify an action as intentional based on mental-state criteria and then apply moral judgment; rather, the moral valence of the outcome partly determines whether we classify it as intentional at all.

A prominent objection, associated with the philosopher Adams and developed by linguists applying Gricean conversational implicature, claims that the effect is not conceptual but pragmatic. On this view, saying 'he did it intentionally' is often a way of expressing blame rather than describing a mental state. The asymmetry arises because people are more motivated to blame for harm than to praise for help, so they deploy the word 'intentionally' differently in conversation.

I think this objection is weakened by later empirical work. Cross-cultural studies in Japanese and Chinese populations have replicated the asymmetry using non-verbal behavioral measures—such as reaction-time judgments of agency—suggesting the effect operates below the level of linguistic choice. Furthermore, similar patterns appear in attributions of causation and responsibility in non-linguistic tasks, which the pragmatic account cannot easily explain. Therefore, the linguistic explanation fails to account for the full range of data, and the conceptual interpretation remains clearly stronger."
