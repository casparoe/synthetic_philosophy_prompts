I hold a reconstructed version of Berkeley's master argument, extended beyond physical bodies to abstract objects and modal facts. The core claim is conceptual rather than merely epistemic: any attempt to specify what it would be for X to exist unperceived necessarily embeds X within a representational frame, and that embedding contradicts the very mind-independence being asserted. This is not the standard formulation about failing to form a positive idea, but a stronger thesis about ontological specification—namely, that describing the conditions under which X exists without being perceived already treats those conditions as available to a specifying mind, thereby undermining independence. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: which arguments in contemporary metaphysics—concerning grounding relations, negative existential quantification, indirect reference, or the conceivability-possibility gap—would be decisive, and which merely awkward? Are there well-described cases in which we successfully specify mind-independent conditions without tacit mental representation? Has the view been qualified—through appeals to primary qualities, distinctions between direct and indirect conception, or restrictions on what counts as "specification"—into unfalsifiability, and if so, does that insulation weaken or protect it? Please distinguish objections that defenders of the argument should themselves concede from those they could consistently reject, note what predictions this view makes that rival metaphysical accounts do not, and assess whether the position still risks anything substantive or has been rendered immune to counterexample.
