I'm currently working through the philosophy of mind literature on original versus derived intentionality, particularly in light of Dennett's two-bitser argument against Searle's claim that only biological systems possess intrinsic intentional states. Having discussed this with friends who study cognitive science, I've come to hold a specific variant of the gradient view: I think Searle is wrong to treat original intentionality as a binary, biologically anchored property, and that Dennett is essentially correct that interpretation can generate genuinely intentional states in non-biological artefacts—provided those states are embedded in a sufficiently dense network of functional and interpretive relations. More precisely, I take the boundary between original and derived to be a matter of interpretive density and counterfactual sensitivity rather than substrate, which means that even a highly complex artificial system could, in principle, sit somewhere near the middle of the spectrum rather than at the purely derived end. I want to know what would genuinely have to be true for this position to be wrong.

What observations, conceptual distinctions, or empirical findings would count against my gradient view? Would it collapse if biological intentionality turned out to operate via mechanisms that are in principle non-replicable in artificial substrates—say, a form of intrinsic aboutness that does not reduce to functional role—or is that merely an awkward qualification that forces me to relocate the biological anchor without abandoning the gradient? I want you to rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, including what my view predicts that a hard binary account does not, and to say whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counter-example can be absorbed by adjusting where the biological origin lies in the chain—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a position of this kind. Specifically, I'd like you to distinguish between what defenders of hard biological original intentionality in the Searlean mould would themselves concede as counting against them, and what they would dismiss as missing the point, and conversely what a committed gradient-theorist ought to concede as genuinely risky rather than merely inconvenient. I'm particularly interested in a niche case: a 'closed-loop interpreter' that assigns intentional states solely to its own internal representations, with no external biological origin in its causal history. If such a system lacks even derived intentionality, does that undermine my view, or can it be accommodated without collapsing back into a hidden biological dependency?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind who actually work on this topic, and do not present a contested reading—such as whether Dennett's argument establishes genuine original-level intentionality in artefacts or merely licences an instrumentalist stance—as though it were the consensus view.
