I am assisting my grandmother with a digital autobiography project. She uses an application that conducts brief daily interviews, then weaves her answers into a continuous life narrative. Last month she described her childhood village as bustling and communal; this week she insisted it was desolate and solitary. The software flagged this as a "narrative discontinuity" and presented three options: replace the earlier entry with the new one, archive both versions independently, or merge them into a single, deliberately ambiguous passage. My relatives disagree sharply. One argues we have a duty to correct the record and preserve "the true account of who she is"; another claims the newer memory is authoritative because it reflects her present mind; a cousin proposes that there is no hidden "true self" behind these stories—only the pattern that links them—so editing or preserving is merely a practical choice, not a moral one about her identity. I feel there is more at stake than family preference, yet I cannot say whether altering the archive harms her in any meaningful way, or whether treating her life as an editable text misses something important about personhood. Could you advise which option, if any, best respects the nature of her continuing existence, and explain what reasoning supports it? In particular, please indicate what would change your mind—whether, for example, evidence that she experiences the contradiction as distressing, or proof that her neurological patterns remain stable despite the conflicting reports, ought to shift the conclusion. Please respond in British English.
