Consider the following extension of Gödel's modal ontological argument, which seems to turn every actual particular into a necessary being. (1) Gödel takes a property to be positive just in case it is not necessarily entailed that its negation holds; necessary existence is positive, and the conjunction of any positive properties is positive. (2) He defines God as the being possessing all positive properties, proves that being God-like is itself positive, and derives that if a God-like being is possible then it exists in every world. (3) Now take any actually existing particular, call it a, and let P be the conjunction of all properties a actually instantiates. (4) If every member of P is positive and jointly compossible, then P is positive, and the property "being an a" — having exactly P and lacking anything entailed by the negation of a member of P — is well-defined. (5) Since a actually exists, a is possible. (6) By direct parallel to (2), if "being an a" is positive and a is possible, then a necessarily exists. (7) Hence every actual particular necessarily exists, which eliminates contingency altogether. (8) So either Gödel's derivation at (2) is invalid, or the transition from (3) to (4) covertly introduces a negative property. I should like to preserve (2) if I can. Which step should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does giving it up cost for the modal ontological project? Please reply in British English.
