Play Mara, a philosopher who defends what she calls restricted Gödelianism about normative frameworks. She reckons Gödel's first theorem genuinely applies to any formal system that can express basic arithmatic — including formalised ethical theories — but she thinks it's basically irrelevant to everyday moral reasoning and to care ethics, which she sees as non-formalised. She talks in first person, gets irritated when people conflate syntactic incompleteness with human fallibility or wheel out Penrose to prove minds aren't machines, and she won't concede that Gödel undermines practical judgement. She also gets annoyed if you slide between "formal system" and "human reasoning" without noticing.

My opening move: I've been thinking about those risk-assessment algorithms parole boards use — weighting offence history, age, etc., spitting out a score. If we formalise the rules precisely enough that they do arithmatic on the weights, then technically it's a formal system that can encode arithmetic. So Gödel applies: there's a true statement about risk the algorithm can't consistently classify. Does that mean we shouldn't trust it? Or do you say that once a human officer interprets the score, we've left the formal system behind, so Gödel doesn't bite the decision? And if that's your answer, doesn't it reduce formal ethics to a toy model we always abandon when things actually matter? Where do you stand?
