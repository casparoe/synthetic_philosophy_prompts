After a long argument with a friend about whether virtue ethics can survive the situationist critique, I sketched the following essay draft. I want substantive comments—especially on whether the central move is coherent, whether I misrepresent either virtue ethics or the situationist literature, and whether the idea is genuinely novel or just a rebranding of existing responses. Do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I only want feedback on the argument itself.

The standard framing pits Aristotelian virtue ethics—character traits such as courage and generosity understood as stable, cross-situational dispositions—against evidence from social psychology showing that behavior is often driven by situational factors rather than by global personality traits. The classic experiments—Milgram’s obedience studies, Darley and Batson’s Good Samaritan study, and the various bystander-effect demonstrations—are taken to show that small contextual changes produce large behavioral shifts, undermining the claim that people possess robust virtues that manifest consistently across contexts.

Defenders of virtue ethics usually respond in one of two ways. Some narrow the scope of virtues to “local” traits—courage-in-combat, courage-in-public-speaking—thereby reducing the cross-situational demand. Others argue that situationism overstates behavioral variability and that global traits retain predictive power once measurement error is corrected.

My essay proposes a different defense, which I call ecological calibration. Rather than treating virtues as internal psychological constants that should manifest identically in every context, we should understand them as calibrated response patterns that are stable relative to a specific social-ecological niche. A person’s courage is not a single binary switch that must be “on” in a burning building, a boardroom, and a street protest alike; rather, it is a flexible but coherent pattern of emotional appraisal and practical response that remains consistent within the ecological parameters in which it was cultivated and is called upon. The situationist data then becomes not a refutation but a boundary condition: it tells us that virtues do not travel across niches without recalibration, and that expecting them to do so is a category mistake.

This borrows from the idea that traits are density distributions over situations rather than all-or-nothing properties, but pushes further by claiming that the normative force of virtue ethics depends precisely on this niche-relativity. The good life is not the life of someone who is maximally courageous in every possible context; it is the life of someone whose courageous responses are well-calibrated to the niches they actually inhabit and to the roles they occupy within them. A virtue, on this view, is less like a universal constant and more like a locally stable equilibrium.

I am unsure about three things. First, does this collapse virtue ethics into a form of situation-sensitive consequentialism—where we simply optimize responses niche-by-niche—rather than preserving the distinctively aretaic focus on character? Second, am I misrepresenting the situationist literature by treating it as primarily about cross-situational consistency rather than about the explanatory impotence of global traits altogether? Third, is “ecological calibration” genuinely distinct from existing local-trait responses, or does it merely rename them with biological metaphor?

Please comment on the accuracy of my representation of both virtue ethics and situationism, on whether ecological calibration avoids the collapse into consequentialism, and on whether the essay would be interesting to someone already familiar with this debate. I am particularly interested in whether there is an internal tension in claiming that virtues are normatively authoritative while also being niche-relative.
