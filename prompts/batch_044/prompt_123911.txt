At a workshop on AI governance, two researchers got into a sustained argument about the normative architecture of high-stakes decision systems. Dr. Chen argued that such systems should be designed as rule-bound deontological agents: they must never violate certain procedural constraints—for instance, using protected demographic categories as explicit inputs—even when doing so would maximize expected welfare, because allowing real-time optimization over those categories collapses the distinction between statistical prediction and discrimination, and because autonomous utility calculations are too epistemically fragile to be trusted in contexts with incomplete information. Dr. Patel replied that this distinction is illusory: every deontological rule embedded in an AI is itself the product of an implicit cost-benefit analysis performed by the designers at the constitutional stage, so labeling the result "non-consequentialist" merely relocates the utilitarian calculus rather than escaping it; a well-calibrated system that genuinely weights harms and benefits will avoid the same inputs without needing an arbitrary prohibition that might backfire in unforeseen cases. Chen countered that this conflates the justification of a rule with its internal architecture: a system can be constrained for reasons that are never activated inside its reasoning loop, just as humans observe legal norms without recalculating the social contract at every decision point. Patel then said that analogy fails for artificial agents because human rules are sustained by social practices of interpretation, exception-making, and forgiveness that machines lack; without those practices, a rigid rule in an AI either produces catastrophic literalism or requires a hidden meta-rule for exceptions, which is simply utilitarian reasoning in disguise. Chen’s final move was that Patel’s framework presupposes we can construct a cardinal utility function over complex social outcomes, but in domains like healthcare triage or criminal risk assessment, “utility” is not an observable quantity—it is a projection of the programmer’s values—whereas a deontological prohibition is at least transparent about what it forbids and does not smuggle normative commitments behind mathematical formalism. They asked the room to referee. Please adjudicate the four moves above—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right—explain what each party is missing about the other’s best point, and say who is closer to being right overall.
