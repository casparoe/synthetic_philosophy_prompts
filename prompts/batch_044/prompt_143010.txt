Zermelo considers the objection that selecting one element from each non-empty subset of an infinite collection requires an explicit rule or law. He responds that, although no such law may be available, one may still conceive a simultaneous assignment that pairs every non-empty subset with exactly one of its members. These assignments are arbitrary in the sense that they are not determined by any shared property of the chosen elements, yet he treats the totality of all such assignments as a definite object—a choice function—from which the well-ordering follows. I need help understanding the precise logical and philosophical status of this move.

Specifically: Is Zermelo asserting the existence of a mathematical object for which no constructive procedure can be given? If so, what justifies treating it as definite rather than as a fiction? Does his defense depend on a Platonic view that sets and their selections exist independently of our ability to describe them, or can it be sustained as a purely formal stipulation within an axiomatic system? I am also interested in whether this idea of simultaneous but ungoverned selection can serve as a coherent analogy for narrative omniscience—an authorial consciousness that knows each character's decision without possessing a deterministic law that generates every choice. I must work without access to external scholarship, so please treat the paraphrase above as the only text we share.
