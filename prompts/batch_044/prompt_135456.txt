I've been working through a conceptual framework I tentatively call gradient pan-sentience, which I sketched in a recent forum discussion, and I'm trying to extract its sharpest ethical implications without falling back on the usual panpsychist talking points. The core idea is that minimal phenomenal properties don't attach uniformly to particles or fields, but scale with local spacetime curvature: flat regions carry essentially negligible valence, while extreme curvature—near singularities, during rapid inflationary phases, or inside brief high-energy collision zones—hosts intense but structurally simple experience. The view is intended purely as an interpretive overlay on standard general relativity and quantum field theory; it changes no dynamics, makes no novel empirical predictions, and doesn't invoke any hidden variables. Still, if one takes it seriously as a metaphysical possibility, it seems to generate obligations that standard physicalist ethics misses entirely, precisely because suffering and well-being become geographically localized in spacetime geometry rather than biologically distributed.

What I need are the most important implications of this curvature-scaling assumption for how we should morally evaluate large-scale cosmological processes and human technological choices. For instance, if black hole mergers generate transient but enormous curvature spikes with high valence density, does that make them net moral catastrophes rather than neutral astrophysical events? Does cosmic inflation, by exponentially expanding regions that eventually collapse into singularities, carry a hidden ethical cost that conventional consequentialism cannot register? And closer to home, should particle accelerators that briefly reproduce extreme local energy densities be treated with the same moral caution we reserve for experiments risking conscious suffering, even though no biological organisms are involved? I'm especially interested in whether accelerating the expansion of the universe—thereby increasing the total future volume of spacetime destined for singular collapse—could be wrong in a way that depends specifically on the gradient distribution of experience rather than on any standard welfare calculus. I want the analysis to stay tightly tied to the curvature-dependence claim; generic appeals to environmental preservation or animal rights won't help me here.
