The debate between defenders of grand-narrative history—accounts that trace broad causal arcs from cognitive and agricultural revolutions through state formation, exemplified by Harari's Sapiens and Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel—and critics such as Graeber and Wengrow, who stress archaeological diversity, local contingency, and the absence of deterministic stages, has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than empirical. Defenders treat counterexamples as local noise within a valid macro-pattern; critics treat the same macro-patterns as cherry-picked teleologies. Both sides can absorb nearly any new excavation or ethnography by adjusting the scope of their claim, and neither has specified in advance what finding would genuinely falsify the framework. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is driven by an unacknowledged disagreement over what I will call explanatory elasticity: whether a historical explanation must hold at every local scale to count as valid, or whether it can be genuinely explanatory at a macro-scale despite frequent local exceptions. Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement verbal—perhaps about whether 'cause' here means local trigger versus macro-condition—or is it substantive, turning on incompatible standards for what counts as a historical explanation? Or are the two camps answering different questions altogether, one about predictive or structural regularities and the other about moral-political narratives that present themselves as inevitable history? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement by engaging with what each side actually says about evidence, scale, and counterexample, not by surveying interpretations. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal criterion for scale-appropriate explanation, a specific class of archaeological or ethnographic finding that breaks the elasticity, or an argument that the dispute is irreducibly normative and should be reconceived as such.
