You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in metaphysics, philosophical logic, and early analytic philosophy, particularly the problems of relations and propositional unity. Before giving your final diagnosis, please start with some brief brainstorming: map out the logical form of the argument below, flag any hidden premises or scope shifts, and note where an equivocation between domains might occur. Think carefully about the costs of rejecting each premise.

Here is a reconstructed argument my study group calls the composition regress argument. We built it by extending Bradley’s classic problem about the unity of the proposition into a broader claim about all structured entities, then applying that broader claim back against itself.

(1) Any structured entity—whether a proposition, a fact, or a causal explanation—requires not just its parts but a principle that actively unifies them into one thing rather than leaving them as a scattered plurality.

(2) In a proposition such as Socrates being wise, the unifying principle is a relation of predication that actually binds the subject to the predicate.

(3) But this binding relation is neither identical to Socrates nor identical to wisdom; it is a distinct entity that relates them. So it too must be unified with Socrates and with wisdom.

(4) Therefore we need a second-order unifier U1 to bind the first relation to Socrates and to wisdom.

(5) By parallel reasoning, U1 requires a third-order unifier U2, generating Bradley’s regress.

(6) If the regress never terminates, then no structured entity is ever fully constituted; there is only an infinite, incomplete sequence of parts and partial binders.

(7) Now apply the same reasoning to causation. In the explanatory claim that a spark caused a fire, the causal relation must bind spark and fire into one unified explanatory structure. So causation is subject to the identical regress.

(8) But if causation collapses into an infinite regress, then no causal explanation is ever complete, which makes successful science impossible.

(9) Science is clearly successful and possible.

(10) So the regress must be blocked somewhere—perhaps by treating relations as internal, adopting an adverbial theory, or accepting a primitive, unanalyzable notion of combination.

(11) Any blockage that saves causation can be applied to propositions as well. Conversely, any blockage denied to propositions must also be denied to causation if we are consistent.

(12) Therefore either we accept the regress for propositions and deny the possibility of science, or we save science and dissolve propositional unity into a mere aggregate.

(13) Both outcomes are absurd.

(14) So our initial premise (1) must be false: structured entities do not require a distinct unifying principle.

But here is the suspicious reversal. If (1) is false, then propositions are not genuinely unified; they are only lists or aggregates. Yet this very argument is presented as a single reasoning that establishes (14). If propositions lack unity, then steps (1) through (14) are just an aggregate of assertions, not one argument that can compel acceptance of the conclusion. So (14) undermines its own status as a conclusion.

We feel something slipped between (11) and (12), or perhaps in the move from propositions to causation in (7). Where does this argument actually go wrong? Which specific step should we reject, and why did that step look so plausible? In particular, we are reluctant to abandon (1) entirely, because without some principle of unity it seems we have only scattered lists rather than thoughts. But we also want to keep (9). Can both (1) and (9) be preserved together? If not, which is less costly to give up, and what does each option require?

Please identify the precise failure—whether it is an equivocation between logical and causal structure, a hidden modal slide, a category mistake in applying propositional analysis to explanation, or the self-undermining structure of (14)—and then price the alternatives. Explain what a defender of Bradley’s original regress, a modern relationist who takes external relations as primitive, and an adverbialist about predication and causation each have to pay in order to resolve the tension consistently.
