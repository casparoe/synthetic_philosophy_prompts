I'm working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right, specifically the discussion of international relations around §§323–324, and I'm trying to make sense of the following passage on war:

"War is not to be regarded as an absolute evil and as a purely external accident, which itself therefore has some accidental cause, be it injustices, the passions of nations or the holders of power, &c., or in short, something or other which ought not to be. It is to what is by nature accidental that accidents happen, and the fate whereby they happen is thus a necessity. Here as elsewhere, the point of view from which things seem pure accidents vanishes if we look at them in the light of the concept and philosophy, because philosophy knows accident for a show and sees in it its essence, necessity. It is necessary that the finite - property and life - should be definitely established as accidental, because accidentality is the concept of the finite. From one point of view this necessity appears in the form of the power of nature, and everything is mortal and transient. But in the ethical substance, the state, nature is robbed of this power, and the necessity is exalted to be the work of freedom, to be something ethical. The transience of the finite becomes a willed passing away, and the negativity lying at the roots of the finite becomes the substantive individuality proper to the ethical substance."

My difficulty is this. Hegel claims that in the state nature is "robbed of this power," so that the passing-away of the finite becomes a "willed passing away" belonging to freedom rather than to natural mortality. Yet he also insists that war—an essentially contingent, external relation to a foreign state—is the specific moment where this ethical negativity is actualized. I'm trying to determine whether the passage commits him to the stronger metaphysical claim that a state which eliminated all external enemies—say, through a universal federation that made war structurally impossible—would thereby cease to possess the "substantive individuality proper to the ethical substance," because it would lack the external occasion for this willed nullification of finite interests. Or does the logical structure of the concept allow the state's negative self-relation to be satisfied internally, through domestic constitutional dynamics, without requiring an actual foreign adversary?

More precisely: does the analogy drawn in §323 between the state's internal organic powers and its external war-relation imply that both are necessary conditions for state individuality, or only that they share the same logical form? And if the latter, what in the text prevents us from reading the "ethical health" argument—where prolonged peace breeds corruption—as a contingent historical observation rather than a conceptual necessity? I'd appreciate help unpacking whether the passage genuinely entails that perpetual peace is incompatible with statehood in Hegel's specific sense, or whether that conclusion imports a stronger thesis about the ineliminability of external negation than the argument actually supports.
