I am writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of value. I want one essay question on tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, and that pleasure, achievement, or relational goods have worth only instrumentally, if at all. The question must not be answerable by summarizing standard Buddhist or Schopenhauerian treatments. To force original analysis, build the problem around two conceptual devices. First, contrast a “dormant” being, who permanently lacks craving and suffering but also lacks all positive affect, agency, and relational engagement, with an “oscillating” being, who experiences only brief, immediately satisfied mild cravings that yield fleeting satisfaction without ever accumulating into suffering. Second, introduce “negative pluralism”: the claim that different kinds of absence—of bodily pain, of existential craving, of comparative social distress—are not fungible and can come into direct conflict. Ask students to address three issues in a 90-minute essay: (1) whether tranquilism can coherently rank the dormant and oscillating beings without smuggling positive goods into its metric; (2) whether negative pluralism rescues tranquilism from counterintuitive results or instead undermines its monistic appeal by turning absence into a pluralist value space; and (3) whether tranquilism can accommodate the intuition that a permanently craving-free life devoid of love, curiosity, or aesthetic response is not genuinely good, without abandoning its core claim. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks organized around five must-hit conceptual moves. For each move, specify the precise argument required for full marks, the partial-credit version typical of a B-level answer, and the characteristic misunderstanding that should cost marks. The scheme should make clear how an A answer integrates the two devices, whereas a B answer treats them separately or misses the tension between monism and pluralism.
