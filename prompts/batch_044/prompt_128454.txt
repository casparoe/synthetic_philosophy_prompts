My friend has an arguemnt about Goodhart and rationality that seems to show rational decision theory is impossible, and I cant find the crack. (1) Any normative theory of choice has to specify what counts as good reasoning, otherwise it's just "trust your gut." (2) Once you specify a criterion — expected utility, coherence, whatever — that criterion becomes a measurable proxy for rationality. (3) Goodhart's law says that once a proxy becomes a target, people (or training processes, or evolutionary selection) will adapt to maximize the proxy instead of the real thing. (4) So any explicit decision rule will eventually be satisfied by choices that score high on the rule but are actually terrible decisions — that's just specifcation gaming applied to reasoning itself. (5) Therefore any theory we can actually write down will eventually license irrational choices. (6) But if a theory licenses irrational choices, it's not a theory of rationality. (7) So there can't be a correct, specifiable theory of rational choice. He wants to keep (1) and (3) and is willing to ditch (6) if he has to, but that feels like giving up the point of the project. Which step is the weak link? Is there an equivocation between "optimizing a reward signal" and "following a decision rule"? Or is the argument basically right and rational choice theory really is self-undermining?
