I serve on the Advisory Board of the National Institute for Long-Range Policy Assessment (NILRPA), and we are required to circulate a formal recommendation before next month’s plenary on whether the Institute should retain, reject, or conditionally maintain the so-called Delayed-Coupling Resource Model (DCRM) within our official forecasting toolkit. The DCRM is essentially a modern reformulation of Malthusian dynamics, modified by what its proponents call “recursive baseline adjustment” — a methodological move I want you to examine with real precision rather than treating it as jargon. In the 1970s the model predicted that agricultural output per capita would enter sustained decline by 2000 unless population growth fell below 0.5 per cent annually; that outcome did not occur, owing to the Green Revolution, synthetic fertiliser expansion, and short-run productivity gains. For two decades it was treated as a textbook case of falsified long-run prediction and was removed from government planning documents.

Now, an interdisciplinary group argues that the DCRM was never genuinely falsified, only “phase-shifted”: they claim the model operates on a 120- to 180-year validation window, and that the 1970–2020 divergence was an explicit “technological rescue phase” that the framework allows but does not treat as indefinite. Drawing on soil-phosphorus depletion curves, groundwater-salinity trajectories, and what they term “hidden-carrying-capacity debt,” they project that per-capita agricultural resilience will fall below replacement thresholds between 2060 and 2090. Critics respond that this is precisely the ad hoc immunisation strategy philosophy of science warns against — extending the prediction horizon, introducing unobservable debt variables, and redefining the baseline so that any future result can be interpreted as confirmation. The stakes are concrete: if retained, the DCRM will inform long-term land-use and water-rights allocations; if rejected, the Institute will rely exclusively on short-cycle economic models that omit ecological carrying-capacity dynamics altogether.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, addressed to the Board and written in British English. It should recommend a definite course — retain the DCRM outright, reject it, or retain it subject to explicit, operational conditions — and defend that recommendation with clear reasoning. You must take a committed position rather than laying out options for us to choose between, though you should acknowledge honestly where empirical proof ends and institutional judgement begins. In particular, engage seriously with the strongest argument for the opposite view: is “right but early” a coherent epistemic category for institutional forecasting, or does permitting such rescue conditions collapse the model into unfalsifiability? Evaluate whether “recursive baseline adjustment” preserves genuine predictive content or drains it, and address what conditions — if any — would make the model’s retention responsible rather than reckless. Avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the reasoning should stand on its own for a board that includes non-specialists, but include enough conceptual precision that the underlying issues about falsification, temporal scope, and theory rescue are unmistakable.
