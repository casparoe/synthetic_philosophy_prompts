I'm designing a decision-tree taxonomy for an advanced applied-ethics module on pest management, specifically contrasting broad-spectrum neurotoxic insecticides—such as neonicotinoids and organophosphates—with targeted behavioral-disruption techniques like pheromone-based mating confusion and RNA-interference sprays. The tree should be built from logical commitments rather than from author names, with three sequential branching questions: first, whether invertebrates—particularly eusocial insects and larval Lepidoptera—possess welfare interests that carry direct moral weight; second, whether the ecological replacement value of a pest population (pollination services, trophic cascade effects, decomposition pathways) generates independent constraints on lethal control even if direct duties to invertebrates are denied; third, whether epistemic uncertainty about nociception and subjective experience in organisms without centralized neural architecture supports a precautionary default against lethal methods, or instead licenses lethal intervention where human-health and economic benefits are substantial. At each leaf, provide a descriptive label or mark it unnamed, followed by a one-sentence statement of the position's most awkward commitment—for instance, its stance on whether urban integrated pest management can ethically deploy broad-spectrum agents when pheromone disruption is technically feasible but costlier. Where the logical space produces a combination that appears unoccupied, flag it and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature. The instrument should allow a respondent to locate their view by answering concrete case-based commitments without knowing the philosophical labels in advance, and it should include at least one branch that turns on the distinction between lethal population reduction and non-lethal reproductive suppression.
