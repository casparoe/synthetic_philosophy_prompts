I have recently been managing chronic migraine, and during a recent flare-up I found myself genuinely uncertain whether I "knew" my own pain at the precise intensity I reported to my neurologist. That experience made the abstract debate about whether phenomenal states are luminous suddenly feel personal. For a reading group I constructed the following argument, but I now worry it proves far too much and collapses into an absurd global conclusion rather than the familiar localized failure.

Here is the argument, with premises numbered.

(1) A phenomenal condition C is luminous only if, necessarily, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains.

(2) The KK principle holds for phenomenal knowledge: if a subject is in a position to know that C obtains, then she is in a position to know that she is in a position to know that C obtains, iterating without end.

(3) Take a gradable state such as feeling cold, which admits of borderline cases where small increments in temperature do not change whether the state obtains.

(4) If luminosity holds, then in clear, non-borderline cases the subject knows she feels cold.

(5) By (2), this generates an infinite hierarchy: she knows she knows she feels cold, and so on, with each level requiring its own distinct satisfied epistemic-access condition.

(6) Because human cognition is finite, no subject can simultaneously satisfy infinitely many distinct epistemic-access conditions at a single moment.

(7) Therefore, if both (1) and (2) hold, no human can ever be in a luminous gradable state, even in perfectly clear cases.

(8) But people clearly do know they feel cold in clear cases, and similarly for pain.

(9) So at least one of (1) or (2) must be false, and in fact false not merely for borderline states but for all phenomenal conditions.

The conclusion overreaches: instead of showing that luminosity fails only near a threshold, it uses iterative self-knowledge to destroy first-person authority globally. I would prefer to keep both (1) and (2) if possible, since abandoning luminosity undermines direct self-knowledge and abandoning KK weakens the transparency of knowledge. Where exactly does the slip occur? Is the error in treating the hierarchy as requiring simultaneous satisfaction (step 5 or 6), in assuming gradability is incompatible with sharp epistemic boundaries (step 3 or 7), or elsewhere? Please diagnose the weakest link, explain why it looked plausible, and price the cost of rejecting it versus giving up one of the premises I want to preserve.
