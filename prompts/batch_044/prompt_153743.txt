I'm closing a two-day workshop on epistemic peer disagreement with a brief literary interlude, and I want a poem that actually engages the philosophical substance rather than decorating it. Please write a 16-line blank-verse poem spoken in the first person by a researcher who has just received anonymous peer review that contradicts her central finding; she recognizes the reviewer as a full epistemic peer—identical training, identical dataset, equivalent methodological competence—yet she also knows that her own analysis turned on a subtle, defensible decision about how to weight an outlier observation that the reviewer likely handled differently. The poem should be structured so that its form tracks the debate: lines 1 through 8 should enact the conciliationist pull, with the speaker's syntax growing more tentative, her confidence visibly softening, and the meter loosening or breaking at precisely the moments where she considers lowering her credence; line 9 should serve as a volta that does not resolve the tension but instead exposes the hidden premise shared by both sides—namely, that reasoning is transparent to its owner—thereby opening space for the steadfastist response.

In lines 10 through 16, the speaker should defend maintaining her belief not through arrogance but through disciplined first-person opacity: she can cite her own internal reasoning about the outlier without circularly assuming her conclusion, and the language should regain firmness without becoming dogmatic. The poem should leave the disagreement suspended rather than resolved, closing on an image—perhaps two calibrated instruments measuring the same anomalous event and producing divergent but internally consistent readings—that literalizes the stalemate. The rendering should be accurate enough that a philosopher familiar with conciliationism (the view that peer disagreement requires downward revision of confidence) and steadfastness (the view that rational maintenance is permissible when one has independent, non-circular reason to trust one's own assessment) would recognize both positions correctly rendered, not caricatured; avoid reducing either view to mere stubbornness or mere capitulation.
