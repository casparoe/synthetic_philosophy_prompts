I've noticed a recurring pattern in the collapse of social norms that doesn't map cleanly onto the explanations I'm familiar with — not moral argument, not shifting material interests in the Bernard Williams vein, and not the honor-cascade dynamics Kwame Anthony Appiah describes in cases like dueling. The pattern is this: a practice doesn't disappear because people decide it's wrong or costly; it dissolves because the community gradually loses shared criteria for what counts as an instance of it. Take dueling: it wasn't only that honor codes stopped requiring satisfaction, but that people could no longer coordinate on whether a particular slight was of the kind that warranted a challenge, or whether issuing one was courageous or foolish. The norm became, in a sense, unindexable — defenders couldn't point to a clear exemplar of proper dueling to justify preservation, and critics couldn't isolate a clear exemplar of abuse to condemn. I think I saw something structurally similar with certain local shunning rituals in early-twentieth-century communities: the behaviors that triggered exclusion became so contested that the practice faded not through repeal but through an inability to apply it consistently to any specific case.

Is there an established term for this kind of normative collapse — where a rule dies not through rejection of its content, but through the epistemic unmooring of its extension? If so, how does it differ from ordinary vagueness in rules, from Appiah-style loss of honor's grip, and from interest-based accounts that rely on changing payoffs? If not, what's the right way to carve the phenomenon: is it a failure of common knowledge about application conditions, a semantic drift in the concept of the practice itself, or a distinct mechanism? And what are its boundaries — does it cover any ambiguous rule, or only cases where ambiguity is strategically exploited by both defenders and critics?
