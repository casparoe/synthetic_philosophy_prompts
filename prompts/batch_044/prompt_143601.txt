Student answer:

"Sor Juana holds that women must be educated because they have rational souls identical to men’s, which means ignorance can never be a virtue for them. She cites learned women in scripture—such as St. Catherine of Alexandria—to prove that divine approval extends to female study. She applies a Thomistic idea: the intellect is a potency that needs actualization, and leaving a woman’s mind idle renders her soul imperfect in a way that does not apply to male souls, since women’s intellectual nature is more fragile. To the domestic-duty objection, she responds that household labor is intellectually deadening and that women with a scholarly vocation should therefore be released from it. She also gives a reductio: if ignorance were truly virtuous, then bishops and priests ought to remain ignorant, which is absurd. Her fundamental principle is that education is a positive law granted by ecclesiastical authority, so if the Church withdraws permission, the entitlement vanishes. This shows her argument depends on institutional approval rather than on nature, and it anticipates Wollstonecraft’s modern demand for state-enforced equality in schooling."
