I am drafting an essay on Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment. Rather than treating it as a straightforward proof that the soul is an immaterial substance separable from the body, I want to defend a more restricted interpretation: the argument establishes only an epistemic asymmetry—self-awareness is given immediately and non-inferentially, whereas awareness of the body and the external world is mediated and therefore can be suspended—without necessarily committing Avicenna to ontological dualism.

Specifically, I plan to argue that the suspended man knows he exists not by grasping an essential nature but through an irreducible indexical reference: he is aware of being the subject of awareness even when all qualitative, spatial, and sensory content has been eliminated. I then want to press this reading against a precise feature of the scenario itself. Avicenna imagines the man suspended in empty air with limbs spread and eyes covered. If every sensory channel—including proprioception—is genuinely absent, the argument succeeds; but if the spread-limb posture and the implicit feeling of suspension provide a minimal bodily schema, then self-awareness may still be anchored to an implicit sense of embodiment. On that alternative, Avicenna’s position might be closer to a minimal embodied-cognition view—where self-reference requires a bare spatial-bodily frame—than to Cartesian substance dualism.
