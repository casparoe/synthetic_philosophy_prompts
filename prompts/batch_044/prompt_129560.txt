I'm writing this after a tense session of a review panel convened to examine why a mid-sized manufacturing cooperative collapsed in 2024, five years after its board approved a controversial vertical-integration strategy. One panel member, an organizational psychologist, has presented what she terms an "inevitability narrative": she traces a direct causal chain from the 2019 leadership's "hubristic" forecasting habits—specifically an overreliance on internal models and dismissal of external risk consultants—to a sequence of strategic missteps that, in her telling, made bankruptcy practically predetermined. She insists these cognitive flaws were "obvious" in the 2019 board minutes and that anyone who failed to object was professionally negligent. I was present during those 2019 deliberations, and my memory is genuinely different: the forecasting data was split, the consultants endorsed integration with caveats that seemed prudent given the information then available, and the cooperative's final collapse in 2024 was triggered by an unforeseen geopolitical supply disruption in 2023 that no internal or external model had incorporated.

When I challenge her reconstruction, she responds that outcomes reveal true underlying probabilities and that my defense amounts to retrospective rationalization by a participant protecting his reputation. I am not seeking legal advice about board liability; rather, I want to understand whether there is a principled conceptual distinction between judging the 2019 decision by the epistemic position of the deciders at the time and judging it by what the 2024 outcome revealed. Specifically, does knowing that the cooperative failed systematically license us to say the failure was "in the cards," or does it distort our assessment of what was genuinely knowable versus what only appears obvious in hindsight? And separately, I am troubled by the neatness of her plot: she links personality traits to strategic errors to bankruptcy with almost literary coherence. Is she offering a genuine causal explanation, or is she substituting a satisfying narrative arc for the messier, possibly overdetermined reality of organizational decline—imposing determinism on events that may have involved substantial contingency? I need help identifying the precise philosophical structure of these two intertwined errors, particularly since she claims her account is "just following the evidence where it leads."
