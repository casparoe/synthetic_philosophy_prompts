Below is a set of my intuitive moral verdicts regarding meat consumption and wild-animal suffering, especially the comparison between beef and chicken, the moral significance of crop-cultivation casualties, and indirect harms. I want to know whether they cohere.

1. Eating beef from conventional feedlots is seriously wrong. The animal’s prolonged confinement, capacity for suffering, and longer lifespan make the harm grave in a way that is not easily offset.

2. Eating chicken is, in my judgment, somewhat less wrong than eating beef for a comparable nutritional unit, even though I know that far more individual birds suffer and die per calorie consumed. The lower sentience and shorter lives of chickens seem to diminish moral weight, yet I remain uneasy because the aggregate suffering may actually be larger.

3. The incidental killing of field rodents, insects, and birds during mechanized planting and harvest is not a decisive reason to reject plant-based diets. The harm is unintended, structurally unavoidable for anyone who eats, and lacks the intentional, sustained cruelty that characterizes factory farming.

4. Eating wild-caught fish is worse than eating farmed beef. Wild capture involves prolonged, unregulated suffering and broad ecological disruption, whereas farmed beef—though clearly bad—occurs within a system that at least permits minimal welfare oversight and predictable endpoints.

5. Consuming lab-cultivated meat that requires no ongoing animal suffering, even if the original cell line involved a one-time biopsy, is morally permissible. Once the process is decoupled from living animals, the central wrong is removed.

6. The case that unsettles me: I consistently feel that beef is the greater evil, yet when I attempt to apply a pure aggregate-suffering calculus, chicken production appears to generate more total suffering per unit of nutrition. I keep reversing myself on whether individual cognitive capacity or total number of affected beings should dominate, and I cannot stabilize my intuition.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving a weighted interaction of sentience, lifespan, intentionality of harm, and substitutability—that captures these judgments? Please state it precisely, indicate which clauses are motivated by which verdicts above, apply it to two cases I have not listed—specifically, (a) farmed crickets raised for protein at high density, and (b) a hypothetical diet of exclusively foraged wild plants that avoid mechanized agriculture but still cause some unavoidable accidental insect mortality—and tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without distortion. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
