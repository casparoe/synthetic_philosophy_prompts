I am preparing an independent research presentation for my high school philosophy of science seminar on whether artificial intelligence models of analogical reasoning can illuminate conceptual change in the natural sciences. For this project, I want to apply Douglas Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell’s Copycat architecture—specifically its account of fluid concepts, analogical slippage, and reasoning within tightly constrained microdomains—to a familiar but philosophically under-examined episode: the International Astronomical Union’s 2006 redefinition of Pluto and the discovery of Eris shortly thereafter. I am not asking for an astronomical or ecological account of these bodies; rather, I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions, using Copycat to illuminate the taxonomy dispute and using the dispute to test whether Hofstadter’s view of concept fluidity holds outside artificial letter-string domains.

Please treat the IAU debate and the 2006 resolution as your central case study. First, map the microdomain precisely: identify the entities being compared analogically—Pluto, Eris, Ceres, Neptune, and perhaps exoplanets—and specify what plays the role of Copycat’s “letters” or “groups” in this scientific context. Second, trace the conceptual slippages: show where the category “planet” shifts its boundaries under analogical pressure, analogous to how Copycat allows a concept like “successor” or “letter-group” to slide into “numerical-order” or “alphabetical-position.” Did the IAU’s introduction of the “clearing the neighborhood” criterion function as an artificial rigid boundary imposed to halt that slippage, or was it itself a form of conceptual slide? Third, evaluate whether this episode supports Hofstadter’s claim that real-world concepts are inherently fluid rather than classically defined, and note exactly where the Copycat analogy breaks down—for instance, whether the institutional authority of the IAU introduces a top-down constraint absent from Copycat’s parallel, bottom-up processing, and whether that institutional factor undermines or merely complicates the comparison. Conclude by saying whether Pluto’s demotion reveals that scientific taxonomy behaves like a Copycat microdomain, or whether it shows that institutional decision-making can freeze fluid concepts in ways the model never anticipated.
