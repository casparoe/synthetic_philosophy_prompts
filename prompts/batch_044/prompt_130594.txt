I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science with training in formal logic and epistemology, though without specialized background in mathematical decision theory. I am trying to define a dissertation-scale research agenda centered on the problem of induction in the wake of Goodman's new riddle. The familiar responses—appeals to natural kinds, lawlikeness, pragmatic entrenchment, and syntactic constraints on projectibility—are well-trodden, and I want to avoid a purely expository project. What interests me is a less-explored tension: Goodman treats predicates as descriptively anchored, yet much contemporary work in conceptual engineering treats certain predicates, particularly normative ones such as "rational" or "just," as partly stipulative or functionally defined. This suggests a niche but I think tractable line of inquiry—whether the grue problem gets off the ground for engineered or normative predicates, or whether it transforms into a problem about the stability of functional roles across inductive contexts.

Given this framing, which open questions or lines of work in the post-Goodman landscape should I prioritize over three to four years, and in what order? I am especially interested in the following comparisons:

- Extending projectibility analyses to normative or engineered concepts, and determining whether Goodman's symmetry arguments collapse, mutate, or require reconceptualization;
- Re-examining the metaphysics of natural kinds with explicit attention to inductive support, and assessing whether this is now largely downstream of other commitments;
- Formalizing projectibility conditions within non-classical or modal frameworks, versus pursuing conceptual analysis of inductive practices in scientific taxonomy.

Please defend your ordering with explicit criteria: upstream versus downstream dependencies, tractability for a philosopher working largely alone, and the kind of result (including a negative result) that would cause you to reorder these priorities. I want an agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. In particular, address whether treating predicates as engineered rather than discovered changes which questions about induction are live, and whether that shift makes some traditionally central debates overrated for my purposes.
