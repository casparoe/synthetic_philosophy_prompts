I'm preparing an audit of my views on individual obligations within collective-harm structures for a workshop on moral aggregation, and I want to know whether the architecture actually holds together. Here is where I stand:

(i) I distinguish two metaphysical structures of collective harm: "threshold-aggregated" harms, in which individual acts are causally inert below a collective tipping point (e.g., climate emissions), and "practice-constituted" harms, in which the wrong exists only in virtue of widespread participation but is not reducible to summed individual wrongs (e.g., industrial animal agriculture).

(ii) For threshold-aggregated harms, I accept the standard inefficacy objection in full: my personal carbon reduction makes no counterfactual difference to climate outcomes, so it cannot be wrong on direct causal grounds.

(iii) Despite (ii), I hold that I am obligated to reduce my carbon footprint, but the ground is not causal efficacy; it is an "equal-sacrifice" principle—each agent should bear a proportionate share of the collective mitigation burden, regardless of whether that share is decisive.

(iv) For practice-constituted harms, I reject the inefficacy objection entirely. Eating meat is wrong not because I cause an extra animal to suffer, but because I participate in a practice that systematically treats sentient beings as mere resources, and this wrong is agent-relative: it applies to me even if universal compliance elsewhere were achieved.

(v) I extend the equal-sacrifice reasoning to democratic participation: in a large electorate, my vote is causally negligible, yet I am required to vote for legitimate candidates because civic maintenance is a collective good to which each citizen owes a fair share.

(vi) I endorse a general conditionality principle for obligations justified solely by collective goals: if the goal is fully secured by others' compliance, my contribution lapses. So if global mitigation targets are met without me, or democratic legitimacy is maintained by near-universal turnout elsewhere, my corresponding obligations disappear, even though the physical acts (using electricity, casting a ballot) are unchanged.

(vii) At the same time, I maintain that genuinely moral obligations must be "counterfactually robust"—whether I ought to φ should not depend on contingent facts about what others happen to do, otherwise morality collapses into a coordination game rather than a system of individual duties.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severely. For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs; then rank the tensions by severity. Also tell me which single revision—giving up one commitment—would clean up the most conflicts. Be specific about whether (ii) and (iii) can genuinely coexist, whether (v) survives (vi), and whether (vii) can be reconciled with the conditionality in (vi) without abandoning the agent-relativity claimed in (iv). I have no prior commitment about which one to drop.
