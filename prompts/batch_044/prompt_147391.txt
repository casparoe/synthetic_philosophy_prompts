Consider the argument in Aristotle's Politics, Book I, Chapter 5, concerning the natural slave. Aristotle observes that some people appear able to understand rational principles well enough to follow commands and take part in shared deliberation, yet do not possess the deliberative faculty as an authoritative, originating power in their own souls. He concludes that such individuals are, by nature, instruments belonging to another: they participate in reason only by perceiving it, not by producing it, and therefore their proper role is to serve as animated instruments through which a master who possesses full practical reason acts. He further suggests that this relationship is mutually advantageous, because the slave gains the direction of reason that he lacks, while the master gains the labor required for a good life.

I am trying to understand whether this reasoning conflates two distinct kinds of dependence. On one hand, there is cognitive dependence: the need for external guidance in forming practical judgments. On the other, there is agential dependence: the absence of legitimate authority over one's own labor and its products. The text seems to move from the observation of the first to the assertion of the second without an explicit bridge. Could you help me reconstruct the logical structure? Specifically, does Aristotle offer any independent justification for the claim that being unable to deliberate authoritatively for oneself necessarily entails being rightfully subject to another's command over one's work? Or is that inference left as an unstated premise? Moreover, if we imagine a person who genuinely requires another's practical wisdom for complex decisions but retains full ownership and control of their labor, would Aristotle's criteria still classify them as a natural slave, or does his concept implicitly require both forms of dependence to coincide? Please parse the passage carefully, distinguishing where cognitive subordination is described from where agential subordination is prescribed, and indicate whether the argument can stand if the two are separated.
