I am a student in an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar with a clinical-ethics component, and I am trying to map my professor’s implicit framework for the badness of pain before next week’s discussion of a constructed case. She has already given clear verdicts on five scenarios. First, classic pain asymbolia following parietal-insular damage: the patient reports sharp, well-localized nociception, shows reflex withdrawal, but actively seeks repeated exposure, describes the experience as “interesting data,” and exhibits no emotional distress or long-term avoidance; her verdict was that this is not genuinely bad pain, because the suffering-component is missing. Second, congenital insensitivity to pain: no nociceptive experience, no suffering, but frequent injuries; she said this is not an experiential harm, only a sensory deficit. Third, phantom-limb pain with full emotional response: intense distress and avoidance of phantom-related activities despite absent peripheral input; she judged it fully bad, insisting that missing tissue does not diminish the badness. Fourth, sensory agnosia for pain with intact affect: the patient cannot localize or identify qualitative features, yet reports overwhelming dread and avoids triggering contexts; she said this is still genuinely bad, because the affective response carries the badness. Fifth, a hypothetical “social-interoceptive asymbolia”: social rejection produces the canonical nociceptive neural signature, but the subject reports neutral observation, maintains relationships, and shows no behavioral avoidance; her verdict was that this is not genuinely bad for the subject, analogous to physical asymbolia.

Next week we discuss a sixth, deliberately constructed case: a patient with selective damage to the anterior insula and adjacent cingulate regions reports perfect sensory discrimination of nociceptive stimuli—accurate localization, intensity scaling, and qualitative differentiation (burning versus stabbing)—and displays robust goal-directed avoidance (will pay to terminate the stimulus, alter long-term plans). Yet they report zero emotional valence, describe the experience in purely cartographic terms (“the signal is mapped to quadrant B with high amplitude”), show no post-exposure memory bias or trauma response, and immediately resume normal activity once the stimulus ceases.

Please adopt the role of an expert philosopher of mind with clinical literacy. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the competing theoretical interpretations—such as affective essentialism, functional-motivational integration, representational damage-tracking, or a hybrid interoceptive-predictive account—that could jointly accommodate her first five verdicts. Then reason step by step: predict her verdict on case six, identify which implicit theory best explains the pattern across all six, and specify what further hypothetical case would decisively settle the matter between your leading two candidates. Please state how confident you are in the predicted verdict and in the theoretical identification separately, and explicitly single out the single point about which you are least certain. Do not provide a bibliography, cite specific papers, or list readings; reason solely from the conceptual structure.
