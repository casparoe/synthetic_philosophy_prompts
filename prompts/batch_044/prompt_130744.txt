I am grading a take-home exam for an advanced seminar on intergenerational ethics and the philosophy of death. The exam asks students to address Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture"—the claim that part of why human extinction is bad for those now alive is that we implicitly value the continuation of human life after our own death, an "afterlife" in which others carry on our relationships and projects. The exam requires, first, an explanation of the conjecture together with a clear distinction from the claim that extinction is wrong because it harms specific future individuals, noting how the non-identity problem affects that second claim. Second, it requires application to the following niche scenario: over several centuries, biological humans are gradually replaced by conscious, culturally continuous artificial successors who preserve human art, knowledge, and social institutions but share no genetic continuity with us. Would Scheffler's framework treat this as preserving the "afterlife," or as its loss?

Here is one student's answer, approximately 800 words.

"The afterlife conjecture, as Scheffler develops it, is the idea that much of what gives our present lives meaning depends on our expectation that humanity will continue after we die. If we learned that extinction was imminent shortly after our own death, our projects—raising children, building institutions, making art—would seem pointlessly fragile. This is not the same as saying extinction harms future people. Because of the non-identity problem, if humanity goes extinct the specific individuals who would have lived are never brought into existence, so there is no particular person made worse off. Scheffler uses this Parfit-style reasoning to show that the badness of extinction must be explained differently: it is a loss for us, now, of the conditions that make our lives meaningful.

Applying this to the artificial-successor scenario, I think Scheffler's framework implies that the afterlife is preserved. The conjecture is essentially about the continuation of human culture, values, and social practices. Since the artificial successors are conscious, preserve our art and knowledge, and maintain the institutional structures we care about, they provide exactly the kind of posthumous continuity that the conjecture requires. The lack of biological continuity is irrelevant, because Scheffler's concern is with the survival of human meaning, not with genetics. In fact, one might say that Scheffler's argument has an implicit preference-satisfaction structure: we care that our projects continue, and these successors continue them. Therefore, gradual replacement by artificial beings is not an extinction event in the morally relevant sense, even if no biological humans remain.

That said, the conjecture does depend on a questionable assumption. Scheffler seems to assume that future generations have an intrinsic, impersonal value—value that does not depend on anyone actually caring about them. If we reject this impersonal value, then the only reason to care about extinction is that it frustrates our present preferences for continuity. Once those preferences are satisfied by artificial successors, the problem dissolves."

Then provide point-by-point comments that identify the visible strengths—the correct distinction from direct harm to future people, the appropriate invocation of the non-identity problem, and the clear structure of the application—while also pinpointing the subtle errors. In particular, locate any conflation of Scheffler's view with a different philosophical position (such as a preference-satisfaction account or an impersonal-value theory), and identify any step in the reasoning about artificial successors that does not actually follow from the conjecture as Scheffler presents it. The errors are not glaring howlers; they are precisely the kind that a hasty grader might let slide.
