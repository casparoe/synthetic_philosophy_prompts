I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy of mind, and I have been trying to understand the debate over whether large language models can be said to genuinely hold beliefs. In my circles, people often reach for an analogy from software design: attributing beliefs to an AI is like treating an object-oriented abstraction—say, a UserAccount class that "knows" its credentials and "intends" to authenticate—as if it were a mental agent. The claim is that this is purely instrumental: it makes the system maintainable, but no one thinks the object has an inner life, so we should treat LLM belief-attribution the same way.

I'd like a proper audit of this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: in the AI system, what plays the role of the class definition, the instantiated instance, the encapsulated state, the method call, the inheritance hierarchy, and the garbage collector? Second, show where the mapping fractures—particularly around the fact that an LLM's weights are updated globally via backpropagation across a training corpus, which lacks any clear analog to an object's localized state mutation or to a programmer's explicit design intent. Third, identify what the analogy quietly smuggles in: does it presuppose that mental-state attribution is legitimate only when backed by a designer's explicit specification, and if so, does that unfairly rule out evolutionary or learning-based origins of belief?

Finally, evaluate whether the instrumentalist conclusion—that talk of AI beliefs is just pragmatic shorthand, like OOP abstraction—survives once these disanalogies are visible, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work. If there is a more apt software-engineering analogy for the intentional stance—perhaps involving distributed systems, type inference, or debugging by observing outputs—propose it and explain where it holds and where it collapses.
