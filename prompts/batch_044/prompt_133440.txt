I write fiction in which characters recall events out of sequence and construct coherent pasts from fragments, and this has led me to accept a particular view of conscious timing. I hold that the correct interpretation of the color phi phenomenon and related temporal anomalies is Stalinesque rather than Orwellian. When a moving dot changes color and observers report seeing the new hue midway through its trajectory, there is never a conscious experience of that intermediate color at that intermediate location. The brain does not reach backward to revise the content of an already-completed experiential episode, as if rewriting history books after the fact; instead, it manufactures in the present a unified report—like staging a trial with false witnesses—so that the appearance of continuous change is assembled only after both stimuli have arrived at cortical processing centers. I take this to mean that the actual timing of experience tracks the arrival and integration of neural information, not the subjective order in which things seem to unfold, and that first-person testimony about precisely when something was seen is therefore an unreliable guide to the temporal architecture of consciousness.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the observations, arguments, and formal results that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. Are there neurophysiological findings—about the latency of recurrent feedback loops, the conditions under which postdictive masking fails, or the temporal dynamics of prefrontal integration—that would demonstrate genuine retroactive insertion of content into an earlier experiential window rather than forward-constructed narration? Which phenomenological descriptions or first-person reports, if taken at face value, would be fatal to the Stalinesque story rather than merely awkward? Could evidence from other domains—studies of confabulation in narrative memory, the persistence conditions of perceptual objects across saccades, or analogies with fictional reconstruction—undermine the comparison I am relying on, or could they instead collapse the Stalinesque account into its rival? I also want to know what defenders of this view would themselves concede as genuinely counting against them: perhaps specific reaction-time asymmetries, the possibility of interrupting the illusion at precisely the intermediate spatial point, or patterns of error that a purely forward-constructed model cannot reproduce. Furthermore, I need an honest assessment of whether this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every contradictory subjective report can be dismissed as just another manufactured narrative, is that a sign of explanatory robustness or a vice that empties the view? And which of the live escape routes are principled: for instance, weakening the claim from “no experience whatsoever” to “no accessible or reportable experience,” and does that retreat preserve the core insight or abandon it? I would find it most persuasive if you could rank the defeaters from those that would be fatal to those that are only embarrassing, and say clearly where the position is genuinely exposed and where it is well insulated.
