I'm trying to clarify my commitments on vagueness by testing them against an artificial sorites series. Consider a digital image that begins as pure noise (frame 0) and is transformed, one pixel at a time, into an unmistakable photographic portrait (frame 10,000), with no hidden jumps. My reactions:
(1) Frame 0 is definitely not a portrait.
(2) Frame 10,000 is definitely a portrait.
(3) At frame 5,000, statistical face-detection algorithms register strong correlations, yet no human observer recognizes a face; I say it is not a portrait.
(4) At frame 6,000, trained experts consistently identify a face though casual viewers do not; I say it is a portrait, merely an obscure one.
(5) At frame 7,500, where experts split evenly and casual viewers are at chance, I think there is genuinely no fact of the matter.
(6) Nevertheless, frame 5,500 — minimally different from 6,000 — pulls me to call it a portrait too, which threatens to slide all the way back to frame 0.
(7) Learning that the rendering software contains a hidden threshold at frame 6,237 should not by itself determine whether that frame is a portrait.

Is there a coherent principle behind these? Does (3) versus (4) turn on perceptual accessibility, expert authority, or statistical structure? Can I maintain both (5) and (7) without treating vagueness as mere ignorance of a sharp boundary? Which judgment — (4), (5), or (6) — is the outlier that should be dropped, and what does sacrificing it cost? Also, what should I say about frame 6,250, where trained experts are exactly divided, and about two observers with identical visual processing who disagree?
