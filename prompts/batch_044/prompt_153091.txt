I am helping to design a three-year research agenda for a small interdisciplinary group—political philosophers, a formal decision theorist, and an institutional designer—who want to move beyond standard treatments of the paradox of voting. They have enough resources for sustained collaboration on one major conceptual problem, but they want to avoid simply rehearsing pivotal-probability arithmetic or restating the instrumental-rationality dilemma in familiar terms. Instead, they are drawn to structural questions that sit at the boundary of the paradox and institutional design: for instance, whether the rational-irrationality framing holds up once voting is embedded in layered delegation chains where cognitive labor and authority are separated; whether expressive or constitutive accounts of voting genuinely dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it to a different normative register; and what the paradox implies for the legitimacy of algorithmic or proxy-mediated aggregation systems in which individual pivotal probabilities become computationally opaque. There is also a deeper methodological tension—whether resolving these issues requires preserving the unitary rational-agent framework or abandoning it for distributed or collective reasoning models.

Given these directions, which two or three open questions or lines of work should the group prioritize if their goal is not merely to refine the philosophical taxonomy but to produce results that could actually reshape how democratic legitimacy and institutional design are evaluated? I want a defended ordering: for each, explain why it is upstream of the others, what makes it tractable for this particular mix of skills over three years, and what kind of result—formal, normative, or institutional—would count as progress. I am especially interested in the trade-offs between tractability and downstream impact, and in whether any of these questions are actually overrated: that is, directions that appear profound but, if settled, would change very little about either theory or practice.
