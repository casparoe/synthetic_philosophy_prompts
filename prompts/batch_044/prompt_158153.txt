Question: Buridan's ass starves between two identical hay bales because it lacks any reason to prefer one. Now change the setup: the ass has to walk left or right around a fence to reach the exact same hay bale. Both paths are equal in length, risk, and outcome. The ass only cares about eating, not about which side it comes from. Is this still Buridan-style paralysis? And if the ass flips a coin to pick a side, does that solve the problem or just push the paralysis back onto the coin?

Student answer (abridged):

The classic case involves symmetric outcomes, so the ass has no rational basis to choose one bale over the other. In the fence version, the outcomes are literally identical—the same hay—so there is actually no choice problem at all. Rational choice theory only cares about outcomes, not paths, so the ass should just go either way without drama. Paralysis only happens if you wrongly think you need a reason for the path. Using a coin isn't solving anything; it replaces rational indecision with arbitrary noise, and the coin itself has no stake in the result. Some philosophers like Kant might say we need a transcendental will to break symmetry, but that's really about moral agency, not animal choice. So randomization is just irrational, and the fence case isn't Buridan's ass at all—it's a pseudo-problem.
