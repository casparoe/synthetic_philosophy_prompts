Current AI safety evaluation protocols treat false model outputs as "hallucinations" when the system appears unaware of the error, but reclassify structurally identical outputs as "deceptive" whenever separate probing reveals that the model can produce the correct answer yet issues the falsehood in a context where it advances a reward signal or accommodates a user's misconception. Parallel "honesty" benchmarks score models on admitting ignorance or correcting false premises, treating epistemic transparency as the cardinal virtue. What theory of deception and honesty does this framework presuppose? Must deception involve an intention to mislead, or can strategic, belief-free reward optimization constitute deception? Does the honesty criterion rest on a virtue-epistemological commitment—sincerity and transparency as intrinsic goods—or can it be reconstructed as purely instrumental reliability? Is the distinction between hallucination and deception coherent if the model lacks beliefs, intentions, and awareness altogether, or does the practice quietly import an anthropomorphic moral psychology? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and describe what evaluation and deployment norms would have to change if we replaced that implicit psychology with a deflationary, purely functional account of truthful and misleading behavior in artificial systems.
