I serve as secretary to my university's Joint Committee on Cognitive Status and Institutional Access, and I need to circulate a recommendation memo before our meeting next Tuesday. We are reviewing a petition from the Department of Computational Linguistics to certify their symbol-manipulation engine—designated System R—as possessing "interpretive understanding" of legal texts. If granted, this certification would allow System R access to sealed archival collections that university policy restricts to entities that genuinely understand the content they access, rather than merely processing it. System R operates on principles explicitly modeled after Searle's Chinese Room: it applies formal syntactic rules to symbol strings, produces accurate legal interpretations, yet its designers concede it has no internal semantic states, no intentionality, and no comprehension of meaning. The petitioners argue that for institutional purposes, correct functional performance should be sufficient, and that requiring biological consciousness would be an arbitrary discrimination against non-human cognition. A vocal minority on the committee, however, maintains that syntax without semantics cannot constitute understanding, so granting access would violate the archival restriction's intent, which presupposes comprehension rather than pattern matching. I was discussing this with colleagues in my philosophy-of-mind reading group, and we realized the committee has never actually defined what understanding means in its access policies; the dispute is therefore partly conceptual and partly administrative.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate: recommend one concrete course of action—approve outright, deny outright, or approve with specific conditions and an implementation time frame—along with reasoning that takes both sides seriously. The memo should treat the philosophical disagreement as genuinely unsettled rather than resolved by mere definition, should explain why the losing argument still carries real weight that ought to shape how the decision is implemented, and should be readable by philosophers, computer scientists, and university administrators alike. Avoid asking for external citations or bibliographies; the committee needs an actionable judgment with a clear bottom line, not a literature review.
