I'm a high school student preparing a presentation for my philosophy and literature course, and I'd like to read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the lens of David Lewis's account of convention and the evolution of norms. Rather than using the broad summary often given in textbooks, I'm working with a stricter framing: a Lewisian convention is an arbitrary regularity in behavior that solves a coordination problem, sustained by common knowledge that each person expects the others to continue conforming, and where each prefers to conform on condition that they do. Some critics contend that this framework overstates the role of arbitrariness and common knowledge, suggesting instead that many norms are focal points or evolve through repeated interaction without that precise epistemic structure. I want to apply this more technical, contested framing to the novel.

Read Lord of the Flies through this lens. Focus on the conch shell, the rules about the signal fire, and the protocols for speaking at assembly: do these function as genuine Lewisian conventions, or do they fail one or more of Lewis's conditions—perhaps because they are not truly arbitrary (the conch is the loudest object available), or because common knowledge fractures once Ralph and Jack divide the group? How does Golding dramatize the evolution of norms—from the initial democratic procedures to the hunting rituals and the violence that replaces them? Does the novel suggest that these norms are culturally specific British school conventions that collapse when adult authority is removed, or does it present them as universal coordination devices? Where does the philosophical framework illuminate a scene—say, Piggy's insistence on the rules versus Jack's rejection of them—and where does the novel resist being read this way, perhaps by showing behavior driven by fear or desire for dominance rather than by failed coordination?

Please treat both Golding's text and Lewis's theory with equal seriousness: don't reduce the novel to a mere illustration of convention theory, and don't distort the theory to match a plot point. If you argue that the boys' norms are or are not conventions in Lewis's sense, justify the claim with reference to specific dynamics in the book. Finally, indicate what Lord of the Flies might contribute to philosophical debates about convention—something that formal argument about coordination problems alone might miss.

One request about how you present your analysis: please clearly distinguish what is relatively settled in the philosophical literature on Lewisian convention—for example, the basic conditions of common interest, arbitrariness, and mutual expectation—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether conventions require explicit common knowledge or whether focal points should be treated separately. Do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus view among philosophers who study this topic.
