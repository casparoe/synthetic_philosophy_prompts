Over the past year, since I became the primary caregiver for my mother, who has advanced dementia, I have found myself performing daily acts—administering medication, managing finances, soothing confusion—that I initially undertook from a sense of filial obligation but that have gradually become interwoven with exhaustion, resentment, and a strange, persistent affection. I have begun to wonder not merely whether these acts are right, but whether they retain any moral worth given that my motives are so irreducibly mixed. To explore this, I am moderating a colloquium interview with a philosopher who has recently defended what she calls the non-proxy thesis of moral worth: the view that an act possesses moral worth only if the agent is moved by the normative reason itself, not by a representational proxy or heuristic stand-in that merely tracks the right-making feature, and that this requires a kind of structural transparency in which the agent grasps the reason’s authority without treating it as one weight among others in a cost-benefit calculus. She further holds that motivational residues—dormant countervailing desires that play no causal role—do not undermine worth, but that motivational overdetermination by both a direct moral reason and a proxy reason typically does, unless the direct reason is genuinely causally efficacious rather than epiphenomenal.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of the thesis. I want at least one question on each of the following: (1) the overdetermination problem, specifically how she distinguishes a causally efficacious direct reason from an epiphenomenal one when both are present, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—anticipating that she will invoke a counterfactual dependence test; (2) the status of motivational residues, particularly whether a residue can be causally inert yet still color the phenomenology of the act in a way that undermines worth, with a follow-up keyed to her likely denial that phenomenological coloring is normatively relevant; and (3) higher-order ignorance, namely whether an agent who acts for the structurally transparent reason but falsely believes she is acting from a proxy reason can still possess moral worth, and whether her false self-conception matters, with a follow-up keyed to her likely defense of a de re rather than de dicto requirement on motivation.

Additionally, include at least two further questions that engage objections from the literature on moral worth—such as the worry that her view makes moral worth excessively rare, or that it cannot accommodate supererogation—without allowing her to deflect with generic appeals to idealization. Each of these should also carry a targeted follow-up.

The questions should be suitable for a public colloquium audience of philosophers and advanced students: rigorous, precise, and respectful, but unafraid to expose tensions in the view.
