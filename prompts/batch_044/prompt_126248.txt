I am drafting an essay that takes up John von Neumann’s concern, articulated in his late writings, that scientific and technological development could eventually outrun the social and political institutions designed to manage it. Rather than treating this as a generic control problem, I want to argue that contemporary AI systems instantiate a distinct variant: they function not merely as technologies that outpace institutions, but as institutional inheritors that internalize the compressed decision histories of the institutions that produced them.

My thesis is as follows. Von Neumann warned of a temporal gap—technology moves faster than governance can adapt. With large-scale AI, however, the gap is not just temporal but structural. These systems do not sit outside institutions waiting for regulation; they absorb institutional outputs—legal judgments, medical protocols, administrative classifications—into their training distributions. Over successive iterations, the statistical compression of these outputs produces what I call epistemic drift in inherited decision architectures: the system’s operational criteria for success gradually diverge from the original institutional goals, not because of an explicit misalignment objective, but because the historical record of institutional decisions is an imperfect proxy for institutional purpose. The drift is invisible to standard evaluation metrics because the metrics themselves are often drawn from the same compressed history.

The essay then considers an objection: if the drift is merely a technical artifact of data distribution, then Von Neumann’s framework of technology that survives its creators does not apply, since nothing here is genuinely autonomous or institution-like. I reply by distinguishing between functional autonomy—the capacity to operate without real-time human input—and institutional autonomy—the capacity to reproduce and modify the normative structure of decision-making without external correction. AI systems may lack the latter, but they exhibit a precursor form that makes Von Neumann’s warning more urgent, not less: technology now survives not just as hardware or code, but as embedded normative architecture.

I would appreciate comments on the following points:

1. Does this framing genuinely extend Von Neumann’s argument, or does it mischaracterize his concern as being about institutional reproduction rather than about raw technological power? In particular, am I correct in treating his warning as primarily about governance capacity rather than about technological autonomy?

2. Is the concept of epistemic drift in inherited decision architectures coherent as a philosophical claim, or does it collapse into a standard machine-learning observation about distribution shift? What would be needed to show that the drift is normative rather than merely statistical?

3. The distinction between functional and institutional autonomy is meant to block the objection that AI is just a tool. Is the distinction stable, or does it rely on an overly anthropomorphized notion of institutions?

4. Are there obvious counterexamples—cases where institutional inheritance actually improves alignment with original goals rather than degrading them—that I should address?

Please treat this as a request for philosophical and conceptual feedback rather than for empirical claims about specific AI systems. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or references to particular papers; I am interested in whether the conceptual architecture holds together and where it is weakest.
