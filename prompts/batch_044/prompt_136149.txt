I'm auditing an upper-level ethics seminar in which we're working through Kant's 1797 essay on the supposed right to lie out of philanthropy, and after class two study partners—Leah and Marcus—continued arguing about whether his absolute prohibition can be sustained within his own moral system. They asked me to referee, but I genuinely could not tell at several points who was right, so I want a move-by-move adjudication rather than a broad summary.

Leah began by insisting that Kant's rejection of lying to the murderer at the door is not consequentialist but rests on a perfect duty owed to rational nature itself: to utter a falsehood corrupts one's own capacity for truthful communication, regardless of the outcome. Marcus agreed the argument is not utilitarian, but he claimed that Kant's universalization reasoning in the essay covertly depends on a social-contract premise—if lying were universal, promises and testimony would collapse, harming everyone—which is an empirical harm-avoidance consideration that conflicts with the deontological framework of the Groundwork. Leah countered that the contradiction is formal, not empirical: universalized lying makes rational discourse conceptually impossible, because truthful assertion is a condition of communication among rational beings, so no consequentialism is smuggled in. Marcus then introduced a distinction he said Kant neglects, between uttering a falsehood (falsiloquium) and strategic silence or ambiguity. He argued that if one can protect the hidden victim by equivocating—without technically lying—then the practical urgency of the case evaporates, and Kant's absolute rule becomes a merely verbal prohibition that permits the same protective result through linguistic maneuvering. Leah replied that this confirms rather than undermines Kant: the moral failure is the willing of falsehood, not the withholding of information, so Marcus's workaround preserves exactly the boundary Kant draws. Finally, Marcus pressed what he called an internal tension. If lying violates a perfect duty to oneself—corrupting one's rational nature—then the duty is owed primarily to the liar, not to the murderer; yet by refusing to lie and thereby allowing the murderer to use one's truthful statement as a means to kill, one permits oneself to be instrumentalized in a gravely immoral act. He concluded that Kant's own Formula of Humanity—never treating rational nature merely as a means—actually supplies a reason to lie in this very case, producing an unresolved conflict between two formulations of the categorical imperative.

Can you adjudicate their exchange step by step—rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—and say who is more right overall? In particular, address whether Marcus is correct about an internal tension between universalization and the Formula of Humanity, whether Leah is right that strategic ambiguity is compatible with Kant's view rather than an evasion, and what, if anything, both are missing about how Kant's distinction between perfect and imperfect duties bears on the case.
