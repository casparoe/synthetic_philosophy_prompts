I am working through the problem of religious arguments in democratic deliberation, but I find the standard framing—broadly, Rawlsian exclusivism against inclusive liberalism—too coarse for my purposes. I want the full specification of what a theory of public justification must actually achieve to count as adequate in a pluralistic society characterised by two conditions I will call “doctrinal opacity” and “threshold recognisability”: the first being that citizens hold comprehensive commitments whose full inferential implications are not transparent even to the holders themselves; the second being that a reason counts as publicly accessible not if every citizen actually accepts it, but if it lies within the cognitive reach of anyone who shares the basic democratic premises, regardless of whether doctrinal barriers prevent actual uptake. Please write the list of desiderata such a theory must meet: the specific cases it has to handle (for instance, a legislator invoking sacred text on environmental policy; a citizen refusing vaccination on theological grounds yet offering a parallel secular rationale; cross-doctrinal coalitions formed on economic redistribution), the distinctions it cannot collapse (such as between reasons being inaccessible due to cognitive limitation versus being inaccessible due to doctrinal commitment, or between exclusion from public justification and exclusion from private motivation), and the findings from political psychology or the sociology of religion it must not contradict—each with the consideration behind it. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular camps (for example, neutrality of aim versus neutrality of effect, translation requirements versus direct accessibility). If some subset of these desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable—say, full transparency of reasoning combined with respect for doctrinal opacity—say so explicitly, as that tension is precisely what I want mapped. Finally, indicate which single requirement does the most damage to the most familiar existing positions. A table is acceptable, but the conceptual weight should rest on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular theorists. Please provide your answer in British English.
