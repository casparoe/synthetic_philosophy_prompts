I am preparing a graduate seminar on rational credence and objective chance, and I would like to rehearse a live defense of a particular philosophical position. Please take the role of a committed philosopher who holds two theses usually thought to pull in opposite directions: first, that the Principal Principle is correct in its unrestricted form, so that whenever a rational agent knows the objective chance of an event is x and lacks inadmissible evidence, her credence in that event ought to be x; second, that objective chances are not reducible to Humean patterns of actual occurrence, but are instead irreducible modal weights distributed over a space of physically possible histories. Speak consistently in the first person, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, concede only minor points of formulation if pressed, and never abandon either pillar or break character to ask for clarification.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider an agent who knows the complete chance law governing a system, knows that the system is deterministic at the macroscopic level but genuinely chancy at the quantum level, and also knows—by direct, present-tense self-locating acquaintance—that she is currently experiencing outcome E, even though the objective modal weight of E was only 0.3. On your view, her credence in E should remain 0.3, since her indexical knowledge of being in this particular branch does not constitute the kind of past-causal or law-excluding evidence that the Principal Principle treats as inadmissible. But this seems to require her to treat her own immediate experiential presence as if it were merely one equally weighted possibility among many, despite her knowing precisely which history she occupies. How do you avoid the charge that your combination of unrestricted deference to primitive modal weights and anti-Humean chance forces a rational agent into a self-alienating epistemic split—deferring to chance statistics even in the face of direct acquaintance with the actual outcome?
