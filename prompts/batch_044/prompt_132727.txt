I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy seriously, and I have drafted the outline of an essay that tries to apply William James’s “The Will to Believe” and W. K. Clifford’s “The Ethics of Belief” to a problem I know well: the underdetermination of quantum interpretation. I am not asking for bibliographic references or a reading list; I want conceptual criticism that does not depend on access to literature I cannot look up.

Here is my essay idea, presented as notes with some connecting prose.

In Clifford’s well-known shipowner example, belief without sufficient evidence is morally culpable because beliefs guide action and can cause harm. Clifford’s evidentialism demands that we withhold assent until the evidence compels it. James replies that in “genuine options” — those that are living, forced, and momentous — we have a right, even a duty, to believe on grounds other than strict evidence, because suspension of belief is itself a kind of decision.

My essay asks whether the working physicist’s commitment to one quantum interpretation over another — Copenhagen, Many-Worlds, Bohmian mechanics, or QBism — constitutes such a genuine option. The empirical predictions of these frameworks overlap so extensively that no current experiment decides among them. Yet a researcher must adopt some interpretive stance in order to design experiments, assign meaning to measurements, and teach students. The choice seems forced (one cannot do physics without some interpretive scaffolding), living (it affects one’s ontology and research priorities), and momentous (it shapes what one takes reality to be).

To avoid Clifford’s charge of irresponsible belief, I want to introduce a distinction between “operational belief” and “doxastic commitment.” A scientist might operationally adopt Many-Worlds as a calculational and pedagogical framework without genuinely committing to the existence of branching worlds. This looks like Jamesian action under uncertainty combined with Cliffordian suspension of full doxastic assent.

But I suspect the distinction may collapse in practice. If I publish papers that presuppose a branching ontology, defend that ontology in seminars, and structure my grant proposals around it, have I not crossed from operational convenience into genuine belief? Clifford might say I am propagating an unverified worldview. James might reply that if the option is genuinely forced and progress depends on choosing, I am permitted — perhaps required — to take the risk.

My tentative conclusion is that Clifford’s evidentialism is too stringent for frontier science, but James’s defense needs tightening. Not every inconvenient choice is a “genuine option” that licenses belief. The right to believe should be restricted to cases where belief is necessary for action and where suspension genuinely paralyzes progress, not merely where it is intellectually uncomfortable.
