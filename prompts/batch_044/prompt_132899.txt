Student answer:

In Quine's essay "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," the analytic-synthetic distinction is attacked along with reductionism. Analytic statements are true by meaning; synthetic by fact. Quine shows that no clear line can be drawn. First, explaining analyticity via synonymy is circular: to say two expressions are synonymous is to say they are interchangeable salva veritate in all contexts, but determining whether a substitution preserves truth requires prior knowledge of which substitutions are analytic—so we already assume what we aim to explain. Definitional abbreviation fails similarly, since defining a term presupposes prior synonymy.

Second, Quine's holism—the "web of belief"—shows that individual statements do not face experience alone. Any statement can be held true by adjusting beliefs elsewhere. This applies to logic and mathematics: they sit near the center of the web and are highly entrenched, but they are not immune to revision. Thus there is no categorical epistemic divide, only a continuum of centrality.

Third, Quine's critique of reductionism—the idea that every meaningful statement can be translated into statements about immediate sensory experience—is largely independent of his attack on analyticity. Reductionism concerns empirical grounding of individual statements, while the first dogma concerns the meaning-fact boundary.

That said, Quine can draw on Carnap's principle of tolerance to argue that the choice of logical framework is conventional, which reinforces that synonymy is not a factual relation but a matter of linguistic decision. From this conventionalism it follows that logical truths are ultimately empirical hypotheses: like physical laws, they are justified by their contribution to predicting sensory experience, and they differ from physics only in degree of entrenchment, not in kind. Because no statement is de jure immune to revision, the analytic-synthetic distinction collapses, leaving only pragmatic differences in how easily we abandon a belief.
