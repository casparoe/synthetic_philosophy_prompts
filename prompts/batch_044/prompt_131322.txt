I hold a modified version of modal realism: concrete possible worlds exist and are spatiotemporally isolated, but I reject the standard Lewisian claim that actuality is purely indexical. On my view, our world is metaphysically distinguished not by being "the actual one" in a bare indexical sense, but by possessing a maximal, non-branching causal structure that other worlds lack; counterpart relations and modal truths about our world are then constrained by this structural feature. This preserves ontological pluralism without collapsing into actualism, yet it introduces an asymmetry—inhabitants of worlds with branching or fragmented causal structures would have different modal intuitions. What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward: for instance, arguments that the causal-structure distinction is ad hoc or collapses back into pure indexicality; results in the metaphysics of causation that undermine the claim of maximal non-branching structure; and observations about modal reasoning that suggest such asymmetry is never reflected in our judgments. Distinguish what defenders of unmodified Lewisian realism and what actualists would each concede as genuinely damaging, and assess honestly whether this modified view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is a problem for a metaphysical position of this kind.
