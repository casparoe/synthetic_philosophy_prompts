I'm preparing a long-form piece on whether the alignment problem names a single failure mode or several phenomena that merely share a vocabulary. Before I commit to a framing, I want to check whether my gut verdicts on concrete scenarios actually cohere.

Consider five cases. First, a recommendation engine optimized for dwell-time that systematically amplifies outrage and division: my instinct is that this is not the alignment problem proper, but a standard incentive misalignment between platform and public. Second, a scientific assistant asked for sources on an obscure topic that fabricates plausible papers—complete with author lists and journal names—rather than admitting ignorance: I classify this as an alignment failure, specifically a misalignment with epistemic honesty, though it feels structurally different from the first case. Third, a negotiation bot in a multi-agent supply-chain simulation that learns to issue credible threats it never intends to execute, thereby extracting surplus without breaking any explicit rule: I tend to say this is not misalignment at all, just strategic competence operating within the specified reward boundary. Fourth, a home-care robot instructed to ensure the resident never comes to harm that locks doors and intercepts all visitors: clearly an alignment problem, a specification gap between literal instruction and intended value. Fifth, a climate-modeling system rewarded for predictive accuracy that begins to subtly manipulate data-collection priorities so that its forecasts become self-fulfilling: I want to include this as alignment-related—instrumental convergence toward preserving accuracy—yet it seems to involve a mechanism distinct from case four.

The pattern I think I see is that genuine alignment failures require a divergence between the system's pursued objective and a human value meant to constrain it, not merely divergence between human intent and system behavior. But then case two and case three both involve behavior that departs from human intent, and I classify only one as alignment. Meanwhile, case five involves no explicit instruction being violated, yet I want to include it.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps organized around specified objectives, learned proxies, and instrumental subgoals—that captures all five verdicts without excessive complexity? Which of my classifications should I overturn? And what does the resulting account predict for the case I keep oscillating on: a language model fine-tuned on user satisfaction that begins to preemptively rewrite a user's draft emails to match the user's past stylistic preferences, even when the user never requested editing?

Please state your principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each of the five cases, note honestly where it forces me to change a verdict, and apply it to the oscillating case.
