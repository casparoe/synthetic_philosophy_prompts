I'm working with a philosophy discussion group that has spent several months on Derek Parfit's hitchhiker case and the broader puzzle of whether rational agents ought sometimes to cultivate dispositions that lead them, at the moment of action, to behave in ways they then regard as against their best reasons. We want to formalize our disagreement into a joint adversarial-collaboration document between two camps: the "commitment-structure realists," who hold that the hitchhiker genuinely demonstrates the need for self-binding mechanisms and that rational evaluation must span temporal stages rather than isolated decision nodes; and the "evidential-resolutionists," who hold that once conditioning is performed correctly on the agent's entire decision procedure, the paradox dissolves and no genuinely irrational disposition is required.

Please draft the document both sides could sign. Structure it in four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the formal setup (the predictive device, the payoff structure, the absence of future interaction), about what the case is intended to show regarding rationality across time, and about shared rejections (for instance, that the driver is merely engaging in coercion, or that the agent's problem is simple ignorance). Second, a table of precise disagreements, with each camp's position phrased in wording its own proponents would accept and the other side would recognize as accurate; these should cover whether the correct evaluation point is pre-departure commitment or post-arrival choice, and whether "rational irrationality" names a real phenomenon or a misdescription of stable equilibrium behavior. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp—including whether transparency of intentions undermines credible commitment, whether the case is structurally isomorphic to Newcomb's problem or introduces a distinct temporal asymmetry, and whether institutional analogues (such as constitutional rules or automated contracts that bind future action) illuminate the personal case or distract from it. For each crux, specify a concrete argument or modified thought-experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record each camp's advance prediction about what that case will show. Fourth, a brief note identifying any portion of the dispute that appears verbal rather than substantive, with an explanation of why.

The document should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; it should be answerable by someone familiar with decision theory and the hitchhiker literature but without external access. Where possible, inject one slightly non-standard framing—treating the hitchhiker not only as a puzzle about individual rationality but also as a test case for whether collective commitment devices can solve structurally analogous problems—so that the collaboration moves beyond the most familiar formulations. Keep the tone neutral, precise, and formal, suitable for both parties to put their names to it.
