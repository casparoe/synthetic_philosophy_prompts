Write a sestina in American English from the point of view of a split-brain patient’s left hemisphere after a partial callosotomy that spares the anterior commissure — a configuration in which emotional signals can still cross but propositional content and voluntary motor commands cannot. Use exactly these six end-words, in this order for the first stanza and then rotating according to the standard sestina form: bridge, command, other, name, whole, silence.

Make the poem’s recursive structure perform the philosophical problem: each stanza should advance the left hemisphere’s attempt to claim sole ownership of the body and biography — confabulating explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere, insisting on its verbal “name” for the self — yet the circling repetition of “other,” “silence,” and “bridge” should prevent the narrative from ever closing into a single “whole.” The envoi must place “other” and “whole” in adjacent lines without resolving whether they denote one fractured consciousness or two cohabiting ones.

The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Gazzaniga’s interpreter-module account and Sperry’s hemispheric independence experiments recognizes the left hemisphere’s confabulation and the right hemisphere’s mute spatial competence, but do not cite studies, provide a bibliography, or name specific experiments in the poem itself. Take a definite architectural position: the form should lean toward fragmentation rather than neutrality, and after the poem provide a brief, committed note explaining where the poem takes liberties with the actual neuroscience of anterior-commissure preservation or relies on a contested interpretation of hemispheric autonomy. Do not hedge between the one-mind and two-minds readings; the poem’s form should choose a side, and the note should defend that choice.
