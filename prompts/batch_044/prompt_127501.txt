A study partner summarized supererogation for me as follows: Supererogatory acts—such as donating a kidney to a stranger or spending years in a crisis zone—are morally praiseworthy but not morally required. The summary says the concept was introduced to show that moral obligations have a floor but no ceiling, particularly within deontology, where imperfect duties like beneficence can be satisfied without being maximized. By contrast, it claims utilitarianism necessarily collapses all beneficial acts into obligations, since maximizing value leaves no logical space for genuinely optional heroism. The summary also invokes threshold deontology, arguing that imperfect duties contain explicit satisfaction conditions: once the threshold is crossed, further beneficence is supererogatory rather than obligatory. It concludes that only deontology can explain why moral saints are admirable without being required, and that any theory lacking such thresholds fails to address the demandingness problem. How fair is this characterization? Please separate accurate claims, defensible simplifications, and subtle errors—especially whether threshold deontology actually applies to imperfect duties in the way described, whether utilitarianism is forced to eliminate the optional, and whether the demandingness objection is solved or merely relocated.
