I am drafting a philosophical novel, and in one chapter two thinkers debate embedded agency over dinner at a writers' retreat. I was present as a silent observer and did not intervene; what follows is my reconstruction of their exchange.

M, a cognitive scientist, began: an embedded agent can never genuinely step outside its own decision procedure, because any act of reflection is merely another computation running inside the same architecture. Claims that such an agent might choose to choose differently are therefore incoherent—there is no external vantage point from which to revise the machinery.

T, a philosopher, replied that this conflates causal influence with logical evaluation. Even when reflection is physically embedded, it can represent counterfactual versions of itself and evaluate them against criteria. A chess program analyzes its own opening book without leaving the computer; analogously, an agent can model alternative decision rules and select among them.

M then introduced what she called the shadow-substrate problem: if the reflective module shares physical resources—memory, cycles, energy budgets—with the decision module, then observing its own computation necessarily alters the computation being observed. The interference is not merely causal but structural, since allocation patterns are part of the system under evaluation. Consequently, there is no stable object for reflection to grasp; the mirror consumes the image.

T responded that this assumes reflection must be live and concurrent. An embedded agent could instead rely on discrete snapshots—serialized copies of its state stored in isolated buffers—to examine itself without interference. The snapshot is a dead representation, to be sure, but accurate evaluation requires only faithful representation, not live interaction.

M countered that a snapshot is not the agent but a fossil. The agent can act only on its present computation. Evaluating a snapshot therefore gives it no direct reason to alter its current course unless it constructs a bridge from past representation to present action—and that bridge is itself just another embedded loop, not an escape. The agent remains trapped inside whatever computation governs the bridge.
