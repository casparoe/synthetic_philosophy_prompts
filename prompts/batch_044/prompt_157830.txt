I have recently found myself confronting the possibility that consciousness extends far deeper into nature than I previously assumed, a line of thought that acquired personal urgency as I have watched a close family member gradually lose the integrated awareness that seemed to constitute her moral presence. Out of this experience, I have assembled a cluster of commitments about panpsychism, suffering, and scientific method, and I need to know whether they form a coherent package or whether I am inadvertently helping myself to incompatible premises.

My commitments are these. First, I take constitutive panpsychism to be more plausible than emergent physicalism, because I regard the hard problem of consciousness as genuinely intractable unless some form of experience is attributed to fundamental physical entities. Second, I believe that if consciousness is indeed ubiquitous, then the boundary of ethical consideration should extend, at least in principle, to any system that instantiates consciousness, regardless of substrate—whether biological, artificial, or elementary physical. Third, I hold that direct moral status requires not bare consciousness but suffering, and I understand suffering as an integrated, negatively valenced experience sustained over a minimal temporal window—something analogous to the perceptual present in cognitive science—together with an organizational architecture capable of unifying that experience. Fourth, applying these criteria, I maintain that quantum-level processes lack both the organizational structure and the temporal integration necessary for suffering, so despite being conscious in some minimal sense they are not moral patients and do not contribute to a cosmic calculus of harm. Fifth, I insist that scientific theory choice must remain entirely independent of ethical desirability: even if panpsychism implied a universe saturated with suffering, that implication alone could not count as a scientific objection to the view. Sixth, I think that conceivability arguments such as the zombie scenario retain limited but real force for macroscopic organisms, yet they do not clearly apply to fundamental micro-experiences, since our conceptual apparatus is shaped by macroscopic phenomenology.

Please audit this set. Specifically, I want to know whether there are destructive tensions among these six positions. Does my substrate-neutral extension of moral scope in the second commitment conflict with my integration-based restriction in the third and fourth, and if so, which must yield? Do my organizational and temporal criteria for suffering in the third and fourth implicitly reintroduce the kind of emergent structure that my acceptance of panpsychism in the first was meant to avoid, thereby undermining my own motivation? Is there a hidden inconsistency between my methodological separation of science and ethics in the fifth commitment and the evident emotional weight I attach to the ethical implications of the first—am I letting ethical horror shape my denial of quantum suffering even while I claim ethical considerations play no role in theory choice? And is the sixth commitment genuinely compatible with the first, or does my restriction of conceivability arguments to macroscopic cases weaken the downward extension of consciousness that panpsychism requires?

Order the tensions by severity, distinguishing those that are genuinely destructive from those that dissolve once the commitments are properly parsed. For each genuine tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Identify the one apparent tension that is not real upon inspection. Finally, tell me which single alteration to this package would resolve the greatest number of conflicts with the least damage to my core view.
