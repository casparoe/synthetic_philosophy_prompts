I'm working out my considered judgments on cross-modal recognition after sensory restoration, treating them as data points for reflective equilibrium. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1 (classic Molyneux): A congenitally blind adult distinguishes a cube and sphere by touch. Sight is suddenly restored with full acuity. Shown both objects on a table without being allowed to touch them, I judge they cannot immediately identify which is which by vision alone; cross-modal mapping requires learned correlation.

Case 2 (sensory substitution): A congenitally blind adult spends a decade navigating with a high-fidelity tactile display that renders spatial geometry—edges, distances, and volumetric shape—onto the tongue. They have rich non-visual object representations. Sight is then restored. I judge they still cannot immediately match the visual cube and sphere to their tactile-display representations; prior spatial richness in another modality does not automatically transfer.

Case 3 (infant blur then darkness): An individual had severely blurred but present vision until age two, then total blindness until age thirty-five, with no retained visual memories. Sight is then fully restored. I judge they likely can distinguish the cube and sphere visually without prior tactile consultation; something about early cortical development, not retained information, enables immediate cross-modal alignment.

Case 4 (pure geometric knowledge): A congenitally blind adult masters projective geometry and can derive the precise two-dimensional visual profiles of polyhedra and spheres. Sight is restored. I judge they still fail immediate visual identification; explicit structural knowledge does not bridge the gap.

Case 5 (machine analog): A neural network is trained only on haptic and proprioceptive shape-classification, with zero visual input. It is then shown images. I judge it initially misclassifies, but its failure is purely representational—there is no phenomenal gap—whereas in Cases 1–4 there is both a representational and (in humans) a phenomenal component.

Case 6 (threshold ambiguity, the one I keep revising): A blind person receives a retinal prosthesis that initially delivers only coarse, low-contrast edge information, gradually improving over months. At the very first activation—before any learning—do they have even a minimal visual recognition? I go back and forth. Sometimes I think the initial signal is too impoverished for any recognition, so failure is expected; other times I think that if Case 3 succeeds despite no memory, then any functioning visual pathway should either succeed immediately or fail for the same underlying reason, which makes the threshold idea incoherent.

Is there a single principle that organizes Cases 1–5? I am particularly interested in whether the decisive factor is (i) critical-period cortical development, (ii) the modality-specific format of prior representations, (iii) the presence or absence of a phenomenal character gap, or (iv) some interaction among them. Which of my verdicts—especially Case 2 versus Case 3—must be sacrificed for simplicity? Please state the principle precisely, specify which clause is motivated by which case, predict the outcome for an unconsidered scenario (a congenitally blind expert echolocator who receives sight), and tell me honestly which verdict the principle cannot accommodate.
