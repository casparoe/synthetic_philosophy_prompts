In discussions of AI safety via debate, the disagreement over so-called obfuscated arguments has stalled without either side conceding ground. Proponents treat these arguments as an adversarial dynamics problem: a debater can construct technically valid but strategically convoluted reasoning chains that exploit a judge's bounded computation, yet with better judges, longer contexts, or stronger cross-examination, the hidden false premises should collapse. Critics treat the same phenomenon as an epistemic architecture problem: if the judge lacks direct causal access to the domain being debated, no amount of adversarial pressure can reliably distinguish a compressed valid argument from one that conceals its flaw in structural complexity, so debate can never exceed the judge's independent verification capacity. Both camps agree that debate succeeds for transparent factual disputes and cite the same illustrative failures; neither has persuaded the other. Is this stall verbal—centered on whether “explanation” means transparency to a specific bounded agent or to any rational evaluator—or substantive—about whether justified belief requires direct causal access that debate can never supply? Or are the sides answering different questions, one about information revelation in adversarial games, the other about the conditions for knowledge? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually takes to count as progress, and say whether a formal result about verification complexity, a distinction between rhetorical compression and logical concealment, or a redesigned protocol could move it, or whether the impasse marks a bedrock divergence about epistemic authority that no argument can bridge.
