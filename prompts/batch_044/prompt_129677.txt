You are an expert in the philosophy of archaeological inference, specializing in how beliefs and institutions are reconstructed—or over-interpreted—from material remains. Before answering, briefly brainstorm three distinct interpretive principles that could govern an archaeologist’s judgments about whether an artifact pattern justifies inferring a shared belief or social institution.

My research partner—an experienced interpretive archaeologist—has consistently made the following judgments about material patterns at several sites, none of which have accompanying texts:

1. In a cemetery lacking any writing, every interred body has red ochre applied only to the left side of the face and torso; tools and wall surfaces in the same site receive no such treatment. She infers a directional cosmological belief tied to burial orientation.

2. Identical ceramic seals appear both in an administrative archive and scattered through domestic trash pits across the settlement. She rejects the inference of a centralized bureaucratic institution, arguing instead for decentralized household practices that mimicked administrative forms.

3. At a river crossing far from any settlement or burial ground, she finds a deposit of miniature metal weapons arranged circularly; none show combat damage, and no defensive architecture surrounds the spot. She infers an institutionalized ritual of passage rather than practical warfare or defense.

4. Storage jars in clearly domestic contexts bear geometric carvings that precisely match motifs in nearby rock art. She rejects any shared symbolic system linking domestic storage and ritual expression, attributing the jar markings to practical functions such as crack-prevention or content identification.

5. Within one settlement, adult remains show highly uniform dental modifications. She refuses to infer an institutional rite of passage, noting that the patterns align exactly with what a shared local diet and consistent use of specific tool-types would produce.

Now consider this new case. In a single rectangular building inside a settlement—one that also contains sleeping mats and food-preparation debris—excavators recover thousands of small clay beads. Every bead is pierced identically, yet microscopic wear-analysis shows no evidence of ever having been strung. The clay is chemically non-local, matching a distant source, and the beads occur nowhere else in the site: not in burials, not in other structures, not in exterior refuse deposits.

Preliminary Brainstorming (approx. 100 words): List the leading candidate rules—such as principles based on functional surplus, contextual segregation, or cross-domain correlation—that could jointly explain her five prior verdicts.

Candidate Interpretive Rules (approx. 250 words): State the two strongest principles precisely. For each, show explicitly how it delivers her acceptance in cases 1 and 3 and her rejection in cases 2, 4, and 5.

Divergent Predictions for the Bead Case (approx. 200 words): Apply each rule to the building with the beads. Say clearly whether each rule predicts she should infer an institutionalized ritual or belief centered on that structure, or whether it predicts she should reject such an inference, with the reasoning.

Best Verdict and Closest Precedent (approx. 250 words): Give your best guess about what she will actually conclude, with explicit reasons for preferring one rule. Identify which prior case is the closest analog. State honestly what underlying principle she would be adopting if she accepts the institutional inference, and what principle she would be adopting if she rejects it.

The Crucial Test Case (approx. 150 words): Propose one further hypothetical archaeological scenario that would sharply distinguish the two leading rules. Explain exactly what verdict each rule demands in that scenario.
