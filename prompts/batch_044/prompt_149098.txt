I've been thinking about what it really means for something to have a hidden ability or tendency. I came across the basic idea that a sugar cube is still soluble even when it is locked in a jar—the jar just masks the disposition—while a live wire can be "finked" if it has a breaker that cuts the current the instant it is touched. Those two cases make sense separately: one involves an outside obstacle, the other an internal self-defeat that activates precisely when the triggering condition arrives.

But I have been trying to imagine a more tangled case, and I want to know whether the distinction still holds. Picture a vault door engineered to unlock when it detects a specific tone. The catch is that the tone-detection circuit is wired so that receiving that exact frequency instantly melts the gears that would turn the bolt. At the same time, the entire mechanism is sealed inside a soundproof shell. So the mask—the shell that blocks the sound—and the fink—the melting gears—are not independent accidents; they are built into the same design. If you removed the shell to let the sound reach the circuit, the gears would melt. If you hardened the gears to survive the sound, you would have to dismantle the shell to reach and repair them.

Given this kind of internally coupled or "locked" blockage, I want you to argue that we should deny the vault has any disposition to open whatsoever—not merely a masked or finked one, but none at all. Defend the view that when the conditions that would reveal a disposition are structurally identical to the conditions that destroy or suppress it, attributing that disposition is a mistake. Explain why we should instead treat the vault as possessing a stable, negative disposition to remain shut, rather than a hidden positive disposition that is doubly obstructed. I am not asking for references, bibliographies, or citations; I want the reasoning developed directly from the concepts, with particular attention to why treating this as a "blocked" disposition leads to strange or contradictory results when we imagine trying to intervene and "fix" the situation.
