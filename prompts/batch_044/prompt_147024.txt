I'm a doctoral student in metaphysics and philosophy of language, working through the implicit commitments behind my advisor's seminar rulings on compositional vagueness. Over the past term she has judged whether various entities count as a single "integrated system" rather than a mere aggregate, without ever stating her principle. Her record so far: a human body with integrated prosthetic implants is determinately one system; an archipelago linked only by occasional boat traffic is determinately not; a termite mound with shared ventilation tunnels is determinately one; a starling murmuration is determinately not; a slime mold in its plasmodial stage is determinately not one but many; a symbiotic lichen is determinately one; and a distributed quantum-computing grid running a single algorithm across nodes with no central controller is determinately not. Next week we will discuss a case I designed for the session: a "bio-digital mesh" comprising thousands of genetically modified coral polyps interwoven with microprocessors, arranged in a continuous lattice with no discernible outer boundary, where the nodes share both metabolic resources and data streams, and where removing any thirty percent leaves the remainder functioning identically. I want to predict her verdict—determinately one system, determinately not, or genuinely indeterminate—and, more importantly, to identify the rule she is running.

The interpretations that seem equally consistent with her track record are, first, an epistemicist view according to which there is a sharp boundary of integrative unity that she tracks but cannot articulate; second, a supervaluationist view on which admissible precisifications are anchored either by biological continuity or by informational unity, yielding indeterminacy when those anchors conflict; and third, a contextualist view on which the threshold shifts with the explanatory purpose of the classification. For the bio-digital mesh, these diverge: the epistemicist should commit to one side or the other based on where she implicitly draws the line, the supervaluationist should declare the case indeterminate because biological and informational precisifications conflict, and the contextualist might vary her answer depending on whether we are asking a biological or an engineering question. Which interpretation best explains why she counts the termite mound but not the quantum grid, given that both involve distributed coordination? What further case—say, a system whose boundary dissolves gradually over time, or one in which metabolic and informational integration come apart incrementally—would best discriminate among these three? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning that connects her prior rulings to this new one, and in the precise point at which her pattern forces us to choose between hidden sharpness, semantic indeterminacy, and context-sensitive boundaries.
