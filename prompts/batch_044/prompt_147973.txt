I defend a stringent reading of G. E. Moore's axiology: friendship and beauty are each intrinsic goods that constitute irreducible organic unities, carrying non-natural value properties that are simple, unanalyzable, and not reducible to pleasure, functional coherence, or any empirical feature of their parts. The open-question argument applies distinctly to each—whether a particular friendship or a particular beautiful form is good remains genuinely open—because value supervenes on the organic whole in a way that eludes conceptual analysis. I take this pluralism in a strong metaphysical direction: there is no common metric of value, not even a partial ordering, that could adjudicate between gains in friendship and gains in beauty; rational choice between them is therefore impossible in principle, not merely epistemically limited. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of familiar rebuttals: which specific intuitions about necessary trade-offs, which formal results in decision theory or axiology concerning incommensurability and rational choice, which psychological or neuroscientific findings about the unified structure of social and aesthetic valuation, and which metaphysical results about supervenience or relational ontology would genuinely threaten it? Which defeaters would a committed defender concede—say, that strong incommensurability collapses normative guidance into arbitrariness, or that organic unity can be decomposed into part-based relations without loss of value—and which would they dismiss as presupposing the very naturalism Moore rejects? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the position's insulation through appeals to simple, non-natural properties is a genuine protective feature or a methodological vice that renders it unfalsifiable.
