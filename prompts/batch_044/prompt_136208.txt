Write the opening remarks for the interdisciplinary workshop Reason Without Reasoners: Competence and Comprehension in Natural and Artificial Systems. The audience is mixed—half are specialists in philosophy of biology, AI ethics, or philosophy of mind who will catch subtle allusions; the other half are graduate students and researchers from physics and neuroscience with no background in these debates. The remarks should run about seven minutes spoken, and the philosophical idea—competence without comprehension—must do real structural work: it should shape what the speaker can and cannot claim about Darwinian natural selection and about Turing-style computation, not merely decorate the introduction.

Build the speech around the strange inversion shared by Darwin and Turing. Darwin argued that cumulative selection produces exquisitely adapted structures without any comprehending agent directing the process or knowing the adaptive value of the variations being selected. Turing argued that a formal system can display behavior indistinguishable from intelligence without any internal comprehension of the rules it applies or the meaning of its outputs. Use this inversion to frame the workshop’s purpose, but maintain a live tension: you must not imply that large language models therefore understand in virtue of their competence, nor that Darwinian evolution is just a mindless mechanism lacking explanatory power. The point is that competence can run ahead of comprehension, not that comprehension is irrelevant.

Connect this to a parallel inversion in Turing’s 1950 discussion of machine learning, where he suggested that a machine might improve its performance through experience without ever constructing an explicit representation of the principles governing that improvement.
