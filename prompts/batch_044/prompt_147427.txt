A manufacturing engineer is revising the forecasting protocol for a precision alloy plant. For the past five years, every batch produced on Line 3 before the installation of a new temperature controller—completed on March 14—and tested with the legacy tensile gauge was classified as Grade A; every batch produced on Line 3 after March 14 and tested with the upgraded digital gauge was classified as Grade B. Both gauges measure ductility, but they rely on different calibration curves, and the new controller altered the cooling rate. Management now asks whether batches that will be produced on Line 3 next quarter—after the controller is fully calibrated but before any further gauge upgrade—should be projected as Grade A or Grade B.

One senior metallurgist argues that "Grade-A-before-March-14-with-legacy-gauge" is not a genuine physical kind but an accidental conjunction of two independent variables (the thermal regime and the measurement instrument), so projecting it onto future batches commits a category error. Another argues that the March 14 cutoff tracks a real discontinuity in the thermal history of the alloy, meaning the time-sensitive classification actually captures two physically distinct material regimes, and therefore one projection is genuinely correct while the other is not.

The engineer recognizes that both accounts fit every past observation equally well, yet they yield opposite forecasts for next quarter. She is not asking for a statistical workaround—she wants to know whether there is a philosophical principle that can legitimately break the tie, or whether the problem reveals a deeper limit on how we justify inductive projections when our descriptive categories depend on when or with which instrument we observe. How should she think about this?
