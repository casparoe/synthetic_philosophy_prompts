Consider a policy instrument that preserves every formal option but restructures framing, sequencing, or social-norm cues specifically to shift citizens' second-order preferences—that is, to make them reflectively want what the welfare calculus prescribes, rather than merely guiding first-order selection. Give your credence (0 to 1) that such a "second-order nudge" necessarily violates autonomy and crosses out of permissible libertarian paternalism, even when the choice set is technically untouched. Decompose the reasoning into three parts: (a) whether choice architecture can coherently be distinguished into first-order and second-order influence; (b) whether the moral license for libertarian paternalism depends essentially on preserving open choice sets rather than on protecting the integrity of reflective preference-formation; and (c) whether autonomy, on the most defensible account, includes a protected sphere for second-order revision that state design should not penetrate. For each part, provide a rough probability and a line of justification; then combine them into your overall number, state which part your conclusion is most sensitive to, and describe what conceptual argument or empirical result would most shift that part and therefore your final credence.
