I am preparing a graduate seminar on Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, and I want to include a sustained piece of satire that takes the argument seriously from the inside rather than mocking it from a distance. Please write a parody—approximately 600 to 800 words—formatted as a formal institutional policy document issued by a fictional “National Endowment for Ethnolinguistic Fieldwork.” The document should lay out grant-application and compliance guidelines for ethnographers working in “radical translation” contexts, specifically those encountering “gavagai”-type stimulus conditions. The satire must arise from a deadpan bureaucratic over-application of Quine’s actual claims: that translation manuals rendering the native expression as “rabbit,” as “undetached rabbit part,” and as “temporal stage of rabbithood” are all empirically equivalent relative to the totality of behavioral evidence; that the selection among them is therefore underdetermined and not a matter of factual discovery; and that analytical hypotheses are the only mechanism by which a fieldworker can bridge the gap, with no “fact of the matter” to adjudicate the choice. Build the policy around these points—for instance, requiring every funded manual to carry a “no-fact-of-the-matter” disclaimer, mandating that peer reviewers certify they have not smuggled in unstated ontological commitments, and establishing a refund and arbitration protocol for indigenous informants who dispute whether “gavagai” has been mistranslated as “rabbit” rather than as “momentary rabbit-flash.” Include at least one deadpan quotation from a fictional “Senior Ontological Auditor” lamenting the administrative burden of policing analytical hypotheses across multiple manual drafts. The piece should stay legible to a reader who knows nothing about Quine, but the humor should depend on precision: distinguish indeterminacy of translation from indeterminacy of reference, treat the comparison to physics with appropriate care, and avoid attributing to Quine any position he does not hold. Keep the tone consistently deadpan and institutional; do not wink, and do not invent false versions of the thesis for the sake of an easier joke. I want the result to feel like a reductio of the bureaucratic impulse to regulate what Quine showed is genuinely underdetermined.
