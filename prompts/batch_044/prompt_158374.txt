During a recent philosophy of biology reading group, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Kessler and Dr. Marcus Okonkwo—got stuck in an argument about whether Gould and Lewontin’s spandrels critique still carries explanatory weight. I listened without signaling a preference; they asked me to referee, and I said I would ask someone to work through it precisely. Here is what they said, in order.

(1) Kessler opened by saying the spandrels critique is largely obsolete. Modern evolutionary developmental biology has absorbed it: we now understand that traits arise from deep developmental constraints and are routinely co-opted into new functions (exaptation). Calling something a spandrel is therefore just a placeholder for “we have not finished mapping the selective and developmental history.”

(2) Okonkwo replied that this misreads the original argument. Gould and Lewontin were not denying selection; they were attacking the adaptationist program that treats every trait as if it had been engineered for its present role. A spandrel is not an unknown adaptation but a structural byproduct—like the human chin arising necessarily from jaw reduction and dental architecture—that exists because of other design features, not because selection favored it directly.

(3) Kessler pushed back that the category collapses over evolutionary time. Feathers, for instance, began as thermoregulatory structures and were later co-opted for flight. Once co-opted, they have a selective history. Over deep time, nearly every spandrel either gets eliminated or acquires a function, so spandrel describes only a temporary stage, not a stable explanatory kind.

(4) Okonkwo answered that this commits a temporal fallacy. A trait’s current utility does not erase its origin as a non-adaptive byproduct. The logical point remains intact: not every feature requires an adaptive story at its origin. The spandrel concept forces us to distinguish “why the structure exists developmentally” from “what selective work it now performs.”

(5) Kessler then raised what she called negative spandrels: traits that reduce fitness but are developmentally locked to highly adaptive architectures—say, deleterious pleiotropic side-effects coupled to immune-response pathways. If selection cannot decouple them, they are unavoidable maladaptive byproducts. But then the spandrel concept becomes unfalsifiable: any persistent maladaptive trait counts as a spandrel, and any adaptive one counts as an exaptation. The distinction loses empirical bite.

(6) Okonkwo countered that the distinction is empirically tractable. Comparative developmental genetics and targeted decoupling experiments can show whether the trait is separable from the adaptive core without destroying it. If separation is impossible, the spandrel claim is supported; if separation succeeds, the adaptationist claim is vindicated. Gould and Lewontin’s critique survives precisely here: it shifts the burden of proof onto anyone who asserts that every feature must be adaptively optimal.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one important issue that both parties are missing—whether conceptual, methodological, or historical. Be precise: do not just declare a winner; show exactly where each speaker is strong and where they slip.
