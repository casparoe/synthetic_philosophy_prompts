Two people in my study group got stuck arguing about Good's theorem — the decision-theory result that learning something can't hurt a rational agent if they still get to choose thier action afterwards. Maya said this basicly means you should never turn down free information, and anyone who pays to avoid a genetic test is being irrational. Leo said she's glossing over the free-choice condition: if you're already locked into a decision — like you've promised your kid you'll go to the park tomorrow regardless of weather — then learning about a thunderstorm doesn't let you cancel, it just ruins your anticipation. Maya replied that even then you can make secondary adjustments, bring rain gear, mentally brace yourself, so the value is still non-negative if you model those adjustments as part of your action space. Leo said that just redefines "action" so broadly the result becomes tautological. Then he raised an untreatable medical condition where there's no umbrella at all — knowing just injects dread. He pushed further: in cases like that, learning doesn't just reveal a state, it can transform your preferences — maybe you stop valuing long-term projects because you feel doomed — so comparing expected utility ex ante and ex post isn't comparing the same agent. Maya said that's a dynamic-preference problem, not a failure of Good's result, which applies snapshot by snapshot. Leo said if people rationaly pay to stay ignorant, either they're all irrational or the static snapshot model misses something essential about rationality. Can you go through thier exchange move by move? Say who's right on each point, what's missing on both sides, and whether there's a way to reconcile them — don't just declare an overall winner.
