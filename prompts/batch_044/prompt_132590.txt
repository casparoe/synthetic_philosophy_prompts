As a retired physicist who has only recently begun attending philosophy seminars, I find myself struggling to distinguish accurate summaries of empirical literature from philosophical overinterpretation. At our last meeting of the university’s cross-disciplinary reading group, a colleague—who has a background in anthropology but no formal philosophical training—offered a characterization of the WEIRD morality literature and its implications for moral objectivity that I now wish to audit with someone who can think clearly about both the psychology and the metaethics. He framed the findings not as simple cultural divergence but through what he called normative calibration: the idea that human moral cognition operates like a measurement instrument with a shared metric but culture-specific zero-points, so that different societies can disagree about where fairness or purity falls on the scale without disagreeing about the scale itself.

According to his summary, the empirical work demonstrates that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations display a distinctive psychological profile: they weight individual harm and impartial procedural fairness far more heavily than loyalty, authority, or sanctity, whereas many non-WEIRD populations distribute moral weight more evenly across these domains. From this descriptive pattern, he drew two philosophical conclusions. First, because judgments about direct physical harm remain remarkably stable across populations—what he termed the objective core—moral objectivity is preserved for harm-based norms but need not extend to fairness or purity judgments, which vary too widely to claim universal truth. Second, he argued that this produces a domain-partitioned model of objectivity: morality is not globally subjective or globally objective, but rather calibrated differently across domains, with harm functioning as an invariant anchor and other domains functioning as culturally relative parameters. He added that this model avoids both crude moral relativism and naive universalism, and that philosophers who invoke the is-ought gap to block all empirical inferences to ethics are therefore ignoring the domain-specific structure revealed by cross-cultural psychology.

Something about this strikes me as too neat, particularly the move from descriptive stability to normative objectivity in the harm domain, and the use of the calibration metaphor to insulate fairness judgments from objectivity claims. I would like a careful evaluation of whether his summary is fair to both the psychological findings and the philosophical debates. Specifically: which parts accurately reflect the WEIRD literature’s actual claims about cross-cultural moral variation, which parts conflate descriptive patterns with normative conclusions, and does the normative calibration framework genuinely help here or does it smuggle in an unjustified assumption of a shared metric? Moreover, if the descriptive stability of harm judgments is granted, does that genuinely support domain-partitioned objectivity, or could the same data be interpreted through evolutionary or functional explanations that carry no objectivist commitment? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want to know whether the reasoning survives correction.
