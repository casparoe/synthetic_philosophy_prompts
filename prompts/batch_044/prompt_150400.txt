I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of rational choice theory and the metaphysics of agency, and I have come to defend a resolution of Kavka’s toxin puzzle that I have not seen developed in the literature. Call it the diachronic structural consistency (DSC) account. On my view, the puzzle is not best understood as a standoff between causal and evidential decision theories, but as an artifact of treating intention-formation as an instantaneous, atomistic mental event. I propose that rational agency is governed by a constraint of structural commitment consistency: an agent’s present deliberative framework must be coherent not only with its actual future states but with all counterfactual deliberative states that would obtain under every causally possible resolution of the incentive structure that currently governs her. Applied to the toxin case, this means that at t1—before the reward is confirmed—the agent can rationally form the intention to drink at t2, because the SCC constraint is satisfied across the counterfactual branch in which she actually receives the money and must then decide. At t2, however, once the causal chain linking the reward to the drinking has been severed, the SCC constraint no longer binds her present state to that prior counterfactual branch; consequently, drinking is irrational not because the payoff has disappeared, but because acting on the prior intention now violates diachronic coherence with her updated deliberative framework. This dissolves the paradox without invoking special psychological mechanisms or abandoning the idea that rational agents can intend what they will not do.

But I am concerned that I may have insulated the view from genuine refutation by defining SCC in terms of counterfactual coherence conditions that are only observable in principle. So I want an honest assessment: what would genuinely count against the DSC account?

Specifically:

1. Which formal results in decision theory—concerning dynamic consistency, the sure-thing principle, or representations of rational preference change—would be incompatible with SCC, and which of those would be decisive rather than merely awkward?

2. Which intuitive judgments about the toxin case, or about diachronic rationality more broadly, does DSC misclassify or fail to accommodate? Are there cases in which an agent seems rationally permitted to maintain an intention despite a broken causal link, and would those be fatal?

3. What does DSC predict about related puzzles—Newcomb’s problem, the two-envelope problem, or cases of rational self-deception—that its rivals (standard CDT, EDT, or commitment-based solutions) do not predict? If those predictions turn out false, how damaging is that?

4. To what extent have I qualified the view into unfalsifiability? Which empirical or conceptual discoveries could still move me, and which ones would I be forced to dismiss as misapplications of the diachronic framework?

5. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal (the view collapses into incoherence or into a rival theory) to merely awkward (requires an ad hoc auxiliary assumption).

I also want to know which of these objections a committed defender of DSC should concede as genuinely counting against the view, rather than dismissing them as misunderstandings of the diachronic framework. Finally, if the insulation problem is severe—that is, if SCC can always be saved by adjusting the specification of the counterfactual deliberative states—is that a fatal flaw for a theory of rational agency, or is some degree of insulation acceptable in this domain?
