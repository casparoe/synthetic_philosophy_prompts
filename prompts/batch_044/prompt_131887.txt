Analyze the 2015 decision by restaurateur Danny Meyer, through his Union Square Hospitality Group, to eliminate tipping and adopt service-included pricing across his restaurants, as a case study in whether everyday economic conventions constitute moral obligations and whether outsiders are bound by them. Before treating the episode, distinguish between two functions the New York restaurant-tipping convention served: a coordination function (signaling service quality, directing labor attention) and a compensation function (allowing employers to externalize part of the wage bill onto diners). Then apply a conventionalist framework that holds conventions generate obligations for participants only when they serve pure coordination and do not embed contested distributive judgments. Under that framework, determine what moral obligations, if any, a non-local visitor to a New York restaurant had under the old tipping regime, and whether the visitor's duty was different from that of a local regular. Next, examine what Meyer's reform reveals: does the move to service-included pricing show that the convention was never purely coordinative but was always a disguised wage mechanism, thereby undermining the claim that tipping was a moral obligation for anyone? Or does it show that conventions can be re-engineered without moral loss, which supports a flexible conventionalism? Finally, assess whether the episode strengthens or weakens the broader claim that visitors must observe local conventions—such as queuing norms or jaywalking rules—when those conventions depend on local institutional contexts that visitors do not share, and identify exactly where the conventionalist view is silent rather than speaking.
