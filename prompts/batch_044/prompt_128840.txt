I keep encountering a pattern in digital discourse that I cannot name precisely, though it seems related to older critiques of the press and the public. Consider two cases. First, a political claim begins with modest engagement, but once its share-count crosses a visible threshold, commentators begin citing that very count as evidence that “the public” has spoken; yet when one examines individual accounts, few held the view before seeing the metric, and many admit they shared it only because it was already prominent. Second, in a professional forum, an opinion about industry standards gains authority not through expert endorsement but through aggregated upvotes, after which members treat the aggregated score as an independent standard that regulates their own contributions, even though each vote was cast in response to the score they observed. In both cases, a collective opinion appears to possess external authority over the individuals who constitute it, yet no individual member ever genuinely held the opinion independently of that authority. This resembles Kierkegaard’s concern that the press creates an abstract “public” which is simultaneously everyone and no one, and which dissolves individual responsibility. However, there is an additional reflexive layer: here the metric or aggregation is not merely a medium that reports opinion, but is itself cited as the ground of opinion, creating a loop in which the public produces the authority and the authority produces the public. Is there an established philosophical or sociological term for this exact reflexive configuration? If the nearest concepts are manufactured consent, the bandwagon effect, or Kierkegaard’s own analysis of the press and levelling, please explain exactly how the described phenomenon differs from each, and if no established term captures the loop precisely, propose a definition with clear boundary conditions. Please respond in British English.
