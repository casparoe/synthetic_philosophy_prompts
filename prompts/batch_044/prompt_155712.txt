In debates about whether modern secessionist claims can be analogized to the birth of nineteenth-century nation-states, I keep encountering the formulation that nationalism invented the nation rather than vice versa. It is usually wielded to undercut appeals to ancient ethnic continuity: if the nation is essentially a modern fabrication, then assertions of primordial belonging are mere mythology. I want the slogan’s precise machinery laid bare. Could you separate the distinct theses this formulation might actually express—an ontological denial that any pre-modern collective satisfied the conditions for nationhood; an epistemic claim that collective identity is retrospectively constructed through narrative and institutional practice; a functional claim that the state’s standardizing apparatus (education, census, law) creates the cultural uniformity it then claims merely to represent; a normative claim that ethnic essentialism is illegitimate because its object lacks historical depth; or a causal claim about the temporal direction of influence between cultural practice and political institution? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or conceptually muddled; specify what kind of evidence—archival, demographic, linguistic, or institutional—would discriminate it from the others; and say which version actually supports the dismissal of primordialist arguments in the debate I described. I am especially interested in whether any defensible reading secretly depends on an archival threshold: the idea that bureaucratic record-keeping and mass literacy are necessary conditions for a group to count as a nation rather than merely a demographic category, with mnemonic communities sustained by ritual repetition and oral transmission falling on the wrong side of that boundary. Does that threshold survive once we allow that collective identity can be maintained without textual archives, and if so, does it undermine the slogan’s anti-primordialist punch? Present the readings as a menu I can bring back to the argument, with clear verdicts on which ones do the work the slogan was being made to do and which ones collapse into platitude or confusion once disambiguated.
