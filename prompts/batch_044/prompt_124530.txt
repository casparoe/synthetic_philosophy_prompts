I have been trying to sharpen the cognitive-diversity defense of legislative sortition—the view that random selection is valuable mainly because it maximizes heuristic diversity and eliminates the self-selection biases (careerism, fundraising capture, credentialist homogeneity) that degrade collective reasoning in elected bodies. When I formalize it, however, I get a chain that seems to push me into either endorsing random-selection drug-approval panels or conceding that the justification for citizens’ assemblies collapses. Here are the steps, numbered for reference.

(1) The strongest justification for replacing elected legislatures with sortitive bodies is that random sampling from the general population maximizes cognitive diversity and eliminates systematic corruption biases that distort collective judgment.

(2) If cognitive diversity is the operative good, then it applies to any collective decision procedure vulnerable to the same biases, not only to legislatures.

(3) Expert regulatory bodies—say, panels that approve clinical trials—suffer from analogous capture and homogeneity problems (industry influence, disciplinary groupthink) and make collective judgments that would benefit from broader heuristic diversity.

(4) Therefore, we should apply “filtered sortition” to expert bodies: randomly select decision-makers from a pool that meets a minimal competence threshold (e.g., licensed clinicians), retaining the diversity benefit while excluding the fully unqualified.

(5) In legislative domains, however, there is no minimal-competence filter that can be applied without reintroducing the very class, educational, and ideological selection biases that legislative sortition was designed to eliminate.

(6) So either (a) we reject the cognitive-diversity justification for legislatures because no non-distorting filter is available there, or (b) we accept that expertise requires filters and therefore concede that legislative bodies should also be filtered—thereby undermining the anti-elitist premise of the movement.

(7) Option (a) abandons the best argument for legislative sortition; option (b) concedes that unfiltered random citizens are insufficient for complex governance, which is precisely what defenders of elections claim.

(8) Hence the cognitive-diversity defense either proves too much (it mandates filtered sortition everywhere, including expert domains where it looks absurd) or proves too little (it cannot justify legislative sortition without a filter it refuses to allow).

I feel like there is a subtle scope shift or an equivocation between “cognitive diversity” and “descriptive representation” somewhere between (2) and (5), but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (3), and I am willing to pay a price at (2) or (4) if the damage is contained, but I want to know exactly what I am buying into if I do.
