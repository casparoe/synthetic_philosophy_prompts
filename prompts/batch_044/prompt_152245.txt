I was arguing with my friend about whether you can be morally responsible if you literally couldn't have done anything else. We got onto Frankfurt cases — you know, the ones where some hidden controller makes sure you do X, but you do it willingly anyway, so Frankfurt says you're still responsible even though there's no alternative possibility. My friend thinks the whole debate is just intution-pumping, so I want to test it on a real thing instead of another made-up story.

Take the Miracle on the Hudson in January 2009. Chesley Sullenberger's plane hit a flock of geese, both engines went dead, he had maybe a few seconds, and he ditched in the Hudson River. Everyone survived. The standard story is he had no real alternative — he couldn't reach Teterboro or back to LaGuardia — but he executed the ditching with incredible skill and is treated as a hero. Now map this onto Frankfurt's framework. Is there a hidden Black here — not a person, but the physics and the bird-strike sequence — that guaranteed the ditching outcome regardless of Sullenberger's will? If so, does that mean he lacked alternative possibilities? And if he lacked them, does Frankfurt's rejection of the principle of alternative possibilities help us understand why we still praise him? Or does the case acutally break Frankfurt's model because the intervener here isn't counterfactual — it's actual, it's the geese — and Sullenberger's agency lies in the execution, not the choice?

There's also this weird asymmetry: Frankfurt originally built his cases around blame, not praise. So does applying it to a hero-episode show that the principle of alternative possibilities works differently for positive responsibility? And where does the theory go silent — for instance, about whether the passengers or the air-traffic controllers share in that responsibility? Don't just summarize Frankfurt; actually do the mapping — who plays Black, what counts as the decision point, what are the alternatives being eliminated — and then say what the episode reveals about the theory that we wouldn't see in the made-up thought experiments.
