everyone in alignement twitter keeps comparing process reward models to a math teacher grading proofs line-by-line instead of just checking the final answer. the idea is that outcome-based RLHF is like grading only the answer key, so the model learns to guess or hide mistakes, while process supervision forces it to show correct reasoning at each step. someone used this to argue we should basically never use outcome rewards for reasoning tasks.

i want to audit how far that holds, but with a twist i haven't seen addressed: imagine the student writes a proof where step 3 uses a subtly wrong lemma that technically looks valid in isolation — the local check passes — but it corrupts step 17 in a way that's hard to trace back. the teacher grades every line and still misses it because the error is distributed, not local.

so: lay out the correspondence table. what maps to the student, the proof, the teacher's rubric, the hidden lemma? then the breaks: where does AI reasoning differ from student reasoning (no intent to deceive, no "understanding" of the lemma, the model can generate thousands of variants)? does the conclusion — that process supervision is strictly safer — survive once you realize the teacher can be locally fooled? and if the analogy collapses there, what's a better analogy for why process rewards might still win, or might not?
