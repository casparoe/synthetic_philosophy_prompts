I have just come away from a prolonged and somewhat fractious argument with a friend over the moral distribution of labor and rest. We kept returning to three touchstones: the Protestant work ethic, with its premise that sustained toil is a form of secular virtue; the 35-hour week, treated not as an economic adjustment but as an institutional claim that leisure is a right rather than a reward; and karoshi, which my friend insisted should be read not as an accidental mortality but as the extreme terminus of a culture that treats exhaustion as proof of moral seriousness. To close the conversation without conceding ground, I want a joke—intellectually sharp, not slapstick—that turns precisely on the tension among these three positions. The joke should operate from a slightly unorthodox framing: treat leisure not as the opposite of work but as a kind of unreimbursed liability within a moral economy of effort, so that the 35-hour week becomes a legislated debt-forgiveness program and karoshi the case in which the debtor refuses bankruptcy. It should be intelligible to someone who knows what karoshi means and who grasps the basic Weberian idea, but it should avoid generic office humor or named scholars. Write it in a polished, formal register, as if you were delivering it to settle an argument rather than to entertain a crowd.
