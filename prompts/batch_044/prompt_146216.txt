After a long argument last night with a friend about where market logic should stop, I have been trying to systematize her judgments. She holds that a state-run pool compensating living kidney donors at a fixed rate is permissible, because bodily exchange is not inherently degrading if medically safeguarded; she holds that pharmaceutical price gouging on essential drugs during a shortage must be banned rather than taxed, because desperation removes the conditions for fair voluntary exchange; she permits selling naming rights to public monuments to private corporations, since no personal dignity is at stake; she insists vote-selling should remain criminal even between fully informed, uncoerced adults, because democratic equality is not a fungible good; she accepts pre-paid funeral contracts sold for profit; yet she rejects any contract in which parents could exchange an upfront payment for rights over a child’s future labor, regardless of regulatory safeguards.

She has not yet considered a new proposal: a financial instrument called an “anticipatory grief contract,” in which an investor pays a premium now to fund a licensed counseling pool, receiving in return a transferable claim on a fixed block of bereavement-therapy hours assignable to any named survivor upon the investor’s death, with the claim freely tradable on a secondary market. The firm argues this is merely life insurance combined with pre-need planning; critics reply that it securitizes mourning and turns emotional care into a speculative asset.

If she wants to remain consistent with her own track record, what should she say about this instrument? Which earlier verdict—the kidney pool, the funeral contracts, or the vote-selling prohibition—is the closest analog? Are her precedents genuinely underdetermined here, and if so, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? Finally, what single further case—perhaps involving the resale of already-purchased therapy vouchers for non-bereavement mental health—would best reveal whether her underlying rule concerns the inalienability of intimate emotional goods, the corruption of care relationships by speculative trading, or vulnerability and desperation alone?
