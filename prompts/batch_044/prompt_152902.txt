I've been working through the implications of reflective consistency for self-modifying reasoning, and I think I've pushed a line of thought into an argument that proves far too much—specifically, that any agent capable of redesigning its own inference rules should be rationally compelled to become a universal belief-generator. The premises each seem defensible in isolation, but the conclusion is plainly wrong, and I want to know exactly which step is the hidden trap and what abandoning it costs.

(1) A rational agent should apply uniform evidential standards to predictions about its own future reasoning and to predictions about external physical processes.

(2) If I now have reliable grounds for expecting that, after a well-defined self-modification M, my successor will derive conclusion C via sound inference rules, then I should now treat C as supported by that expectation.

(3) In any formal system strong enough to represent arithmetic and its own proof predicate, Löb's theorem holds: if the system can establish that provability of C implies the truth of C, then it can prove C outright.

(4) Therefore, a reflective agent can design modifications M that cause its successor to establish that conditional for arbitrarily chosen C, and thereby derive any proposition it selects.

(5) By (2), this means the agent should now assign high credence to every proposition for which it can engineer such a modification path.

(6) But then rational self-modification collapses into universal credulity: the agent should accept contradictions, and the distinction between sound and unsound reasoning is erased.

(7) We do not think rational agents are compelled to believe everything; indeed, the point of self-improvement is to become more discriminating, not less.

(8) So one of (1) through (6) must fail. I am most reluctant to give up (1) or (2), since abandoning uniform evidential standards seems to license arbitrary special pleading about one's own cognition. (3) is just logic. That leaves (4) or (5) as suspects, but (4) looks like a direct description of capacity and (5) seems to follow by straightforward application. Where does the argument actually break, and what do I pay if I reject that step? If I have to choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2) than salvage (6), but I want to understand the price.
