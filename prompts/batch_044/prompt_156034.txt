My seminar leader in moral epistemology, Dr. S. K. Lenz, has published explicit verdicts on six case studies involving testimonial transmission of moral belief. Case A: a layperson formed the belief that industrial animal agriculture is gravely wrong solely from a documentary presenting graphic conditions and expert narration; she could not reproduce the ethical argument but could correctly identify the specific suffering conditions shown. Verdict: moral knowledge. Case B: the same layperson formed the belief that effective altruism requires donating ten percent of income solely from an economist’s podcast, without grasping expected-value reasoning and knowing the podcast was advocacy-sponsored. Verdict: no knowledge, citing a source-bias defeater. Case C: a child formed the belief that causing unnecessary pain is wrong from parental instruction, retained it into adulthood with no counterevidence, but could articulate no supporting reason beyond bare assertion. Verdict: justified belief, but not knowledge, on grounds of lost cognitive access to the grounding chain. Case D: a juror formed the belief that a defendant acted with reckless disregard based on an expert witness who translated statistical risk data into moral language; the juror correctly understood the statistics but applied them through a mistaken legal definition of recklessness she held independently. Verdict: no knowledge, explicitly attributed to dependence on a false intermediate lemma about legal culpability. Case E: a climate activist formed the belief that present generations have direct obligations to specific future individuals based solely on a scientist’s testimony about carbon harm, despite not understanding the non-identity problem and implicitly holding that those specific individuals would exist regardless of present policy. Verdict: no knowledge, this time emphasizing the false intermediate belief about personal identity across policies rather than source unreliability. Case F: a philosopher formed the belief that moral realism is false based on a colleague’s concise summary of evolutionary debunking arguments; she trusted the colleague’s disciplinary expertise, had no defeaters, but could not reconstruct any inferential step. Verdict: knowledge, on the grounds that in domains of high theoretical complexity testimonial transmission can preserve knowledge when the recipient correctly identifies the disciplinary domain and the absence of peer disagreement, even lacking reconstructive capacity.

Next week’s case is Case G: a social worker forms the belief that a particular welfare-policy reform is unjust solely from a trusted senior colleague’s testimony. The social worker correctly understands the empirical predictions about the reform’s effects, can accurately articulate the colleague’s normative framework (the capabilities approach), but holds a false intermediate belief that the capabilities approach necessarily entails a specific distributive principle it does not in fact entail; crucially, however, the colleague’s testimony never invoked that distributive principle, and the social worker’s false belief plays no causal role in her inference path—she reaches the correct conclusion about this reform via the correct premises, with the erroneous theoretical commitment present only as background noise.

Predict Dr. Lenz’s verdict on Case G. Identify which implicit rule—source-reliability tracking with defeater exclusion, reconstructive understanding, prohibition on any false intermediate belief regardless of causal role, or prohibition only on causally operative false lemmas—best explains her pattern across Cases A through F, and justify your choice by showing how each competing rule mishandles at least one of those six precedents. Then specify the single further hypothetical case that would definitively adjudicate between the “any false belief” rule and the “causally operative false belief” rule in her framework.
