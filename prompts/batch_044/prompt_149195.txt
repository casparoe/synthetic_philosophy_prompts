I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, currently working on a feature about transgenerational justice and the philosophy of collective responsibility. In my reporting I have reconstructed the implicit case judgments of a contemporary moral philosopher who works within Karl Jaspers's taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—applied to national and institutional accountability.

The reconstructed verdicts are these. First, a nation-state that retains its original constitution, borders, and administrative apparatus nearly two centuries after a genocide, whose present citizens are direct descendants of both perpetrators and victims: the philosopher affirms that the nation bears political guilt, that citizens share in it collectively, but denies that any living individual bears criminal or moral guilt solely by membership. Second, a successor state founded on a new constitution that explicitly repudiates the prior regime, yet occupies the same capital, uses the same treasury buildings, and inherits the prior state's tax base: he holds that political guilt is diminished but not extinguished, and that reparative obligations persist because institutional life remains continuous despite ideological rupture. Third, an empire formally dissolved by treaty, its legal personality extinguished, its assets liquidated and distributed with no traceable successor, and a modern democratic republic established on part of the same territory with entirely new institutions: he denies that collective guilt attaches to the republic, though he allows that citizens may feel a metaphysical solidarity with past suffering. Fourth, a diaspora religious community that emigrated before the atrocity, maintains no territorial or institutional link to the perpetrator state, but preserves cultural narratives of the event: he rules that no political or moral collective guilt applies, though metaphysical guilt—an existential awareness of human solidarity in failure—may be individually cultivated.

These four judgments seem jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule. One rule might hold that institutional and legal continuity is necessary and sufficient for political collective guilt. Another might hold that benefit derived from unjust enrichment combined with territorial presence is sufficient, even absent legal succession. A third might require a diachronic collective intentional identity—a continuous "we"—without which no guilt attaches regardless of buildings or benefits.

Here is the new case. In 2042, a voluntary digital federation—the Confederation of the Southern Basin—was formed by citizens living on the territory of the dissolved empire from the third case. The Confederation adopted a constitution drafted through AI-assisted deliberation, repudiated all prior regimes, and holds no legal succession to the empire. Its citizens are almost entirely descendants of the empire's population. The federation's land-registry algorithm, however, still operates on the empire's 1932 cadastral classification taxonomy, maintained not by the state but by a private technology consortium that licenses the data back to the government. The tax code implicitly rewards families that retained property expropriated during the empire's crimes. The old treasury buildings are now private museums with no governmental function.

A claim for collective reparations is brought against the Confederation. Predict the philosopher's verdict on whether the federation bears political guilt and whether its citizens share in it. Identify the competing rules that fit his earlier four judgments equally well, state what each rule predicts about the Confederation, and give your best-guess verdict with reasoning. Then name the further hypothetical case—specifying the institutional, territorial, or technological conditions—that would best discriminate between the rules. Also identify which of the philosopher's earlier four cases is the closest analog, and explain what principle the body would be adopting regardless of which way it decides.

Please provide your analysis in American English, with a formal, polished tone appropriate for a long-form feature.
