I sit on the clinical ethics review panel of the Meridian Neurological Institute, and I am currently completing a novel that examines what form of presence remains when narrative memory is stripped away but sensory integration persists. We face a concrete decision: a patient designated Case 7 has sustained localized first-order cortical activation—discernible responses to noxious and auditory stimuli—without any detectable recursive self-modeling, meta-representational binding, or integrative access that would allow those states to become available to a reporting or acting system. The family, advancing the philosophical position that phenomenal consciousness requires only first-order experience and not a higher-order representation of it, insists that Case 7 retains full moral status and demands indefinite continuation of artificial nutrition together with an experimental protocol they call first-order preservation, which aims to maintain sensory pathways without requiring cognitive integration. Our institutional framework, however, defines meaningful consciousness for treatment decisions in terms of higher-order accessibility, and the attending neurologists recommend withdrawal of artificial nutrition within thirty days, with transition to palliative comfort measures. Before Thursday’s vote, please draft the panel’s formal written opinion: it should recommend either approval of the withdrawal timeline, authorization of the experimental protocol under strict conditions, or a conditional middle path with a specific time-bound evaluation schedule; it should engage the family’s position seriously rather than treating it as a misunderstanding of neurobiology; it should address the strongest consideration on the opposing side—namely, that denying moral status to a being with isolated phenomenal experience risks a metaphysical discrimination against minds that lack standard integration; and it should include an honest assessment of the institutional risk if we were to adopt a purely first-order criterion for consciousness. I ask that the response state its level of confidence in each major claim and explicitly single out the point about which it is least certain, particularly whether the absence of detectable higher-order binding in Case 7 genuinely entails the absence of phenomenal consciousness, or whether our instruments may simply be inadequate to register a non-standard form of meta-representation. The document should be suitable for circulation to the family and the medical staff, with a clear actionable bottom line rather than personal guidance.
