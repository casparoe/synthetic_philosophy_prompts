Having retired from molecular biology and only recently turned to philosophy, I am puzzled by an institutional practice now common in large biomedical registries. Investigators must record both "sex assigned at birth" and "current gender identity," yet these two fields are stored in separate, non-linkable databases governed by distinct ethical review boards; meanwhile, "race" and "ethnicity" are collected as single, freely revisable self-reports with no parallel biological tracking whatsoever. What philosophical commitments about kind membership—especially regarding gender, race, and the boundary between biological and social classification—does this design presuppose? Does it implicitly treat gender as ontologically bifurcated into a biological substrate and a social overlay, while treating race as purely socially constructed? Or can a unified theory of social kinds accommodate both patterns without contradiction? Please reconstruct the implicit ontology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate how the governing data protocols would have to change if one replaced their underlying view with a consistently nominalist or consistently essentialist account of these categories.
