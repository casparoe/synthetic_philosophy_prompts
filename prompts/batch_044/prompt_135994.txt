Write the joint statement a strict-constructionist and an expansive-naming activist could both sign after arguing about genocide as a legal and moral category. The strict camp defends the 1948 Convention's narrow protected-groups list and specific-intent requirement, arguing that broadening the term to cover cultural destruction or systemic violence without explicit orders dilutes its unique moral weight and makes legal conviction impossible. The expansive camp holds that Lemkin's original draft included cultural genocide and political groups, that the 1948 exclusions were politically motivated, and that refusing the label to structural destruction — forced assimilation, engineered famine, and so on — repeats the original erasure, even if it risks dilution.

Agreed claims (numbered): both accept that Lemkin's original concept was broader than the final Convention; both accept that international tribunals since the 1990s have required documented specific intent; both accept that 'genocide' carries a distinctive moral and legal status other atrocity-labels don't replicate; both accept that the politics of labelling affects survivors' recognition and state accountability.

Then a disagreements table: each side's precise position in wording they'd accept for themselves.

Cruxes: (A) Is dilution a genuine moral harm, or is exclusion the greater harm? (B) Can systemic patterns stand in for direct documentary proof of intent?

For (A) both should treat the Canadian residential school system as the test case — systematic cultural destruction, high mortality, no single order for total physical annihilation. The strict side predicts that calling it genocide weakens the term's power to distinguish Rwanda-style extermination; the expansive side predicts that withholding it reproduces the 1948 silencing.

For (B) both should treat the Holodomor (1932–33) as bearing on it — widespread famine and displacement, contested archival proof of intent to destroy the group as such. The strict side predicts that without clear intent documentation the legal category shouldn't apply; the expansive side predicts that bureacratic scale and targeting patterns make intent inferable, and that demanding direct proof sets an impossible bar for modern state violence.

Finally, ask them to say whether part of the dispute is verbal — e.g. one using 'genocide' as a legal verdict and the other as a historical-moral status — and if so which part, and record each side's predicitions about whether resolving that verbal point would settle anything.
