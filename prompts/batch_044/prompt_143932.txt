I'm drafting a scene in my novel where my protagonist opens a small memorial symposium for her adivsor, who died last winter. He spent forty years on Bradley's regress and the old problem of how a propostion actually holds together — if a relation binds terms, what binds the relation to the terms, and so on. The audience is split between metaphysicans who lived through this with him and family members who think he studied butterflies. I need the speech to turn on the idea structurally: she wants to say he's still present in the work they share, but every time she tries to name a relation — his influence in this room, his ideas in our papers — she bumps into the regress, the need for another binder. So she can't just declare unity or legacy without faking it. She shouldn't cite texts or drop names. Write it like she's speaking live, nervous, maybe a little messy, around three hundred words, and let the philosophical problem actually constrain what she can honestly claim about him being "with us" still.
