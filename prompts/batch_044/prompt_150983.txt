The examination question reads as follows. In standard derivations of Bell's theorem, locality is decomposed into parameter independence and outcome independence. Explain the functional role of each in reaching the factorization condition, then examine a superdeterministic hidden-variable model of the kind associated with Gerard 't Hooft. Does such a model violate locality in Bell's precise sense, or does it undermine the theorem by rejecting a different premise? Be specific about which assumption is sacrificed and whether that sacrifice ought to be counted as a failure of local causality.

The student's answer is reproduced below.

Bell's theorem derives constraints on correlations in any theory satisfying local causality, which is decomposed into parameter independence and outcome independence. Parameter independence requires that the probability distribution of outcomes on one side be independent of the setting chosen on the other side, conditional on the hidden variable λ. Outcome independence requires that, conditional on λ and both local settings, the outcomes themselves are statistically uncorrelated. Together these yield the factorization condition P(A,B|a,b,λ) = P(A|a,λ)P(B|b,λ) that Bell uses to bound correlations.

In his original 1964 derivation, Bell begins with a deterministic hidden-variable model, in which the outcome on each side is fixed once λ and the local setting are specified. This determinism already guarantees outcome independence, so Bell needs only parameter independence to reach factorization; he does not assume outcome independence as a separate premise. A superdeterministic model, such as 't Hooft's cellular automaton interpretation, allows λ to be correlated with the measurement settings a and b through a shared past. Because the hidden variables are not statistically independent of the choices, parameter independence is violated: the setting at one wing is not independent of the hidden state that determines the outcome at the other. There is still no direct causal influence across space, but since parameter independence is the precise formalization of the absence of such influence, superdeterminism actually abandons locality rather than statistical independence. Consequently, Bell's theorem is not undermined by a different premise; instead, the model fails the locality condition directly, and the common-cause explanation is merely a redescription of this non-local correlation.
