Design a structured 90-minute activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 12 students who have encountered basic accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory. The aim is to make participants feel, through their own rule-governed choices, the tension between a strict accuracy-first commitment—that belief’s primary epistemic value derives from correspondence with reality—and the methodological pull of retaining what this prompt will call scaffolding beliefs: representations that the group recognizes as technically false or imprecise but that organize evidence, coordinate inquiry, and unlock branching research pathways. Do not open with a lecture naming this distinction. Instead, divide students into three groups of four and assign each group the role of a research collective with a shared point pool. Give each group identical evidence cards describing a hypothetical complex phenomenon—say, a social-dynamics pattern with noisy indicators—along with two proposal templates they must choose between in each of three rounds. The conservative proposal scores high on an explicit accuracy metric (low false-positive risk, precise parameter estimates) but closes off further investigation: selecting it yields fixed individual points and ends that group’s round. The scaffolding proposal scores lower on accuracy (it posits a simplifying model the cards explicitly flag as technically false) but activates a branching tree of follow-up questions; each branch carries collective bonus points that compound if other groups also select compatible scaffolding choices, creating inter-group coordination incentives. The payoff formula should be printed on the board: individual accuracy points are linear, but collective branching bonuses are multiplicative and only unlock when at least two groups accept the same imprecise framing. Structure the three rounds so that groups adhering to pure accuracy-first reasoning accumulate steady but capped individual scores, while groups that accept calibrated imprecision can reach far higher collective totals—provided they coordinate. Include explicit rules for renegotiation: after round two, groups may revise their framing belief if they pay a penalty in accuracy points, simulating the cost of abandoning a scaffold. Materials needed: printed evidence cards, proposal templates with pre-printed accuracy scores, a visible scoreboard tracking both individual and collective pools, and a decision sheet for each round. Timeline: 10 minutes for rules and role assignment; three 15-minute decision rounds with 5-minute inter-round negotiations; 20 minutes for a debrief; 10 minutes for failure-mode discussion. In the debrief, map specific moments—such as the first round where one group notices the multiplicative bonus, or the renegotiation penalty in round three—onto the conceptual distinction between value derived from correspondence and value derived from structural epistemic utility. Ask each group to state where they landed and whether they experienced the choice as a failure of epistemic duty or as a legitimate trade-off. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: it stages instrumental success, but it does not capture the normative accuracy-first claim that false belief is intrinsically defective regardless of downstream utility, and the facilitator must supply that normative framing verbally. Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time: groups will likely misinterpret the multiplicative bonus as a trick and over-select conservative proposals out of risk aversion; correct for this by running a 5-minute practice round with no points at stake, and by appointing one student per group as a skeptic whose explicit role is to argue for the accuracy-first option so the tension is voiced rather than suppressed. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
