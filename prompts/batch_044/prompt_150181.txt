In public debate, honouring sunk costs is routinely likened to remaining in a dreadful cinema because the ticket is already paid: the cost is irrecoverable, and only future enjoyment should matter. Lately, however, some institutional economists have begun treating “sunk commitments” — maintaining legacy structures or long-term partnerships whose running costs now exceed their direct benefits — as credibility mechanisms in repeated interactions rather than as reasoning errors. Audit the cinema analogy: set out the correspondence table (ticket price, film quality, viewer discomfort, and the decision to leave, mapped onto maintenance costs, current benefits, stakeholder expectations, and withdrawal); identify precisely where the mapping fractures, especially regarding whether prior investments are fungible and whether future-interaction effects are present; and state whether the conclusion that honouring sunk costs is always irrational survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along.
