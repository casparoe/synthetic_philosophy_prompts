Having spent thirty-five years in experimental physics and only recently turned systematically to philosophy, I am constructing a graduate examination in epistemology and scientific reasoning. I seek a single essay question—designed for a ninety-minute sitting—that treats the inside view and the outside view not as forecasting tools to be defined, but as competing frameworks for epistemic self-assessment. The question must center on a precise conceptual tension: any attempt to apply the outside view to one's own judgment requires selecting a reference class, yet that selection is itself an inside-view judgment about which similarities are epistemically relevant. I want the prompt to require students to explain why this circularity is not vicious, to articulate what kind of epistemic modesty is demanded by holding both perspectives simultaneously, and to evaluate whether such dual adoption collapses into a self-undermining skepticism or can sustain rational action. Please supply: (1) the examination question itself, framed so that it cannot be answered by mere summary and demands conceptual analysis; (2) a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that makes explicit the distinction between reference-class selection and base-rate application, resolves the circularity without dissolving the inside-outside distinction, and characterizes the required modesty as structural rather than psychological; and (3) a marking scheme that assigns specific weight to each of these moves, clearly separates an A-level response—which preserves both views in productive tension—from a B-level response that describes them accurately but misses the structural-modesty point, and identifies common errors that should cost marks, such as conflating epistemic modesty with global skepticism or treating the outside view as purely statistical. The assessment should assume no access to external literature.
