I was chatting with a friend who works at a climate-policy think tank, and she mentioned they still use those old computer tournaments — you know, the ones where people submit algorithms to play repeated prisoner's dilemma against each other and tit-for-tat usually comes out on top — as a kind of design manual for international agreements. The idea is that treaties should be structured like the winning strategies: be nice first, punish defection imediately, then forgive. I find that weird. It feels like they're treating evolutionary stability in a simulated, fully-observed repeated game as if it were a normative blueprint for human institutions where actions are often hidden and interactions might genuinely be one-off.

So what philosophical commitments does that practice actually carry? Does it presuppose that cooperation among states is best understood as an evolutionary outcome rather than a moral or rational choice? And does it quietly collapse the distinction between one-shot and iterated dilemmas, assuming that whatever works in iteration will scale to singular, high-stakes negotiations? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind borrowing tournament results for treaty design: what it says about rationality, about whether allowing harm through non-punishment is different from causing harm through sanctions, and about whether cooperation needs internal motives or just the right payoff structure. Say whether the practice is consistent with a view that says true cooperation requires agents to value the cooperative outcome for its own sake, not just as an equilibrium strategy. Also, is it philosophically underdetermined — could a contractarian, a virtue ethicist, and a purely evolutionary biologist all sign off on the same treaty architecture for totally different reasons? And tell me what part of the design, if any, genuinely forces one philosophical view over the others. Finally, what would have to change in how these institutes advise governments if they genuinely believed that in human affairs, defection is often not a strategic choice but a failure of recognition — that is, if they thought the problem wasn't rational self-interest but something like misaligned perception?
