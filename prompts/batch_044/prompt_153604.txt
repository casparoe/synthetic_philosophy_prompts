I am advising a municipal ethics board that must decide whether to commit a fixed budget to restoring a nineteenth-century market hall or to expanding a community mental-health clinic, two options that appear to protect goods that are genuinely incommensurable: architectural heritage and collective memory on one side, and immediate access to care together with reduced morbidity on the other. In trying to frame the deliberation, I have begun working with an idea I call threshold pluralism, which holds that incommensurable values share an unarticulated baseline—perhaps a minimal form of human dignity or social recognition—but diverge irreducibly once we move above that floor. From this premise I have drawn what strikes me as an unavoidable conclusion: if heritage and health are truly incommensurable at the level of policy comparison, then any direct weighing of one against the other is not merely difficult but conceptually incoherent, analogous to comparing temperature with melody, and therefore a rational board cannot legitimately choose between them unless it first uncovers the hidden unitary metric—maybe a measure of long-term communal flourishing or a regret-minimized welfare function—that secretly renders both options commensurable and restores the possibility of standard optimization.

Given that framework, could you explain precisely how such a board ought to construct that underlying metric, apply it to this specific allocation problem, and defend the resulting decision against the objection that it has simply arbitrarily privileged one incommensurable good over another? I am especially interested in whether threshold pluralism genuinely supplies the shared baseline I am presupposing, or whether it instead collapses into a disguised form of value monism that negates the very incommensurability it was introduced to accommodate, and I would like you to address whether there remains any rational structure to the choice once the illusion of a hidden conversion factor is abandoned. Please present your analysis in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were composing a single formal letter.
