I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure in debates over whether historical mass atrocities may be compared without being ranked. Typically, one interlocutor introduces a comparison restricted to a narrowly specified axis—bureaucratic architecture, temporal density, or demographic scale relative to the affected population—while explicitly disclaiming any judgment about aggregate moral severity or about the subjective suffering of victims. The other party responds not by disputing the factual basis of the comparison, but by asserting that selecting any measurable axis already treats the victims' experiences as interchangeable quantities, thereby implicitly constructing a hierarchy of evil regardless of the speaker's intent. The first interlocutor then distinguishes comparison of causal mechanisms from comparison of moral worth, insisting that cross-case structural analysis is indispensable for prevention and that an absolute prohibition on comparison forecloses moral learning. At this point the debate usually stalls: both sides reject the quantification of suffering, yet they divide irreducibly over whether a non-evaluative comparison necessarily carries an implicit moral ranking as its unavoidable shadow. The pattern recurs across very different pairs of events, suggesting it is a stable discursive form rather than an artifact of particular cases. Is there an established concept—whether in moral philosophy, the philosophy of history, or political theory—that captures precisely this deadlock, in which the disagreement concerns not whether comparison is epistemically possible but whether it is morally entailed? If no precise term exists, can the phenomenon be named and defined in a way that distinguishes it from assertions about the uniqueness of particular evils and from broader disputes about the legitimacy of moral comparison itself?
