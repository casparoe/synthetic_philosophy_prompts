I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop I'm convening on inheritance taxes, dynastic wealth, and the structural conditions of equal opportunity. The audience is deliberately mixed: half are philosophers and economists who have spent years on intergenerational mobility and distributive justice, and half are representatives from two family foundations—both descendants of long-established dynasties that fund university chairs—who have agreed to attend but are not academics themselves.

I want the address to do more than set a polite tone; I want it to be shaped structurally by a specific conceptual distinction that I think cuts through the usual debate but is rarely made explicit: the difference between inheritance as a simple inter vivos transfer of purchasing power (the framing that dominates tax-policy discussion) and inheritance as a mechanism of positional lock-in, by which dynastic wealth doesn't just enrich a recipient but actively reserves social, educational, and political option-space across generations in a way that markets don't price and individual merit can't overcome. Because of this framing, there are real constraints on what the speech can say: I must be able to acknowledge and thank the foundations sincerely for their institutional support without treating their family fortunes as morally neutral private property, and I must be able to present inheritance taxation as a correction to an intergenerational externality rather than as redistribution born of envy—without ever turning the remarks into a lecture or making the family representatives feel like defendants. The tone should be formal, precise, and appropriate for a rationalist-adjacent audience that values conceptual clarity over rhetorical flourish; please write roughly ten minutes of material, and don't include any bibliography, reading list, or citations—the point is conceptual architecture, not literature review.
