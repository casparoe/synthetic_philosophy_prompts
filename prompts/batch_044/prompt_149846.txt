For an advanced seminar in moral philosophy, design a taxonomy tree that sorts philosophical responses to Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument—the line of reasoning that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui for one’s own mental nature, that nothing can be causa sui, and therefore that true moral responsibility is impossible. Do not organize the tree around familiar labels such as “compatibilist,” “libertarian,” or “hard determinist”; instead, build it from the logical space of answers to three concrete diagnostic questions that a student could answer without knowing which position each answer produces.

Root branch: Must an agent be the ultimate originator—causa sui—of her own character, motives, and deliberative capacities in order to be truly morally responsible for actions that flow from them?

Second branch (for paths that answer yes at the root): Is being causa sui metaphysically possible for a finite, embodied agent, or is it impossible in principle?

Third branch (cross-cutting, applied separately to each path through the first two): Can an agent acquire retrospective moral authorship through diachronic self-modification—gradually reshaping the causal sources of her actions through sustained reflective endorsement—thereby breaking the transmission principle that would otherwise deny responsibility for actions rooted in an unchosen nature? For each path, treat this option as available, incoherent, or irrelevant.

Structure the deliverable as follows. First, lay out every logically possible combination of answers to these three branch points, marking the diachronic option’s status in each cell. Then place named positions inside that grid: Strawson’s own view (true responsibility impossible, but reactive attitudes remain justified without desert-grounding), agent-causal libertarianism, hierarchical compatibilism of the Frankfurt variety, reasons-responsive semi-compatibilism, and any other view you think genuinely belongs. Where a combination is unoccupied, label the leaf “unnamed” and supply one sentence diagnosing the vacancy—whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate.

For every leaf, give the position’s name (or “unnamed”) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the specific cost it pays for occupying that cell. Keep the tree to eight or ten leaves, address it to graduate students in ethics, and ensure that each branch question turns on a concrete claim about agency or causation rather than on self-identification with a philosophical school.
