I've been trying to stabilize my own view on mental causation and physicalism after working through the exclusion literature, and before I write anything up I'd like a consistency audit. Here is the package: (1) I accept causal closure of physics in a strong metaphysical sense—every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, with no gaps for non-physical intervention. (2) I am a non-reductive physicalist about phenomenal and intentional properties: they are realized by neural processes but not identical to them, and I reject both eliminativism and type-identity. (3) I insist that mental properties are causally efficacious in a genuinely productive, not merely correlational, way—when I form an intention to raise my arm, that intention plays a real explanatory and productive role in the movement, not just tracking prior brain states. (4) I am inclined toward a powers-based ontology of causation rather than an event-causal framework: causation is the exercise of capacities, and mental properties are capacities of organized physical systems. (5) I think the standard Kim-style exclusion argument presupposes an event-causal ontology that a powers view undermines, but I also think supervenience alone only secures covariance, not causal relevance, so I cannot solve the problem just by citing realization. (6) I reject epiphenomenalism on both phenomenological and evolutionary grounds, and I think the evolutionary-utility argument against it is stronger than its critics usually allow.

My concern is that (1), (2), and (3) together seem to recreate the classic exclusion problem in full force, and my move in (4) might be doing hidden work that (5) admits is insufficient. Specifically: if closure holds and mental properties are non-identical realized properties, does insisting on productive mental causation commit me to systematic overdetermination, or to a covert failure of closure? And if I respond that powers are not events and therefore do not compete with physical causes for the same causal work, am I offering a legitimate dissolution of the exclusion argument or just redefining "causal efficacy" to avoid it? I want you to map which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension versus which only appear so, rank the tensions by severity, and say whether the powers-based move is a substantive solution or a definitional escape. If there is a revision that cleans up the most with the least philosophical cost—perhaps weakening closure to a statistical regularity, moving to a form of emergentism rather than realization, or conceding that mental causation is only structural or downward-constraining—identify it and spell out exactly what I would have to give up.
