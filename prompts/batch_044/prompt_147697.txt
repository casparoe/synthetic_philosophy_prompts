I've been asked to write down my methodological commitments on heuristic reasoning and independence for a philosophy of science reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present. In doing so, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus. Here is my profile:

(1) I accept a default principle of indifference: when we lack structural information about coupling between variables, we should distribute credence evenly over the possibilities.

(2) I believe the formal presumption of independence—treating an unknown joint distribution as the product of its marginals—is justified primarily by symmetry considerations, not merely by ignorance or pragmatic convenience.

(3) I reject applying that formal presumption to self-locating cases such as the Sleeping Beauty problem; the experimental design creates a dependency between my temporal position and the coin outcome, so the variables should not be treated as independent.

(4) I regard arguments like the statistical-mechanical uniform distribution over microstates as purely heuristic shortcuts grounded in ignorance, with no legitimate appeal to symmetry or formal independence rules.

(5) I deny that there is a meaningful distinction between epistemic independence and causal independence: to treat variables as epistemically independent just is to model them as causally unconnected.

Please go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear tense but dissolve once the motivations are made explicit, and for each real tension specify which single revision is least costly. Rank the tensions by severity. Be precise about which commitment supplies the motivation that another undermines. Also indicate, for each point, whether the underlying issue is largely settled in the relevant literature or remains actively disputed—particularly regarding the role of symmetry in justifying independence and the status of self-locating belief.
