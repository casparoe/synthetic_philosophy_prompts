I am a novelist accustomed to thinking about consciousness through the machinery of narrative form — focalisation, free indirect discourse, unreliable narration, diegetic nesting, manuscript revision, and what I call the editorial palimpsest: successive drafts of a single scene, each claiming to correct its predecessor, with no authoritative master text standing outside the sequence. I wish you to restate, as precisely and charitably as you can, the philosophical disagreement between the Cartesian theatre — the notion of a unified, central observer within the mind for whom experience is displayed — and the so-called multiple drafts model, using only that narrative vocabulary. First, render the Cartesian theatre as a narrative structure: specify its point of view, its editorial authority, and its diegetic position. Next, render the multiple drafts model through my palimpsest form: a sequence of competing versions of an event, none privileged, lacking any external archivist. Once the translation is complete, provide the residue report. Which philosophical claims survive intact? Which can only be accommodated by distorting either the philosophy or the craft vocabulary — for example, by treating a neurological hypothesis as if it were an editorial decision? And which claims find no expression whatsoever in narrative terms, and does that absence indicate a deficiency in the language of fiction or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it seemed? Please answer in formal British English, without bibliographies, citations, or appeals to sources I could not consult unaided; I am interested solely in the fidelity of the translation and in what it reveals about the limits of both frameworks.
