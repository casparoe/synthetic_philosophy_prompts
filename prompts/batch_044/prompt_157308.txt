I've been discussing semantic externalism with friends after a philosophy of language course, and I've collected verdicts about where meaning is fixed by the world versus by our practices — but they don't line up neatly.

Consider the familiar Twin Earth case: Oscar on Earth uses 'water' for H2O, while Twin-Oscar, internally identical, uses 'water' for XYZ. I say they differ in meaning because the substance they actually interact with differs. Then take 'arthritis': on Earth the term applies only to joint inflammation, while on Twin Earth the community applies it to thigh conditions too, and Twin-Oscar uses it for his thigh. I still say he means something different, but here the driver is social usage, not hidden chemistry. So both hidden structure and communal practice can fix reference.

Now two invented cases pull against each other. First, 'neuro-signal': Earth scientists coin it for a brainwave, then discover it's a scanner artefact; the term sticks to the artefactual pattern, and on Twin Earth the same word tracks a real but different brainwave. I feel Earth usage refers to the artefactual pattern — social practice wins. Second, 'synthetic emerald': Twin-Earth jewellers grow chemically identical stones but call them 'fakes', reserving 'emerald' for mined ones. I want to say 'emerald' still refers to the chemical kind there — hidden structure wins. For normative terms like 'justice', I think Twin-Earth practices that differ from ours change the meaning, yet I also sense an external corrective pressure if our concept proves incoherent. So is there a single principle — perhaps that different kinds of term have a stronger pull towards hidden structure or towards social practice depending on their domain — or must I split my view? Which verdict should I drop: the neuro-signal social-practice reading, or the emerald structure reading?

Please say what argument about the emerald versus neuro-signal conflict would change your mind — either unifying them under one rule or forcing me to abandon the domain split. End with Verdict: followed by a brief summary of whether a unified principle exists and which case it sacrifices.
