Since I began auditing the moral-reasoning outputs of language models for a governance project, I have found myself returning repeatedly to the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. The classic difficulty—whether an expressivist semantics for moral language can preserve logical validity when moral sentences are embedded in unasserted contexts such as conditionals or disjunctions—strikes me as structurally similar to a pattern I observe in these systems: they process embedded moral claims with full compositional transparency, as though the embedded clause carries a stable descriptive content, yet the outputs often lack the kind of attitudinal coherence that expressivists take to be distinctive of moral judgement. This parallel has convinced me that the philosophical stalemate is not merely historical but actively reproduced in new domains, which is why I want to understand its roots.

Could you diagnose why the debate over the Frege–Geach problem has remained so intractable? I am not asking for a survey of every position, but for a disciplined diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it is—whether verbal, about the correct semantics for embedded contexts; substantive but masked by shared vocabulary; or a case in which expressivists and their descriptivist critics are answering different questions about language, cognition, and normative commitment. Please ground your diagnosis in what each side actually says about embedding, compositionality, and the nature of moral judgement, and separate clearly what is settled among philosophers who specialise in metaethics from what remains genuinely contested—particularly regarding whether quasi-realist strategies successfully dissolve the problem or whether hybrid expressivist accounts genuinely avoid it. Do not present any contested interpretation, such as the claim that the problem is purely technical and solved by standard semantic machinery, as though it commanded consensus.

Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result in the semantics of attitude contexts, an empirical finding about how moral cognition handles embedding, or a conceptual distinction that reveals the original problem to be ill-posed? Finally, does the behaviour of large language models when they embed moral sentences—treating them as transparently compositional—actually illuminate the philosophical issue, or is it a red herring that merely replicates the same ambiguity at a different scale?
