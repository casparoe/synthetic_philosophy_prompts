Right, I'm running an upper-level philosophy of science seminar — about 12 people, one 90-minute session. I want an activity that makes them feel why interpretations of probability actually matter, not just as taxonomy, but in a case where frequentism, subjectivism and propensity all seem to give different answers and there's no obvious tie-breaker.

Set it up around a genuinely singular event: a synthetic polymer engineered to undergo one irreversible fold — either it reaches the correct conformation or collapses. There's no population, no repetition, no statistical mechanics that applies. Split the class into three groups of four and give each hidden role cards: the Frequency Bureau (can only cite observed relative frequencies, but I hand them three conflicting referance classes — 0.2, 0.5, 0.8), the Belief Market (must assign a precise credence based on coherence; they each get a hidden prior — 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 — and may only update on public evidence, of which there is none), and the Disposition Lab (told there's an objective physical propensity of strength 0.5, but they may not invoke it without independent empirical access, which they don't have).

Give everyone 10 fake "credibility chips". Phase one: 20 minutes in groups to agree on a single number p and a payout rule. Phase two: 25 minutes in plenary. They must vote on one shared p to govern a collective bet — everyone stakes 3 chips, I reveal a sealed envelope (folded / collapsed) and pay out according to the odds set by p. If they can't agree within 15 minutes the bet is void and everyone loses 2 chips, so there's real pressure to co-ordinate.

The rules should do the work: the Bureau deadlocks over which reference class is legitimate; the Market discovers that coherence allows rational divergence and that betting just redistributes chips according to prior disagreement; the Lab ends up asserting a number they can't justify to others without circularity. In the debrief map each deadlock onto the formal problem — reference-class ambiguity for frequentism, intersubjective divergence for Bayesians, epistemic inaccessibility for propensities. Say what will go wrong: the Bureau will smuggle "similarity" intuitions in, the Market may false-converge if one voice dominates, the Lab will slide into "design intention" which collapses into subjective belief. Be frank: the activity can't reproduce the actual ontological reality of a propensity — it only stages the epistemic gap — and you'll need to supply that metaphysical part yourself. Also, the co-ordination failure is artificial; in real life people just walk away, but here the penalty forces the tension into the open.
