I am writing a short commentary for my philosophy of rationality seminar, and I have been working through a draft argument about Parfit's hitchhiker scenario that departs somewhat from the standard causal-versus-evidential framing. In my essay, I propose that the central difficulty is not merely the hitchhiker's causal impotence over his future self once he is dropped off, but a deeper problem about diachronic authority: any precommitment that makes payment credible—whether through reputation, an internalized disposition, or what I call a self-induced restructuring of one's future utility function—seems to alter the very preferences that made the original promise rational in the first place. To sharpen this, I draw an analogy with psychiatric Ulysses contracts, though I stress that unlike those cases, the hitchhiker's constraint is not external but consists in transforming himself into the kind of agent who genuinely prefers to pay. This leads me to suggest a kind of rationality trap: successful precommitment eliminates the strategic need for it, while failure confirms the driver's prediction of defection. I am unsure whether this distinction between causal impotence and preference restructuring is genuinely present in Parfit's discussion or whether I am importing an idea that belongs to a different debate about personal identity and temporal parts.

I would be grateful for specific comments on two points. First, does my framing misrepresent Parfit's own resolution—particularly his appeal to causal decision theory—or is it fair to say that causal decision theory addresses the strategic interaction but leaves this diachronic-authority problem untouched? Second, does the comparison to Ulysses contracts strengthen the analysis by clarifying the difference between external and internal constraints, or does it distract from the logical structure of the hitchhiker case by introducing clinical and legal considerations that do not apply? I am also interested in whether the idea of a self-induced restructuring of the utility function is coherent, or whether it collapses into either standard preference change or simple self-deception. I do not need bibliographic suggestions; I am working only from the course texts and my own notes, and I want to know whether the argument holds together on its own terms.
