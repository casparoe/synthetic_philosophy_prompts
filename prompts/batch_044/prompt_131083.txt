I'm preparing a graduate seminar on collective responsibility and have become stuck on a tension that seems to fall between the cracks of the standard treatments. Could you write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—Dr. Lena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—debating whether a nation can bear guilt across generations when its population has been almost entirely replaced. They should discuss a concrete scenario: a democratic state that retains the same constitutional framework and treaty obligations established during a prior authoritarian regime that committed documented mass atrocities, but in which, over three generations, voluntary migration and demographic change have meant that virtually none of the current citizens are descendants of either the perpetrators or the victims, and none of the original institutional actors remain. Voss should argue that the state possesses a diachronic institutional identity that can sustain a non-distributive form of metaphysical guilt—an obligation of repair that binds the polity as a continuing subject rather than any particular living individual—and that reducing this to personal culpability misses the structural reality of inherited injustice. Chen should resist by attacking the coherence of this diachronic subject, contending that without psychological continuity or direct causal contribution, calling the present polity guilty is only a rhetorical device for distributing moral costs onto innocent people, and that institutional continuity is merely legal fiction. Each should respond directly to the other's arguments: for instance, Chen should press Voss on how a purely legal entity can suffer the kind of moral burden that guilt implies, and Voss should answer that collective responsibility need not be felt to be binding, just as a corporation can owe debts without feeling remorse. The exchange should avoid strawmen, should not ask for bibliographies or citations, and should treat the relevant distinctions—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—through paraphrase rather than direct quotation, since I'm working away from my library and need to see how the conceptual architecture holds up on its own.
