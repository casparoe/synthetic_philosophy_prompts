I'm in a philosophy course on parts and wholes and there's this pattern I keep running into that I can't tell if it's a real named problem or just my own confusion.

People who reject universalism—like, they don't want to say there's a real object made of my left shoe and the moon—usually defend that by pointing to some kind of toghetherness: physical connection, causal interaction, or working as one system. But then I notice that inside perfectly normal objects, you can find these little pockets that also have that toghetherness with each other, maybe even more strongly than with the rest of the big thing. For example, the bacteria in your gut are causally interacting with each other in really dense ways, and they're also interacting with your body, but it feels like they could be their own object living inside you. Or in a computer, the CPU and the GPU are both part of the machine, but they barely talk to each other compared to how tightly the CPU's own cores are linked—so why isn't there a separate 'CPU-object' that's more real than the computer?

So the rule meant to block scattered random objects seems to also break up ordinary objects into competing sub-objects that have just as good a claim to being unified. I've heard of 'organic unity' and maybe 'functional integretion' as criteria, but those sound like proposed answers, not a name for this specific rebound where anti-scatter conditions over-fragment the very things they were meant to save. Does this configuration have a name—maybe something about mereological redundancy or internal scattering? If not, how should I think about its boundaries: is it just vagueness about parts, or is it a distinct failure mode for restrictivist theories? Please don't just drop a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are, with boundary cases. You are an expert metaphysician, so think carefully, start with a quick brainstorm of how contact, fusion, and functionalist views each handle the gut-bacteria and CPU examples, and then tell me whether I'm describing something established.
