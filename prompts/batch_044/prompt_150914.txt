Last night I spent nearly three hours arguing with a close friend about whether moral compromise between people with genuinely incommensurable value systems—say, a secular consequentialist framework and a tradition-bound virtue-ethical framework—can ever yield what economists call a safe Pareto improvement for both parties, or whether it necessarily corrupts at least one side’s structurally protected commitments. We kept circling the same examples without ever pinning down exactly where we agreed, where we merely used the same words differently, and what evidence would actually move either of us. I want us to correct that with a proper adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign.

Name the camps precisely but neutrally: the Inter-Systemic Trade Theorists, who hold that heterogeneous moral priority structures allow for gains-from-trade-style compromises in which each party secures closer realization of their own highest-order values by conceding on lower-order commitments, and the Normative Integrity Theorists, who hold that moral values within a coherent system are structurally integrated and protected, so that any concession demanded by an alien normative framework amounts not to exchange but to dilution, betrayal, or adaptive degradation of one’s own framework.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the reality of value pluralism, the phenomenology of real-world moral compromise, the formal conditions for a Pareto improvement, and specific shared rejections (for instance, that all compromise is strategic deception, or that all moral values are reducible to a single cardinal metric). Second, a disagreements section in which every residual dispute is stated twice—once in wording the Trade Theorists accept for their own view, once in wording the Integrity Theorists accept for theirs—with explicit labeling of which disagreements are substantive and which are verbal (for example, whether “corruption” is the correct description of a reordered priority). Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp—including specifically whether cross-system compensation is coherent without a shared cardinal scale, whether long-term stable compromises represent revealed preference for cooperation or adaptive degradation of protected values, and whether there exists a meta-normative principle of cooperation that is itself value-system-neutral. Fourth, for each crux, present one concrete scenario or line of reasoning that both sides agree is genuinely relevant, followed by each camp’s explicit prediction of what that scenario demonstrates and what finding would cause them to revise their view.

Keep the framing rigorous and formal, as if representatives of both camps will put their names to the document. Do not treat either position as a straw man, do not ask for bibliographies or citations, and do not invoke hyper-specific empirical studies or obscure theoretical apparatus that would require outside lookup; instead, construct the conceptual machinery—such as value-system-relative exchange rates, protected-commitment structures, and inter-systemic surplus extraction—directly within the prompt’s own terms, so that the task is one of precise philosophical drafting rather than literature review.
