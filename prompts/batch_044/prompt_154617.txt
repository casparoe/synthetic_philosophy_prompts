Consider the following reconstructed passage from Michel de Montaigne’s essay “Of Cannibals” (Essais, first published 1580), in which he examines the customs of the Tupinambá people of Brazil. He observes that they live without written laws, without monarchs, and without the arts of navigation and arithmetic that Europeans prize, yet they maintain a social harmony that European societies, torn by religious war, conspicuously lack. He describes their cannibalism not as gustatory appetite but as a ritual of vengeance: they roast and consume enemies to demonstrate that hatred extends even beyond death. He then asks whether this practice is more barbarous than European practices of torture, execution, and dismemberment performed upon living bodies in the name of justice and faith. He concludes that every nation calls barbarous whatever differs from its own habits, and that the true barbarism lies not in foreign rites but in the cruelty Europeans display toward those they deem inferior, especially when that cruelty is masked by law and religion.

Please help me understand a specific tension in this reasoning. Montaigne appears to advance a descriptive form of cultural relativism—each society defines barbarism relative to its own norms—while simultaneously issuing a normative judgment that European cruelty is genuinely worse than Tupinambá cannibalism. How can both commitments be sustained without contradiction? In particular, does Montaigne implicitly operate with a distinction between standpoint-dependent evaluation (judgments valid only within a particular cultural framework) and standpoint-transcendent evaluation (judgments that hold across frameworks by appealing to a shared human dignity or natural reason)? If so, where in the passage does that distinction do its work? Please explain the logic of his position without importing modern anthropological terminology, though you may introduce a precise conceptual distinction—such as that between conventional judgment and natural judgment—to clarify the structure of his argument.
