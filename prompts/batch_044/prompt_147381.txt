I am drafting a six-month research proposal for an independent philosophy-of-logic fellowship. The topic is Carroll's tortoise and the justification of deduction, but I want to move past the familiar framing of whether logical rules require circular or infinite justification. Instead, I want to investigate a more specific gap: whether the tortoise's regress exposes a structural asymmetry between the conditions that make a logical rule valid and the conditions that make an agent entitled to apply that rule, and whether this asymmetry survives in formal frameworks that explicitly separate object-level inference from meta-level acceptance—such as provability logic or explicit-modal proof theories. The proposal should include: a precise statement of the research question; an explanation of why standard responses—primitivism about logical rules, infinitism, and coherentist collapse of the distinction—fail to address this specific asymmetry rather than merely avoiding the regress; the proposed method, including how examining formalizations with explicit modality operators can illuminate whether the gap can be closed without either accepting an unjustified primitive entitlement or collapsing the two conditions into one; a description of what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure (e.g., if the asymmetry turns out to be merely notational); and an honest assessment of scope, specifically whether the project risks requiring original technical results more appropriate to a mathematics dissertation rather than a philosophical analysis of existing formal tools. Do not request or include a bibliography, citation list, or reading list; refer to positions and arguments descriptively, noting that references will be supplied separately.
