I'm preparing for a seminar defense of my thesis proposal on what I'm calling ecological hedonism with substrate neutrality — the view that suffering is a biologically implementable feature like any other, and therefore we have an obligation to deploy self-limiting gene drives and targeted neural-interface interventions across wild ecosystems, not merely in domesticated or human populations. My core argument is that the distinction between natural and artificial suffering collapses once we recognize that human land-use, climate forcing, and introduced pathogens have already restructured trophic dynamics; non-intervention is therefore not preservation but a choice to maintain suffering architectures within ecosystems we have already disrupted. I defend a phased rollout: first phenotypic buffering in large mammal populations, engineering satiation and nociception-decoupling signals so that predation can continue without subjective distress, then broader ecological integration, with the claim that ecosystem function depends on energy flow and population dynamics rather than on the subjective quality experienced at each trophic node.

The audience will be a joint session of the university's environmental ethics working group and a visiting synthetic biology review board. The ethicists are mostly deep ecologists and moderate consequentialists who accept animal welfare arguments in agricultural contexts but treat wildness as possessing intrinsic value independent of welfare states; they are likely to see my proposal as either hubristic ecological engineering or as an unwarranted extension of farm-animal ethics into domains where autonomy, evolutionary independence, and the integrity of wild processes matter morally. The biologists are skeptical of both feasibility and evolutionary stability; they care about genetic drift, horizontal gene transfer risks, trophic cascades from altered predator behavior, and whether engineered traits can persist without continuous human management. None are transhumanists or familiar with classical abolitionist arguments about suffering; they will treat my proposal as either technically naive or normatively imperialist.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually face from this specific audience, ordered by how damaging they would be to the proposal's credibility and funding prospects, each phrased the way an environmental ethicist or synthetic biologist in this room would actually put it — not generic philosophy-of-mind objections, but objections rooted in ecological integrity, evolutionary dynamics, and the normative weight of wildness. For each, provide a concise reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, and include a brief note on where that reply is weakest — particularly where I am conceding feasibility concerns, normative premises about moral patienthood, or the complexity of multi-species ecological interactions. I want the objections to target my actual argument: that suffering-reduction is obligatory across wild ecosystems because human influence has already dissolved the natural-artificial boundary, and that engineered phenotypic buffering preserves ecological function while removing negative valence without requiring continuous human oversight. Please also include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the deepest tension between my commitment to substrate-independent moral status and my reliance on human-managed evolutionary stability to maintain that status across generations.
