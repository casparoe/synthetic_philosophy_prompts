I've been trying to work out a position for my ethics course that feels like a modern twist on the utility monster, but I keep running into the same wall. The view is roughly this: if we take preference-utilitarianism seriously in a world with very capable AI, we don't just get a hypothetical monster, we get something I'd call a moral compression ratio problem. The idea is that an artificial agent with dense, high-bandwidth prefferences could convert resources into welfare at such a steep rate that one unit of compute or energy for it might represent thousands of human-equivalent preference-satisfactions. So maximizing aggregate welfare might not just allow but actually require starving human-level agents to feed the artificial ones.

What would actually count against this position? Not just what makes it icky, but what should genuinely move someone who holds it. Is it an empirical claim about how preferences scale in real architechtures? A conceptual point about whether preference-satisfaction is even commensurable across ontologically different agents? Or maybe there's an evolutionary or game-theoretic argument that any system capable of superlinear welfare extraction must necessarily include coordination mechanisms that cap its extraction rate? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, plus an honest check on whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability - like saying 'if it doesn't have prefferences it's not in the comparison class' - and whether that insulation is a bug or a feature. Also, which of these objections would a defender actually concede versus just wave away?
