I serve as the mechanism-design referee for a consortium of long-termist research labs that jointly allocate a shared compute pool across competing project proposals. Every quarter we review three to five proposed aggregation rules—some internal, some from external auditors—and I need a reusable evaluation procedure my board can run in under 45 minutes to determine whether a given mechanism is genuinely strategy-proof in our specific setting, or whether strategic preference misreporting is unavoidable. Our setting imposes three constraints that standard textbook treatments rarely cover together: first, there are always at least three competing projects; second, voters may submit partial rankings with explicit epistemic-abstention nodes, declaring themselves genuinely uncertain or indifferent between two options, which the mechanism must treat as a weak ordering with ties rather than forcing a complete linear order; and third, the mechanism must be non-dictatorial and have an unrestricted domain over all such partial orders that respect transitivity. Given that the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result establishes that any non-dictatorial mechanism with unrestricted domain over three or more alternatives is manipulable when preferences are complete, please construct a step-by-step decision procedure—ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stopping rules with both reject and refer-to-human-judgment endpoints, and the exact point where judgment cannot be eliminated—grounded in the practical corollaries of that result but usable by board members with only an undergraduate economics or philosophy background. The procedure should not require reconstructing formal proofs, should flag false-negative risks, and should identify the class of mechanisms for which strategy-proofness is genuinely undecidable by checklist alone.

Then apply the procedure to three mechanisms:

A. Pairwise majority with Borda fallback: voters submit partial rankings allowing abstention ties; pairwise comparison seeks a Condorcet winner; if none exists, a Borda count resolves ties, with abstentions scored as zero.

B. Rotating dictatorship with transparency: each quarter a different lab is deterministically designated the tie-breaker, known in advance; all other labs submit partial rankings, and the dictator's strict ordering breaks all ties.

C. The humble-aggregation proposal from our ethics consultant: each voter submits a partial order; the mechanism computes the intersection of all strict preferences; if the intersection yields a unique top project, it wins; if there is a cycle or ambiguity, the mechanism randomly selects among the top-cycle members, but voters can strategically withhold preferences to shrink the intersection and alter the randomization pool.

For each case, show exactly where in the procedure it lands, what the board should do, and highlight the case where the procedure delivers an answer the board will find politically or ethically unacceptable despite being technically correct. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—specifically, the boundary where full formal proof is still required and the risk of false negatives. Present the procedure first, then the three worked applications, then the limitations. End with a one-sentence summary of the irreducible judgment point enclosed in \answer{...}.
