I'm running an upper-level philosophy seminar on metaphysics of mind for about twelve graduate students, with ninety minutes available. We've been working through Buddhist analyses of anattā and the skandhas, particularly the Abhidharma breakdown into form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness, and I want an activity that makes the students feel the practical tension between conventional ascriptions of moral responsibility and the ultimate denial of a persisting agent, rather than just discussing it abstractly. Design a structured role-play or deliberation—call it a stream tribunal—in which the class is divided into groups representing each of the five aggregates at three different temporal stages of a single life: an earlier moment when a harmful act was committed, a present moment of judgment, and a future moment that will suffer the consequences. The rules must allow participants to speak only in conventional language (using pronouns and names) and to reach a binding collective decision about whether punishment is warranted, but they must also maintain an internal ultimate register in which they can only justify moves by pointing to causal continuities and aggregate replacements, never to a self that endured. I want the payoff structure—perhaps points awarded for coherence, compassion, and logical consistency—to be arranged so that the groups experience genuine pressure to hold someone responsible even as their own framework denies the someone exists, and I want them to construct the reasoning steps themselves rather than hearing them named in advance.

The deliverable should include the precise division of roles, the timeline for the ninety minutes, the rules governing speech in the conventional versus ultimate registers, the small-stakes payoff scheme (no real money, but something that creates real tension like public commitment or graded contribution), and a debrief that maps each crisis point in the tribunal back onto a specific feature of anattā or the two-truths framework. Crucially, the debrief should be honest about what the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the soteriological dimension in which recognizing anattā is meant to dissolve suffering rather than create a practical paradox—and should flag what will predictably go wrong the first time, such as groups collapsing the distinction between registers or one dominant student speaking for all aggregates. Also, please inject one conceptual twist that is not standard in introductory treatments: frame the tribunal around a niche scenario in which the future aggregates are not merely successors but are causally affected by the present judgment in ways that mirror dependent origination, so that the decision to punish actually reshapes the aggregates that will receive it, making the problem of agency and consequence circular in a way standard personal-identity puzzles usually avoid.
