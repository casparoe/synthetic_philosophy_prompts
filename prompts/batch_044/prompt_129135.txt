I'm designing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of logic seminar, and I want a single essay question that forces students to engage with the problem of logical omniscience at a structural level rather than reciting its textbook formulation. To prevent pure memorization, I have embedded a specific, self-contained scenario: an artificial reasoning system called Alpha is built with a hard cognitive budget that permits it to derive logical consequences only when the shortest available proof requires five or fewer inference steps. Alpha knows that P and knows that P entails Q, yet it does not know Q because every derivation exceeds the budget. Defenders claim this replaces the closure principle of epistemic logic with a resource-sensitive rationality condition; critics reply that once Alpha knows its own budget, it either collapses back into omniscience about its failures or violates a minimal norm of rational belief formation.

Please draft the exam question that presents this Alpha case and asks students to determine whether the budget model genuinely resolves logical omniscience or merely relocates the problem. The question must require them to address: (i) why logical omniscience is structurally damaging to formal epistemic logic; (ii) whether a syntactic step-limit can be justified on genuinely epistemic grounds rather than purely pragmatic or computational ones; and (iii) how Alpha's state bears on the distinction between explicit and implicit knowledge. Along with the question, provide a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific conceptual moves the best responses must make—such as identifying the self-reference problem when Alpha knows its own limit—and the specific errors that should cost marks, such as treating the step-limit as only a practical constraint without addressing its epistemic status. The exam is 90 minutes; students should not provide bibliographies, citations, or references to particular texts, arguing solely from conceptual analysis. Keep your full response—question, model answer, and marking scheme—within 1,100 words.
