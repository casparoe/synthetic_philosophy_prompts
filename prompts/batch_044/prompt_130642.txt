Please argue against the view that large-scale political and institutional history is strongly path-dependent in the economic sense—where an early, possibly arbitrary advantage becomes self-reinforcing and irreversibly locks in a specific outcome. Instead, defend a position of contingent canalization: early critical junctures do not fix particular institutional forms, but they constrain the space of viable later developments so that different paths converge on functionally equivalent regimes through structurally distinct routes. Take a definite, committed stance on whether, within this framework, we should conclude that the same macro-level outcomes "would have arrived anyway" (merely delayed, rerouted, or reshuffled), or whether canalization still permits genuinely open-ended variation within its bounds. Develop your reasoning explicitly rather than surveying options neutrally; choose one interpretation and show why it follows from the logic of contingent canalization.
