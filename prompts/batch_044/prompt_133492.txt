Consider the Able Archer 83 crisis of November 1983. The salient facts are these: NATO conducted a large-scale command-post exercise simulating nuclear-release procedures, including realistic movement of forces, use of encrypted communications, and a simulated progression through alert levels up to DEFCON 1; Soviet intelligence detected these activities and interpreted them as possible cover for an actual first strike; in response, Soviet nuclear forces were placed on heightened alert, including preparations for rapid deployment; the crisis de-escalated partly because a Soviet double agent embedded in Western intelligence—Oleg Gordievsky—had warned Western officials of Moscow’s mounting paranoia, and partly because Soviet leadership ultimately chose caution over preemptive action. There was no direct public back-channel comparable to the Cuban Missile Crisis hotline exchanges.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Thomas Schelling’s theory of brinkmanship—specifically his analysis of “the threat that leaves something to chance” and the linked “stability-instability paradox”—but inject and apply the following conceptual refinement: call it the simulation paradox in deterrence. The paradox is that credible brinkmanship depends on actions that genuinely risk unintended escalation, yet exercises are deliberately calibrated to avoid real risk. Consequently, a simulation can be read either as a harmless rehearsal or as a masked first-strike preparation, and the very features that make an exercise safe also destroy the costly-signaling properties that would allow an adversary to distinguish rehearsal from attack. Use this framing to examine how Soviet belief-formation worked during Able Archer.
