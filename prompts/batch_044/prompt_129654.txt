Consider the standard debate about advance directives, where philosophers often oppose honoring an earlier refusal of life-sustaining treatment against overriding it for the patient's current welfare by framing the conflict as one between precedent autonomy and present best interests. Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises in this domain but does not map neatly onto that framing—specifically, a scenario in which the earlier directive and the later patient's preferences are not in direct opposition regarding the same medical intervention, yet honoring or disregarding the directive still raises a distinct normative problem. The scenario should involve a rational, non-pathological transformation in the patient's values—such as a fundamental shift in what counts as a dignified life rather than a simple reversal about a particular treatment—such that the later self is not merely refusing what the earlier self accepted, but operating within an altogether different evaluative framework. Describe the case concretely and identify the precise conceptual tension at stake, explaining why it cannot be resolved solely by appealing either to continuity of personal identity or to the present patient's welfare.
