So I was in this study group last week with Maya and Leo, both philosophy majors, and they got into it over whether virtue ethics can survive the situationist critique. I mostly just listened but they asked me to referee, and I honestly can't tell who's landing punches and who's just dodging. Maya kicked things off by citing those classic situationist experiments—like the good-samaritan study where people in a rush walk right past someone in need—saying they prove character traits don't have the cross-situational stability that Aristotelian virtue needs. She said if hexis is real, then knowing someone is courageous should let you predict they'd speak up in a meeting and also stand up to a bully on the street, but the correlations across those seperate contexts are basically near zero, so virtue ethics is empirically dead.

Leo said that's a strawman. He argued Aristotle never meant virtues are context-blind reflexes—practical wisdom is literally about reading the situation and responding appropriately—so variability is a feature, not a bug. A courageous person might stay quiet in a meeting because speaking up would be reckless, not because they lack courage. Maya shot back that this sounds like an unfalsifiable retreat: if every behavior can be explained away as "wisdom" or "context-sensitivity," then calling someone courageous just redescribes what they did, and virtue ethics becomes circular. You identify the virtue from the action, then cite the virtue to explain the action.

Leo replied that she's mixing up normative ethics with descriptive psychology. Virtue ethics tells us what dispositions to cultivate; it's not trying to be a predictive science. But he also made a descriptive claim: if you measure behavior ecologically over months instead of in one-off lab snapshots, people do show stable "behavioral signatures." He proposed something like an ecological threshold model—traits express consistently only once situational pressure crosses a certain intensity, which is why single experiments miss them. So the situationists are measuring noise, not absence.

Maya wasn't buying it. She said Leo is just relabeling situationism: "traits exist but only show up when the context is right" is exactly the kind of post-hoc rescue that makes virtue ethics immune to evidence. She asked whether he can name any specific pattern that would count against his view. Leo said sure—if people showed literally zero correlation between, say, helping a stranger and donating anonymously across hundreds of observations with no moderating variables, that'd be real trouble. But he thinks we don't see that, and Maya's circularity charge only sticks if you pretend virtue ethics has to function like a personality quiz.

They turned to me and asked who's right at each step. Can you go through thier exchange round by round—Maya's opening, Leo's first reply, Maya's circularity charge, Leo's normative/descriptive split plus the threshold idea, Maya's post-hoc rescue objection, and Leo's falsifiability answer—and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief reasons? Then say who is more right overall, and what both are missing that actually matters for whether virtue ethics is salvageable. Don't just summarize; I want the adjudication to engage with the arguments exactly as they made them.
