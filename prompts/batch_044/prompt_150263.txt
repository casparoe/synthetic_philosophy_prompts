I'm on the committee drafting this year's philosophy PhD comprehensive exam, and after a long discussion with a colleague who writes about multi-agent alignment and contractualist ethics, I want a question that forces candidates to do more than recite the standard aggregation objection to Scanlon. The exam should ask students to work through a rarely emphasized crux: whether the aggregation problem in contractualism is fundamentally a failure of summing comparable harms, or whether it runs deeper—whether Scanlon's pairwise justification structure breaks down when affected parties present structurally heterogeneous complaints that resist translation onto any common metric, such as one party suffering bodily deprivation while another suffers a dignity-based exclusion that is not reducible to welfare loss. Candidates should be asked to evaluate whether the reasonable-rejection criterion can generate a single principle in such cases without either collapsing into utilitarian aggregation or allowing every principle to be vetoed by some party with an incommensurable grievance, and whether this reveals a boundary in contractualism's account of wrongness rather than merely a practical difficulty in applying it.

Please design one 90-minute essay question worth 25 marks for a closed-book exam at the PhD level. Include a model answer that identifies the five essential philosophical moves a top response must make—each tied to the architecture of pairwise justification, the incommensurability challenge, and the distinction between practical aggregation failure and structural justification failure—along with a marking scheme that assigns specific marks to each move totaling 25, plus a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs candidates points on each item. The difficulty must come from the conceptual crux, not from obscure references: a student who has only memorized textbook labels should be unable to answer well, but a student who genuinely understands how individual reasonableness operates should be able to reason through the heterogeneous-complaint scenario on the fly. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or page references.
