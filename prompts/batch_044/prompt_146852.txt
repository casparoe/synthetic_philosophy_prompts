In Hegel scholarship on war there is a persistent interpretive deadlock that I have watched stall even sympathetic interlocutors. The dispute concerns the status of war in the Philosophy of Right: specifically, whether Hegel treats war as an ethically necessary expression of state sovereignty—where the readiness for death preserves the state against the particularism of civil society and secures the recognition of individuality—or as a structurally tragic limitation that exposes the inability of ethical life (Sittlichkeit) to resolve its contradictions internally and forces an appeal to world-historical judgment. Both sides cite the same architecture of the text—the distinction between internal and external sovereignty, the figure of the soldier's sacrifice, and the claim that war preserves the ethical health of the state—yet they read these elements in opposite directions: one sees an ethical justification embedded in the logic of the state as a concrete universal, the other sees a phenomenological description of an unavoidable breakdown that ethical life cannot normatively endorse.

The debate has persisted for decades without convergence. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are the two camps employing different conceptions of ethical justification—one treating sovereignty as constitutive of ethical life, the other treating ethical life as normative only where it avoids coercive violence—such that they are not disagreeing about the text but about the criteria for ethical evaluation? Or is there a hidden formal question about how Hegel's concept of individuality (Einzelheit) relates to particularity (Besonderheit) in the international sphere that neither side has articulated precisely, leaving them to talk past one another? Could it be that one side is answering a question about the internal coherence of Sittlichkeit, while the other is answering a question about the moral limits of state action, with both using the single heading Hegel on war? And if so, is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed—demanding that a logical account of sovereignty simultaneously serve as a moral justification—or merely that the relevant distinctions have not been drawn sharply enough?

Given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress. Would a formal clarification of the relation between internal sovereignty and external sovereignty in Hegel's logic of the concept resolve the stalemate? Would an empirical or historical comparison—say, with modern constitutional states that have abolished conscription—function as a test case? Or should we conclude that the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about whether violence can ever be constitutive of ethical life, such that no argument internal to Hegel interpretation should be expected to move either side?
