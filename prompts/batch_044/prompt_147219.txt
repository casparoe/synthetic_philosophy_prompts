In moral and political philosophy, international foreign aid is often analogized to domestic welfare redistribution: wealthy nations are cast as affluent taxpayers, impoverished nations as disadvantaged co-citizens, and national borders as administrative districts rather than moral boundaries. The analogy is typically used to license the claim that justice requires institutionalized, obligatory transfers rather than discretionary charity. Having spent my career studying open thermodynamic and ecological systems, I am struck by how this framing quietly assumes systemic closure, a shared currency of entitlement, and a central authority capable of enforcing reciprocity—conditions that do not obtain globally. Could you provide an explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the taxpayer, the welfare institution, the social contract, and the currency of obligation?), then distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the normative conclusion from those that are harmless? In particular, does the absence of closure and centralized enforcement defeat the analogy's ability to ground robust aid duties, or might an open-system model—say, energy or resource flow across boundaries without a governing center—serve as a more apt analogical base? Finally, if the domestic-redistribution analogy collapses on these structural points, does any analogical argument for strong obligations of aid survive, or was the conclusion carrying the analogy all along?
