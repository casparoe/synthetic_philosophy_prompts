A colleague in environmental ethics summarized the scale argument about insect sentience for me like this: "Because there are roughly ten quintillion insects alive at any given moment, even a very low probability of sentience—say, one percent—yields an expected population of about one hundred quadrillion sentient beings. On any framework that aggregates moral value linearly across individuals, the total welfare at stake for insects therefore dwarfs the welfare of all vertebrates combined. This means that, unless you can drive the probability of insect sentience essentially to zero, consistency requires treating insect welfare as the dominant moral priority, outweighing most conventional human and animal concerns." Something about the leap from "vast expected numbers" to "dominant moral priority" feels too quick to me, especially since I have heard philosophers distinguish between counting individuals and weighing capacities. How fair is this summary? Please go through the argument claim by claim: which parts capture the actual scale argument used in discussions of insect sentience, which parts conflate it with stronger aggregation principles that its defenders do not necessarily accept, and whether the conclusion about moral priority actually follows from the corrected premises. I am especially unsure whether the "one percent" framing is a standard formulation or a rhetorical device, and whether linear aggregation across species is part of the argument or an unstated assumption being smuggled in.
