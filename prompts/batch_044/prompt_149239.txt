I was discussing Cantor’s distinction between actual and potential infinity with a fellow student who had just read a long-form popular essay on the philosophy of mathematics. She summarized the view like this: “Cantor’s great achievement was showing that infinity isn’t a single thing—there are different sizes, like ℵ₀ and ℵ₁, established by arguments like the power-set proof. Hilbert’s hotel captures the defining trait of an actual infinity: a proper subset can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the whole, which violates Euclid’s old axiom that the whole is greater than the part. This is only possible because Cantor treated infinite collections as completed totalities rather than as open-ended processes. Finitists reject this move, but not because they deny mathematics can speak about very large numbers; rather, they hold that any legitimate collection must be constructible in finitely many steps, so they accept potential infinity—the idea that you can always keep counting—but deny that an infinite totality can ever exist as one object. That is why Hilbert’s hotel strikes them not as a false description of rooms but as a category error: it treats an unfinished process of assignment as if it were a finished building with a definite cardinality. So finitism is best understood as the claim that mathematics should deal only with objects for which a verification procedure can, in principle, be completed, which makes Cantor’s transfinite arithmetic not false but literally without meaning.”
