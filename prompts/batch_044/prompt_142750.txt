Question: Timothy Williamson argues that mental conditions such as feeling cold are not luminous: it is not true that whenever one is in such a state, one is in a position to know that one is in it. He uses gradual-change cases to challenge versions of the KK principle (if S knows p, then S knows that S knows p) applied to mental-state knowledge. (a) Reconstruct the argument from imperceptible incremental change, stating the reliability condition on self-knowledge explicitly. (b) Now consider a restricted principle, call it K-art: if S possesses explicit, conceptually articulated perceptual knowledge that p, then S knows that S knows p. Would Williamson's anti-luminosity reasoning defeat K-art? Answer by examining whether reliability conditions apply at the meta-level of articulated judgment.

Student answer: Williamson starts from the fact that feeling cold varies continuously. If knowing one's mental state requires being able to discriminate it from close alternatives, then in case C₀ where S knows she feels cold, she should also know it in C₁, where the temperature is imperceptibly higher. Repeating this yields a case where S feels cold but lacks the discriminative reliability to know it, so luminosity fails. This undermines KK for mental states because KK requires that first-order knowledge be accompanied by second-order knowledge, and without luminosity there is no bridge between the two levels.

Regarding K-art, the argument fails. Explicit conceptual articulation introduces a discrete threshold: either S can verbally defend the judgment or she cannot, with no imperceptible gradations in conceptual competence. Therefore the gradual-change premise cannot be applied. The reliability conditions constrain first-order phenomenal awareness, but once articulation is achieved, the meta-level is shielded from anti-luminosity concerns by the binary nature of conceptual possession. Hence K-art survives.
