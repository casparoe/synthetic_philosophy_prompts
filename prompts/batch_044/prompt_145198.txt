I am writing this after a long discussion with my study group in our upper-level seminar on medieval political theology, where we have been examining Aquinas’s account of tyrannicide and sedition in the Summa Theologiae. We focused on the part of his treatise on justice—specifically his discussion of whether overthrowing a ruler is ever permissible—where he distinguishes between a usurper who seizes power without legal title and a legitimate ruler who subsequently becomes tyrannical. In that section, Aquinas reasons that a private individual must never take it upon himself to kill a tyrant, both because judging the ruler exceeds private authority and because such acts tend to produce worse disorder; he allows that a public authority may remove a usurper, and that a community whose lawful ruler has become oppressive should first seek correction through established legal channels, turning to direct action only when all institutional remedies are exhausted and even then at grave risk of chaos.

The reasoning that confuses me concerns the precise interaction between these two conditions—the absence of legal remedy and the danger of disorder—because it is not clear whether the prohibition on private action collapses once the institutional framework itself has been destroyed, or whether the moral duty to avoid chaos remains binding regardless. To frame the problem, my professor introduced the idea of a residual authority problem, in which every hierarchical layer capable of judging the ruler has been captured or dissolved, leaving the moral weight of obedience unassigned but not annulled. Does Aquinas treat the risk of disorder as a contingent, consequentialist caution that weakens when institutional failure makes chaos inevitable anyway, or is it a structural, deontological feature of political order that persists even when no authority remains to enforce it? I want to understand whether, on his view, the breakdown of all legal channels transforms direct resistance into a permissible last resort, or whether it instead deepens the tragedy by removing both the right and the remedy simultaneously.
