I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the scientific study of consciousness, and our instructor has asked me to deliver the opening remarks for the department's annual workshop on first-person methods, which brings together neuroscientists, clinical psychologists, and philosophers of mind. The audience is sharply divided: half will be skeptical that subjective reports can be treated as texts without dissolving clinical trust, and the other half will worry that any interpretive framework erases the very phenomenon it aims to study.

I want the remarks to center on heterophenomenology, but specifically through the lens of what I call interpretive debt — the reciprocal obligation that arises when an interpreter renders a subject's report into a third-person narrative that must then be offered back for recognition, revision, or refusal. This framing should do structural work: it needs to reassure clinicians that patient testimony survives interpretation, persuade researchers that the method remains empirically disciplined, and avoid conceding to philosophers any direct, unmediated access to experience that heterophenomenology denies. Please compose the remarks as exactly five sequential movements, each consisting of one or two sentences, arranged without a separate introduction or conclusion, so that the idea of interpretive debt shapes what each movement can and cannot claim.
