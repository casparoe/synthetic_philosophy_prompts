I've been trying to get my head around disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, and there's a move in these debates I keep running into but can't find a label for. You start with a clean case — straight stick in water, looks bent, but it's actually straight. The disjunctivist says good perception is direct contact with the world, bad case is a totally different mental kind with no real object involved. Fine. But then someone brings up what I'd call a "leaky" middle case: the stick really is there, but because of some weird retinal glitch or lighting trick, the subject reports seeing two sticks — one bent, one straight — and swears both feel "present" in the same direct way. The critic says this proves there's a shared representational layer that can carry error even when the object exists. The disjunctivist has to call it a hybrid or deny it's perception at all. The thing is, once you admit one hybrid, you seem to open the door to a whole gradient — partial awareness, noisy contact, degraded directness — and the sharp boundary the disjunctivist wanted starts to look artificial. Is there an established name for this specific dialectical pattern, where conceding one borderline case forces you into accepting a sliding scale of states? And is there a standard taxonomy for these "leaky" cases where an object is present but the experience includes properties that don't match it, or do people just fold them into ordinary illusion talk? I'd be happy with a coined term if there's genuinely nothing in the literature, but I want to know exactly what distinguishes this pressure from the usual problem of drawing lines in sorites-style arguments, and whether this occurance has been discussed under some other heading I haven't seen.
