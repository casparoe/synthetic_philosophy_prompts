I cover science and philosophy of medicine for a broadsheet, and I am drafting a feature that sets out my own working position on disease classification. Before I file, I want a consistency audit rather than a lecture. Here is the package I have settled on:

(a) For bodily conditions I am a biostatistical naturalist: a state counts as disease when an internal mechanism fails to perform the function for which evolutionary selection designed it, irrespective of whether the bearer suffers or is socially disadvantaged.

(b) For mental and behavioural conditions, however, I take social disablement to be necessary: without impaired functioning in social roles, there is no disorder, even if a neural mechanism is operating outside its typical range.

(c) Health is nothing more than the absence of disease; I reject the World Health Organisation's classic formulation—which treats health as complete physical, mental and social well-being—as normatively bloated and clinically useless.

(d) I accept the evolutionary-mismatch account of many modern chronic conditions, but I hold that a mismatch should be classified as disease only when it produces subjective suffering or measurable disability, not when it is merely a statistical deviation from an ancestral baseline.

(e) Consequently, asymptomatic carriers of genetic variants—such as those with sickle-cell trait—are not diseased, despite departing from the species-typical functional design.

Please tell me which of these hang together, which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to do so. Rank the tensions by severity, state the least costly revision for each, and say which single change would resolve the most conflicts. Do not treat all tensions as equally damaging, and do not tell me which commitment I should drop—only what each option costs.
