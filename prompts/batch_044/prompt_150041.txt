I've been teaching Pascal this semester and I keep comeing back to that bit in the Pensees where he says justice without force is powerless, but force without justice is tyrannical — you know the one. Most of my students read it as the usual pragmatic pairing: justice sets the norms, force backs them up. But I think there's a stronger, weirder reading hiding in there. What if Pascal isn't just saying force enforces justice, but that the very content of justice in a given society is constituted by whatever the dominant force happens to stabilize? Not just that the strong make the rules, but that 'just' just names the equilibrium point of coercive power. Call it the constitutive-force reading.

So here's my question: I hold this constitutive-force reading of Pascal. What would actually count against it? Not just the obvious objection that Pascal clearly distinguishes justice and force conceptually — I want the deeper defeaters. Which themes or arguments in the Pensees or in the Provincial Letters does this reading have to strain to accommodate? What about the theological layer — does the idea that divine justice is independent of human power structures blow up the reading, or can the constitutive claim be restricted to human political justice? And are there results in other areas — say, about institutional legitimacy or about the nature of law — that this Pascalian view predicts differently from a Hobbesian or a modern legal-positivist account? Rank them: fatal, awkward, or dismissible. Also, be honest: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If I keep retreating to 'it's only about human justice,' does that save the reading or just hollow it out?
