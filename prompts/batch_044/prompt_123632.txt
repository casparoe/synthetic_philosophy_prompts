I am currently auditing a graduate seminar on practical reasoning, and a debate in our last session has left me genuinely unsettled about a personal decision I face. I have been offered two year-long research fellowships—one housed in the East Wing of a well-known interdisciplinary institute and one in the West Wing. Every quantifiable parameter is identical: the stipend, the health benefits, the square footage of the assigned office, the publication expectations, the mentorship roster, and even the walking distance to the nearest library branch. Over the past two months I have constructed increasingly baroque spreadsheets to capture indirect differences—ambient noise levels, the direction of morning light, the median age of current fellows—and each comparison cancels out precisely. I have no preference, yet I feel a strange obligation to possess one. A fellow student insists that rationality permits, perhaps even demands, that I break the tie by an arbitrary mechanism—flip a coin, draw lots—because the reason for my choice is constituted by the act itself rather than preceding it. Another acquaintance argues that resorting to chance is an abdication of agency, and that I must keep excavating hidden values until a genuine, non-arbitrary reason finally emerges. I am stuck not because the options differ, but because the symmetry seems to attack the very idea of what a reason for choosing could be.

What troubles me most is a further complication that neither of my interlocutors addressed. I know that, whichever fellowship I accept, I will spend a year cultivating relationships, developing projects, and adapting my habits to that specific environment. By the end of the year, the path not taken will almost certainly seem to me not merely different but clearly inferior—my attachments will have retroactively manufactured the asymmetry that was absent at the start. So I wonder whether my paralysis is actually a rational response to the fact that these two options are only temporarily identical, and that choosing one actively creates the reason for having chosen it. Is it a failure of reason to lack a preference when all objective reasons are genuinely exhausted, or is it a failure of reason to invent a preference in order to avoid the discomfort of arbitrary choice? And if the self I become after choosing is partly constructed by that very entry into one path, does this kind of deadlock dissolve once we recognize that the chooser is not a fixed point standing outside the options, but something shaped by entering one of them?
