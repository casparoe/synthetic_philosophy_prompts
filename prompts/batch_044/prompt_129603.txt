Hey, I'm stuck on this argument my partener cooked up about Newton's bucket and I think it accidentally kills both sides of the spacetime debate. Can you help me find the exit?

1. If spacetime is purely relational, rotation is just bodies moving relative to each other.
2. Newton's bucket shows the water climbs when it spins, apparently relative to distant stars or something.
3. So either the distant stars causally pull the water up (Mach's idea), or there's absolute space.
4. If it's Mach, then spinning the entire universe around a still bucket should also make the water climb.
5. We can't empiricly rule that out.
6. So relationism either sneaks absolute space back in, or it says rotating the bucket and rotating the cosmos are physically identical.
7. If they're identical, there's no real fact about which thing is rotating.
8. Generalize: every velocity is symmetric between A and B.
9. Then inertial frames are just bookkeeping, not physically real.
10. But GR's geodesic equation picks out real curvature that distinguishes free fall from acceleration.
11. If frames are just bookkeeping, curvature is a coordinate artifact with no physical bite.
12. So relationism makes GR incoherent, which is too high a price. Reject relationism.
13. Okay, so substantivalism is true: spacetime points exist on their own.
14. That means an empty region with no matter is metaphysically possible.
15. But Einstein's hole argument shows that without matter, spacetime points have no physical identity.
16. So substantivalism plus the hole argument implies empty regions are not just unobservable but metaphysically indeterminate.
17. That violates the principle of sufficient reason: there'd be missing facts about what's there.
18. So substantivalism also collapses.
19. But these are the only two options.
20. Therefore there's no consistent philosophy of space and time.

I feel like step 11 or step 16 is where the sleight of hand is, but I'm not sure which. I'd rather keep GR being meaningful, but I'm also not super attached to the sufficient-reason principle. Which premise do I actually have to give up, and what does giving it up cost? Walk me through it without just saying "both sides are wrong" — I need to know where the argument cheats so I can keep one view intact.
