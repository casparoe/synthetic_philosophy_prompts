I've been running a small philosophy reading group and we're trying to settle our programme for next term — about ten sessions, no empirical budget, just conceptual work. We've been looking at Wittgenstein's language games and family resemblance, but specifically applied to artificial language use, and we're stuck on where to actually spend our energy. There's the standard question of whether an LLM is playing a language game without a form of life, but that feels a bit tired. So we've come up with four slightly more specific lines: (a) whether family resemblance can be formalised just enough to distinguish statistical pattern-matching from genuine language use without falling back into essentialism; (b) if the private language argument has any real bite against models that build internal representations we can't interpret; (c) whether relaxing the 'form of life' condition destroys the whole framework or just updates it for synthetic agents; and (d) a newer angle about cross-modal family resemblance — whether vision-language systems share a family resemblance cluster with human multimodal practices or form an entirely seperate kind of game.

We can't do all four justice. So I'm asking: if you had to advise our group on which two or three deserve the bulk of our time, which ones are upstream of the others? I'm not after a survey — I want a defended agenda. Which open questions, if cleared up, would truely change how philosophers talk about meaning in synthetic systems, and which are overrated distractions that sound edgy but don't shift anything? Also, does answering (a) make (c) easier, or are they pulling in different directions? I keep mixing up whether 'language-game' needs a hyphen, but that's not the point — the point is I don't want us to pick questions just because they sound niche. Give me the ranking with reasons, and flag where we might be helping ourselves to inconsistent motivations.
