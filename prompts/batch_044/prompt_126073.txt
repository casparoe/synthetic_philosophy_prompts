I run a philosophy seminar, 12 grad students, 90 minutes. I want a classroom activty — not a lecture — that gets them to feel Arendt's specific point about political lying: not just "politicians lie" but the modern thing where you don't deny a fact outright, you dissolve its shared meaning by substituting a coherent narrative for reality, so people stop being able to coordinate not over values but over what world they're in.

Design the game or role-play. I need three roles: Administration (3 people), Press (3 people), and Citizens (6 people split into two neighborhoods). Materials should just be index cards and chairs, no tech or money. Give everyone "hard fact" cards — contamination levels, machine logs, etc. The Admin's job is to maintain stability; they can issue official narratives each round, but they are never allowed to say something literally false. They have to use recontextualization, substituting causes, or turning facts into interpretations. The Press gets three verification tokens each; they can spend one to check whether a specific fact in the narrative has been dissolved rather than just framed. Citizens start with 5 shared-reality points and must reach unanimous agreement to take collective action (evacuate, protest, vote) each round. The decision requires agreement only among citizens who still have points. If you hit zero you're "disoriented" — move your chair away from the table, you can't vote, but you still hear everything. Run 4 rounds. Each round Admin uses 2 fact-cards and 1 reframing move. If Press exposes a dissolution, citizens in that neighborhood regain a point; if not, affected citizens lose one point. Unused Press tokens vanish after each round — they don't accumulate.

The idea should emerge from what happens, not from me explaining it up front. Then a debrief that maps round 1-2 (feels like strategic deception, people think there's a hidden truth) vs round 3-4 (truth isn't hidden, it's been made irrelevant) onto Arendt's distinction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — e.g., Admin tries to flat-out deny a fact, Press hoards tokens, Citizens break character and meta-discuss — and exactly how to handle it. Also be honest: what's the feature of Arendt's view that 90 minutes can't reproduce, and how should I supply it verbally in the last 10 minutes? Don't give me a bibliography or reading list; I'm not asking for citations. Just make sure the activity doesn't depend on students knowing obscure texts beforehand — the citizens recieve their roles and rules cold, and the force of the idea comes from playing.

Make sure the rules are clear enough that I can run it without you being there.
