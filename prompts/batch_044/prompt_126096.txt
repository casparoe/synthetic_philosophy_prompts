In my philosophy-of-mind discussion group we have spent months going in circles about whether moral status tracks sentience on a continuous biological gradient—running from insects through nematodes to thermostats, with each step carrying proportionally less weight—or whether it is a binary switch that activates only when a unified phenomenal field is present. The scalarists cite integrated information, homeostatic regulation, and behavioral flexibility as evidence for a smooth ontology with no principled cutoff, which would seem to grant a thermostat a vanishing but nonzero sliver of considerability; the thresholdists reply that without a singular “what-it-is-like” perspective there are literally no interests to violate, so attributing even minimal status to a thermostat is conceptually incoherent rather than empirically false. Despite broad agreement on the neuroscience and on the wrongness of suffering, neither camp has conceded ground, and my working hypothesis is that the dispute is not about consciousness per se but about whether “sentience” functions as an ontological description of phenomenal structure or as a normative attribution condition that licenses moral concern.
