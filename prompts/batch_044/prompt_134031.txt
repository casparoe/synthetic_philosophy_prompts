The divergence must arise from the internal structure of the case rather than from stipulation: the covering-law account should invoke an optimal, genuinely predictive generalization about collective behavior under economic constraint, correctly accounting for the outbreak, but should leave entirely untouched the specific question that the participants themselves ask—why this particular sequence of recognitions and betrayals unfolded in this order. The narrative account should answer that question by configuring the actions into a meaningful whole with beginning, middle, and retrospective closure, yet should be incapable of supporting any counterfactual about what would have happened had conditions varied, and should contain no disguised law or statistical regularity. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why; isolate the structural feature—for instance, an agent’s later reinterpretation of an earlier commitment—that makes the narrative explanation relevant despite the law’s predictive success; demonstrate what happens to both explanations if that feature is excised; and state how a defender of the covering-law model would attack the narrative account as mere redescription rather than explanation.
