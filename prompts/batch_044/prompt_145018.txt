I'd like to apply this framework to a well-known empirical episode: David Rosenhan's pseudopatient study from the early 1970s, in which healthy individuals gained admission to psychiatric hospitals by reporting a single fabricated symptom and were then detained for weeks despite behaving normally.

What I want is a disciplined, bidirectional analysis. First, using Szasz's distinction between genuine medical diseases and metaphorical "illnesses," explain what the study's outcomes—systematic misdiagnosis, prolonged detention, and staff inability to detect sanity—actually demonstrate. Do they show that psychiatrists are poor at applying a valid medical taxonomy, or do they show, as Szasz might claim, that the taxonomy itself functions as a normative judgment about social deviance rather than as an empirical discovery? Second, examine a tension in the study's design: Rosenhan framed his project as a test of diagnostic reliability, which seems to presuppose that there is a correct medical classification that clinicians are failing to apply. From Szasz's perspective, does this framing commit the study to the very medical model it is often taken to undermine? If so, does the episode weaken Szasz's position—by revealing that even critics of psychiatry rely on its conceptual framework—or strengthen it, by showing that the framework is so entrenched that dissent cannot be articulated without it? Third, assess whether Szasz's account can explain the specific institutional harms experienced by the pseudopatients—being ignored, medicated, and stripped of autonomy—without treating those harms as mere failures of medical science. Finally, conclude with what this case reveals about the limits of Szasz's myth thesis: are there features of psychiatric practice that his framework illuminates but that the Rosenhan study itself obscures, and vice versa? Please do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I'm working only with the conceptual mapping.
