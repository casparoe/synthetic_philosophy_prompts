I am a retired physicist who has recently begun serious study in moral philosophy. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a small interdisciplinary workshop on obligation and systemic harm: half the audience are moral philosophers well acquainted with Peter Singer's drowning-child argument, and half are climate scientists and family members with no philosophical background. Please write a brief address—about five minutes—that takes Singer's pond analogy not as a simple prompt to charity but as structural scaffolding for a definite, committed argument: specifically, whether the concept of "rescue" remains coherent when harm is mediated by institutional structures rather than by direct physical proximity, or whether the analogy collapses and requires an altogether different moral framework. The speech must be rigorous enough that experts recognize the move, yet fully accessible to non-philosophers; it must land on one side with clear reasons, not survey options and retreat. Please include no citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts—the reasoning should stand alone for a listener without books at hand. Finally, append a brief note indicating where your framing takes liberties with Singer's original argument or relies on contested interpretations of the duty to rescue.
