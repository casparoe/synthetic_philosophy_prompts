Please write the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes, or about 1,200 words—for a one-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Revising the Present." The audience is split between philosophers of mind and cognitive neuroscientists, who will recognize technical distinctions, and artists, filmmakers, and archivists, who will not. The workshop's organizing tension is the Orwellian versus Stalinesque interpretation of the color phi phenomenon: the Orwellian view holds that conscious experience retrospectively revises its own immediate past to construct a seamless present, as if rewriting historical records after the fact; the Stalinesque view holds that the brain fills in missing perceptual frames post-experience, leaving the present moment intact but fabricating a false record of what just occurred. The speaker is the workshop's organizer, not a specialist in either field, and needs to frame the day's program—pairing neuroscience talks on temporal perception with an installation about reconstructed eyewitness memory—without resolving the debate or using jargon that excludes half the room.

The speech must do three things structurally. First, it should open with a brief, specific personal anecdote about misremembering the color of a vehicle during a near-accident—an anecdote that initially sounds like ordinary false memory but gradually reveals the deeper ambiguity: was the color corrected in the moment of perception, or inserted afterward? Second, the speaker must explain, in plain language but with one precisely formulated conceptual point, why this ambiguity matters for both the scientists and the artists: it determines whether we treat consciousness as an author of its own history or as a reader of a history that was never fully written. Third, the remarks should set explicit ground rules for the day—that no speaker may invoke "the brain does X" without specifying whether X is happening in the present or being reconstructed in retrospect—thereby making the philosophical distinction do real organizational work rather than serving as decoration. The tone should be formal but warm, and the speaker should acknowledge that neither interpretation offers comfort to anyone hoping for a transparent, immediate access to reality.
