Two friends of mine, Jules and Sam, got into it last weekend over whether dropping a freelance columnist from three indie news outlets counts as punishment. The columnist had written that meritocracy is a useful fiction we should maintain even if it's false, and the outlets said they "part ways" with her, which sounds corporate but teh intent was clear -- she lost gigs, her name got dragged, and the editors tweeted about "accountability." Jules said that's clearly punishment: she suffered a tangible loss because of a wrong she allegedly did, and the outlets meant to hurt her career so others wouldn't do the same. Sam said that's wrong -- punishment needs an authority with rules, like a state or an employer with a contract. These outlets have no authority over her; they just ended voluntary associations. Calling it punishment is a category error that makes people demand due process where it doesn't belong.

Jules came back: but boycotts and Amish shunning are punitive even without state authority. The harm is intentional and it's meant to make her "pay" for violating a norm. That's enough. Sam replied that then every unfollow is punishment, which is absurd. The key is punishment imposes a burden that wasn't there before; here they just stopped giving her a microphone she never had a right to. Jules said she had built an expectation over years -- it's a deprivation of an established benefit, not withholding a new gift. And she brought up this evolutionary game-theory thing: distributed sanctions reduce the defector's payoff exactly like punishment does, so the structure is identical even without a central enforcer. Sam said that proves it's a sanction, not punishment. Punishment has a normative structure: it's done by an authority in the name of the community. Without that it's just social selection. And if we blur the line, we can't tell a mob from a court.

Jules answered: you're assuming there's no punishment without authority. But courts are just one form. The real question is whether the moral conditions apply -- wrongness, intent to harm, proportionality. Here they don't check proportionality, which is exactly why it's bad punishment, not non-punishment. Sam said you're assuming the conclusion again -- if there's no authority, there's no punishment to evaluate for proportionality. It's just people making choices about who they work with.
