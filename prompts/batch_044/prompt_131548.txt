I'm finalizing the examination for my upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and after discussing the syllabus with a colleague I realized that every previous final has allowed students to pass the Twin Earth unit by simply recounting Putnam's water scenario without actually grasping the machinery of semantic externalism. I want to avoid that this year by using a less familiar kind-term case that forces them to work through the argument in real time. Rather than H2O and XYZ, the prompt should present a modified scenario: on Earth, speakers apply the term "jade" to objects that share a cluster of macro-properties—color, hardness, translucency—while experts are genuinely divided over whether the underlying minerals, jadeite and nephrite, constitute one natural kind or two; on Twin Earth, identical macro-properties obtain, but the underlying chemical structures are entirely different minerals matching neither Earth compound, and the local division of linguistic labor is structured around unanimous expert agreement that there is exactly one kind. The exam question should ask students to determine whether Earth and Twin Earth speakers share the same reference for "jade," whether the expert disagreement on Earth undermines the externalist claim that reference is fixed by actual environmental kinds, and whether an internalist descriptivist account can accommodate the case without committing ordinary speakers to massive reference failure.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 700 words calibrated for a 45-minute response within a 90-minute closed-book exam, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The scheme must separate an A-level response from a B-level response by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain—particularly regarding the interaction between the division of linguistic labor and ontological pluralism among experts—and it should list the precise misconceptions that should cost marks, such as treating expert disagreement as an automatic refutation of externalism, conflating semantic externalism with a causal theory of reference, or assuming that macro-property identity is sufficient for meaning identity. Because students cannot search online or access texts, do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; the question should be answerable entirely through conceptual analysis of the scenario provided.
