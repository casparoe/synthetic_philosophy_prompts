In archaeological pedagogy, museum curation, and environmental policy briefs, there is a durable institutional practice of grouping the Late Bronze Age crisis circa 1177 BCE, the Classic Maya political fragmentation in the ninth century, and the ecological degradation of Rapa Nui into a single category of societal collapse. These three cases are routinely presented together—as in university syllabi, heritage narratives, and sustainability reports—as paradigmatic warnings about institutional overreach and ecological overextraction. By contrast, societies that endured comparable stress but reorganized without maintaining large-scale monumentality, palace economies, or dense urban networks—such as Postclassic Maya polities or smaller-scale Levantine communities after the Bronze Age—are typically narrated not as collapsed societies but as transformed or continuous cultures. What philosophical commitments about the identity conditions of a society does this asymmetrical labeling practice presuppose? Must it assume that a society possesses an essential institutional form—defined perhaps by writing, state bureaucracy, or particular energy regimes—such that the loss of that form constitutes the society's death rather than its metamorphosis?

The framing also appears to embed a normative judgment: collapse is treated not as a neutral descriptive transition but as a failure, implicitly measured against a standard of complexity or organizational scale that the society ought to have preserved. Is that normative loading intrinsic to the concept of collapse, or could one consistently describe the Maya transition or the Bronze Age fragmentation as value-neutral reorganizations—changes in social morphology—while reserving failure-language only for cases involving catastrophic declines in human welfare? Reconstruct the implicit theory of social value, descriptive neutrality, and continuity that renders the canonical trio coherent. Then determine whether that theory strictly requires the exclusion of reorganized societies from the collapse category, or whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, remaining compatible with rival accounts—essentialist, functionalist, or narrative—of what distinguishes a society's end from its alteration. Finally, indicate what shifts in curatorial practice or policy rhetoric would be necessary if we adopted a view of social identity that treated radical institutional discontinuity as fully compatible with societal persistence, rather than as collapse.
