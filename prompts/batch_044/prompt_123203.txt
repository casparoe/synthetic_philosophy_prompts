I am currently drafting the second chapter of my dissertation, which concerns the ontology of normative reasons and whether they can exist independently of any reasoning subject. Last week, during a workshop on contractualism and moral realism, a senior colleague offered a summary of what she called the "structural-realist" position—a view she attributed primarily to Scanlon's framework in What We Owe to Each Other, though she insisted that recent defenders have radicalized it well beyond Scanlon's own intentions. Her gloss ran roughly as follows: normative reasons, on this account, are not mental states, dispositions, or agent-relative responses, but objective structural relations holding between states of affairs—relations such as "minimizes suffering" or "respects autonomy"—that obtain regardless of whether any conscious being registers them; she referred to these as "free-floating rationales." She claimed that Scanlon's decisive contribution was to detach reasons from individual preference by grounding them in irreducibly relational properties between persons, but she added that Scanlon mistakenly retained what she termed a "psychological accessibility" condition—namely, that an agent must be capable of grasping the reason in order for it to bind—whereas contemporary structuralists reject this condition precisely so that reasons might bind even non-cognitive systems or artificial agents lacking practical reasoning capacities altogether. From this, she drew a stronger conclusion: if reasons are genuinely free-floating in this way, then normative ethics collapses into a kind of formal value-geometry. Once the structural relations are correctly mapped, one possesses the full normative landscape, and no supplementary theory of practical reasoning, motivation, or agency is required.

I find the summary philosophically suggestive, but I am concerned that it may conflate Scanlon's actual relationalism—which, as I understand it, remains tied to justifiability to other persons—with a far stronger realist thesis about non-agentive normative structures. I also worry that the claim about dispensing with agency altogether overstates even the radicalized view, since it is unclear how a "reason" could have normative force for a being that lacks any capacity for action or recognition. Before I cite this gloss in my chapter, I would like an accuracy audit: could you work through her summary claim by claim and indicate which parts a careful reader of Scanlon would accept as fair simplifications, which parts involve subtle misrepresentations (for instance, conflating relational justification with structural independence, or overstating the rejection of accessibility conditions), and which parts are simply wrong? In particular, please address whether her conclusion—that normative ethics needs no theory of agency if free-floating rationales exist—actually follows from the premises she assigns to the view, or whether it relies on one of the errors embedded in the summary. I am not asking for a bibliography or further reading suggestions—only for a precise separation of what survives correction.
