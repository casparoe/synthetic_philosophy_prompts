I'm delivering the opening remarks—roughly five to six minutes—at a workshop titled "Counterfactual Freedom and Institutional Design." The audience is mixed: philosophers of religion familiar with Molinism, and applied ethicists who study predictive risk and preemptive intervention but rarely read scholastic theology. Our working premise is deliberately hypothetical: we are treating Molina's distinction among natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge purely as a formal logic of counterfactual dependence, detached from any theological commitment. Specifically, we want to ask whether the idea that there can be true counterfactuals about free choices prior to any actual decision or decree—what Molinists call middle knowledge—offers a useful structural model for secular problems such as algorithmic hiring filters, predictive policing, or preemptive clinical intervention. I need to welcome both groups, explain why an ethicist should take this framework seriously, and set a ground rule for the weekend: the theological question of whether God actually possesses such knowledge is bracketed, but the logical structure of counterfactual freedom is not. The philosophical idea should do real structural work: I cannot present Molinism as settled doctrine, I cannot dismiss it as mere intellectual history, and I must frame the parallel between divine middle knowledge and secular predictive ethics precisely enough that the philosophers recognize the logic while the ethicists feel they are in a normative workshop rather than a theology seminar. Write the remarks so they establish that framing, respect the mixed expertise in the room, and open space for debate about whether counterfactual dependence operates the same way in institutional design as it does in the classical problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom.
