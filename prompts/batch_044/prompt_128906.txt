As a doctoral student in philosophy of language with some background in cognitive science, I am trying to clarify a conceptual tension in critiques of statistical machine translation of the kind Douglas Hofstadter has pressed. Hofstadter argues that statistical systems translate without understanding because they manipulate surface correlations rather than grasp meaning. I want to push on what grasping actually requires here. Suppose we accept an inferentialist picture on which understanding a sentence is partly constituted by mastery of its inferential relations to other sentences within the same language. A statistical model maps distributional patterns across languages, and if those patterns genuinely track inferential roles, then the model may preserve semantic structure despite lacking compositional syntax or intentional directedness. On the other hand, one might insist that translation requires not merely cross-linguistic correlation but an internal representation of both source and target structures—what we might call a genuine interlingua that processes monolingual syntax in each language rather than bypassing it.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake. Specifically, consider a hypothetical system that operates through a pure shared semantic manifold: it aligns high-dimensional vector representations across languages without ever parsing monolingual syntax in either source or target. Does the possibility of accurate translation via such a system show that Hofstadter’s charge of shallowness conflates statistical correlation with absence of semantic access? Or does it instead reveal that his notion of understanding implicitly requires a form of intentional engagement or compositional processing that statistical alignment necessarily lacks? In your answer, clarify whether translation can be conceptually separated from understanding the languages being translated, and what philosophical costs attend each separation—particularly for how we should think about meaning, representation, and the boundaries of cognitive agency.
