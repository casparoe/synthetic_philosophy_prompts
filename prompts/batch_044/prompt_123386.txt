As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I want to test whether Rawls's separateness of persons objection targets cross-personal aggregation itself or a more specific structural feature of moral reasoning. Construct a realistic, single-paragraph thought experiment set in a hospital or clinical research setting—no trolleys, no demons, no exotic physics—in which a utilitarian framework aggregates a non-lethal burden (such as an algorithmically distributed epistemic risk or a procedural burden across trial participants) over many identifiable subjects, and the separateness objection blocks this aggregation on the grounds that persons are not interchangeable locations in a welfare calculus. Engineer the case so that, in the mirror-image version, an equivalent total burden is concentrated on one subject without any cross-personal aggregation, and here the separateness objection permits the action while a contractualist or rights-based framework forbids it—precisely reversing the usual alignment. The harm should be one that a working scientist recognizes immediately, not death or physical injury. After presenting both versions of the scenario, show exactly what the utilitarian, the separateness-objector, and the contractualist each say and why; then identify the precise structural feature—other than mere numbers or total magnitude—that forces this reversal. Finally, take a definite position and defend it with reasons: does this reversal demonstrate that the separateness objection is genuinely about the illegitimacy of treating individuals as fungible parts of a collective pool, or is it tracking something else, such as the absence of individual consent or the non-transferability of particular burdens? Do not survey the options neutrally; commit to one interpretation and explain what it implies for the scope of the objection. Avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to specific texts; if you invoke an argument associated with a named philosopher, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
