I chair the appeals and dispute panel for an interdisciplinary research consortium that publishes open-access monographs in the natural and human sciences. Every quarter we receive formal, peer-reviewed challenges to previously published findings — not allegations of misconduct, but substantive objections: competing models that reproduce the same data, flawed experimental controls, misapplied mathematical formalisms, or newly discovered archival evidence that complicates an established narrative. The original authors respond, often insisting that they have considered the objection and maintain their original conclusion. My three-person panel — composed of senior researchers with scientific or historical expertise but no formal training in philosophy — must decide, within a single forty-five-minute session and with only the challenge document, the author’s reply, and the original publication in front of us, whether to require a published correction, mandate a formal response article, or close the file with no action. The stakes are asymmetric: requiring correction damages professional standing, while closing a file on a valid objection erodes institutional trust.

The philosophical difficulty is what I call the dogmatism paradox: rational belief maintenance demands a certain resistance to revision, since constant updating renders sustained inquiry impossible, yet that very resistance becomes epistemically vicious when it refuses genuine engagement with counter-evidence. To operationalize this tension, I need a reusable decision procedure my panel can run repeatedly without resolving it into a mere feeling. The procedure should open with ordered preliminary tests that classify each objection according to what I term a “depth of incision” framework — surface (a single measurement or data point), structural (the evidentiary architecture or auxiliary assumptions that support the core claim), and foundational (the conceptual framework that makes the claim intelligible in the first place). For each depth, it should specify criteria that distinguish adequate authorial engagement — substantive revision or explicit defense of the targeted layer — from inadequate engagement, such as mere citation of the original study or dismissal on disciplinary grounds. It must include explicit markers for where judgment is irreducibly normative, particularly the step at which we assess whether the author’s steadfastness reflects epistemic virtue or mere dogmatism, and clear stopping rules that yield “require correction,” “mandate response,” or “close file.” The procedure should also flag its own misfire points: cases where a technically adequate response masks conceptual evasion, and cases where persistent reasonable disagreement means no action is warranted despite collective discomfort.

Apply the procedure to three cases. First, an ecologist who maintains a population-collapse prediction despite a competing agent-based model that reproduces her field data within identical confidence intervals; she replies that her mechanistic interpretation remains superior but does not address the structural equivalence of the outputs. Second, a theoretical physicist who defends a specific interpretation of quantum decoherence after a philosophical critique demonstrates that her measurement assumptions conflate operational and ontological claims; she responds that the critique misapplies the formalism but offers no revised account of how the operational-ontological boundary should be drawn. Third, a historian of institutional decline who stands by a causal narrative despite newly digitized archives that complicate — without directly falsifying — the sequence of events she describes; she argues that the new material enriches rather than undermines her framework. At least one of these applications should yield an answer that the affected research community will find unacceptable. Conclude with an explicit statement of what the procedure is powerless to adjudicate — for instance, whether maintaining belief in the face of persistent, well-formed disagreement is ever genuinely rational or merely psychologically inevitable — and identify the precise step where a panel chair must make a values-based call that the procedure itself cannot legitimate.
