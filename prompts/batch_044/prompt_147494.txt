I am a novelist preparing a seminar defense for an interdisciplinary audience at an institute for narrative theory and philosophy of history. My thesis is that historical fiction should abandon what I term retrospective causal stitching—the implicit narrative convention that, once a macro-historical outcome is known, every preceding micro-event can be read backward as possessing deterministic weight. In its place I propose contingent cascade architecture: a plot structure in which small perturbations—unsent letters, missed trains, a farrier’s missing nail—generate genuinely open branches at the moment they occur, and the narrative refuses to collapse those branches into inevitability even after the macro-outcome has arrived. The audience includes structuralist historians who believe macro-demographic and economic forces render micro-variation epiphenomenal; literary critics committed to Aristotelian plot coherence and catharsis through necessary sequence; and one applied mathematician who works on nonlinear dynamics but distrusts humanistic borrowings from chaos theory. I need a prep sheet, not a defense: list the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrase each as that specific interlocutor would phrase it, note which have real force, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold without qualification, and how to reply within the time available. Include the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss—likely the charge that preserving genuine openness at every micro-node dissolves character agency and makes rational decision-making within the plot impossible for a reader to follow. Finally, identify the one objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises, and draft the concise reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the session. Keep the framing formal; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I will supply those separately.
