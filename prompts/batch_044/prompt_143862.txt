I've been arguing with my roomate about Molyneux's problem — you know, the old thought experiment about whether a blind person who learns a cube and sphere by touch can tell them apart the instant they suddenly see. We got wondering if modern vision-restoration clinics take an implicit philosophical side without ever saying so out loud. There's this pre-surgical protocol some centers use where they deliberately withhold tactile geometery training before surgery, telling patients they should learn shapes "purely" from visual input afterwards. The reasoning seems to be that feeling a cube beforehand might "contaminate" the visual concept, or maybe that real spatial knowledge has to be acquired through the "right" sensory channel.

What view of perception, concept formation, and cross-modal representation does that institutional practice actually presuppose? Does it assume spatial concepts are locked inside specific sensory channels — so touch-knowledge of a cube just can't feed into vision — or does it secretly rely on there being one amodal concept that could be "used up" by the wrong sense? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology here, say whether it's internally consistent or smuggles in contradictory commitments, and tell me whether a clinic running this protocol could also hold an empiricist view of concept learning or whether it needs something closer to nativism. And if we replaced its hidden theory with a thoroughly modality-independent account of the mind, what exactly would have to change in how they design pre-operative education?
