Act as a senior examiner in political philosophy finalizing assessment for an upper-level seminar on justice and the welfare state. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual crux: standard debates about workfare, benefit fraud, and the “undeserving poor” usually treat conditionality as either an incentive mechanism or a moral intrusion, but they rarely ask whether an administratively feasible system can sustain a philosophically sharp distinction between “desert-tracking” conditions (targeting perceived moral failure or lifestyle choice) and “capacity-tracking” conditions (targeting demonstrable structural barriers to labor-market participation). Think carefully about why that distinction collapses under information asymmetry, and what it costs—morally and epistemically—to defend either horn.

Please design one 75-minute final-exam essay question worth 30 marks. The question must move well beyond asking students to summarize familiar policy debates or invoke rhetorical categories. Instead, construct the following specific tension: imagine a proposed welfare regime that formally divides claimants into two tracks—those who are “structurally excluded” due to verified capacity deficits, and those who are “agentively non-participating” due to desert deficits—and applies strict behavioral conditions only to the second group. Ask the student to evaluate whether this two-track system is conceptually coherent and administratively sustainable, or whether it necessarily collapses into either (i) an invasive moral audit of private life that violates norms of respectful treatment, or (ii) a de facto universal abandonment of conditionality once verification costs become prohibitive. Include in your framing the idea of an “epistemic burden reversal”: under strict conditionality the claimant bears the burden of proving compliance rather than the state bearing the burden of proving fraud, and this reversal alters not merely administrative costs but the moral framing of the claimant-state relationship.

Your deliverable must contain three clearly labeled parts. First, the exact exam question, worded for students who have no access to external texts; do not ask for citations, bibliographies, named theorists, or specific policy statutes. Second, a model answer outlining the argument structure and conceptual moves that a top-scoring response should make. Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical points, with the marks allocated to each (summing to 30) and a one-line note on the typical student error or omission for each point. Ensure that the difficulty comes from conceptual discrimination—separating structural from agentive explanations of non-participation and tracing their interaction with bureaucratic verification—rather than from recall of particular welfare programs.
