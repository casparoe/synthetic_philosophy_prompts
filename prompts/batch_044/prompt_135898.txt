Analyze the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794 — the arrest and execution of Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Couthon, and the rapid dismantling of the Committee of Public Safety — through the lens of the claim that revolutions devour their own children, but do so with explicit attention to contingency and institutional design rather than to inevitable historical destiny. First, map the decision space on 8–10 Thermidor: identify the relevant actors (the remaining Jacobin deputies, the Thermidorian coalition, the Paris sections, and the Committee itself), the institutional constraints created by the merged executive-legislative structure and the Law of 22 Prairial, and the options actually available to each party. Then apply the theory: does Robespierre's fall look like a revolution consuming its architects because their ideological commitment to "virtue" and transparency made any opposition indistinguishable from corruption and therefore intolerable? Or does it look like an ordinary parliamentary coup made possible by contingent factors — the news of victory at Fleurus, Robespierre's refusal to name targets in his 8 Thermidor speech, and the precise coalition that assembled? Next, turn the episode back on the theory: what does Thermidor reveal about the limits of structural interpretations (whether class-struggle, ideological, or institutional) of 1789–94, and where exactly is the "devouring" narrative silent? Be disciplined about the mapping: do not force the theory to speak where the institutional details or the contingent sequence do the explanatory work. End with a one-sentence summary of whether this case confirms, complicates, or undermines the claim that revolutions necessarily eat their children.
