I've been trying to follow this debate in environmental history about treating germs, climate shifts, and volcanoes as 'historical actors' — not just background conditions but agents with real causal weight. One camp wants to say stuff like "Tambora caused the Year Without a Summer, so Tambora is an actor in 1816 European politics." The other camp says that's just environmental determinism with a postmodern gloss, and that calling a volcano an 'actor' either means nothing or it strips human decision-makers of responsability.

The thing is, both sides agree on the basic science — yes, Tambora erupted, yes, crops failed, yes, people responded in various ways. They agree suffering is bad and that humans adapt. But they've been talking past eachother for years and nobody moves an inch. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word 'agency'? Or are they answering different questions — one about causal explanation and one about moral accountability? Or is there a hidden substantive point about whether non-human causes need 'counterfactual robustness' — like, the volcano could have done otherwise — before we can call them actors?

I keep thinking the crux is whether attributing agency to a volcano forces you to say societies aren't responsible for adapting badly, but I'm not sure if that's in the debate or just my own confusion. What's the real diagnosis? Is it bedrock intuition, a hidden factual dispute, or just words? And given whatever it is, could a distinction or a concrete case actually move it, or should we accept it's stuck?
