I have been reflecting on karma as a theory of moral desert, specifically on a recurring argumentative structure I encounter when cosmic justice is invoked to explain particular distributions of welfare. I should like to know whether philosophers have given this structure a specific name.

Consider the following pattern. When an individual who is presently suffering is considered, that suffering is typically accounted for as the discharge of a prior moral deficit: the present harm is treated as the causal effect of past wrongdoing. Yet if that same individual subsequently enjoys good fortune, the improvement is rarely interpreted as proof that the deficit was overpaid; rather, it is explained away either as the accumulation of fresh merit or as the spending-down of a hidden surplus that had been stored from earlier lives. The reverse holds for those who prosper unjustly: present success is attributed to unexpended merit carried forward, but any subsequent misfortune is not read as the depletion of that same reserve; it is taken as fresh punishment for a newly incurred wrong. Thus, for any observed sequence of welfare and harm, the direction of moral accounting — whether the present state is an effect of the past or a cause shaping the future — appears to be adjusted post hoc so that the appearance of perfect desert is preserved.

This seems to me more specific than a general claim of unfalsifiability. A single unfalsifiable thesis can at least be applied with consistency, whereas here the very rules of temporal attribution seem to invert depending on which narrative protects the theory. Is there an established concept — perhaps in the philosophy of religion or in the study of normative accounting — that captures exactly this bidirectional, self-sealing bookkeeping? If there is no precise term, how ought one to demarcate it from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis in a desert-based framework, and what boundary conditions would distinguish it from simple post-hoc rationalisation?
