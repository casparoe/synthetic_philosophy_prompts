Design a 75-minute classroom simulation for a seminar of 16 upper-level philosophy students studying rational agency and intertemporal decision-making. Adapt the dollar auction so that participants feel escalation of commitment not as a mere cognitive bias, but as a tension between dynamic consistency and fidelity to one's prior bidding strategy, interpreted through planning-theoretic accounts of intertemporal agency. Specify the rules: bids are made with non-monetary stakes (modified course-credit points), the winner receives the prize minus all bids, and every bidder—including the loser—incurs a visible commitment cost proportional to their total bids. Introduce roles: bidders, a chronicler who records public justifications for each escalation, and an arbitrator who may force a stop once. Structure the bidding so that each new offer is registered as a binding pledge to one's earlier plan, and repudiating it requires an explicit social forfeiture. Provide a phased timeline, the payoff scheme, and instructions that prevent students from recognizing the trap before entry. Then outline the debrief: map three gameplay moments onto philosophical distinctions—sunk-cost reasoning versus rational persistence, present-biased reversal versus plan stability, and collective escalation versus individual rationality. Predict what will go wrong on first run, such as anticipatory refusal to play or neglect of the chronicler role, and suggest remedies. Finally, state frankly which normative feature of planning-theoretic agency—specifically, the claim that prior intentions possess binding authority over present preferences independent of expected utility—the simulation can only represent analogically and therefore must be supplied by the facilitator rather than generated by the rules alone.
