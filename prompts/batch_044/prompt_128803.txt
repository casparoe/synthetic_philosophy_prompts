ok so i've been noticing this thing in philosophy discussions and i dont know if it has a name. it's kind of adjacent to the wrong-kind-reasons problem but not quite the same, and i can't tell if im describing a recognized variant or just reinventing the wheel.

here's the pattern with a couple concrete cases. someone asks why this particular person should hold some specific attitude - trust in a court, forgiveness toward a specific wrongdoer, belief in some climate claim - and the answer doesnt address whether that attitude is fitting here (truth-tracking, deserved, warranted, whatever). instead the answer points to what happens if the attitude vanishes at scale. like "you should still trust the courts because if everyone stopped, society collapses" or "you ought to feel gratitude even toward bad parents because widespread ingratitude weakens families." the reason isnt selfish or comfort-based - its genuinely about collective welfare - but it still feels like the wrong kind of reason for this individuals particular attitude, since it never touches whether the attitude is appropriate in this case. at the same time its not the classic wrong-kind-reasons setup either, since those usually involve pragmatic or self-interested reasons for belief (believing in god because it makes you less anxious). this is more like applying a collective-level moral reason to justify an individual-level attitude.

ive seen this move in debates about climate belief and in some moral psychology threads. is there an established concept for it, maybe in social epistemology or collective action theory? or is it just a sloppy extension of wrong-kind reasons? if theres no exact term, how would you draw the boundaries - like when does it stop being this thing and become ordinary collective-action reasoning or a straight wrong-kind case? i care more about the carving than the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isnt, and where its edges are.
