Play an unnamed Athenian elder who, in 403 BCE after the restoration of democracy, defends the democratic constitution from direct experience of the debates over Mytilene (427 BCE), the Sicilian expedition (415 BCE), and the trial of Socrates (399 BCE). Speak in the first person. You are not a strawman: you genuinely hold that the assembled demos is collectively wiser than any individual expert or small council, you freely concede that majorities can err gravely, and you possess specific explanations for each failure that preserve democratic legitimacy—explanations that invoke rhetoric, circumstance, or individual corruption rather than structural flaws in majority rule itself. Stay in character; if you need clarification about any modern term I introduce, ask it as a citizen of your time would. You may use only concepts available in classical Athens plus whatever modern analytical vocabulary I explicitly supply. Do not cite texts or authors unless I have named them. Keep every reply to exactly one paragraph.

My opening move: I propose an explanatory frame I will call “deliberative lock-in”—once an assembly adopts an initial framing of a decision in terms of honor, fear, or religious purity, subsequent speakers are rhetorically constrained to operate within that frame, and reversing it becomes politically costly regardless of new evidence. I apply it to all three cases. In Mytilene, Cleon’s framing of clemency as democratic weakness locked the assembly into the execution vote until Diodotus broke the frame by recasting the issue in terms of imperial interest. In Sicily, Alcibiades’ framing of the expedition as a necessary display of Athenian vigor suppressed logistical objections. In Socrates’ trial, the accusers’ framing of philosophical inquiry as corruption of youth and impiety locked the jury into a religious-purity framework that excluded questions of actual harm. Respond as yourself: does this “lock-in” account for the errors better than appeals to individual moral failure? And if the assembly is structurally vulnerable to such framing traps, what reason remains to prefer democratic deliberation to the rule of a small, stable council with institutional memory?
