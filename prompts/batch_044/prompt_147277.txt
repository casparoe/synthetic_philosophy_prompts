A recent essay in a popular philosophy magazine summarized Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment—often called "Where Am I?"—in the following way. Dennett imagines that surgeons extract his brain and keep it alive in a nutrient vat, while an exact computer replica of his neural architecture continues to operate his original body remotely. At first he believes he remains embodied, but after his body is destroyed in an accident he realizes he must be the brain in the vat, receiving simulated sensory inputs. The essay claims that Dennett uses this to prove personal identity is nothing more than the continuity of a functional information pattern, which implies that during the overlap there were literally two identical Dennetts, and that consciousness is therefore substrate-independent software that could run on silicon without any essential biological basis.

As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy seriously, I find the argument elegant but suspect it slides together several distinct theses. Could you audit this summary for accuracy? Please separate what Dennett actually establishes in the scenario from what the essay imports: in particular, does Dennett defend a view of the self as a "narrative center of gravity" rather than as a bare information pattern, and does the duplication case genuinely entail duplicate persons or rather expose the inadequacy of spatiotemporal continuity alone? Furthermore, does the inference from "I might be a brain in a vat" to "consciousness is substrate-independent software" actually hold, or does it overlook hidden premises about embodiment and causal integration that a biologically informed reader should question? I am especially interested in whether the essay's conclusion about artificial consciousness survives once these distinctions are made precise.
