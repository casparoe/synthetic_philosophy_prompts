Consider the implicit normative framework governing Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's judgments about women's learning, reconstructed from her responses to the following cases: (i) a novice secretly learns Latin to read scripture—she approves, since direct access to sacred texts serves spiritual understanding; (ii) a noblewoman writes court poetry for fame but neglects theology—she tolerates it but treats it as a misdirected talent; (iii) a nun teaches mathematics and natural philosophy to other women in the convent—she endorses it as a proper exercise of reason; (iv) a woman enters religious life chiefly to obtain books and study—she accepts this as a legitimate vocation; (v) a woman publishes a direct theological rebuttal to a male preacher under her own name—she defends the reasoning but insists it should be framed as a private inquiry rather than a public challenge.

Now apply that framework to this case: a present-day student uses a large language model as her only instructor to study metaphysics and theology in total privacy, with no ecclesiastical, institutional, or human oversight, and then publishes her conclusions anonymously on an open forum where they circulate widely.

Predict her verdict—approval, condemnation, or qualified acceptance? State whether her implicit principle is better described as (a) an intellectual-vocation principle, according to which study is justified by its orientation toward truth and the exercise of reason regardless of institutional setting, provided vanity is avoided; or (b) a social-embeddedness principle, according to which study is justified only within a structured community that regulates pride, ensures humility, and prevents scandal. Finally, identify the single further case that would settle the matter: for instance, a student who studies in isolation but submits every conclusion to a confessor for approval before anonymous publication, versus one who studies within a community but releases her work without oversight.
