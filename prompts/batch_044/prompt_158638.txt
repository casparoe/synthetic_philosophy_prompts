W. E. B. Du Bois maintained that all art functions as propaganda and that Black artists should wield aesthetic form in explicit service of racial liberation. Apply this framework to a present-day problem he never encountered: large-scale generative image models, trained mainly on Western museum collections, tend to interpolate non-Western visual traditions—African diasporic patterns, Indigenous iconographies—into canonical European styles, treating culturally specific grammars as noise to be smoothed away in latent space, and reproducing curatorial hierarchies that label such work folk rather than fine art. Would Du Bois regard these systems as an extension of aesthetic propaganda serving dominant racial interests, as technically neutral tools, or as something more ambiguous? Would he counsel Black artists to boycott them, to appropriate and redirect them, or to construct parallel institutional infrastructures? Reconstruct his likely position, justify it by reference to his commitments on propaganda, collective self-determination, and the political duty of art, and deliver a definite verdict on whether his philosophy supports an optimistic, hostile, or strategically ambivalent relationship to algorithmic image production.
