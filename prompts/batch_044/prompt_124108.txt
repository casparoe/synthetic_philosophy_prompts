I have been thinking about the dollar auction—where people bid on a small prize, but both the highest and the second-highest bidders must pay their bids, so the loser also loses money. I saw a version of this at a work fundraiser: someone bid thirty dollars for a ten-dollar gift card, and I realized he was not really buying the card; he was buying proof that he had not been foolish to start bidding in the first place. You are an expert philosopher and poet with a strong grasp of escalation dynamics and behavioral traps. Before composing, please spend a moment brainstorming: how can the poem’s own structure—its rhyme scheme, repetition, or line breaks—actually enact the feeling of commitment tightening rather than merely describing it?

Please write a 14-line poem in American English, spoken in the voice of an ordinary observer at that fundraiser. In the opening lines, present the auction rules as if they are simple and fair. Around the ninth line, turn sharply to reveal the hidden cruelty: the runner-up pays too, and quitting now means admitting you were wrong from the start. In the closing lines, show that what is truly being auctioned is not the gift card but the right to tell the story of who was wise—or foolish—enough to stop first. Let the form mirror the trap: perhaps returning phrases that close in on themselves, or rhymes that lock together more tightly as the stakes rise. Do not use technical jargon like “sunk-cost fallacy”; let the bidding and the silence after it speak for themselves. Keep the poem polished, precise to the logic of the auction, and focused. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific scholars or texts.
