I'm designing a graduate seminar that moves between close-grained microhistorical reconstruction and large-scale institutional histories of state formation, and I want students to stop treating history from below as merely a sentimental inversion of elite chronicles rather than a methodologically distinct project. Before we proceed with further readings, I need a precise specification of what any framework genuinely committed to writing history from below must be able to deliver—conceptually and evidentially—to avoid collapsing into either romantic particularism or macro-structural determinism. I am especially concerned with how such a framework handles what I will call the archival-silence paradox: the fact that the missing or distorted record of a subaltern group functions simultaneously as an epistemic obstacle and as structural evidence of exclusion, and with whether it can distinguish collective agency from the mere aggregation of individual actions without anachronistically importing modern categories of citizenship or class.

Write the desiderata as a numbered list of exactly five items, each expressed in one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. For items one through four, state a single requirement—marking it non-negotiable or negotiable—together with the specific historical or conceptual case that motivates it, such as a peasant revolt visible only through hostile court records, the scale-inversion problem in crowd behavior, or the risk of treating a single recovered life as representative of an entire social formation. In the fifth item, identify the pair of desiderata you judge jointly unsatisfiable given the limits of available evidence, and briefly indicate how a microhistorical close-reading approach and a top-down state-archive approach each fail against at least one of the first four requirements.
