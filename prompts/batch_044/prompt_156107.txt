Write a short story that dramatizes Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—by depicting them not as separate biological species but as successive cognitive regimes within a single artificial agent adapting to an environment that actively rewards misrepresentation. The agent should begin as a purely reactive system shaped by selection over random variations, then acquire reward-sensitive conditioning, then develop an internal capacity to generate and kill hypotheses before acting, and finally reach a stage where it uses external symbolic tools—language, diagrams, or shared representations—to extend its reasoning beyond its own memory and perception. The narrative should take a definite philosophical position: either defend the view that the Gregorian stage is genuinely discontinuous with the Popperian stage because symbol-use introduces a form of extended or collective cognition that individual hypothesis-testing cannot approximate, or defend the opposing view that Gregorian cognition is merely Popperian cognition accelerated and externalized, with no special ontological status. After the story, include a brief note identifying exactly where the narrative takes liberties with Dennett's framework—for instance, whether the taxonomy is treated as a linear developmental sequence rather than an evolutionary classification, whether language is treated as necessary rather than merely sufficient for Gregorian status, or whether internal misrepresentation is given a functional role that Dennett's original account does not emphasize—and state clearly which contested interpretation of the taxonomy the story favors.
