I sit on my university's research ethics board and we have a vote next week on a cognitive-science proposal that plays with color-phi illusions — you know, the thing where a dot jumps and changes color and people swear they saw the shift happen in the middle, before the second dot even showed up. The PI's twist is too test whether giving subjects a subtle post-hoc cue (a brief tone paired with a leading question) actually rewrites their prior phenomenal experience — an Orwellian revision — or just makes them confabulate a false report, which they'd call Stalinesque. To check this they record EEG during the illusion, then in a second session ask subjects to "correct" their initial report and see if the neural signature of the original experience is still there or if it got overwritten.

The deception part is that subjects are told its a reaction-time study, and only after the second session do they learn about the illusion and the cue manipulation. Half our board says this is routine deception with proper debriefing. The other half says if consciousness really gets revised after the fact, then asking people to retroactively edit their experience crosses a line — informed consent can't cover mental revision you don't know you're signing up for.

Draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the PI could actually respond to. Take a definite position, don't just list pros and cons, and include the strongest argument for the side you reject. I specifically want you to take a stand on whether the color-phi case actually supports an Orwellian reading over a Stalinesque one, since that drives the ethical worry. Don't just handwave with citations; I need a real call with reasoning the researchers can answer.
