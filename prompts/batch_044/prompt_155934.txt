I am presenting my thesis defense seminar in our philosophy of mind reading group next week, defending what I call the "inter-draft friction" model of consciousness. The core claim is that Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater should not be read as full-blown eliminativism about phenomenal experience, but rather as a denial that any single "draft" achieves global broadcast to an internal observer. I combine Dennett's framework with hierarchical predictive processing to argue that phenomenal character—specifically the sense of ownership—is not the content of a winning draft displayed in a theater, but an unstable, dynamically maintained relation of tension between competing local predictions that fail to converge on a single global model. Consciousness, on this view, is not "what it is like" to read a finished newspaper in the mind's eye; it is the editorial friction of newspapers being written, revised, and suppressed simultaneously, with no publisher ever selecting a final edition and no central audience receiving it.

My audience is precisely this mix: two global workspace theorists in the Baars-Dehaene tradition who will insist that without broadcast integration there is only unconscious processing; two phenomenologists influenced by Zahavi who will treat first-person self-givenness as a primitive datum that cannot be dissolved into relations between drafts; one cognitive scientist who works on predictive coding in perception and will ask whether my "friction" is empirically distinguishable from ordinary prediction-error minimization; and several generalists who have read Dennett's Consciousness Explained but are not familiar with the recent predictive-processing literature on consciousness. They care about conceptual coherence and empirical tractability, not about textual scholarship or historical exegesis.

Prepare my speaker defense sheet for the Q&A. I need the objections ordered by how damaging they are likely to be, each phrased in the vocabulary of the subgroup that will raise it—talk of "broadcast," "integration," "self-givenness," "transparency," "free energy," "empirical distinguishability." For each objection, draft a concise reply of about two minutes that actually addresses the point rather than changing the subject, and then flag exactly where the reply is weakest: where I am conceding ground, where I am relying on a speculative analogy, or where the predictive-processing machinery and the phenomenological data pull in opposite directions. I also want to know what I should concede upfront in my opening remarks to lower the temperature before the attack begins, and I want the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the objection that exposes the central unresolved tension in my view, where I have not yet shown how distributed "friction" can account for the specific unity and transparency of a single conscious field. Do not include any request for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the prep should be constructible from the conceptual materials alone.
