I think I have found a pattern that standard labels miss, and I want to call it his doctrine of "organic metamorphosis": the idea that a healthy state, like a plant, can only change by unfolding its own internal form, never by being cut and grafted onto a foreign structure. This, I believe, explains both his revulsion from the French Revolution and his willingness to serve the Duke of Weimar on administrative reform. Next Thursday I will present this interpretation to a literature-and-politics seminar at a local university. The room will hold about fifteen people: mostly scholars of Weimar classicism who know the texts far better than I do, plus one political historian who specializes in Holy Roman Empire administrative law and will be alert to anachronism. I need a Q&A prep sheet—not a defense of Goethe in general, but a defense of my specific reading—ordered by how likely and damaging each objection is, phrased in the vocabulary these listeners would actually use. For each objection I want a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest one-sentence note on where that reply is weakest. I also need to know what I should concede up front to keep credibility, which single reply must survive for my interpretation to stand, and the one question I am praying nobody asks, with a sketch of how to answer it honestly without pretending to evidence I do not have.
