From a software engineering perspective, I am trying to understand Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self—specifically the symbol "I"—is a strange loop: a self-referential pattern in the brain that acquires causal efficacy by feeding back on itself. I want to compare this with an alternative framework that treats the self not as a discrete symbol with a pointer-to-self, but as a stable fixed-point attractor in a high-dimensional neural dynamics space—something closer to a recursive equilibrium in a distributed system than to a quine or self-modifying pointer.

Please compare these two accounts across the following dimensions.

1. The mechanism of self-reference: Does Hofstadter's strange loop require an explicit "I"-token that literally denotes the system containing it (like a process holding its own PID in a registry), or can the same functional role be played by implicit, distributed feedback loops with no centralized symbol?

2. Phenomenological ownership: How does each account explain the first-person feeling that a thought or action is "mine"? Can an attractor model generate this ownership quality without a symbolic loop that tags representations with an owner, or does it reduce ownership to mere dynamical stability?

3. Split-brain and pathological unity: In split-brain patients, the corpus callosum is severed, yet subjects often report a single self. Does this support the strange-loop view (the loop is resilient and can be reconstructed locally in each hemisphere) or the attractor view (unity is an emergent property of coupled dynamics that can persist with reduced bandwidth)? Which view predicts that a complete hemispheric separation should eventually yield two distinct selves?

Finally, state which account you find more conceptually coherent, and propose one concrete prediction—either empirical or logical—on which the two views genuinely diverge. Please avoid bibliographic references; I am interested in the conceptual architecture, not in citation history.
