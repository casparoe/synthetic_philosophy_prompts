Analyze the refusal of the French General Staff between 1896 and 1899 to reopen Alfred Dreyfus's court-martial—after Colonel Georges Picquart had identified Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy as the actual source of the leaked military intelligence and had documented that the bordereau and the secret dossier contained forged or misattributed material—through the lens of the dogmatism paradox, specifically in what I will call its reflexive-corruption form. In this form, an agent acquires second-order evidence that the first-order reasoning process producing her belief is unreliable, yet assessing that second-order evidence requires deploying the very same process now in question, so that downward revision undermines its own grounds and refusal to revise amounts to dogmatism. Map the episode precisely: identify who held the first-order belief in Dreyfus's guilt, who held second-order beliefs about the reliability of military intelligence and the secret dossier, and what the institutional options actually were—full retrial, suppression of Picquart's findings, partial admission without overturning the original verdict, or continued insistence on the original evidence. Then say what the reflexive-corruption version of the paradox predicts should happen to rational belief in such a structure, whether the Staff's conduct—particularly the decision to forge additional documents to protect the original conviction—matches the paradox's dynamics or instead reveals forces the paradox treats as external noise, such as institutional honor, anti-Semitic bias, or the strategic need to conceal intelligence methods. Finally, assess whether the Dreyfus Affair supports the claim that the paradox describes a genuine rational impasse, or whether it shows that what looks like epistemic paralysis is usually rationalized by non-epistemic commitments that the paradox itself does not capture. Be disciplined about where the theory is silent, and do not treat the paradox as explaining motives it does not claim to cover.
