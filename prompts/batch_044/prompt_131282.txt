I'm on the board of trustees for the West Yorkshire Civic Archive, which is meant to be a new public institution accountable to local residents rather than to Westminster or anyone else. We've been offered a £4 million endowment from the Crown Heritage Foundation, but the agreement includes what they're calling 'symbolic patronage' — the monarch appoints one trustee (no voting rights, but they sit on the board and can speak), we display the royal crest in our main atrium, and every year we present our annual report in a formal ceremony at the archive. Our charter says we're governed by democratic mandate, but the lawyers say there's no legal bar to accepting ceremonial roles.

I was chatting with a mate who reads a bit of philosophy and he mentioned something about 'asymmetric ceremonial obligation' — the idea that when a democratic body performs deference rituals towards an unelected figure, it doesn't just symbolise respect, it actually restructures how citizens see the institution's authority, even if the role is technically powerless. I'm not definatly sure if that's a real term or just his idea, but it stuck with me. Meanwhile, half the board thinks this is just harmless tradition that saves jobs and keeps the archive open, and that rejecting it would be cutting our nose off to spite our face. The other half thinks accepting it violates the democratic dignity of the institution — that citizens shouldn't have to see their public archive performing loyalty to someone they never voted for, especially when the trustee can still shape discussions even without a vote.

We have to decide by Thursday's meeting, and if we reject we need to notify the Foundation by Friday morning. If we accept, we need to have the patronage agreement drafted by the end of the month, and if we want amendments we need to propose them by Wednesday so the lawyers can check them. Could you draft the memo I'd circulate before Thursday? It should recommend one concrete course — accept, reject, or accept with specific amendments — with the time frame clear, and it should take the 'harmless tradition and funding' argument seriously rather than just dismissing it. Don't just quote legal definitions of constitutional monarchy at me; I want reasoning the board can actually use to vote, including an honest treatment of the strongest point on the side that loses.
