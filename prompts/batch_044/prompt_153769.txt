Two posters in a long-running rationalist forum thread on value theory argued over tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering carry intrinsic value, while pleasure and satisfied desire are at best instrumentally neutral. (1) The first poster defended this by claiming that desire-satisfaction is a regenerative treadmill: every satisfied want seeds a new preference structure, so a world of happy people still contains an uneliminated reservoir of future craving and therefore remains flawed; by contrast, a world with no conscious beings and therefore no craving or suffering is strictly better. (2) The second poster replied that this collapses tranquilism into negative utilitarianism, and that the first has not shown why the absence of craving is good independently of the absence of suffering. She drew a distinction she called quietism versus eliminativism: quietists treat absence as positively valuable, eliminativists treat only suffering as negatively valuable, and tranquilism seems to borrow from both without explaining which dimension does the work. (3) The first came back with a more specific claim: craving is not merely an instrumental cause of future suffering, but an intrinsic distortion of rational agency—like accumulated noise in a Bayesian update—so its absence is a separate axiological dimension, analogous to signal clarity rather than mere harm reduction. He added that satisfied craving is therefore like suppressing a single error term locally while allowing systemic corruption to persist globally. (4) The second responded that this analogizes craving to epistemic error without justification, and that if any motivation—including the motivation to reduce suffering—counts as craving that corrupts agency, then the tranquilist is committed to the eventual cessation of all agency, including the very advocacy that promotes tranquilism; she concluded that the view is either self-defeating or must smuggle in a hidden positive value for rational action.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. In particular, address whether move (3) succeeds in distinguishing tranquilism from negative utilitarianism without begging the question, and whether move (4) genuinely exposes a paradox or relies on an equivocation between craving and motivation generally. Finally, identify the single most important issue both posters are missing—something that, if resolved, would most change which view is defensible.
