In the novel I am writing, a logician character refuses to evaluate any decision problem that violates Savage's sure-thing principle—what she calls the independence axiom—treating it not as a normative constraint on rational preference but as a law of logical consistency analogous to transitivity. She cites the Allais paradox, particularly the common-consequence formulation, as proof that expected utility theory is self-contradictory: she claims that the same preference ordering both satisfies and violates the principle merely by redescribing the outcomes in terms of shared probabilities, and that this reveals an internal flaw in the axiomatic system rather than a descriptive deviation in human choice. Consequently, she argues that anyone who accepts the paradox's framing is logically compelled to reject all lotteries with non-unique representations. I suspect her reasoning conflates descriptive violation with formal contradiction, but I want to be precise: does the Allais setup actually generate a logical contradiction within von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility, or does it only demonstrate that actual preferences can systematically depart from Savage's P2? And if the latter, does that departure necessarily undermine the normative authority of the axiom, or merely limit its psychological scope?
