I'm trying to work out, by reflective equilibrium, what I actually think distinguishes an intended side effect from one that is merely foreseen, and I'm finding that my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases don't line up as neatly as I hoped. To organize my thinking, I've been sketching a tentative distinction between effects that are "priced into" an agent's practical reasoning—treated as part of the cost-benefit structure that makes the action worth taking—and effects that are "priced out," where the agent regards them as unfortunate externalities to be regretted or minimized but not as elements that help constitute the decision itself. I want to test whether this distinction, or some repaired version of it, captures my judgments, or whether I need to abandon it for something more complex.

Here are my verdicts, case by case. First, the familiar scenario from discussions in experimental philosophy: a CEO approves a highly profitable program knowing with near-certainty that it will severely damage the local river ecosystem, and she is genuinely indifferent to that damage, caring only about the financial return. My verdict is that the ecological harm counts as intentional, or at least occupies the intentional category in a way that feels robust. Second, a parallel case in which the same CEO approves an identical program knowing it will accidentally restore a degraded wetland nearby, and she is equally indifferent to that ecological outcome, caring only about profit. Here my verdict is that the restoration is not intentional; she did not mean to bring it about, despite knowing it would occur. Third, a surgeon removes a life-threatening tumor using an anesthetic that carries a known risk of permanent nerve damage; she weighs that risk against the life saved, proceeds with appropriate care, and the damage occurs. My verdict is that the nerve damage is not intentional—it is a regretted risk that was priced out of her reasoning, not priced in. Fourth, a surgeon performs an elective cosmetic procedure knowing that the specific surgical technique will permanently alter a patient's facial nerves in a distinctive way, and she performs the operation precisely because she wants that particular expressive result, though the patient consented only to the cosmetic change and not to the nerve alteration. Here the nerve alteration feels intentional, even though it is technically a side effect of the technique. Fifth, an urban planner approves a highway expansion that will displace a long-standing informal market; she anticipates the displacement, includes compensation funds in the budget, and proceeds because the traffic benefits are overwhelming, but she genuinely regrets the loss of the community. My verdict here is genuinely torn—the displacement feels somewhat intentional, because she knowingly brings it about and builds it into the plan, yet it does not feel as fully intentional as the CEO's ecological harm in the first case, and I keep going back and forth. Sixth, the same planner approves a different route that accidentally preserves an old industrial building by creating a protected noise-buffer zone around it; she never intended preservation and is surprised by the effect. Verdict: clearly not intentional. Seventh, a software engineer deploys an update that removes support for legacy file formats; she knows this will cause some users to lose access, considers that loss an acceptable cost of simplifying the codebase, issues a warning, but takes no further steps to prevent it. Verdict: this feels closer to intentional than the planner's displacement, perhaps because she actively eliminates the support rather than merely allowing a consequence to unfold, though I am not fully settled. Eighth, a different engineer deploys an update that accidentally creates a backward-compatibility feature for legacy files, though she never designed for it and regards it as irrelevant. Verdict: not intentional.

The tension that most troubles me is between the fifth and seventh cases: in both the agent foresees harm, accepts it within a practical decision, and takes no preventive action beyond minimal measures, yet the planner's displacement feels less intentional than the engineer's removal of support, and both feel different from the first CEO case, where indifference seemed to push the harm firmly into the intentional column. I also remain unresolved about a case I keep returning to: a parent tells her young child that the family dog went to live on a farm rather than admitting it died, knowing the statement is false but believing the comfort it provides outweighs the deception. Is this deception intentional in the same way as the surgeon's nerve alteration in the fourth case, or is there a mitigating factor—perhaps the protective purpose—that places it closer to the planner's regretted displacement in the fifth?

What principle, or small family of competing principles, best captures these verdicts? I want each candidate stated precisely in continuous prose, with the specific case or clause that motivates it, and with an honest accounting of which of my eight verdicts it can accommodate and which it must overturn as a mistake. The principle should also deliver a clear verdict on the unresolved dog case and on one further case I have not considered: a researcher publishes a finding knowing that a malicious actor will likely misuse it, though she takes no steps to prevent that misuse and considers scientific openness an overriding value.
