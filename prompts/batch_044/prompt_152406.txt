Question: Compare Millikan’s biofunctional teleosemantics with Dretske’s indicator-based account regarding misrepresentation in the frog-dot case. Specifically, explain how each determines representational content when a frog’s prey-detection mechanism fires at a small black dot that is not a fly, and identify one significant weakness in each approach.

Student answer: Both theorists reject the idea that mental content requires an interpreter. Millikan argues that content is fixed by direct proper function: the neural state was evolutionarily selected to indicate flies, so firing at a dot is misrepresentation. Because selection history targets flies specifically, the disjunction problem—why the content is not “fly or dot”—is dissolved; present correlation with dots does not alter evolutionary meaning. Dretske holds that an internal state becomes an indicator through being recruited during learning to guide behavior; its content is the condition present during successful recruitment. Applying this to the frog, if the mechanism was calibrated solely on flies, the dot-trigger is misrepresentation, but if dots were also present and reinforced, the content becomes disjunctive. One weakness of Millikan’s view is that direct proper functions require evolutionary history, so learned representations must rely on derived proper functions, which critics consider an ad hoc extension. One weakness of Dretske’s view is that because recruitment depends on correlation within a learning window, environments with both flies and dots naturally yield disjunctive indicator contents, which means his account fails to solve the disjunction problem in precisely those cases where it arises.
