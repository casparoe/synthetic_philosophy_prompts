I have been reviewing a summary of two-dimensional semantics circulated in a recent philosophy-of-language workshop, and before I assign it to my graduate students I want a careful audit of its accuracy. The author distinguishes two intensions for every expression: a primary intension, determined entirely by a priori reasoning, which assigns reference in worlds considered as actual; and a secondary intension, which assigns reference in worlds considered counterfactual and tracks metaphysical modality. For proper names, the summary claims that the primary intension is a constant function returning the actual-world bearer, so that names are rigid designators in the secondary dimension but descriptively anchored—via direct acquaintance conditions—in the primary. Applying this to 'water,' the author says the primary intension selects whatever substance plays the water-role in a world, making 'water is H2O' contingent in the primary dimension yet necessary in the secondary. The summary then argues that this framework collapses Kripke's a-posteriori-necessary distinction for natural-kind terms but preserves it intact for proper names, since the latter's primary intension never shifts across worlds. Finally, the author concludes that two-dimensionalism thereby rehabilitates a restricted descriptivism about names: although names lack explicit descriptive content, their reference in the primary dimension is fixed by an epistemically transparent relation that holds in every world in which the name is evaluated.

Please evaluate this summary point by point. Which parts faithfully represent the standard two-dimensional framework? Where does it conflate epistemic possibility with psychological accessibility, mischaracterize the rigidity of names across dimensions, or overstate the a priori status of primary intensions? And does the final claim—that descriptivism is rehabilitated for names—actually follow, or does it confuse the role of primary intensions with a theory of reference-fixing?
