You are a senior philosopher of mind with particular expertise in self-deception, belief formation, and the architecture of rational agency. I am drafting an essay for a philosophy journal and would like your critical comments, suggestions for tightening the argument, and an assessment of whether the central idea is genuinely novel or merely a redescription of familiar positions.

Please begin by briefly brainstorming the main conceptual risks: does my proposal avoid the classic paradox, or does it relocate it? Then address the essay notes directly.

Here is the outline and core idea.

Working title concept: "Write-Only Archives: Diachronic Partitioning and the Retrieval Asymmetry of Self-Deception"

The standard paradox is well known: if I deceive myself, I must simultaneously know that p is true and cause myself to believe that p is false. This seems to require that I intend to deceive myself, which in turn seems to require that I am already aware of the deception—thereby undermining it.

I want to defend a model I call retrieval-asymmetry archivalism. On this view, self-deception does not involve two simultaneous, contradictory beliefs held in the same cognitive workspace. Instead, it involves a single agent who, at time t1, genuinely acquires and encodes belief B—for example, "my collaborator is untrustworthy." At t2, the agent's explicit, deliberative reasoning system is selectively blocked from retrieving B—not because B is erased, but because the retrieval pathway is inhibited by a motivational filter that operates below the threshold of explicit intention. Meanwhile, B remains active in what I call an archival mode: it guides emotional response, perceptual salience, and behavioral avoidance, but it cannot be cited as a reason or subjected to evidential scrutiny.

This is not standard divided-mind theory, because I do not posit two co-equal sub-agents or a fully partitioned personality. It is also not mere wishful thinking, because B was genuinely believed and is genuinely retained; nor is it simple distraction, because the agent is not merely avoiding the topic—the belief is present but read-blocked.

The analogy I use is to a write-only or append-only log in computing: the system can record data and that data can influence downstream processing, but there is no direct read command available to the executive module. I want to explore whether this analogy illuminates the phenomenology of self-deception—particularly the sense that the deceived self "knows" something that the reasoning self denies—or whether it misleadingly reifies belief as stored data.

My specific questions for the essay are these:

1. Logical coherence: Does retrieval-asymmetry genuinely dissolve the paradox, or does it just push the contradiction into the architecture—so that the agent both knows B (in the archive) and does not know B (in working reasoning) at the same time?

2. Relation to existing accounts: How does this differ in substance from Davidson's division model or from more recent motivational accounts? Am I just renaming a partition theory with a technological metaphor?

3. The analogy's limits: Beliefs are not files. Where does the computing analogy break down, and does breaking it destroy the model?

4. Practical and moral implications: If the archive can be opened—through therapy, trauma, or accidental cueing—does that mean the agent was responsible for maintaining the lock? Is there a meaningful ethical distinction between this kind of self-deception and lying to another person?

Please comment on accuracy, coherence, and interest. In particular, tell me whether the essay's central distinction—between belief-erasure, belief-partition, and retrieval-blocked archival retention—holds up, and whether you think a philosopher of mind would find it a substantive contribution rather than a notational variant.
