I'm working out my own rule for which group to include myself in when I'm trying to predict what will happen to me, and my instincts seem to pull in opposite directions.

Case one: I'm a smoker in my forties worried about heart disease. I feel I should compare myself to other smokers my age, not to all people my age. Narrowing the group feels correct.

Case two: I'm buying a lottery ticket and wondering about my odds. I feel I should compare myself to all ticket buyers, not just to people who share my birthday or my initials. Widening feels correct, and inventing a false subgroup feels wrong.

Case three: A doctor tells me that "patients with your profile" have a 70% success rate, but that profile is built only from age and diagnosis. I also carry a rare genetic marker. Among the three documented patients with that marker, two failed. Still, I feel I should stay with the larger group rather than retreat into that tiny one, even though the tiny one is technically more specifically "me."

Case four: My friend is opening a restaurant and asks what her chances are. I think she should look at all new restaurants in her city, not only ones owned by people with her background. She says that erases her reality; I think the prediction should erase it. That feels right, yet I can't explain why it doesn't apply to case one, where I insisted on keeping the smoking category.

Case five: If I ask whether I'll be happy retiring to the coast, I reject every survey of retirees. I think "I'm me," and averaging others is irrelevant. So here I reject every reference group except myself.

The tension is that in medicine I stay loyal to the broad group despite a specific exception; in happiness I reject the broad group entirely; in the lottery I reject false narrowing; and in my friend's case I reject identity-based narrowing. Is there one principle that tells me when to stay with a statistical group and when to claim exemption?

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: I'm considering learning a risky sport. The beginner injury rate is high, but I'm unusually cautious and physically fit for a beginner. Should I count myself among all beginners, or among cautious, fit beginners? Choosing the second feels like rigging the answer, yet in case three I stayed with the broader group even when a narrower one existed.

If there is a coherent principle behind all this, what does it say about predicting whether I'll adapt well to a career change—should I look at surveys of people who switched fields, or only at my own past patterns of adaptation? And if the principle can't save every verdict, which one should I drop?
