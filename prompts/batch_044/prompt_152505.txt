I've been thinking about chaos, predictability, and determinism on my own, mostly through long conversations with a friend who has stronger and more consistent opinions than I do. Over the past year she has taken clear positions on five scenarios, and I'm trying to understand the underlying rule she's actually running rather than just repeating slogans.

Her verdicts so far are these. First, on Earth weather a century out: she says the system is deterministic—governed by fixed physical laws—but unpredictable in practice because of chaos, and she insists the unpredictability lies in our epistemic limits, not in nature itself. Second, on the exact decay moment of one specific tritium nucleus: she calls this genuinely indeterminate; quantum mechanics, she says, means there is no hidden fact of the matter about when it will happen. Third, on a spacecraft traveling through a three-body gravitational system: she puts it in the same category as weather—deterministic chaos, predictable in principle with perfect measurements but not in reality. Fourth, on the path of smoke rising from a single candle in a sealed, still room: she says it is unpredictable, but she attributes this entirely to turbulent chaos and denies that quantum randomness bubbles up to affect the macroscopic path in any meaningful way. Fifth, on a mechanical lottery-ball-drop machine with precisely calibrated air jets: she says the outcome is predictable in principle and largely predictable in practice; the apparent randomness comes only from our ignorance of initial conditions, not from any indeterminacy in the dynamics.

Now I want to know what she should say about a new case if she wants to stay consistent with herself.

The case is this: imagine a clockwork mechanism built with gears arranged so sensitively that displacing one gear's initial alignment by the width of a single atom changes which hour the clock displays after ten years. The displacement is not caused by human error or thermal noise, but by the genuinely random quantum tunneling position of one proton in the gate that releases the first gear. After that initial release, the gears evolve for a full decade according to ordinary classical mechanics, with extreme sensitivity to that starting shift.

Should she class this clock with the weather and the spacecraft, or with the tritium nucleus? Which of her five earlier verdicts is the closest analog? And if her past rulings genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would she be adopting by placing it in one camp versus the other?

More generally, please lay out the two or three most plausible rules that could jointly explain all five of her earlier verdicts. For each rule, say exactly what it predicts about this clock. Then tell me which rule you think she is actually running, with your reasons, and name one further concrete case I could ask her about that would best distinguish those rules from one another.

Finally, please separate what is well settled in the physics and philosophy of chaos and determinism from what is genuinely contested among people who understand the topic, and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
