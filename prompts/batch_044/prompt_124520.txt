I am teaching a graduate seminar in philosophy of education, and we have been working through Alfred North Whitehead’s account of mental growth—specifically his claim that learning moves through three rhythms of romance, precision, and generalization, and that the gravest danger is not ignorance but the accumulation of inert ideas: precise, well-organized knowledge that is never applied or reintegrated into fresh experience. In our discussions, a recurring pattern has emerged that I want the students to feel rather than merely analyze. When a group is given an open, genuinely puzzling problem—say, how a school might redesign its assessment to honor both creativity and rigor—they initially display the wide, excited curiosity that Whitehead associates with romance. But as soon as they begin acquiring precise distinctions—categories of assessment, metrics of creativity, historical case studies—their energy shifts: they become expert at sorting and refining, yet the original problem fades. The precision does not serve synthesis; it replaces the romantic impulse. By the session’s end they have often produced an elaborate taxonomy that answers no practical question, and they do not even notice that the synthesis they set out to achieve has disappeared. I want an in-class activity, for about fourteen students and lasting roughly seventy-five minutes, that reproduces this specific failure mode. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants begin with a broad, ill-defined challenge, are then offered tools for ever-finer classification and analysis, and discover for themselves—before any debrief—that they have become trapped in precision and lost the capacity for generalization. They should experience firsthand how adding individually reasonable distinctions can collectively block the very synthesis those distinctions were meant to enable.

The setting is a philosophy-of-education seminar with graduate students; the group is small enough for intense collaboration but large enough to split into competing subgroups. I do not want a lecture or a named thought experiment presented up front. Instead, design a structured game or simulation with concrete materials—perhaps cards, assigned roles, or staged deliberative choices—so that the dynamic emerges from what participants do. They should have some small, non-monetary stakes, such as a collective score or the social satisfaction of resolving the opening problem, that make the trap feel real rather than hypothetical. Include a timeline with phases that correspond to the shift from romance into precision and then into stagnation, a payoff or reward structure that inadvertently rewards deeper classification over practical resolution, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Whitehead’s three stages. The debrief should distinguish this stagnation from ordinary confusion or from simple lack of information, and it should be frank about which feature of Whitehead’s long-term developmental rhythm—its natural unfolding over months or years rather than minutes—this compressed exercise necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Please avoid framing the exercise around any highly technical or obscure pedagogical theory that students could not be expected to recognize; keep the conceptual vocabulary close to Whitehead’s own, but present the problem in everyday terms. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
