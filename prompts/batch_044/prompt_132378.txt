I have been working through von Neumann's comparison of the brain to a computer—the lectures published after his death, where he discusses neurons as unreliable gates corrected by statistical redundancy and error-correcting codes—and I think I have pushed his view into a contradiction. I want to know which wall is weakest.

(1) Von Neumann treats the brain as essentially an analog computing machine: neurons behave like logic gates, but they are noisy, so the brain achieves reliable computation through statistical redundancy and error-correcting codes.
(2) If a system is essentially a computing machine, then its mental properties depend only on the computation performed, not on the physical material running it.
(3) Therefore, a perfect digital replication of the brain's computation—running on silicon—should possess the same mental properties, including consciousness.
(4) Yet von Neumann also argues that the brain operates with a statistical, thermodynamic language that is not the language of logic or mathematics, and that the digital computers we actually build do not share this language at all.
(5) If mental properties depend on this specific statistical-thermodynamic language, then a silicon duplicate—lacking that language—would not possess them.
(6) So his view seems to entail both that a silicon copy of me would be conscious and that it would not be.
(7) That is a direct contradiction, which means at least one premise must fail.

I suspect the error is either in assuming that being essentially a computer makes mental properties substrate-independent, or in treating the statistical language as essential to the mental properties rather than merely part of the implementation. But I cannot tell which rejection costs less. Which step should I give up? I would rather preserve the intuition that mental life is not automatically transferable to silicon, but I do not want to do that by simply ignoring what von Neumann said about computation. What does each option cost?
