As a voting member of my hospital’s Clinical Ethics Committee, I am drafting the opinion for Thursday’s meeting, and the issue has become personally unavoidable since my father’s recent encounter with an algorithmic end-of-life forecast. Our institution now produces high-confidence six-month survival predictions for advanced heart-failure patients. The proposal before us would require disclosure of these forecasts to families upon request, even when the attending physician judges that knowing the number will alter the patient’s clinical path. The case that triggered this review involved a patient who, upon being told he had under three months, immediately withdrew from active management, relocated to hospice near an estranged child, and died within six weeks. His family argues that this was a “Damascus trajectory”: the prediction did not change the endpoint—he died, as the model implied he would—but it determined the path he took to get there, and had he never been told, he might have remained in treatment elsewhere for longer. They ask the committee to recognize a distinction between endpoint-fatalism and path-sensitive fatalism, and to permit physicians to withhold algorithmic forecasts when disclosure is likely to reroute care in ways the model cannot account for. The opposing members hold that autonomy requires disclosure of quantitative predictions when requested, that any causal effect of knowing is an empirical matter for the clinician to manage, not an ethical exemption, and that allowing “path judgment” to override transparency collapses into disguised paternalism.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s session. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the mandatory-disclosure proposal, reject it, or approve it with conditions—together with a specific implementation or review time frame. The opinion must take the “Damascus trajectory” argument seriously on its own terms: examine whether path-sensitive fatalism is a coherent ethical category in clinical disclosure, whether a physician’s prediction about “likely path alteration” can be operationalized without pure speculation, and whether withholding a requested forecast violates autonomy in a way that cannot be repaired by debriefing or counseling. Do not restate statutory definitions of informed consent; instead, engage the family’s position philosophically and identify the single strongest consideration on the side you do not recommend. Include one criterion the committee can apply to future requests that does not depend on counterfactual guesses about how long the patient “would have” lived without disclosure.
