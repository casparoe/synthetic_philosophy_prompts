I'm working through different accounts of freedom for a seminar on political philosophy, and I've noticed that my intuitive judgments about concrete cases don't line up neatly with the standard taxonomy of negative, positive, and republican liberty. I'd like to see whether there's a coherent principle behind them, even if it forces me to abandon one or two judgments.

Here are my verdicts. In each case, I'm assessing whether the subject's freedom is genuinely diminished, and if so, in what way. Case 1: A prisoner is locked in a cell with a heavy door; the guard never actually turns the key, but the door is physically secured. Verdict: clearly unfree, and the unfreedom is negative interference. Case 2: A talented musician grows up in a community with no instruments, no teachers, and no money for either, though no law prevents her from learning music elsewhere. Verdict: her freedom is diminished, but this feels different from Case 1; it's a lack of capacity rather than an obstacle placed in her path. Case 3: A citizen lives under a benevolent monarch who never issues commands, never punishes dissent, and in fact provides excellent public goods, but retains the legal power to seize property or imprison anyone without trial or appeal. Verdict: she is unfree in a distinctive sense, even though her daily life involves no actual interference. Case 4: A software engineer works for a firm that uses an algorithmic management system. The system never explicitly forbids her from taking breaks, but it continuously adjusts task allocation, performance metrics, and visibility to other employers in real time, so that any deviation from the expected workflow makes her future employment prospects collapse in a way she can observe but cannot contest or predict in advance. Verdict: she is unfree, but I'm unsure whether this is best described as hidden negative interference, as a deficit in positive self-mastery, or as domination without interference. Case 5: In a direct-democracy municipality, every law is put to a popular vote. A minority resident finds that she is consistently outvoted on issues affecting her daily life—zoning, noise ordinances, work-hour regulations—but she retains full formal rights to speak, vote, and exit the jurisdiction. Verdict: I feel she is not dominated in the republican sense, because the power over her is exercised collectively and she participates in its authorization, yet her effective range of choice is severely narrowed. Case 6: A refugee is housed in a camp where exit permits are granted automatically by an algorithm that applies uniform criteria. There is no human official who could arbitrarily deny her request; however, the algorithm's parameters are set by a single administrator who can alter them without notice, public justification, or judicial review. The refugee knows that leaving is technically permitted today, but she cannot know whether it will be permitted tomorrow, and she has no recourse if the rules change against her. Verdict: this feels like republican unfreedom—she lives at the mercy of an arbitrary will—even though the actual gate is never closed and no human being directly interferes with her movement. Case 7: A state introduces a right-to-disconnect law that prohibits employers from sending work-related messages after hours. A junior employee notices that her career advancement depends on her responsiveness, and she chooses to check messages constantly despite the legal protection. Verdict: her negative liberty is protected—the law removes an obstacle—but I hesitate to say her freedom is fully intact, because the social and economic structure still makes her choice heavily constrained.

I keep going back and forth on Case 4 and Case 7: both involve structures that don't physically block action but shape the costs of choice so precisely that resistance seems irrational. Meanwhile, Case 5 and Case 6 both involve collective or automated authority, but I want to say Case 6 involves domination while Case 5 does not. Is there a single principle—perhaps refining the standard three-way distinction, or adding a fourth dimension—that captures all seven verdicts? If so, state it precisely and show which clause handles which case. If not, present the two or three best competing principles, say which verdict each forces me to drop, and explain what each predicts for a new case: a worker whose employment contract includes a loyalty clause enforced not by a court but by an industry-wide reputation algorithm that automatically lowers her credit score and rental eligibility if she is flagged by any participating employer, with no individual employer holding direct power over the flagging mechanism.
