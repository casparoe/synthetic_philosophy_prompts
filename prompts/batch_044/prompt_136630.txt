Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy in retirement, I am struck by institutional practices that seem to carry hidden theoretical baggage. One puzzling case is the design of the post-war international military tribunals convened at Nuremberg and Tokyo: these courts were constituted solely by judges appointed by the Allied powers, applied legal categories—such as crimes against peace and crimes against humanity—that lacked clear basis in the positive international or domestic law binding the defendants when the acts occurred, and rejected the defense of superior orders while declining to examine comparable Allied conduct, including strategic bombing and ongoing colonial occupation. A further asymmetry rarely emphasized is that the Tokyo bench included representatives of European colonial powers—Britain, France, and the Netherlands—whose own imperial presence in Asia went unscrutinized even as Japanese territorial expansion was prosecuted as criminal aggression, and the tribunals derived authority not from any pre-existing international legislature but from the victors’ joint declaration, yet presented their judgments as applications of universal moral-legal principles rather than as impositions of victors’ will.

What implicit theory of law and moral responsibility must such an institutional arrangement presuppose? Does it require a natural-law commitment that certain acts are inherently criminal regardless of enacted statute, or can a modified legal positivism accommodate retroactive punishment once sovereignty has collapsed? And what conception of political legitimacy permits a court composed exclusively of victorious and, in some cases, colonial powers to claim impartial jurisdiction over defeated enemies without undermining its own authority? Please reconstruct the philosophical commitments that make this practice coherent, distinguish those that are strictly necessary from those that merely fit well with it, and indicate where the institutional design is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival theories of justice, sovereignty, and legal morality.
