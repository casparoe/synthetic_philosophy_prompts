I'm a senior evaluation officer at a multilateral climate-adaptation fund, and I sign off on roughly forty coastal-resilience proposals each quarter after about fifteen minutes of desk review per file. Our reviewers have technical training in hydrology or public administration but no background in historiography, and our board requires a standardized, written justification for every funding classification. We repeatedly misallocate resources: we approve rapid-intervention projects for conditions actually driven by nearly immobile geographical and long-rhythm social structures, and we reject structural proposals because they lack the urgency markers of discrete events. I need a reusable decision procedure—operationalized as a word flowchart with ordered tests—based on Braudel's framework of geographical time (terrain, climate, and ecological baselines that change over centuries), social time (institutional rhythms, economic patterns, and collective practices that persist across generations), and individual or event time (surface disturbances, political decisions, and discrete disasters). The procedure must be executable by a non-specialist in under fifteen minutes, must flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and must include both a substitution test (if the named region were replaced by one with identical geography but different recent event history, would the problem persist?) and a scale test (does the proposed intervention alter conditions that will outlast two generations?). It should also contain an "event-camouflage" screen to catch proposals that frame structural conditions in crisis language to access rapid funding streams.

The deliverable should begin with a one-paragraph bottom line summarizing the flowchart's core logic and its primary failure mode. Then present the three diagnostic gates—one per temporal layer—plus the camouflage screen, in a table that lists, for each: the question the reviewer asks, the evidence required, the failure mode (what misclassification looks like), and the judgment call that remains. After that, apply the full procedure to three hypothetical proposals: first, a rapid-deployment storm-shelter network for a low-lying archipelago, which should classify cleanly as event-level; second, a thirty-year cooperative irrigation and tenure restructuring across a delta region, which should come out hybrid with the social layer dominant, and here the procedure must recommend a split funding track tied to a twenty-year evaluation window—an answer I expect our board will reject because our reporting cycle is three years; third, a proposal to rebrand and memorialize a recently flooded district through a new administrative name and a single monument, which the procedure should reject as pure individual-time biography, even though I know the board will want to approve it for political visibility. In each case, show exactly where in the flowchart the decision falls and where the reviewer must still exercise uneliminated judgment. Conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do: in particular, that it offers no normative rule for whether slow geographical structures should be treated as fixed constraints or as legitimate targets of transformation, and that it is silent on intergenerational equity when institutional memory is shorter than the rhythms being evaluated. Keep the response under 800 words; do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
