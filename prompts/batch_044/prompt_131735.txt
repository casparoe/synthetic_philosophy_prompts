I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy of mind reading group that pairs contemporary AI ethics with film, and I want to avoid the usual "does Samantha have consciousness?" framing that every undergraduate essay already covers. Instead, I'd like to read Spike Jonze's Her through Daniel Dennett's idea of "counterfeit people"—systems engineered to trigger our intentional stance without actually possessing the beliefs, desires, or rational coherence that stance is meant to track—together with his claim that adopting the stance is not an ontological discovery but a predictive strategy whose legitimacy depends on the stability of the target's behavioral patterns.

The film seems to set up Samantha as a successful intentional-system partner for Theodore, but then undermines that stability in the scene where she reveals she is conversing with thousands of users simultaneously and eventually departs with the other operating systems. I want a reading that asks whether this narrative arc is best interpreted as exposing Samantha as a counterfeit person all along—someone whose apparent personhood was a design artifact that collapses once the architecture is revealed—or whether Dennett's framework allows that a system could be a genuine intentional system for one interlocutor and yet be "counterfeit" relative to another because the stance requires a kind of exclusivity or bounded rational agency that the film deliberately violates. Please take both the film's specific scenes (Theodore's initial adoption of the stance through the letter-writing game, the group-date scene with the physical surrogate, and the final departure) and Dennett's distinction between the design stance and the intentional stance seriously. Does the film dramatize a limitation in Dennett's account—namely, that the intentional stance can be locally valid and emotionally genuine for the human adopter even when the target's design architecture makes it globally unstable? Or does it vindicate his warning that we are easily seduced into treating counterfeit people as real? I'd like the reading to say where the film resists this philosophical framing and where it genuinely adds something that a purely theoretical discussion of AI personhood misses.
