I have been working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on my own, without formal philosophical training, and I want to present a specific reading at a university philosophy society meeting. The audience will be mostly moral-philosophy graduate students who have encountered Smith in a standard course on the Scottish Enlightenment, two cognitive scientists who study empathy and predictive processing, and one historian of eighteenth-century thought who is protective of textual fidelity. They will be skeptical of a non-specialist reinterpretation and quick to accuse me of either anachronism or of rediscovering Hume under different language.

My thesis is this: Smithian sympathy is not, as the textbooks present it, an imaginative projection in which I place myself in your circumstances and then compare my simulated feeling to yours. Rather, sympathy operates as a direct assessment of emotional coherence between your observed situation and your likely affective response, mediated by the impartial spectator functioning not as an internalized social voice but as a calibration mechanism for emotional prediction error. On this reading, moral judgment does not arise from sympathy; it precedes and structures it, because the spectator sets the prior expectations that make sympathy possible at all. I am not importing modern neuroscience by name, but the structure I describe should sound familiar to the cognitive scientists without being tied to any specific contemporary model.

Please prepare me for the question period. I need the eight or so objections I will actually receive from this particular room, ordered by how damaging they are to my credibility and to the argument itself. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that kind of audience member would put it—whether that is a textual challenge from the historian, a conceptual distinction from the graduate students, or a methodological objection from the scientists. Give me a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and flag precisely where that reply is weakest or where I should concede ground. Identify what I should concede upfront in my opening remarks to lower the temperature. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with a brief note on why it is so dangerous for my position and whether I should try to preempt it or stay silent and hope. Keep everything in the vocabulary of Smith's framework—sympathy, the impartial spectator, propriety, merit, demerit—without asking me to cite page numbers, editions, or secondary literature I could not verify from memory.
