I'm presenting at our philosophy of life and labor working group next Thursday, and I need a prep sheet for the Q&A. The thesis I defend reconstructs Moritz Schlick's well-known 1927 essay on the meaning of life—where he distinguishes activities done for an intrinsic end, which he associates with play, from those done purely as means, which he associates with work—and argues that Schlick's binary misses a category I call "instrumentalized absorption." On my reading, Schlick was right that meaning arises when an activity is pursued for its own sake, but he assumed that the boundary between play and work is phenomenologically stable. I claim that modern cognitive labor—creative coding, grant-driven research, "passion" careers, and gamified productivity—produces a hybrid: it borrows the felt quality of absorption and spontaneity that Schlick attributes to play, yet it remains structurally subordinate to external metrics, deliverables, and institutional evaluation. The result is not meaningful work but a distinctive form of meaning-erosion that Schlick's framework cannot diagnose because it looks like play from the inside and like work from the outside. I do not claim Schlick anticipated this; I claim his diagnosis needs a third term to remain adequate to post-industrial labor.

The audience is a mixed graduate seminar with precisely these commitments: two logical-positivism historians who have worked through Schlick's essay in detail and will defend the standard interpretation against anachronism; three philosophers of work who are skeptical of any essentialist distinction between labor and leisure; several well-being and practical-philosophy researchers who treat "meaningful work" as a measurable psychological construct; and one sociologist of post-industrial labor who is likely to ask whether my category is just a redescription of alienated labor with new jargon. They are not hostile, but they care deeply about historical fidelity, phenomenological plausibility, and whether my claim has empirical traction. I want the objections ordered by how damaging they actually are for this room—not generic philosophy objections—each phrased in the voice of the person who would raise it, with a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft or concedes too much. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, which is probably the historian's charge that Schlick's own text already contains the resources for this distinction and that my "third term" is therefore a misreading presented as an innovation. Finally, tell me how to defend the weakest point in my own position—the instability of the phenomenological boundary—rather than hoping they miss it.

If my framing rests on a confused premise—if Schlick's essay in fact anticipates this very distinction, if "instrumentalized absorption" is incoherent, or if the audience I've described would not actually raise these objections—say so plainly and revise the prep sheet accordingly instead of constructing objections around a false thesis. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want a speaker's Q&A sheet I can use without access to sources during the session.
