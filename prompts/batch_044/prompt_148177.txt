Please explain the conceptual issue of whether generational cohorts such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z constitute real ontological kinds rather than reified statistical aggregates. In your explanation, address the following tension: the properties typically used to define a generation—shared historical experiences, technological socialization, and macroeconomic conditions—are distributed continuously across birth years rather than clustering discretely, which suggests that treating these cohorts as natural kinds may involve committing an ecological fallacy at the ontological level. At the same time, defenders of generational realism might argue that these cohorts function as social kinds with causal powers, analogous to institutional facts, even if they lack essentialist boundaries. Evaluate whether the age-period-cohort identification problem, which demonstrates that cohort effects are mathematically confounded with aging processes and historical period, undermines the claim that generations possess independent causal efficacy, or whether it merely reveals that their causal structure is relational rather than intrinsic. Your answer should clarify whether accepting generations as real kinds requires endorsing a form of ontological pluralism about social entities, or whether a deflationary constructivist account can preserve the practical utility of generational analysis without committing to their mind-independent reality.
