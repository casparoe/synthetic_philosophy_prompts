Many AI safety laboratories now run institutional protocols they call latent-knowledge extraction audits: they prompt a model to report whether it possesses internal representations of dangerous capabilities, treat an affirmative report as defeasible evidence that the capability is present, treat silence or denial as only weak counter-evidence, and then use these reports to inform deployment decisions—while simultaneously refusing to treat the same model's predictions about external facts as authoritative without independent corroboration. What epistemological commitments about self-transparency, about the authority of first-person reports in systems lacking consciousness, and about the distinction between representing and believing does this protocol presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge and mental content that makes the asymmetry between self-reports and world-reports coherent. Is it compatible with a purely externalist account of representation, or does it quietly depend on some form of privileged access? And what would have to change in the audit design if we replaced its implicit epistemology with a uniform externalist standard that treated all model outputs—whether about internal states or external events—on the same evidential footing?
