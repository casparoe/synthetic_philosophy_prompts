I spent three decades studying emergent stability and distributed regulation in ecological systems before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind, and I now find myself holding a cluster of commitments about consciousness that I want audited for coherence before I present them to a reading group. First, I reject the Cartesian Theater in the strongest terms: I do not believe there is any single point or process in the brain where parallel sensory streams converge into one canonical representation that is then displayed to an internal observer. Second, I accept the multiple drafts model of perceptual consciousness—the idea that competing, parallel narratives are continuously generated and that there is no authoritative version of an experience prior to some form of probing or report. Third, despite this rejection of central convergence, I remain convinced that there is a robust phenomenological structure I call the specious present: a temporally extended window of roughly two to three seconds within which conscious experience genuinely presents itself as continuous and unified, rather than as a staccato sequence of discrete frames. Fourth, I am a physicalist, but of a distributed rather than localized kind: I take mental states to be identical with large-scale patterns of neural activation rather than with any particular brain region, single neuron, or localized module. Fifth, I hold that first-person reports about one's own conscious states carry a narrow, non-inferential authority—if I sincerely report that I feel a dull, diffuse ache, that report is incorrigible regarding the presence of that feeling, even if I am entirely mistaken about its cause, location, or proper classification. Sixth, drawing analogically from my former discipline, I conceive of consciousness as a system-level property comparable to homeostatic regulation in an ecosystem: just as an ecosystem maintains functional stability without any single organism directing the whole, consciousness emerges from distributed neural dynamics without a central executive, and it is as mistaken to attribute mental properties to individual neurons as it is to attribute ecosystem health to a single bacterium.

Please examine these six commitments for genuine tension, not merely surface disagreement. I am particularly uncertain whether my endorsement of the specious present and of first-person authority can survive alongside my rejection of any central convergence point and my acceptance of competing drafts; I also wonder whether my ecological analogy commits me to an emergentism that my physicalism should reject, or whether it remains a harmless heuristic. Then indicate which single revision, were I forced to make only one, would dissolve the greatest number of conflicts, and specify exactly what I would have to surrender in exchange.
