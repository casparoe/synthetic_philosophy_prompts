I encountered a summary—presented as "the standard logical resolution" in a philosophy forum thread—of how the surprise exam paradox should be dissolved. The summary states:

"The students apply backward induction correctly. The teacher announces a surprise exam for the coming week, meaning that on the exam morning the students must lack any deductive basis for expecting it that day. From this, the students prove that Friday is impossible: if the exam reached Friday without occurring, they would know it was Friday and thus not be surprised. By iterating the same reasoning, they eliminate Thursday, Wednesday, and so on, leaving no possible day. The error in popular treatments is to salvage the paradox by saying that Wednesday could still be surprising despite the reasoning. In fact, the correct resolution is that the teacher's announcement is self-defeating under common-knowledge reasoning: once it is common knowledge that all rational agents will perform the backward induction, there is no day on which the surprise condition can be satisfied. Consequently, no rational agent can sincerely issue such an announcement, and the students should have rejected not the exam's occurrence but the coherence of the premise itself. This is structurally analogous to Moore's paradox applied to future-tense predictions—asserting 'p, but I do not believe p' is incoherent, just as asserting 'there will be a surprise exam next week' becomes incoherent once the conditions for surprise are common knowledge."

Please audit this claim by claim. Which parts accurately reflect a genuine resolution in the literature—such as the structure of the backward-induction argument, the idea that Wednesday might still be surprising, or appeals to common knowledge? Which parts conflate distinct resolutions, overstate a modest thesis, or present a standard objection as if it were the established view? Specifically, does the conclusion that the premise is incoherent follow from the reasoning given, or does it confuse a failure of the students' deduction with a logical impossibility in the teacher's announcement? Is the comparison to Moore's paradox apt, or does it misapply a feature of present-tense belief attribution to a predictive, future-directed statement? And does the summary fairly represent what defenders of the "Wednesday is still surprising" response actually argue, or does it mischaracterize their position as a mere salvage attempt?
