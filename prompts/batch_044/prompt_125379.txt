Write a dialogue between two people who take Benjamin Franklin's moral-perfection project seriously but disagree about what he actually meant by perfecting virtue. One charcter should defend the view that Franklin's ordering of the thirteen virtues—especially putting Temperance first—forms a kind of dependancy graph: you need clear-headedness before you can practise Silence or Resolution properly, so the weekly rotation is just pedagogical scaffolding for hierachical mastery. The other should argue that Franklin's lifelong failure with Humility, which he admits he never really got right, proves he never believed in linear progress; instead the rotation is distributed practice, and perfection is asymptotic improvement rather than a state you reach. Make them respond to each other properly—no strawmen, no talking past one another. Then have one propose a weird twist: Franklin's little black book, where he recorded daily faults, was basically an accidental prototype of quantified-self tracking, and that writing a failure down acts like a moral-licensing payment—once you've paid by recording it, you're more likely to recieve the same fault tomorrow. The other should defend Franklin against this without just dismissing it outright. Keep the tone casual, with real back-and-forth, and don't treat this like a formal essay—there are a couple of typos in the prompt itself, so just roll with it.
