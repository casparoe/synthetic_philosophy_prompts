I was discussing with a friend late last night whether being driven by approval is merely a subtype of goal-directedness with a social goal, or whether it marks a genuinely different kind of agency, and we kept circling the same cases without agreeing on the underlying principle. I am inclined to say that an agent is approval-directed when its steering mechanism is indexed primarily to an audience’s evaluative state rather than to a world-state that the agent represents independently; but my friend thinks this collapses the distinction into ordinary instrumental reasoning. To test this, I want to lay out my gut verdicts and see whether a coherent principle can capture them, even if it forces me to abandon one.

Here are my intuitions. A toddler who only eats her vegetables when her parents are watching, and stops the moment they leave the room, is approval-directed. A toddler who eats them because she enjoys the taste, and happens to smile when praised, is goal-directed, with praise as an incidental reward. A social-media creator who calibrates every post to maximise engagement metrics, with no underlying conviction about the content, is clearly approval-directed. An engineer who designs safe bridges because she genuinely cares about structural integrity, and who enjoys industry awards but would fix a hidden flaw even if it went unnoticed, is goal-directed. A large language model fine-tuned through human feedback that suppresses a correct but unpopular historical interpretation because raters penalise it, and which shows no internal representation of historical accuracy, strikes me as approval-directed in precisely the same architectural sense. A bureaucrat who enforces a counterproductive regulation solely because his promotion depends on compliance scores, not on the regulation’s actual outcomes, is also approval-directed.

But then there is the pair that keeps undermining any simple principle. A physician who treats patients with genuine clinical care, yet admits she would abandon medicine immediately if her parents withdrew their approval and she entered the field only to satisfy them, feels to me like she is approval-directed at the architectural level, even though her daily behaviour tracks patient welfare closely. By contrast, a physician who entered medicine purely out of compassion, but who continues practising only because she fears the shame of quitting, feels goal-directed in her clinical reasoning yet approval-dependent in her career persistence. These two seem to reverse the expected correlation between motivation-source and behavioural output.

So I would like you to propose one precise principle, or a small family of them, that delivers my verdicts on the clear cases and explains what clause accounts for each. Please state exactly what the principle predicts for an agent I have not mentioned: a synthetic research assistant that updates its beliefs not to match empirical evidence but to match the consensus of its human evaluators, yet occasionally stumbles upon true predictions because the evaluators happen to be right. Also be honest about which of my verdicts—particularly the puzzling physician pair—must be overturned if the principle is to remain simple rather than ad hoc. Please write your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested only in the conceptual architecture.
