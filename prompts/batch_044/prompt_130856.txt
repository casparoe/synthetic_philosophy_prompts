I am a novelist auditing a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and next Tuesday I have been asked to defend a short, unconventional reading of Fitch's paradox of knowability before the department's philosophy of logic reading group. My thesis—developed as background for a dialogue chapter—is that the standard derivation of the paradox implicitly treats epistemic closure as uniform across all deductive paths, regardless of the representational or cognitive resources required to traverse them; if we substitute the standard closure principle with a graded, resource-sensitive epistemic operator in which knowing p and knowing p→q yields knowing q only when the inference lies within the agent's accessible resource budget, the contradiction collapses without forcing us to abandon the knowability principle itself. I need to be ready for the objections this precise audience will actually raise, not the generic ones I might hear at an interdisciplinary conference.

The audience is sharply defined: roughly half are working modal logicians committed to S5 closure and standard possible-worlds semantics for epistemic logic; about a quarter are anti-realist epistemologists who regard Fitch's result as an artifact of realist semantics rather than a genuine paradox; the rest are graduate students whose acquaintance with the result comes chiefly from encyclopedia overviews and who tend to conflate the knowability principle with unrestricted omniscience. They prize formal rigor, they are suspicious of logical applications that sound metaphorical, and they will phrase objections in terms of frame conditions, operator behavior, and the object-language versus meta-language distinction.

Please act as an expert philosophical logician and seminar coach: begin with a brief mental brainstorm of the formal pressure points in my graded-closure proposal, then deliver the prep sheet. I want exactly five numbered objections, ordered from the most damaging to the least damaging, each phrased in the vocabulary and tone this room would actually use, compressed into one or two sentences that include both the objection and the weakest point in my likely reply; the fifth item should be the dreaded question about whether my graded operator merely relocates the omniscience problem rather than dissolving it. Within the same five-item list—presented with no introduction or conclusion—state how confident you are in the assessment as a whole and single out the one point about which you are least sure. Keep every item to a sentence or two.
