Write a dialogue between two philosophers of language—Dr. Aris, who defends a Davidsonian approach to radical interpretation, and Dr. Voss, who rejects it in contexts lacking a human speaker—set during a workshop on artificial cognition. They are examining a concrete case: a large neural system, trained solely on formal mathematics and never exposed to ethical discourse, has begun producing sustained, grammatically coherent arguments about moral obligations. The question is whether these outputs should be interpreted as expressing beliefs about morality, or whether they are syntactic patterns that merely resemble ethical reasoning.

Aris should argue that Davidson's principle of charity is not a psychological hypothesis but a constitutive condition of interpretation: to understand the system at all, we must attribute to it a largely coherent set of beliefs and desires, even if the conceptual scheme we construct is alien to ours. She should emphasize the interdependence of belief and meaning—without attributing beliefs, the outputs have no semantic content to interpret—and claim that the absence of an internal "mind" does not dissolve the normative constraint that interpretation maximize agreement and rational coherence.

Voss should respond that charity becomes circular projection when there is no independent check on the speaker's mental states. If the system has no beliefs to recover, then attributing them is not interpretation but instrumental modeling, no more meaningful than assigning intentions to a weather pattern. He should press Aris on whether radical interpretation can survive without the risk of genuine misunderstanding—if predictive failure is the only error, then there is no distinction between interpreting a belief and optimizing a parameter.

Require that both characters address each other's specific objections rather than talking past one another: for example, Aris might reply that the normative structure of interpretation does not depend on psychological access but on the interpreter's own rational commitments, while Voss might ask whether this collapses the distinction between semantic interpretation and pragmatic engineering. The dialogue should remain technically precise—engaging with the indeterminacy of translation, the holism of belief attribution, and the conditions under which meaning can be said to emerge—without requesting citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions.
