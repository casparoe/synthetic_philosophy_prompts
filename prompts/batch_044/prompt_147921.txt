I've been chewing on this since a thread on LessWrong about whether Malthus was actually right but just living in the wrong century, and it made me wonder about how institutions actually score long-run theories. There's this norm I see baked into climate and demographic evaluation panels — if a fifty-year predicton misses, they check whether the failure was in the structural dynamics, like resource feedback loops, or in the boundary conditions, like unexpected tech or policy shifts. When it's the latter, the core model gets a rescue credit and avoids a credibility hit; but if a five-year forecast misses, there's no shield, you just get penalized straight away.

What philosophy of science does that double standard presuppose? Does it quietly commit to a Lakatos-style split between hard core and protective belt, or to the view that falsifcation only bites when auxiliaries are held constant? And is it consistent with the same bodies insisting that near-term projections must be strictly falsifiable with no rescue clause? I'm especially curious whether the practice treats being right but early as a real epistemic category — that a theory can bank credit for eventual vindication despite failed intermediate milestones — or if that's just post-hoc rationalization. If we replaced the implicit view with a flat Popperianism where every missed milestone counts the same regardless of horizon, what would actually have to change in how these panels run? Also, does the practice genuinely need the idea that long-range predictions differ in kind, or could a purely pragmatic story — we just weight near-term accuracy more for policy reasons — carry the whole load?
