Write the opening remarks—about four to five minutes spoken—for an interdisciplinary workshop titled “Performance and Understanding: Two Inversions.” The room is split between evolutionary biologists and philosophers of biology on one side, and machine-learning researchers and cognitive scientists on the other; roughly half will recognize the philosophical architecture immediately, while the other half should feel welcomed into it rather than lectured. The structural theme is the paired inversion associated with Darwin and Turing: Darwin showed that design competence—exquisite adaptation and functional fit—can accumulate without any comprehending agent directing it, while Turing showed that behavior indistinguishable from comprehension can be produced by mechanisms that do not themselves understand. Your remarks must set a real ground rule for the day: neither camp may smuggle in a hidden mind—biologists should not treat evolutionary success as tacit proof of an implicit designer, and engineers should not treat fluent performance as tacit proof of inner understanding—without letting that rule sound like a reprimand. To make the symmetry concrete for non-specialists, introduce a brief original framing device, such as comparing the “blind watchmaker” to a “blind interlocutor” shaped by selection pressure across millions of interactions rather than by grasping meaning, so that both disciplines see their object in the other’s mirror. Keep the tone collegial, precise, and free of bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write in American English.
