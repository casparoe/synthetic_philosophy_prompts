Write the full desiderata that any adequate theory linking brain size, or a closely related structural neural metric, to moral status must satisfy. I want the specification itself, not a bibliography or a survey of the literature. Start with the boundary cases and phenomena it must cover: human ontogenetic growth and decline; pathological extremes such as macrocephaly and microcephaly; interspecies comparisons involving radically different architectures like cephalopods, corvids, and cetaceans; and the hypothetical case of an artificially scaled brain that preserves connectivity ratios and neuronal morphology but varies absolute volume by orders of magnitude. For each, state the result the theory must yield and why failure there counts as a substantive error rather than an acceptable anomaly. Next, lay out the conceptual distinctions the theory must preserve—between possessing moral status and the degree of that status, between intrinsic relevance and proxy tracking, and between individual-level metrics and species-normalized baselines—and flag which pairs appear jointly unsatisfiable, especially if one requires both that size is never intrinsically relevant and that it tracks some cognitive capacity widely taken to ground status. Then list the empirical constraints from comparative neuroscience and evolutionary biology it cannot violate, particularly allometric scaling relationships and documented dissociations between absolute neuron number and behavioral flexibility. Identify the single desideratum that eliminates the greatest number of popular positions. Finally, briefly score a pure capacity-based view, a strict size-irrelevance view, and a normalization view that weights neural scale against species-typical architecture; keep the scoring terse, since the criteria are the deliverable. Frame this for a discussion group that values both philosophical rigor and actual biology, and do not ask me to read particular papers.
