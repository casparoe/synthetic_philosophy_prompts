I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on interpretations of quantum mechanics, and over the past several weeks we have worked through the Everettian framework without reaching consensus about what subjective experience ought to look like if decoherence genuinely produces branching worlds with equal ontological weight. One case that has generated more sustained disagreement than I anticipated is the quantum suicide thought experiment—not as a practical proposal, but as a limit case for first-person expectation. The specific tension we keep returning to is whether the argument establishes anything like subjective immortality, or whether it collapses once one notices that biological death is rarely a single binary quantum measurement but rather a continuous process of decay and decoherence that might eventually terminate consciousness even across every accessible branch.

For this session I would like a poem that does not merely describe the apparatus or narrate the paradox, but uses its formal architecture to model the branching logic itself. Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains, strict rhyme scheme—spoken from the position of the experimenter inside the device. One refrain should articulate the expectation of subjective continuation; the other should articulate the expectation of termination. The refrain pattern should map onto the repetition of the trial across successive worlds, and the final couplet, where the two refrains finally meet, should stage the selection problem: only the branches in which the speaker survives can complete the poem, yet those surviving branches may carry exponentially diminishing measure. To reflect the distinction between discrete quantum outcomes and continuous biological decay, allow the rhyme or meter to fracture in the lines corresponding to the dead branches—those stanzas should feel truncated or syntactically incomplete—so that the poem's own coherence as readable text depends on the lines that persist. The result should be technically precise enough that someone familiar with the Everettian argument and with the standard objections, particularly the worry that aging is not a quantum measurement subject to branching, would recognize the conceptual architecture rather than merely the vocabulary.
