After a long argument with a friend last night about original and derived intentionality, I want to adjudicate a sharper version of the same dispute between two colleagues, Maya and Julian, who were arguing over Dennett’s minimal hypothetical system—sometimes called a two-bitser, with only two binary internal registers—and whether it undermines Searle’s distinction between original and derived intentionality.

Maya opened by saying that Searle’s distinction collapses once we take Dennett’s intentional stance seriously: even the two-bitser is predicted more reliably when we ascribe beliefs and desires than when we describe its physical transitions. If predictive success is the criterion, then the two-bitser genuinely has intentional states, and since human brains are just more complex predictive targets, there is no principled line between the two-bitser’s “derived” states and our “original” ones; the distinction is one of degree, not kind.

Julian replied that this conflates heuristic utility with ontological presence. Dennett never claimed that ascription creates intrinsic aboutness; he claimed it tracks real patterns. But Searle’s point is precisely that derived intentionality—like the meaning of a stop sign—depends on an original source, usually a conscious mind. The two-bitser’s “beliefs” are observer-relative in a way that my belief about coffee is not. Predictive success explains why we talk that way; it does not turn fiction into fact.

Maya countered that Searle never explains how we access this “intrinsic aboutness” in others. First-person access gives me my own original intentionality, but for everyone else—including other humans—I have only behavioral evidence. So if I apply the intentional stance to you, I am doing exactly what I do with the two-bitser. Searle must either admit that original intentionality in others is also an inference, which collapses the distinction, or claim that biological similarity guarantees it, which is mere species chauvinism.

Julian answered that biological similarity is not chauvinism but causal structure. Original intentionality depends on specific neurobiological causal powers, not evolutionary pedigree per se. A synthetic brain with those powers might have it; a two-bitser lacks them regardless. But he granted her an edge case: if the two-bitser evolves naturally, without a designer, then there is no conscious agent to serve as the original source, yet we might still predict it using the intentional stance. This actually strengthens Searle’s view: the two-bitser’s states, if predictive, are not derived from anyone’s intentions—they are not derived intentionality at all, just useful fiction. Meanwhile, human original intentionality does not need a designer because brains generate it intrinsically.

Maya responded that this makes original intentionality depend on evolutionary contingency in an arbitrary way. If we grow a functionally identical brain in a vat from synthetic tissue, Julian’s logic seems to deny it original intentionality because it lacks evolutionary pedigree—yet it behaves identically. Conversely, if natural selection produces a system functionally identical to a two-bitser but with more registers, Julian grants it original intentionality solely because it is biological. The boundary appears drawn by biology’s brand, not by any functional or ontological feature. Dennett’s point is that once we see intentionality as a real pattern in predictive dynamics, these origin stories become irrelevant.

Julian clarified: a vat-grown brain replicates the causal powers, so it would have original intentionality—the source is the tissue’s functional organization, not its evolutionary history. But the two-bitser, whether evolved or designed, lacks the relevant causal architecture: it has no integrated information structure that produces subjective experience or intrinsic aboutness. The key is not “biology” as a label but the presence of a system that generates first-person intentional content. Her vat example actually proves his point: functional replication of the right architecture is sufficient. The two-bitser fails not because it was designed, but because two binary registers cannot instantiate that architecture, no matter how they arise.
