Design a 45-minute in-class activity for roughly 18 graduate students in philosophy of action that makes them experience, rather than merely discuss, the structural dynamics of escalation of commitment. Use a modified dollar-auction protocol: the prize is not money but the collective authority to fix the interpretive framework — call them Schema I and Schema II — for analyzing a deliberately ambiguous case study distributed beforehand. Bids take the form of publicly recorded, non-recoverable commitments to specific premises within one schema. The highest bidder wins sole authority to apply that schema to the case; all participants, including the winner, pay their final bid cost (the commitment stands regardless of outcome). The rules must make advance coordination to avoid bidding difficult or costly, and you must specify materials, turn order, and a reputation or payoff structure that does not use real money but makes the costs feel binding. After describing the activity, provide a debrief plan (maximum 15 minutes) that guides students to distinguish rational continuation — where the expected value of the decision right exceeds the marginal commitment cost plus sunk costs — from pathological escalation driven by loss aversion or identity-protection, pointing to at least one structural feature of the auction they likely overlooked until it was irreversible. Conclude by identifying one dimension of philosophical escalation — such as the way theoretical commitments become identity-constituting over long timescales — that this single-session simulation cannot capture and that you, as facilitator, must supply in closing remarks.
