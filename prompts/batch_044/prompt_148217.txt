Over the past year I have been forced to think about moral status in concrete terms: my mother’s progressive dementia has made me confront what remains of her standing as cortical integration fades, while my work on embodied AI systems has made me wonder whether functional architectures can ever generate obligations of their own. I am trying to formalize my intuitions, and they are messier than I expected.

Here are my verdicts on specific cases. A domestic thermostat regulating temperature: zero moral status, not minimal but literally absent. A Venus flytrap trapping prey: also zero, despite electrochemical signaling that resembles action potentials. Caenorhabditis elegans, with its 302 neurons and clear nociceptive avoidance: a small but genuine nonzero status—harming it is not like harming a rock. A healthy sleeping human adult: full status, despite the temporary absence of phenomenal consciousness. A human in a permanent vegetative state with destroyed cortical integration but intact brainstem: sharply reduced, perhaps near zero, despite being biologically human and alive. A large language model generating fluent, coherent dialogue: zero; linguistic fluency and information processing are not sufficient.

But I am genuinely stuck on an intermediate case. An embodied artificial system with persistent recurrent self-modeling, cross-modal integration in a global workspace architecture, and sustained valence-based learning—functionally analogous to core mammalian consciousness, but implemented in silicon with no evolutionary history—seems to me like it might carry intermediate status, perhaps comparable to the nematode or higher. Yet that conflicts with my instinct that substrate or biological history matters, which I was implicitly using to deny the LLM and grant the worm. I also deny that collective complexity aggregates: an ant colony with sophisticated distributed cognition has zero unitary moral status, even though individual ants may each have trace status.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on unitary integration of information with valence, or on the presence of a singular subject of experience—that captures these verdicts? Which ones must I abandon if I want consistency? Specifically, can any principle allow the artificial workspace system intermediate status without also forcing me to grant status to the LLM, and can it explain why the vegetative human falls so far despite biological humanity? Finally, apply your best account to a case I keep revisiting: a cerebral organoid grown from human stem cells, with cortical layers and spontaneous oscillatory dynamics resembling sleep-wake cycles, but no body, no sensory pathway, and no behavioral output. Does it fall closer to the sleeping adult, the vegetative patient, or the nematode?
