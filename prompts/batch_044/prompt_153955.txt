I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I am writing in a fairly precise register because I want the conceptual structure to be clear. At work we run distributed infrastructure agents that adjust resource allocation across regions using real-time telemetry. Each agent operates within authorization boundaries encoded in policy files, and it can initiate changes without a human in the loop if certain statistical conditions are met. Last week one agent reconfigured load-balancing rules across three regions. The change was within its authorization, but it combined with a recently updated anomaly-detection model—maintained by an entirely different team—to trigger a cascading failure that took a non-critical service offline for about six hours. When we traced the chain, we found that the rollback protocol had been disabled by a maintenance script executed months earlier, and that the original authorization policy was authored by an engineer who left the company two years ago. Every person currently on the relevant teams either followed correct procedures in their own role or was not present when the conditions for failure were established.

I am not asking for legal liability, HR process, or organizational blame. I want to understand, at a conceptual level, whether this is genuinely a situation in which no one can properly be said to have made a wrong choice, or whether I am overlooking some structural feature that allows the wrongness—or its absence—to be located despite the distribution of actions across time, teams, and automated components. Specifically, does the fact that the failure emerged from the interaction of independently correct parts change whether anyone can be held to have done something they should not have done? And is the absence of a human present at the exact moment of decision decisive, or merely one incidental feature among others? Please walk through the case concretely: identify the distinctions that do the philosophical work, explain what each possibility implies about where fault lies, and point out which feature of the scenario is actually carrying the weight—so that I can see what would happen to the analysis if that feature were removed.
