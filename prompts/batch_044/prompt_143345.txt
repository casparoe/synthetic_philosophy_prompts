Question: State the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem with its standard conditions. Then consider a seven-member faculty hiring committee that uses the following two-round runoff: in round one each member submits a complete linear ranking over candidates A, B, and C; if one candidate receives a majority of first-place votes, she is hired immediately, otherwise the two candidates with the greatest number of first-place votes advance to round two, where members vote by simple majority. A committee member claims that this mechanism is strategy-proof because, whatever happens in round one, round two is just pairwise majority voting, and pairwise majority is immune to strategic misrepresentation. Assess this claim. In your answer, identify whether and how strategic manipulation can occur across the two rounds, and specify exactly which conditions of the theorem are satisfied by this mechanism.

Student's answer: The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem establishes that for any voting rule with at least three possible outcomes, if the rule satisfies universal domain, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship, and if it is onto the set of alternatives, then the rule cannot be strategy-proof—there will always exist at least one preference profile at which some voter can obtain a better outcome by misreporting her true preferences. The result is essentially the strategic-voting counterpart to Arrow's impossibility theorem, since Arrow showed that no social welfare function can satisfy IIA and Pareto optimality without being dictatorial, and Gibbard and Satterthwaite demonstrated that the same structural contradiction reappears when we allow voters to manipulate outcomes directly rather than aggregating preferences honestly.

Applying this to the committee: the two-round runoff operates over three candidates, so the theorem applies. The colleague is correct that round two, being a pairwise comparison between two candidates, is strategy-proof—this follows from May's theorem, which shows that simple majority rule between two options is anonymous, neutral, and strategy-proof. However, the colleague errs in extending this property to the mechanism as a whole. Strategy-proofness must hold for the entire rule, not for a single stage in isolation. A voter with true preferences A ≻ B ≻ C can manipulate the first round. Suppose she expects B and C to advance to the runoff, and she knows that B would defeat A in that pairwise contest. By submitting the ranking C ≻ A ≻ B in round one, she reduces B's first-place support, ensures that B is eliminated (since B now has the fewest first-place votes), and sends A and C to the runoff, where she can then vote sincerely for A over C. Thus she secures her first-choice outcome through misrepresentation in round one, even though she behaves sincerely in round two. The mechanism is therefore manipulable.

The conditions of the theorem are all satisfied: universal domain holds because members can submit any linear ordering; the rule is onto because, depending on the profile, any of A, B, or C can win; and it is clearly non-dictatorial since no single member determines the outcome. The presence of IIA is also satisfied here, because the winner of the runoff depends only on the pairwise comparison in that round, not on the presence or ranking of the eliminated candidate. Consequently, the mechanism violates strategy-proofness, exactly as the theorem predicts.
