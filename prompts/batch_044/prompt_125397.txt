I'm trying to sort out my gut intuitions about perpetrator explanations, kind of in the weeds of the Browning vs Goldhagen debate but stretching it past just the Holocaust. Case one: the reserve policemen in Battalion 101 shooting civilians in Poland. Verdict: mostly peer pressure and authority, not deep eliminationist belief -- the ordinary men story. Case two: a Rwandan local official who lived next to Tutsi families for years but then organized roadblocks once propoganda started. Verdict: culture and ideology activated something dormant, not pure situation. Case three: an Argentine conscript in the Dirty War who tortured dissidents in a basement but went home to be an apolitical dad. Verdict: instutional scripting and moral distancing, situation dominates. Case four: a German civilian in 1941 writing unsolicited letters to authorities demanding harsher treatment of Jews. Verdict: that's culture and individual character, situation can't cover it because there's zero direct pressure. But here's the pair that messes me up: South African death squad officers applying for TRC amnesty. For the ones who were conscripted into the unit I mostly buy the situational story. But for others who specifically volunteered for counter-insurgency squads, I still lean situation because the institutional culture was so totalizing, yet I feel like volunteering should point to ideology. So is there a principle that captures all four verdicts, or close? And what does it say about a case I haven't listed -- say, a perpetrator who participates enthusiastically at first but then frames the whole thing as coercion in front of a truth commission? Which verdict do I have to drop if I want a simpler principle, and does the principle predict anything about perpetrators in transitional justice contexts more generally?
