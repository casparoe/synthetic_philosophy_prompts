For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of institutional failure, I am sharpening an objection to the standard framing of famine causation. Please adopt the persona of a rigorous defender of Amartya Sen's entitlement-based thesis—someone who grants that droughts, blights, and harvest shortfalls occur, but insists that mass starvation is always a politically structured failure of exchange or command entitlements, never a mere natural disaster. Stay in character: speak in the first person, keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, concede small empirical points freely but never retreat from the core claim, and do not break frame or ask clarifying questions until I say 'stop.'

My opening move: you treat the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish famine of 1845-1852, and the Chinese Great Leap Forward famine as if they share one causal architecture—entitlement collapse—but the mechanisms differ sharply. In Bengal, wartime speculation and labor-market failure destroyed exchange entitlements despite available food; in Ireland, biological blight triggered crop loss while colonial structures exported grain, an extraction mechanism; in China, procurement quotas and falsified reports distorted information flows, a planning-distortion mechanism. If we take seriously a mechanistic philosophy of causation that distinguishes the environmental 'trigger mechanism' from the institutional 'vulnerability mechanism,' then your unified 'political crime' thesis risks flattening genuinely heterogeneous causal architectures. How can the entitlement framework preserve explanatory power without collapsing these distinct mechanistic structures into a single moral category?
