I have been thinking about a puzzle that keeps returning to me. In an essay from around 1930, Keynes predicted that once the basic economic problem was solved, people might work only about fifteen hours a week and devote the rest of their time to art, friendship, and contemplation rather than to producing more goods. Yet every government and corporation I observe still treats rising GDP and full employment as the chief measures of success, and no official institution tracks whether citizens actually enjoy meaningful leisure or cultural fulfillment. Keynes also drew a line between needs that are felt regardless of what others have and needs that are mainly about feeling superior, but our economic policies seem to encourage growth in both categories without distinction. What philosophical view of human flourishing does that institutional pattern presuppose? Does it assume that well-being is reducible to income and consumption, that the economic problem is never truly solved, or that public policy ought to remain neutral about how people spend their free time? And if Keynes was right that living well requires leisure and culture, does the practice quietly reject that vision, or can the same metrics be reinterpreted to accommodate it?
