I am preparing a conceptual-methods paper for a comparative-politics seminar and have built an argument about democratic backsliding that now strikes me as suspicious: it seems to prove either that the concept is vacuous or that it applies to almost every institutional reform. I want to diagnose the failure precisely—whether it is an equivocation, a scope shift, a hidden normative premise, or a valid chain with an unacceptable cost.

Here is the argument:

(1) Define democratic backsliding strictly as institutional change that reduces either the competitiveness of elections or the vertical and horizontal accountability of rulers.

(2) Historical institutionalism identifies four formal modes of institutional change—displacement, layering, drift, and conversion—which describe how rules evolve without prescribing democratic or anti-democratic outcomes.

(3) Each of these four modes can, in specific configurations, diminish competitiveness or accountability: for instance, layering a partisan disciplinary chamber onto an existing judiciary (a form of conversion) weakens horizontal accountability.

(4) The transformations in Hungary (the 2010–2014 constitutional overhaul and media-council restructuring), Poland (the 2015–2023 judicial disciplinary mechanisms), Turkey (the 2017 presidential-system referendum and subsequent decrees), and recent U.S. state-level election-administration statutes are all best analyzed as layering or conversion within pre-existing legal frameworks, not as extra-legal seizures of power.

(5) Therefore, by (1) together with (3), these cases do qualify as democratic backsliding.

(6) Yet the same four modes describe the New Deal administrative expansion, the UK Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011, and the post-war design of the German Federal Constitutional Court—changes that are rarely classified as backsliding, despite altering prior structures of accountability.

(7) To exclude those latter cases, one must add an external normative filter—typically the intent to entrench incumbents or the absence of authentic democratic deliberation—because the formal mode alone cannot distinguish them.

(8) Such a filter is epistemically opaque: the relevant intentions are observationally equivalent to ordinary partisan disagreement, and in practice the filter is applied selectively by scholars and international monitors according to the government in question.

(9) So either we reject the filter and accept that backsliding encompasses all institutional change that shifts accountability (which proves far too much), or we retain it and concede that "backsliding" tracks not an institutional form but a partisan judgment about whose legal reforms are legitimate.

(10) I am reluctant to abandon (1), since I believe there should be a non-circular institutional criterion, but I also reject (9)'s cynical conclusion. Which step should I reject? Identify the precise flaw, explain why the step looked plausible, and state what giving it up costs my broader framework—particularly whether I must surrender procedural neutrality.

Please provide a clear diagnosis. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the step number you reject and, in a short phrase, the central cost—e.g., "Verdict: step 2, costs pure proceduralism".
