For my doctoral work on social ontology, I am examining how formal models of convention behave when applied reflexively to the agents who instantiate them. Please write a satirical, deadpan bureaucratic memo of approximately 600 words, formatted as numbered policy clauses, issued by a fictional "Bureau of Linguistic Coordination". The Bureau should enforce David Lewis's conditions for convention—regularity in behaviour, conditional preference for conformity, common knowledge, and arbitrariness of the coordinating solution—with excessive literalism: for instance, requiring citizens to file "common-knowledge disclosure forms" before adopting a greeting ritual, or treating dialect divergence as a contractual breach requiring arbitration. The satire should target the tendency to treat living norms as pure coordination problems while ignoring power asymmetries and moral motivation, but the humour must come from genuinely extending Lewisian logic rather than misrepresenting it. Inject the following niche conceptual twist: include a directive addressing "embedded convention agents"—speakers whose dispositions to conform are themselves constituted by the very convention they are meant to coordinate on—and have the Bureau's proposed remedy (a "pre-conventional reset" of speaker dispositions) collapse into paradox, since resetting the agents dissolves the common-knowledge conditions required for the reset to be recognised as conventional. Maintain an earnest administrative voice that never winks; an invented senior administrator may be named, but no living person should be targeted.

In a brief separate commentary, please distinguish what is settled in Lewis's account (the core conditions for convention, the distinction between convention and explicit agreement) from what remains genuinely contested (whether conventions can bind agents who lack the right kind of reasons for conformity, whether arbitrariness undermines normative force, and whether reflexive embedding destabilises the common-knowledge requirement). Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.

End with a one-sentence summary of the memo's central absurdity on its own line, prefixed "Summary:". Use British spelling throughout.
