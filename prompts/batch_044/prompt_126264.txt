I'm trying to formalize an argument I sketched after reading about tight-loose cultural frameworks and some old forum threads on Schelling points, and it now seems to prove that tight cultures are either unstable or epistemically authoritarian. I'd like to know exactly where the slip happens, because I want to defend the view that demanding rule-following can be rationally endorsed even when everyone knows the rules are somewhat arbitrary focal points.

Here is the chain:

(1) Define a culture as "tight" to the extent that it demands internalized rule-following in domains where explicit case-by-case reasoning could, in principle, reach the same cooperative outcome.

(2) The only non-circular justification for imposing such a demand is that the rule serves as a Schelling point: its normative force derives from being the focal point that rational agents can coordinate on without further negotiation.

(3) But if it is common knowledge among the citizens that the rule is merely one arbitrary focal point among many nearby alternatives, then its status as the unique Schelling point is dissolved; common knowledge of arbitrariness undermines the very coordination the rule was meant to secure.

(4) Therefore, a tight culture can maintain its norms only to the degree that citizens do not fully grasp—or do not commonly know—that the norms are arbitrary Schelling points.

(5) From (4) it follows that rational endorsement of tightness is self-defeating: the moment citizens understand and collectively accept why they are following the rules, the rules lose their coordinating power.

(6) So any society that genuinely demands high rule-following faces a dilemma: either it keeps its citizens in rational ignorance about the source of normative authority (which is epistemically authoritarian), or it allows full transparency and watches its coordination structures collapse.

(7) Applying this to concrete domains—traffic norms, language conventions, promise-keeping—implies that every tight norm should dissolve under reflective scrutiny, leaving only loose, calculative societies.

(8) Yet tight cultures persist, function, and are often rationally endorsed by their members; they do not obviously depend on manufactured ignorance.

(9) Hence the argument proves too much. One of the premises must be wrong, but (2) and (8) both feel hard to surrender.
