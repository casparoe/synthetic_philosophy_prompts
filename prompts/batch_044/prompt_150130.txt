I've been thinking about Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the idea that the prospect of human extinction is uniquely catastrophic not mainly because of the deaths it entails, but because it would render pointless or hollow many of our present projects and commitments that derive their meaning from the expectation that they will continue to matter in a future inhabited by people like us. In a discussion with colleagues, someone raised a scenario that Scheffler does not directly address: suppose that biological humanity is indeed extinguished, but our intellectual, artistic, and moral traditions are not lost. Instead, they are preserved and actively extended by non-conscious archival systems or by synthetic successors that possess no phenomenal consciousness, yet maintain full institutional and cultural continuity. The question is whether such a future counts as the kind of "afterlife" that can sustain the value of present activities.

Please describe the most important implications of the afterlife conjecture for evaluating extinction risk in precisely this kind of functional-continuity scenario. In particular, I want to understand whether the conjecture requires that future bearers be conscious subjects of experience, or whether structural continuity of projects is sufficient to avoid the hollowing-out effect; how this distinction should reshape longtermist priorities between preventing biological extinction and ensuring conscious survival; and whether extending the conjecture to cover radically non-human successors strengthens or weakens its claim that our present obligations depend on a shared human future. You need not provide citations or reading lists; I'm working without library access and want to see how far the conceptual architecture can be pushed on its own terms.
