I've been drafting the philosophy sidebar for a feature on how climate scientists revise beliefs when ensemble models conflict—not merely on parameter values, but on which variables and partitions are even well-defined. In working out the logic, I built an argument that Jeffrey conditionalization collapses under this kind of structural uncertainty, and the conclusion now strikes me as implausibly sweeping: it appears to show that Bayesian belief revision is unusable across much of scientific practice. Here is the chain.

(1) Jeffrey's rule requires partitioning the possibility space into cells E₁, ..., Eₙ and assigning direct probabilities qᵢ to each.
(2) In cases of structural uncertainty—where the evidence does not settle which partition is appropriate—the agent assigns probabilities only to sets of partitions, not to cells within any particular one.
(3) Applying Jeffrey's rule then demands selecting one specific partition from that set, but nothing in the evidence or prior justifies one selection over another.
(4) Because different partitions yield different conditioning spaces, the posterior belief distribution depends arbitrarily on the choice of partition.
(5) So if structural uncertainty is genuine, Jeffrey conditionalization produces no unique rational update.
(6) Much scientific measurement—particularly in multi-model climate projections—involves exactly this structural ambiguity about which variables and partitions are correct.
(7) Therefore, either scientists never face genuinely uncertain evidence in this structural sense, or Jeffrey conditionalization is not the correct norm for scientific belief revision.
(8) The first disjunct is false; climate science clearly faces structural ambiguity.
(9) Hence standard Bayesian updating fails for a central domain of scientific practice.

I want to preserve (6) and (8)—they seem descriptively accurate—but I am reluctant to accept (9). Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Is the slip an equivocation between "no unique update" and "no rational update"? Or does the argument illegitimately assume that structural uncertainty must be handled by Jeffrey's rule itself rather than by a generalized imprecise-probability or higher-order framework? If I must choose, I would rather keep Jeffrey's rule applicable to science than deny that structural ambiguity exists, but I want to understand the price of each option.
