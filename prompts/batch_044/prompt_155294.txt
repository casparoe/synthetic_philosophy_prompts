I've noticed a recurring pattern in debates about institutions, ecological management regimes, and automated governance systems. A speaker will claim that a particular structure—say, a long-standing regulatory framework or an environmental monitoring protocol—is bound by a direct normative consideration: that it "ought" to maintain a specific equilibrium or preserve a certain procedural integrity. When pressed on whose consideration this is—which agents' values, commitments, or interests generate the obligation—the speaker denies that it belongs to any current or past individual, and denies that it is merely an instrumental means to human welfare. The reason is said to belong to the structure itself, and it is treated as genuinely constraining: it can override the preferences of everyone currently involved. What's more, it seems to persist even when all the people who originally established the structure are gone, and even when present participants unanimously reject it. The reason does not appear anchored in any mind or will, yet it is invoked as if it carried direct normative weight.

Is there an established philosophical concept that captures this exact pattern—normative reasons sustained by organizational or systemic features rather than by agents, and treated as binding independently of anyone's endorsement? If the nearest established ideas do not quite fit, I want the differences mapped precisely: how this differs from obligations that reduce to social roles, from collective intentions distributed across members, and from purely abstract moral truths. I am especially interested in the boundary conditions—does such a reason survive the complete dissolution of the sustaining system, or does it necessarily collapse into an instrumental claim about human welfare at that point?

Please state how confident you are in your identification and distinctions, and single out the specific claim or boundary case about which you are least certain.
