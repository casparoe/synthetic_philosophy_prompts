Here is a set of commitments I have arrived at in my thinking about time, value, and narrative form, and I want to know whether they form a stable package or whether I am quietly borrowing premises from one domain to patch over contradictions in another.

(1) I take pure time preference—the devaluation of a future good solely because it occurs later—to be irrational in both personal and collective deliberation; temporal distance, by itself, carries no normative weight.

(2) In constructing novels, I routinely assign far greater emotional and causal density to events near the narrative present than to those in a distant epilogue, even when the distant events are objectively more consequential; this is not merely a convention of form but a structural feature of how readers inhabit a story.

(3) I am inclined to think this artistic practice reveals something real about human agency: we do not discount by clock-time alone, but by what I call "narrative proximity"—the ease with which a future state can be imaginatively inhabited and causally traced from one's present standpoint.

(4) From this, I draw a policy conclusion: in climate ethics, social discount rates for harms centuries hence should be near zero, because moral equality is not contingent on the psychological accessibility of those future lives.

(5) Yet in personal ethics I also hold that it can be rational for an individual to save less for her own eighties than for her forties—not because the older self matters less, but because the narrative continuity that binds present deliberation to future welfare genuinely attenuates over long intervals.

The tensions I sense are these. If pure time preference is irrational (1), can "narrative proximity" (3) serve as anything more than a redescription of that same bias? If narrative proximity is a legitimate psychological factor in personal saving (5), why is it illegitimate in climate policy (4)—or is the distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding choice doing all the work, and if so, do I have an independent argument for that boundary? And finally, am I illicitly deriving normative conclusions about intertemporal ethics from the formal requirements of fiction (2), treating a novelist's craft as evidence about rational agency?

Please audit these commitments in order of severity: which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which are only apparently in tension, and what the cheapest revision would cost me—whether giving up (3), weakening (5), or abandoning the analogy between narrative structure and practical reason altogether. Be specific about which commitment pulls against which, and do not treat all five as equally problematic; I want to know where the fault line actually runs.
