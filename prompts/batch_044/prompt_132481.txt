As a retired physicist now working through philosophy of science and epistemology, I have constructed an argument that extends Sleeping Beauty-style self-locating reasoning into the Everettian framework and seems to undermine empirical confirmation altogether. I would like to know exactly where it breaks.

(1) In Sleeping Beauty, upon awakening Beauty should assign credence 1/3 to Heads, because there are three centered worlds—(Heads, Monday), (Tails, Monday), (Tails, Tuesday)—that are subjectively indistinguishable and, ex hypothesi, equiprobable.

(2) The principle generalizes: whenever a rational agent knows she is one of N subjectively indistinguishable observers distributed across worlds or branches, her credence in any particular world should be proportional to the number of copies of herself it contains.

(3) In the Everettian interpretation, every quantum measurement produces branches with subjectively indistinguishable copies of me observing every physically permitted outcome; for any macroscopic observation I now make, there exists a branch containing a copy of me with an identical mental state but the opposite result.

(4) By (2), since these branches are symmetric with respect to my first-person perspective and vastly outnumber any privileged "actual" branch, I should distribute my credence nearly uniformly across them, irrespective of the Born-rule weights or dynamical laws that produced the branching structure.

(5) Consequently, no measurement can update my beliefs about the quantum state, because every outcome is realized in some copy, and self-locating reasoning requires me to treat all copies as equally credible.

(6) But this is absurd: quantum mechanics is empirically well-confirmed, and I plainly do update on experimental results.

(7) Therefore the self-locating principle in (2) must be false.

Yet if (2) is rejected, the standard "thirder" resolution of Sleeping Beauty collapses, leaving Beauty with no explanation for her update upon awakening. Which step should I reject—perhaps (2) overgeneralizes from epistemic possibilities to physical branches, or (4) illicitly applies a uniform prior where a measure is required—and what does that rejection cost me? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (6) if possible, but I want to understand the price of doing so.
