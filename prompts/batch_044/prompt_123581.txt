I have recently found myself reflecting on the nature of moral excellence in a rather personal way. A close colleague of mine has, over the past several years, systematically relinquished every personal project, friendship, and form of leisure that does not directly serve humanitarian aims, and while I deeply admire her consistency, I find myself unable to call her life unequivocally the best kind of human life. This has led me to a conceptual puzzle that I hope you can help me untangle—not by surveying literature, but by working through the structure of the idea itself, since I need clarity for my own thinking rather than a map of who said what.

The familiar objection—often associated with Susan Wolf—is that moral saints are deficient in personal richness, humor, or non-moral projects. But I want to press deeper: is there a contradiction built into the very concept of "maximal moral goodness"? Suppose we define a moral saint as an agent who optimizes moral value without remainder, treating every practical choice as an occasion for moral maximization. In doing so, such an agent appears to forfeit what I will call "first-personal moral authority"—the reflective capacity to ask whether this life is genuinely one's own—because their entire practical identity has been subordinated to moral output. If the achievement of maximal goodness requires this kind of "moral instrumentality," in which the agent becomes a pure conduit for value rather than a self-authoring subject, then it seems the best moral agent is precisely the one who has lost the conditions for meaningful moral agency. Please explain whether this reveals a conceptual limit in treating moral value as maximizable—perhaps analogous to goods that resist aggregation—or whether it merely reveals a contingent psychological constraint. In particular, I am interested in whether there is an asymmetry between first-personal and third-personal moral evaluation: from the outside, we may call her maximally good, but from the inside, the capacity for such evaluation may have dissolved. Does this asymmetry undermine the coherence of the concept of maximal moral goodness, or can it be resolved by sharply distinguishing moral goodness from human flourishing?
