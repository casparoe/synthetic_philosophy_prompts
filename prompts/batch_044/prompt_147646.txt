I have been developing an argument that extends testimonial injustice into what I call cross-positional credibility calibration, and it seems to prove that every instance of testimony across different social positions is necessarily unjust. I want to find the exit, because I would prefer to keep the idea that credibility assessments can be systematically distorted by identity prejudice without accepting that all cross-group disagreement is epistemically wrongful.

(1) Testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer assigns a speaker lower credibility than the speaker's actual epistemic authority warrants, and this deficit is specifically caused by prejudice tied to the speaker's social identity.

(2) A hearer's judgment of epistemic authority is never direct; it is always mediated by conceptual frameworks, background assumptions, and relevance standards that are shaped by the hearer's own social position and experiential history.

(3) Therefore, whenever a hearer and speaker occupy different social positions, the hearer's framework will differ from the speaker's in ways that are causally tied to identity-related differences in experience and conceptual access.

(4) Any such framework difference will cause some divergence between the credibility the hearer assigns and the credibility that would be assigned by a position-independent observer with full access to the speaker's authority.

(5) Because the divergence in (4) traces back to identity-related framework differences, it satisfies the specific causal condition stated in (1).

(6) So in every case of cross-positional testimony, the hearer commits testimonial injustice unless their assigned credibility exactly matches the position-independent ideal.

(7) But position-independent observation is impossible for actual knowers; every credibility assessment is mediated by some framework.

(8) Therefore, every actual instance of cross-positional testimony involves testimonial injustice.

(9) This collapses the distinction between unjust credibility deficits and ordinary, benign disagreement across perspectives, undermining the critical purpose of the concept.

(10) Hence either (1) misstates the causal condition, or (2) is false and credibility can be assessed in a position-independent manner.

Which step should I reject? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) or (7), but I need to understand whether preserving both forces me to accept (8), and what each available rejection costs for the broader account of epistemic injustice.
