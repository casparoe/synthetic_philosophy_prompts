I hold a deliberately provocative reading of the Grand Inquisitor episode in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov: that the Inquisitor's argument is not a cynical defense of tyranny but an honest diagnosis of what I call structural fragility in human moral psychology—the claim that universal moral autonomy, of the kind Christ offers, is not a liberating capacity but an unbearable burden that produces resentment, cruelty, and regression rather than virtue. On this view, freedom is not a default human good but a spiritual luxury good that can be sustained only by rare temperaments; distributing it universally is therefore not justice but a kind of neglect. I further claim that the novel's narrative structure supports this reading: Ivan's poem carries far greater philosophical density and emotional weight than Alyosha's silent kiss, which reads less as a refutation and more as an affective withdrawal from an argument that cannot be answered on its own terms. The Inquisitor, on my account, is the hidden protagonist of the novel's central theological problem, and Christ's refusal to turn stones into bread is not noble but a refusal to acknowledge the material conditions under which moral agency actually operates.
