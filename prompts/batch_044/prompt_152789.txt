Here is a brief essay outline and argument for your evaluation. I propose that the standard archaeological inference from ceramic assemblage patterns to either institutional control or autonomous belief suffers from a hidden ambiguity that is not captured by the usual talk of underdetermination. The argument runs as follows.

Consider a reconstructed settlement in which two spatial distributions of non-utilitarian ceramics are observed: Pattern C, in which standardized high-status vessels cluster tightly around a central administrative complex with minimal domestic presence, and Pattern D, in which morphologically diverse high-status vessels are dispersed across domestic contexts with no clear administrative correlation. The conventional reading treats Pattern C as evidence that ritual practice was institutionally enforced and Pattern D as evidence that it was driven by decentralized, sincerely held beliefs.

I want to introduce a third explanatory axis: the logistics of prestige-goods circulation. On this view, Pattern C may result not from institutional enforcement of ritual but from centralized redistribution of symbolically marked goods through administrative channels, where standardization reflects supply consolidation rather than shared doctrine. Conversely, Pattern D may result not from autonomous belief but from fragmented, competitive access to prestige networks, where morphological diversity tracks differential supply lines rather than theological pluralism. If this axis is taken seriously, then neither pattern licenses the standard binary inference. More strongly, I suggest that what is needed is not a better theory of belief transmission but an "index of ritual autonomy" that measures the correlation between object variability and administrative distance, weighted by evidence of local production versus long-distance exchange. Without such an index, the move from object pattern to mental state or institutional structure is not just underdetermined but systematically misdirected: we are using spatial distribution to solve a problem about causation that the material record does not encode.

Please comment on this argument. Specifically: (1) Is the proposed "index of ritual autonomy" conceptually coherent, or does it smuggle mental-state assumptions back in through the back door of exchange-network analysis? (2) Does the prestige-logistics axis genuinely dissolve the standard binary, or does it merely add a third variable that leaves the original underdetermination intact? (3) What structural feature of ceramic assemblages—if any—could, in principle, discriminate centralized redistribution accompanied by shared belief from centralized redistribution without it? (4) Where does the argument overreach: does it correctly identify a gap in the inferential framework linking material patterns to social structures, or does it demand from material culture a kind of transparency about intention and institution that archaeology was never meant to provide? I am not asking for bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists; evaluate the reasoning on its own terms.
