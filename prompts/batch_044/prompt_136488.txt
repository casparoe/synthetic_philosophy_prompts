I am preparing a contribution for an interdisciplinary workshop on metaphysics and speculative fiction, and I would like to use a short narrative to illustrate a particular variant of the special composition question that has been underexplored in standard treatments. The classic debate asks whether and when scattered parts compose a unified whole, with familiar positions ranging from unrestricted mereological universalism to highly restricted views that require bonding, life, or functional integration. For this story, I want to move beyond those static options and explore a hypothetical society in which composition is neither permanent nor automatically determined by physical arrangement, but is instead a dynamic, institutionally mediated condition.

In this world, dispersed parts—such as the stones of a demolished bridge, the separated pages of a shared manuscript, or the ashes of an ancestor divided among heirs—do not immediately form a single object, nor are they permanently locked into being a mere plurality. Rather, they exist in a provisional state of loose aggregation until a recognized binder, which might be a ritual office, a legal instrument, or a collective acknowledgment, formally establishes a specific relational structure among them. Once bound, the parts genuinely constitute a whole with unified location, causal powers, and persistence conditions; but the binding can also be revoked, and when it dissolves, the former whole does not merely lose social significance—it metaphysically decomposes back into disconnected parts with no residual identity. The narrative should center on a protagonist who serves as such a binder, maintaining the compositional integrity of public monuments, distributed family archives, or temporary human alliances, and who discovers that a binding they performed years ago is now spontaneously failing, threatening to scatter not just an artifact but the historical and relational meanings embedded in it.

Please write this short story. It should be contemplative and precise rather than didactic, showing the mechanics of binding and dissolution through concrete scenes rather than exposition, and it should avoid treating universalism or nihilism as the obvious metaphysical default. The protagonist’s emotional stakes should arise directly from the instability of the objects they tend—perhaps through a scene in which they attempt to re-establish a binding that the parts themselves seem to resist, not because of bureaucratic error but because the relational conditions have genuinely shifted. Keep the logic internal to the world, do not rely on analogies to quantum mechanics or digital networks unless they emerge organically, and treat the dissolution as a real metaphysical event with observable consequences for location, causation, and identity.
