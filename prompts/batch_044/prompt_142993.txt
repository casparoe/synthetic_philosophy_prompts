I have been debating with a friend whether violence in the world is genuinely declining, and I am trying to understand whether she holds a consistent view or merely selects measurements that suit her mood. Her judgments so far are these: she counts domestic intimidation as violence even without physical injury; she judges a society with one hundred killings among ten million people less violent than one with fifty killings among one million; she treats systemic resource extraction under colonial administration as a form of violence; she denies that an industrial accident caused by a malfunctioning robotic arm is violence in the absence of malicious design; and she considers sustained verbal degradation by an employer violent if it produces lasting psychological harm, but not if it is an isolated outburst. Given these commitments, how should she assess a prolonged military campaign using remotely piloted aircraft that kills ten civilians for every intended combatant, conducted in a region where the population is growing faster than the death toll—would she say violence there is rising or falling? I am less interested in the particular answer than in identifying the principle she seems to be applying—whether it turns on intent, on physical harm, on per capita rates, or on the possibility of moral blame—and in knowing which alternative principles fit her earlier judgments equally well. Finally, tell me what single further case would best reveal which principle she actually holds, especially regarding whether she believes there is an objective fact of the matter about violence or only a choice of how to count.
