I spent last evening in a long argument with a friend about whether Plutarch's Lives genuinely improve their readers, and I have reconstructed the chain that seemed to trap us. (1) Plutarch claims that contemplation of great lives makes us better, because the soul naturally imitates what it admires. (2) Admiration is not neutral but is directed by the reader's pre-existing moral dispositions. (3) Consequently, a vicious reader will admire and imitate the embedded vices of a subject—Alcibiades' ambition, Caesar's pride—rather than any accompanying virtues. (4) Conversely, a virtuous reader already possesses the discernment to distinguish good from bad traits and therefore needs no biography to guide her admiration. (5) So biography improves only those who do not need improvement, and actively corrupts those who do. (6) This problem is not peculiar to Plutarch: any hagiographic or biographical moral education relies on the same mechanism of exemplarity and admiration. (7) Therefore either all such education is self-defeating, or Plutarch's psychological premise in (1) must be abandoned. (8) But abandoning (1) removes the very foundation for learning virtue from lives, leaving no alternative account of how biography could educate anyone. I am convinced something slips between (2) and (3), or perhaps between (5) and (6), yet I cannot identify the equivocation or scope shift. Which premise should I reject, what does rejecting it cost for the project of moral learning from lives, and how might one defend hagiography for readers who are not already virtuous?
