After a long argument with a friend about whether university curricula should still be organized around the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution, I sketched an essay outline I am no longer sure holds together. The core claim is that these three periods are not pure fictions, but “reifications”: we have projected discontinuities in institutional authority onto the history of ideas, then mistaken the projection for a natural kind. I would value comments on three specific points—whether the argument illicitly slides from historiography into ontology, whether my proposed alternative is coherent, and whether I ignore genuine institutional breaks that justify some periodization at all.

Notes for the essay:

1. The labels are retrospective constructions. The phrase “Middle Ages” was coined by humanist scholars to mark a dark interlude before their own recovery; “Renaissance” was consolidated later still; and “Scientific Revolution” is largely a twentieth-century framing device. This does not render the labels false, but it should lower our confidence that they carve history at its joints.

2. Intellectual continuity undermines epochal boundaries. Scholastic logic and Aristotelian natural philosophy remained central to university instruction well into the seventeenth century; alchemical and astrological practices did not vanish with the alleged dawn of modern science. If we periodize by dominant ideas, the boundaries dissolve into prolonged overlap.

3. The real discontinuities are institutional and generic, not epochal. The shift from monastic and university-based production to court-sponsored and academized inquiry is undeniable, but it is distributed unevenly across domains and regions. Rather than bundling these shifts into a single “Revolution,” I want to propose an “epistemic topology” that maps specific practice-transitions—for instance, the gradual replacement of the commentary genre by the standardized experimental report—without imposing a uniform chronology. On this view, the fourteenth through seventeenth centuries form one long transformation with local peaks and valleys, not three discrete blocks.

4. Therefore, the tripartite scheme should be treated as a pedagogical heuristic with ontological risks: useful for organizing textbooks, misleading if taken as a description of historical reality.

The part I find weakest is step 3. My friend objected that by dissolving the periods into a “topology,” I have merely renamed the problem: I still need to explain why certain practices cluster in time, and that clustering is precisely what periodization was meant to capture. I also worry that I conflate “historians constructed this narrative” with “there is nothing there to be narrated.” I would particularly welcome comments on whether the “epistemic topology” survives that clustering objection, and on whether my account of institutional change is too thin to bear the weight I place on it.
