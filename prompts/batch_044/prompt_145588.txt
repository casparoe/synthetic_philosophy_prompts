I hold that institutional entities—e.g., a university chartered in 1850—can genuinely persist across worlds in which they take radically different forms (closing in 1900 versus flourishing today) without being replaced by counterparts, because normative continuity of governance and purpose, not qualitative similarity, fixes their identity. What would count against this institutional-transworld-identity view? I want the strongest defeaters: cases where normative continuity clearly breaks yet intuition insists it is the same institution; cases where continuity holds yet intuition judges it different; whether specifying the continuity relation precisely collapses the view into a disguised counterpart theory; and any formal results about social-object identity that undermine non-qualitative persistence conditions. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging, and assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability.
