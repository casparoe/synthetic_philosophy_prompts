I'm running a grad seminar on Dewey's political writings—mostly Democracy and Education and some later essays on social intelligence—and one of my students keeps dropping this line: the cure for democracy's ills is more democracy. She used it last week to shut down my point that sometimes a problem might need expert or technocratic intervention rather than wider public deliberation. I think she was treating it as self-evidant, but I'm not actually sure what precise thesis Dewey (or anyone) is committing to with that slogan.

Can you lay out the different precise readings it could have? I'm especially interested in: (a) an empirical claim about institutional self-correction, like democratic procedures contain feedback loops that correct errors; (b) a normative claim that legitimacy just requires ever-broader participation regardless of outcome; (c) a definitional move where democracy just names continuous social inquiry, so more democracy means more inquiry and nothing else; (d) a fallibilist claim that collective errors get fixed only through further democratic experimentation, never by importing authority from outside. For each, tell me if it's trivial, if it's actually defensible inside Dewey's experimentalism, and crucially whether it actually supports dismissing the idea that experts should sometimes override democratic processes.

There's also a tension I want flagged: Dewey describes democracy as a mode of associated living but also holds up scientific method as the model for social intelligence. Does the slogan conflate those two, or keep them apart? And if someone uses the line to block debate about whether democratic deliberation can systematically fail epistemically—say, producing false beliefs about climate policy—does any reading actually address that worry, or is it just changing the subject? Lay this out as a menu I can bring to office hours, and don't worry about giving me a bibliography; I just want the disambiguation and which readings do the argumentative work my student was actually trying to do.
