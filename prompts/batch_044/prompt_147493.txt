Consider the problem of why promises generate binding obligations rather than mere predictions or expressions of intent. Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors: one who holds that promises bind through the exercise of a normative power that restructures the promisor’s own deontic landscape, thereby creating a directed duty that can survive even the promisee’s death; and another who holds that promises bind only through the establishment of a second-order assurance structure that makes the promisee’s reliance rationally stable, and who therefore argues that the obligation dissolves when the promisee can no longer serve as the beneficiary of that assurance. Have them debate specifically whether a promise to tend a dying person’s garden after their death creates a genuine duty owed to the estate, a transformed duty of remembrance with no directed object, or no duty at all. Each speaker should directly engage the other’s claims about moral authority, the ontology of directed obligations, and the conditions under which reliance becomes morally protected, and neither side should be reduced to a strawman.
