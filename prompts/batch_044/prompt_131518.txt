I am drafting a scene for a philosophical novel in which two biologists, snowbound at a remote Arctic research station, debate the nature of biological purpose by lamplight. I need a dialogue between Dr. Selene Marchetti, who holds an etiological account of function—insisting that a trait's proper function is determined by the selective history that originally produced and maintained it—and Dr. Rowan Kessler, who defends a causal-role account, arguing that function is constituted by a trait's present, systematically exploited contribution to a containing system's capacities, quite apart from evolutionary genealogy. Rather than allowing them to trade abstract slogans, have them wrestle with a concrete, somewhat engineered case: a synthetic ribozyme inserted into a bacterial stress-response pathway. The ribozyme began as a parasitic replication error with no adaptive value, was subsequently co-opted as an essential regulatory brake during thermal shock, and has recently begun propagating horizontally into unrelated strains where it performs no comparable homeostatic role.

Marchetti should argue that in the recipient strains, describing the ribozyme as having a "function" is a category mistake—without the original selective environment that shaped its structure for that specific contribution, its causal effects are merely accidental, however systematically they occur—while Kessler should reply that if the receiving cell's architecture genuinely recruits the ribozyme into a self-maintaining feedback loop, then evolutionary pedigree is an etiological curiosity rather than a normative criterion for functional ascription. Crucially, they must respond to each other's actual points: Marchetti should not dismiss the causal-role view as naive presentism, and Kessler should not caricature the etiological view as genetic essentialism. By the scene's end, each should have refined her position in light of the objection raised—perhaps disagreeing specifically over whether functional normativity requires a history of selection for that very contribution, or merely stable integration into a system that treats the trait as indispensable. The exchange should be intellectually rigorous, formally composed, and entirely free of citations, bibliographic references, or appeals to specific named texts.
