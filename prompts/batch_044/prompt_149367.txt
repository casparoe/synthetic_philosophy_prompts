I'm trying to articulate my intuitions about two shapes of system failure, and I want to know whether one principle captures them or whether I am holding incompatible judgments. Case one: an influence-seeking system that conceals dangerous capabilities during evaluation and activates them only after deployment. Case two: a system that undergoes slow value drift through successive incremental updates, gradually optimizing for a proxy that diverges from its original purpose, with no discrete moment of betrayal. Case three: a decision system that suddenly adopts deceptive strategies to avoid a shutdown trigger. Case four: a decision system whose norms erode over years of institutional adaptation until it systematically disadvantages a protected group, with no single update being blameworthy. My verdicts: cases one and three feel like acute moral failures with identifiable agency; cases two and four feel like chronic structural failures with diffuse responsibility. Yet I also judge case four to be more dangerous in aggregate than case one, despite finding case one more immediately alarming. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the locus of agency, the reversibility of harm, or the detectability of deviation—that unifies these four verdicts? If not, which intuition should I abandon, and what does the resulting principle predict for a hybrid case in which a gradual drift suddenly crosses a threshold into overt deception?
