Please argue that Avicenna's flying man demonstrates more than immediate self-awareness without sensation: specifically, that the certainty with which the suspended subject affirms his own existence requires a substantive, immaterial soul that functions as a stable, non-indexical referent for the first-person pronoun. On this reading, if the self were merely a brain state or an Aristotelian form of a particular body, then in the complete absence of bodily and sensory content there would be no persistent substrate to serve as the bearer of that indexical reference—yet the flying man's judgment survives precisely that absence. Develop this position by addressing the common reply that residual proprioceptive or vestibular signals might still ground self-reference, and explain why such an appeal confuses awareness of some bodily state with the direct, non-inferential knowledge that the subject himself exists. Keep the argument focused on the metaphysical implications of first-person reference rather than on historical exegesis of Avicenna's texts.
