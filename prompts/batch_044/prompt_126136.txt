Question: The discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation demonstrates that a group of individually consistent agents may, by majority rule, adopt a collectively inconsistent set of judgments over logically interrelated propositions. Consider a three-member panel evaluating whether restitution of a cultural artifact is obligatory (R), where R is logically equivalent to (P ∧ Q): P asserts that the artifact was acquired through coercion, and Q asserts that no subsequent valid legal transfer occurred. The panel votes as follows—Expert 1: P yes, Q yes, R no; Expert 2: P yes, Q no, R no; Expert 3: P no, Q yes, R no. First, demonstrate precisely why applying unrestricted majority rule to P, Q, and R yields an inconsistent collective position. Second, distinguish the premise-based aggregation procedure from the conclusion-based procedure. Third, articulate the central normative tension between democratic responsiveness and collective rationality that any resolution must confront.

Student answer: The discursive dilemma arises when individually rational agents produce collectively irrational judgments through majority voting. Here, two experts affirm P and two affirm Q, yet none affirm R, violating the logical entailment from P and Q to R. To solve this, the premise-based procedure aggregates judgments only on P and Q, derives R deductively, and thereby restores consistency—though at the cost of imposing a collective judgment that every member rejected. The conclusion-based procedure, by contrast, aggregates judgments on R directly and then adjusts the premises to match, which respects the group's explicit stance on the outcome but forces the abandonment of democratically supported premises. Both procedures violate one of Arrow's conditions—universality, anonymity, or independence—showing that democratic aggregation is inherently incompatible with logical consistency. Consequently, the premise-based procedure is normatively superior because it alone preserves the rational unity required for collective agency.
