I was arguing with my study partner about decision theory and we cooked up this weird objection to risk-weighted expected utility, the Buchak-style thing where you apply a risk function r to probabilities instead of treating them linearly. We can't tell if it's a real problem or just a framing trick. Here's the chain we wrote down:

(1) REU says you maximize Σ r(p_i)·u(x_i), and r can be nonlinear so you can be risk-averse about big stakes without being risk-averse about small ones.
(2) The motivation is to solve paradoxes like Allais without giving up independence in some other form.
(3) Now consider one physical gamble that can be described in two ways. Description A lists four final outcomes, each with flat prob 0.25. Description B splits it into a first 50/50 branch, then inside each branch two outcomes with prob 0.5.
(4) The leaf probabilities are identical, but in B you might evaluate stage-by-stage: apply r to the 0.5 branch weights, then evaluate the inner gambles.
(5) If you do that, the total weight on each final outcome isn't r(0.25) but some function of r(0.5) combined with inner weights, which generally differs from r(0.25).
(6) So the same physical lottery gets two different REU values depending on whether you model it flat or nested.
(7) But the state-space partiton is just a modeling choice; rational preference shouldn't flip just because you redrew the tree.
(8) So REU is description-dependent, which is incoherent for any decision theory.
(9) You might reply that REU only applies r to the final outcome probabilities, never to intermediate nodes.
(10) But that reply seems arbitary: if risk attitude is about how you weigh uncertainty, why should it care whether that uncertainty is resolved in one step or two?
(11) And if you do restrict r to flat leaves, you've added a constraint that doesn't come from the risk-weighting idea itself, just to save coherence.
(12) So either REU allows stage-pooling and is incoherent, or it forbids it and loses its principled motivation.

We think the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), like we're assuming stage-evaluation is permitted when the theory actually specifies leaf-only evaluation. But we also think (9) might be too quick, and that (7) captures something real about rationality. Which step do we actually reject, and what does it cost? I'd rather not give up (7) or (8) if I can help it.
