In a graduate seminar on normative ethics, our discussion of what is often called the paradox of deontology has settled into a strange equilibrium. Constraint deontologists maintain that actions such as torturing an innocent or killing one patient to harvest organs for five others are categorically forbidden, grounding this in the inviolability of persons, the separateness of individuals, and the structure of practical reason. Their critics—threshold deontologists and some consequentialists—counter that deontology already permits substantial harm through the doctrine of double effect, legitimate self-defense, and role-based permissions, so the framework appears to allow precisely the outcomes it claims to forbid, only relabeled by a distinction between intended and merely foreseen effects. Both sides can rehearse the canonical cases—the switch versus the footbridge, the transplant surgeon, the ticking-bomb scenario—yet in two years no one has revised a core commitment, and the debate now feels less like an argument over particular judgments and more like a standoff about what kind of thing a moral constraint actually is.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly about bedrock intuitions regarding these cases, but about a hidden disagreement over whether deontological constraints function as infinitely weighted side-constraints within an otherwise aggregative calculus, or as constitutive conditions that determine whether an act counts as the kind of action it is—something closer to a moral topology than to a rulebook. On the first reading, violating a constraint is a quantifiable cost that is always outweighed by preservation; on the second, it is a category error that dissolves the moral identity of the agent. If this is correct, then much of our disagreement is verbal: we use terms like permission, prohibition, and justification for two different logical structures. But I am unsure. The impasse might instead reflect a substantive disagreement in moral metaphysics or in moral psychology—whether ordinary agents experience constraint-violations as breaking a rule or as failing to constitute themselves as practical reasoners—or even a formal disagreement about whether deontic logic can represent genuinely agent-relative reasons without collapsing into consequentialist aggregation. Given what each side actually says about double effect, agency, and the separateness of persons, is this dispute verbal, substantive, or a case of two camps answering different questions—one about justification, one about moral identity—under the single heading of deontology? And if your diagnosis holds, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the paradox, a formal result in deontic logic, an empirical finding about moral cognition, or should we conclude that the framing itself is ill-posed and abandon it?
