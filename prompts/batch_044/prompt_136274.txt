Consider the institutional protocol common to U.S. transplant centers in which a patient is declared dead by neurological criteria—confirmed absence of brainstem reflexes and apnea—while mechanical ventilation sustains cardiac circulation and somatic integration for hours, permitting vital-organ procurement. The same legal framework, anchored in statutes like the Uniform Determination of Death Act, treats this declaration as fully equivalent to circulatory death for ending life-support obligations and authorizing donation, even though integrated biological life-functions plainly persist. At the same time, donation-after-circulatory-death protocols declare death only after two to five minutes of absent circulation, an interval selected largely to preclude autoresuscitation rather than to mark a discrete biological transition.

What philosophical commitments about the ontology of human death and personal identity does this dual institutional architecture strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely accommodating? Does the brain-death pathway implicitly commit to a higher-brain or mentalist view—that the irreversible loss of consciousness and integrated mental life is what makes the person no longer present—while the DCD pathway implicitly treats death as the irreversible cessation of circulatory function? Or can both be reconstructed as pragmatic normative thresholds that do no ontological work, leaving open whether a dead patient is genuinely absent or merely legally unavailable for further treatment?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of death that best rationalize the coexistence of these pathways. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it is only contingently compatible with—for instance, whether it needs a claim about the organism’s loss of somatic integration, or whether it can survive on purely administrative coherence. Address the tension that brain-dead patients sometimes retain hypothalamic function and hormonal regulation, which complicates the claim that all integrative capacity has vanished, and ask whether that residual biology undermines the conceptual basis of the neurological criterion or merely shows that our legal category of death is doing normative labor that outstrips any natural kind.

In your response, separate what is settled—such as the clinical criteria for brain-death declaration and the legal status of the UDDA—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of medicine and bioethicists, including whether brain-dead bodies retain moral status, whether the integrated-organism criterion genuinely applies, and whether the divergence between neurological and circulatory protocols reveals an incoherent dual ontology. Do not present any one contested view as the consensus. I am particularly interested in whether a consistently applied biological account of death could license both protocols, or whether their divergence demonstrates that our institutional concept of death is irreducibly normative.
