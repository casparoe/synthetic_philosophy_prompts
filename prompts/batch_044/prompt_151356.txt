I am preparing a brief closing piece for a seminar on rhetoric and governance, and I should like to finish with a poem that moves beyond the familiar summary of Orwell's complaint about political euphemism. I want instead to take seriously the grammatical argument he advances in Politics and the English Language: that corruption works not merely by hiding meaning but by dissolving agency—turning active, transitive verbs into passive constructions and burying concrete actions beneath abstract nominalisations—until language becomes structurally incapable of attributing responsibility. Rather than describing this decay in verse, I want the poem's own syntax and form to enact it.

Please write a fourteen-line poem in the voice of an unnamed civil servant composing an internal memorandum concerning "the implementation of optimisation measures." Adopt a modified sonnet structure: an octave (lines 1–8) followed by a sestet (lines 9–14), with a sharp volta at line 9. In the octave, each line must contain at least one finite, active, transitive verb with a named or clearly implied human agent, and the rhyme scheme should be ABAB CDCD, rhyming on concrete, predominantly monosyllabic words of Anglo-Saxon origin. At the volta, the grammatical subject must vanish: line 9 should begin with a passive participle or an abstract noun functioning as subject. The sestet must then contain no finite active verbs whatsoever—only participial phrases, gerunds, and nominalised processes—while its rhyme scheme collapses into EFE FGG, with every rhyme falling on a polysyllabic abstract noun ending in -tion, -ment, or -ance, so that the very sound of the rhyme becomes bureaucratic.

The diction should feel authentically administrative—referencing "stakeholders," "deliverables," "process streams," and "efficiency gains"—but rendered with enough precision that a reader familiar with Orwell's essay recognises the grammatical mechanism being performed rather than merely named. The poem should neither condemn nor redeem its speaker; it should simply end in the grammatical fog it has generated, leaving the reader unable to determine who decided what. Please do not quote from Orwell's essay; paraphrase its concerns entirely within the poem's own vocabulary. I should like this for a small audience of students who have read the essay but are not specialists in poetics, so clarity of structure matters more than obscurity.
