I serve as the senior features editor for a quarterly history magazine read by general audiences and secondary-school teachers. Each issue carries two or three long-form articles that trace the causes of major historical turning points, and our writers have begun framing pathogens, climate anomalies, and volcanic eruptions as "historical actors" rather than mere background conditions—forces with explanatory weight comparable to human decisions. I have watched drafts drift into environmental determinism, presenting outcomes as inevitable outputs of temperature drops or ash clouds, and I have watched others anthropomorphize viruses or glaciers into entities with intentions. I need a reusable editorial procedure that staff members with no philosophical training can run in about thirty minutes on any proposed framing. The deliverable should be a sequence of ordered questions or tests; for each step specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a firm rejection of the framing, where it permits the framing only with mandatory qualifications, and where the question is one of values rather than facts and must be escalated to me. The procedure must be executable without outside research during deadline crunch. Apply it to three proposed article angles: first, framing the fourteenth-century Black Death as an agent that dissolved feudal obligations by restructuring labor supply; second, framing the 1815 Tambora eruption as the causal actor behind Mary Shelley’s composition of Frankenstein during the 1816 Geneva summer; and third, framing a hypothetical 2030 influenza pandemic—already modeled by our science desk—as a deterministic trigger for a specific geopolitical realignment, with human policy responses treated as mere epiphenomena. Be sure to include the case in which the procedure returns a verdict the proposing writer will strongly dislike, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle.
