Here is an argument I sketched for a workshop on moral risk, and now it seems to collapse every concrete duty into an infinite ascent. (1) Moral rationality requires maximizing expected moral value among the options actually available to an agent. (2) For any concrete act A with finite value v—say, feeding a hungry child—one can describe a speculative alternative B, such as funding a particular longshot biotechnology, that yields value v·N with probability 1/N for arbitrarily large N; adjusting the payoff slightly gives E(B) > v. (3) So for every available A, there exists in principle a B with strictly greater expected value. (4) If such a B exists in principle, then rationality either requires selecting B or requires showing why B is not genuinely available; mere difficulty of execution is not enough to exclude it. (5) But the same scaling applies to B itself: construct B' with payoff multiplied by N' and probability divided by N', yielding E(B') > E(B). (6) This generates an infinite ascending chain B, B', B'', ... with no maximal element, so that admitting longshots into the choice set means expected-value maximization never authorizes stopping at any concrete act. (7) Consequently, one should never feed the child if one could instead initiate the search for the next, more remote possibility, and by iteration ordinary moral life is paralyzed. (8) I want to keep (1) and the intuition that genuinely remote possibilities can carry moral weight, but I need to locate the break. Is the error a modal slide in (4), treating "exists in principle" as "is available"? Is it a hidden assumption in (2) that probabilities and values are commensurable across radically different domains? Or does the iteration in (5) depend on a quantifier shift—moving from "for each option there is a dominator" to "there is an option that dominates all others"—that does not actually follow? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the role of longshot reasoning in moral choice?
