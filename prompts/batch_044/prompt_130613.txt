In a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and public reason, my advisor drew an analogy between Carnap’s account of rational planning—where scientific reasoning fixes the empirical means to a socially chosen end but cannot justify the end itself—and the division of labor in architectural practice. The architect-engineer determines structural feasibility, load-bearing limits, and code compliance, while the client supplies preferences about form and function that are treated as irreducibly subjective inputs; planning, on this picture, is technically authoritative but normatively hollow, because value commitments sit outside the engineer’s competence. The comparison is used to defend a strong separation thesis: that political planners should optimize for efficiency given democratically selected goals, yet must treat those goals as non-cognitive parameters rather than as objects of rational deliberation.

I want to audit how far this architectural analogy actually carries Carnap’s framework, particularly once we inject the kind of modular, framework-pluralist picture that some recent interpreters find in his later work on language choice and the principle of tolerance. Can you lay out an explicit correspondence table—what in Carnap’s planning theory maps onto the architect, the structural engineer, the building code, the client’s aesthetic brief, and the permitting authority? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the separation thesis: for instance, does the analogy quietly import a private-public asymmetry between expert knowledge and lay preference that Carnap’s own tolerance for alternative conceptual frameworks might dissolve, given that in politics the “client” is a collective whose preferences are themselves shaped by institutional design? Does the conclusion—that planning is normatively hollow—survive once we notice that in architecture the client’s preferences are constrained by budget, zoning law, and structural possibility in ways that make ends and means co-evolve, suggesting that value commitments are not fixed parameters but are partly responsive to technical input? If the analogy breaks down here, what would be a more accurate comparison for Carnap’s view—perhaps a navigation system that computes optimal routes without fixing the destination, or a modular design protocol in which value-frames are interchangeable but not arbitrary?
