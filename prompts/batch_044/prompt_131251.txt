The risk-weighted expected utility crowd and the orthodox Bayesians have been going in circles for years now, at least in the decision-theory corners I hang out in. One side says weighting outcomes by a convex risk function fixes the Allais problem and handles Ellsberg-style ambiguity without giving up on independance in some sneaky way; the other side says you've just renamed prospect theory and called it a day, and that "risk-weighted" is smuggling in violations of seperability that the axioms were meant to block. Same three examples get recylced every conference, formal results are well-known, and nobody's budged in ages. I think part of the stalemate is that one camp is asking a normative question about what an ideal agent should do, while the other is asking a descriptive question about actual human weighting of rare disasters, but they both call it "expected utility theory" and talk past each other. There's also this weird sub-issue about symmetric risk-weighting — applying the exact same convex weight to gains and losses — that only shows up in a couple of papers and seems to break both sides' usual arguments, and I'm not sure if that reveals a hidden empirical disagreement about ambiguity aversion or if it's just a red herring. What's your diagnosis? Is this a verbal dispute about "rationality," a substantive disagreement about hidden standards of evidence, or two different questions running under one heading? And given whatever you think is going on, what would actualy count as progress — a new axiom, a well-designed experiment, or just accepting that the framing is ill-posed and walking away?
