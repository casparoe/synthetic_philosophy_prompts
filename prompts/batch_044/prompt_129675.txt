I am a PhD student in philosophy of language and mind, currently preparing a 75-minute session for an advanced seminar of about fourteen students—mostly senior undergraduates and first-year graduates—who have read some of Wittgenstein’s later remarks on private language but are not specialists. In our previous meeting we worked through the familiar examples, yet the conversation felt stale: the students already knew the punchline, that a language intelligible only to its speaker is impossible, and they treated the beetle-in-the-box and diary cases as puzzles with predetermined answers. I want them to feel the force of the argument from the inside, specifically the moment when the distinction between correctly applying a rule to one’s own private state and merely feeling certain collapses because there is no independent criterion of correctness.

Please design an in-class game or structured simulation that runs in about 75 minutes using only paper, envelopes, and a point-based payoff scheme—no computers, no external references, and no requests for bibliographies or citations. The mechanics should embed the problem rather than lecture it: each participant should generate and attempt to track, across several rounds, a hidden “calibration token” representing an internal phenomenal state that is in principle never exposed to others, while collective incentives seem to require coordination. The rules should be arranged so that students try to establish a private rule for identifying and re-identifying their token, discover that they cannot check their own applications except by bare impression, and experience the instability of the correct/incorrect distinction in practice—before anyone names the difficulty as Wittgenstein’s argument. Include a precise timeline, role or pairing structure, the hidden-information design, the payoff scheme and what small stakes motivate cooperation or competition, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto stages of the private-language argument: instituting a rule, the absence of an external check, and the normative collapse.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this—where students will misunderstand the hidden-information rules, where the point system will produce unintended behavior, and how to handle it without breaking the simulation. Be frank about which feature of the private-language argument the activity cannot reproduce—for instance, whether it can capture the full normative force of “getting it right” versus merely feeling sure—and specify what I must supply in the debrief to cover that gap. To keep the exercise away from over-familiar territory, frame the private state as an internal calibration token rather than pain, color, or a beetle, avoid naming standard secondary literature, and do not include any verbatim quotations from texts. Write the deliverable as a runnable procedure for a practitioner who understands the basic argument but needs the mechanics spelled out clearly.
