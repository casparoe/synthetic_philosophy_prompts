I have been working through the literature for an advanced undergraduate course on moral responsibility, and I encountered a summary in a popular online philosophy essay that attempts to capture P.F. Strawson's position in "Freedom and Resentment." The author writes: "Strawson's central claim is that moral responsibility is nothing more than the emotional reactions—resentment, gratitude, indignation—that people feel when they are wronged or benefited. These reactive attitudes only make sense if we already believe the other person could have acted differently in a libertarian sense; they are essentially non-rational emotional commitments to free will. Once we accept modern neuroscience and determinism, we see that these feelings are based on a false metaphysical picture. Strawson admits this but argues we should keep them anyway because human beings are psychologically incapable of abandoning interpersonal anger and gratitude. So his position is really a kind of pragmatic compatibilism: responsibility survives not because determinism is compatible with freedom, but because we are stuck with irrational emotional habits that we pretend are justified."

Something about this strikes me as too dismissive, and I recall that Strawson's actual argument was meant to sidestep the free-will debate rather than concede it. But I am not sure which parts of the summary are standard pedagogical shorthand and which are genuine distortions. Could you audit this summary for me? Please go point by point: identify what accurately captures Strawson's view of reactive attitudes, what counts as a harmless simplification, and what misrepresents either his description of these attitudes or his stance on determinism. In particular, does the author's conclusion—that Strawson saves moral responsibility only by embracing irrational emotional habits—actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary? I am not looking for a reading list or citations, just an evaluation I can use to correct my own notes and discuss with my study group.
