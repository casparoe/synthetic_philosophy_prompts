I am drafting my contribution for a workshop on aggregation and contractualism, and I want to know whether my package of commitments is coherent before I present it. Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Scanlon's contractualist test: an act is wrong if it violates principles that no one could reasonably reject, with the 'no one' understood as each affected individual taken separately.
(2) I deny that numbers have independent moral weight in rescue cases (Taurek-style), so saving five strangers rather than one is not automatically justified by the greater count.
(3) I nevertheless think contractualism permits imposing a large burden on one to prevent a catastrophic aggregate harm to many, but only if we recognize what I call a 'structural complaint': the single victim is not complaining about the direct harm alone, but about being placed in an institutional pattern that systematically prioritizes diffuse benefits over concentrated costs.
(4) I extend contractualist moral standing to sentient animals via a proxy-deliberation model, where representatives assess rejectability on behalf of beings lacking rational autonomy.
(5) I apply this to climate policy: I believe present people can be required to accept severe economic restrictions to prevent widespread but individually mild future harms, because the intergenerational principle of burden-sharing is not rejectable from any temporal position.

I suspect there is tension between (1) and (3), and possibly between (2) and (3). I also worry that (4) conflicts with the rational-autonomy presupposition of (1). But one of these may be only apparent. Can you audit these commitments? Rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment is the least costly to revise in each case, identify any hidden tension I have missed, and tell me whether there is a single revision that resolves the most conflicts without abandoning contractualism altogether.
