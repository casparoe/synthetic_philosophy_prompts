I am working through George Berkeley's master argument—the claim that conceiving of something as existing necessarily involves conceiving of it as perceived by some mind—in order to test whether it can be applied consistently beyond the usual textbook examples. Rather than arguing in the abstract, I have formed concrete verdicts about several cases that mix traditional physical objects with modern informational and virtual entities, and I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate my pattern of judgments.

Here are my verdicts. First, a tree falling in a forest with no human or animal observers present: I think the tree still exists, sustained in the continuous perception of other minds or in divine perception, so Berkeley's argument does not eliminate it. Second, a blockchain ledger distributed across powered servers but currently unread by any user: I think it exists, but only in a potential or dispositional form, not as actual present ideas. Third, light of a specific wavelength entering a sealed, dark room that contains no retinas, cameras, or conscious observers of any kind: I think there is no color or visible quality actually present; there is only a physical disposition to produce an idea if perceived. Fourth, a mathematical proof written in a notebook that is then destroyed before anyone reads it, with no copy made: I think the proof existed briefly as actual ideas in the author's mind, but once the author dies and the paper is ash, it survives only as a possible idea, not as an actual one. Fifth, a virtual environment running on an isolated server with its display disconnected and no user logged in: I think the virtual world does not exist at all; it is merely code with the potential to generate ideas, not ideas themselves.

The pair that troubles me is the blockchain and the virtual environment. Both depend on running computers, both lack present observers, yet I judge the blockchain to have some form of existence and the virtual world to have none. Is there a principled distinction here—perhaps involving distributed persistence versus centralized simulation—or do I have to overturn one of these verdicts?

More generally, is there a coherent framework—perhaps distinguishing actual ideas, potential ideas, and abstract relations—that captures all five judgments? What would it predict for a case I have not settled: scientific data recorded by an automated instrument in a laboratory after all human life on Earth has ended, with no other minds present? And if full consistency requires sacrificing one of my verdicts, which one should go and why?
