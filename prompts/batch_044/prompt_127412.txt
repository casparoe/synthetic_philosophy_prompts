You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of collective memory and moral repair. I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about whether societies have an enduring duty to remember historical injustices—something in the spirit of the Margalit-style ethical duty to remember—or whether there is an equally serious, Rieff-style case for institutional forgetting and the expiration of grievance. My friend defended the latter with an analogy I had not heard pushed this far: collective social memory, they claimed, operates like adaptive immunity. Past injustices are antigens; commemorative practices are the immune response that trains the body politic to recognize future threats. But a healthy immune system also depends on regulatory tolerance—mechanisms that prevent it from attacking the body’s own tissue. By parallel, societies need active “forgetting protocols” (amnesty, sealed archives, statutory limitation of historical claims) to avoid a kind of social autoimmune disorder, in which the present polity endlessly inflames itself by attacking its own members for sins they did not personally commit. From this they concluded that mandatory remembrance policies are not merely imprudent but pathologically analogous to autoimmune disease, and that a well-functioning democracy should deliberately build in “forgetting T-cells.”

Assess whether the analogy can be repaired with a different biological or social model, or whether the “autoimmune” framing was doing all the argumentative work. Finally, say what a better analogy for the relationship between remembrance and social health might look like, if any exists.
