Restate the argument for epiphenomenalism regarding phenomenal consciousness, together with the associated paradox of phenomenal judgment, entirely within the conceptual vocabulary of distributed-systems engineering—treating neural substrates as message-passing nodes, phenomenal properties as read-only telemetry streams, and judgments of consciousness as log entries that record but do not alter system state. Map each philosophical claim precisely onto its engineering counterpart: identify what plays the role of causal closure of the physical, what corresponds to the claim that qualia lack causal efficacy, and what in the architecture represents the first-person report that one is experiencing red or pain. Then provide the residue report. Indicate which elements of the philosophical position translate without loss, which can only be preserved by distorting either the engineering framework or the original argument—for example, whether a dashboard's self-reference can genuinely mirror phenomenal judgment without becoming causally coupled to the state it monitors—and whether the paradox, that an apparently authoritative judgment can concern a causally inert state, survives as a substantive problem in the engineering analogy or dissolves as a category mistake between causal and representational relations. Conclude by stating whether the translation suggests that the paradox reveals a genuine feature of consciousness absent from any monitoring architecture, or whether it shows that the original philosophical formulation depended on an implicit commitment that the vocabulary of distributed systems explicitly rejects.
