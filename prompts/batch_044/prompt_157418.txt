I am drafting a historical novel set in a fictional Baltic state during the long nineteenth century, and in my research I encountered a summary—published, I recall, in an essayistic literary review—of the work of Hobsbawm and Anderson on the modernity of nations. The summary argues that Hobsbawm showed traditions presented as ancient, such as Highland ceremonial dress and royal ritual, were in fact largely fabricated between roughly 1870 and 1914 to serve emergent state apparatuses; that Anderson then demonstrated nations to be imagined communities made possible by print-capitalism, which permits strangers to conceive of themselves as horizontally connected compatriots; and that, taken together, these two accounts prove that collective identity before mass literacy was necessarily confined to face-to-face localities, that any ritual claiming pre-modern origins is therefore automatically a nineteenth-century political construct, and that Anderson’s framework can be extended backward to oral epic traditions—what the reviewer calls pre-print imagined communities—without altering its essential logic. I am inclined to adapt this framework for my fictional nation’s founding myth, but I wish to be certain I am not building on misrepresentation. Which parts of this summary would a careful reader of Hobsbawm and Anderson accept, which conflate claims the two authors keep separate, which overstate modest theses into absolute ones, and which conclusions do not actually follow? In particular, does the notion of pre-print imagined communities represent a legitimate analytical extension of Anderson’s argument, or does it violate conditions he specified for the formation of national consciousness? Please address the points in order, separating accurate characterization from subtle distortion and from straightforward error.
