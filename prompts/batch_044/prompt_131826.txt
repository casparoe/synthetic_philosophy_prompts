Since Bradley's regress was first articulated, philosophers have divided over whether the unity of a proposition can be explained relationally or whether any such explanation collapses into an infinite hierarchy of relating relations. The relationalist maintains that a primitive binding mechanism—whether conceived as a logical form, a mental act, or an ontological glue—is indispensable if we are not to reduce predication to an unstructured list of terms. The anti-relationalist, following Bradley's own suspicion of external relations, argues that invoking a binder merely replicates the original difficulty at a higher level, and concludes that propositions (or the concrete facts they mirror) must be taken as irreducibly unitary. In recent decades both sides have produced formal reconstructions and appealed to cognitive architecture, yet the debate appears stalled: neither camp regards the other's models as decisive, and both can reinterpret the opponent's central examples within their own framework.

I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication. Is this deadlock substantive—a genuine clash about whether reality contains irreducible unities—or is it partly verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of 'relation', 'unity', and 'proposition'? Might the sides be answering different questions under one heading: one about vertical dependence (how a proposition relies on its constituents plus a binder arranged in levels) and the other about horizontal co-ordination (how terms interrelate within a single plane of being without hierarchical ascent)? Or is there a hidden presupposition—for instance, that every complex must be analysable into simpler parts—that drives the regress without being independently defended? Drawing on what each side actually maintains, analyse whether the disagreement is factual, conceptual, or intuitive at bottom. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about the consistency of primitive binding, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates logical structure with ontological composition. Be direct about whether you think the stalemate should be expected to end.
