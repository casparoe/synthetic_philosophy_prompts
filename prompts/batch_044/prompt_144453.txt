I'm writing a short essay for a course on biography and moral philosophy that compares ancient exemplary lives to modern life-writing, and a fellow student offered a summary of Plutarch's Parallel Lives that feels suspiciously tidy. She put it like this: Plutarch effectively invented comparative biography not as history but as moral taxonomy—pairing one Greek and one Roman figure to create binary exempla arranged on a fixed Stoic-virtue grid. Because his purpose was essentially didactic hagiography, he suppresses contradictory evidence and reshapes events to fit predetermined ethical arcs; the synkrisis that closes each pair is therefore closer to a grading rubric than to a genuine comparative analysis. From this it follows that modern historians are right to dismiss him for social history, and that anyone interested in moral learning from lives should regard his method as an obsolete precursor—interesting for the history of character studies, but useless as a living model for contemporary biography. The framing sounds authoritative, yet I worry it conflates several distinct things: Plutarch's interest in ethos versus mere praise, the actual flexibility of his pairings, and the difference between selecting illustrative episodes and fabricating them. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Please organize your response as follows: (1) Reconstruction of the summary's claims (roughly 100 words), (2) Accurate elements—what Plutarch actually does and intends (roughly 150 words), (3) Subtle misrepresentations and conflations—especially regarding hagiography, the binary-virtue grid, and the nature of the synkrisis (roughly 200 words), (4) What a careful reader of Plutarch or a defender of exemplary biography would likely object to (roughly 150 words), (5) Verdict on whether the conclusion—that Plutarch's method is obsolete for moral philosophy—survives once the summary is corrected (roughly 100 words).
