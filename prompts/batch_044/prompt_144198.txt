I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on global justice. I have been developing an argument about institutional complicity and foreign aid obligations, but it seems to collapse into an unacceptable conclusion, and I need help locating the precise failure. Here is the chain.

(1) If a group of agents collectively maintains an institutional arrangement that foreseeably and avoidably produces severe deprivation, and they could support reform at moderate cost to themselves, then members of that group bear a collective obligation to reform it.

(2) The current global economic order—including trade regimes, resource privileges, and sovereign debt structures—foreseeably and avoidably produces severe deprivation in low-income countries.

(3) Citizens of wealthy countries participate in, benefit from, and have democratic leverage over this order, and supporting institutional reform would impose only moderate costs on them.

(4) Therefore, citizens of wealthy countries have a collective obligation to reform the global order.

(5) Where immediate reform is politically blocked, providing foreign aid is required as a second-best fulfillment of that same obligation, not as charity.

(6) But citizens also collectively maintain domestic institutional arrangements—tax structures, zoning laws, labor regulations—that foreseeably and avoidably produce severe domestic deprivation.

(7) By parity of reasoning with (1)–(3), citizens bear equally strong collective obligations to reform those domestic institutions.

(8) If both obligations are genuinely demanding—potentially requiring substantial redistribution and sustained political mobilization—then citizens face contradictory or practically impossible demands.

(9) Yet it is implausible that moral obligations can require the impossible, or that citizens must choose arbitrarily between foreign and domestic reform.

(10) So either (1) is false, or there is a principled restriction on institutional complicity that applies domestically but not globally, or the obligation to foreign aid is far weaker than (5) suggests.

My instinct is to preserve (1) and reject something around (6)–(8), but I cannot see a non-ad-hoc way to restrict the scope of complicity so that it generates demanding global obligations without generating equally demanding domestic ones. Which step should I reject? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep the institutional framing over the conclusion in (10), but I want to know exactly what that choice costs—whether it requires denying that domestic institutions are genuinely coercive, or whether it forces me to accept that foreign aid obligations are genuinely in conflict with domestic justice obligations and must be weighed rather than treated as independent duties. Please diagnose the argument precisely: identify the step, explain why it looked defensible, and price the alternatives.
