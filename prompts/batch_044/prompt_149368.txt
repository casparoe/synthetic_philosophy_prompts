As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for historical fiction, I want to predict René Descartes’s reaction—were he alive today—to the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia, in which subjects experience intact nociceptive signals yet report no suffering, fear, or motivational urgency, typically following lesions to the insular cortex. Would he take this dissociation as proof that the soul can register bodily damage without being moved, thereby reinforcing his distinction between mechanistic reflex and conscious passion? Or would he maintain that genuine pain necessarily involves the soul’s evaluative response, and therefore deny that these patients feel true pain despite their sensory reports? Please reason strictly from his commitments regarding the union of mind and body, the pineal gland as the site of interaction, and his account of the passions of the soul. To test the consistency of your reconstruction, apply it to the following invented case for my narrative: a soldier with pain asymbolia continues fighting through severe tissue damage not out of courage, but because the injury registers only as neutral information lacking any urgent call to withdraw. Would Descartes classify this soldier’s body as operating as a pure automaton, or say that his soul perceives the damage without being affected? State the single principle that unifies both judgments, and do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
