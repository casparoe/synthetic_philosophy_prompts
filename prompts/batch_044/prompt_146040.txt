As a journalist who covers the philosophy of social science and public reasoning, I recently tried to work through an extension of Amartya Sen’s well-known tension between minimal liberalism and Pareto efficiency—the familiar two-person scenario in which each individual prefers that the other read a particular book, yet both insist on deciding for themselves, producing a cycle that blocks unanimous improvement. During a panel discussion on democratic legitimacy, I formalized the worry into the following chain, but the conclusion strikes me as absurdly strong, and I want to know exactly where the reasoning slips. (1) Minimal liberalism requires that at least one individual hold decisive authority over some personal domain. (2) Pareto efficiency requires that no social choice be adopted if an alternative makes everyone better off. (3) In any society of three or more agents with preferences over at least three distinct binary issues, one can embed interlocking Sen-style cycles across domains, so that honoring one person’s decisive authority on issue A necessarily forces a Pareto-inferior arrangement on issue B for another agent. (4) Therefore, to prevent cyclical aggregation, a collective decision rule must either deny decisive authority to every agent—violating minimal liberalism—or tolerate globally Pareto-inferior outcomes. (5) Consequently, no institutional mechanism can simultaneously satisfy minimal liberal rights and achieve Pareto efficiency in genuinely pluralistic societies. (6) Yet modern liberal democracies claim to do precisely both. (7) Hence that constitutional claim is not merely impractical but conceptually incoherent.

I am inclined to treat (1) and (2) as definitional and therefore non-negotiable, but I suspect the overreach lies between (3) and (4), perhaps in an illicit aggregation of preferences across separate domains, or in a hidden assumption that preference independence fails in a specific way. Could you diagnose the precise misstep—whether it is an equivocation between intra-domain and cross-domain authority, a quantifier shift, or a modal slide that treats local cycles as global necessities? I would also like to know what rejecting (3) versus rejecting (4) costs for anyone attempting to defend liberal democratic institutions: does giving up (3) require abandoning the possibility of multi-issue liberal rights altogether, and does giving up (4) commit us to accepting systematic inefficiency as the price of liberty? If the argument is in fact valid, I want to understand which premise is least costly to surrender and why it initially looked so plausible.
