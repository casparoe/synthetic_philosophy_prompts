I'm preparing remarks for a philosophy seminar on classical ethics and want a consistency check on my position regarding Cicero's treatise on old age before I present. I hold that (a) Cicero successfully shows old age is not miserable provided one retains wisdom and mental activity, since bodily decline is not a true harm; (b) he genuinely intends the recommendation that the elderly withdraw from public affairs and pursue private contemplation (otium) as normative advice, not merely as rhetorical consolation tailored to Cato; (c) his Stoic framework treats physical strength and health as preferred indifferents rather than goods, with virtue and wisdom alone being genuinely good; (d) if wisdom genuinely accumulates with age and experience, then the elderly possess greater—not lesser—moral authority to guide the state, which pulls directly against the withdrawal thesis in (b); (e) Cicero's own refusal to withdraw from politics after Caesar's death and his execution for resisting tyranny reveal an implicit rejection of (b) that the text never resolves; and (f) I read his praise of old-age friendship as largely instrumental, valuable chiefly for mental exercise and practical estate management, rather than as an intrinsic constituent of the good life. Which of these commitments are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, identify any that dissolve on inspection, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
