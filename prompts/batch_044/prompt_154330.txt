I'm not an academic philosopher, but I've been working through Plato's Laws on my own and I'm caught on a strange idea he has there: that getting drunk isn't just a pleasure or a vice, but can be deliberately arranged as a kind of examination of the soul, where loosening reason lets the lower parts show whether they're governed or wild. He pairs this with strict rules about who may drink, who must supervise, and how old one must be, as if the test only works inside a very specific social frame. I'm running a discussion group at a community library — about ten adults, ages 25 to 60, one 90-minute session, no one is a specialist. I want a structured activity, not a lecture, that makes participants feel this tension themselves: they should experience both the diagnostic pull (that intoxication reveals something hidden) and the framing problem (that without the right rules, it just corrupts). Design a role-play or deliberation with assigned roles — say, elder citizens, young trainees, magistrates, and guests — rules about when wine may be served, hidden incentives or token payoffs, and a decision point where the group must vote on whether to allow a "test" for a particular character. Build in an asymmetry: the person being tested learns something about themselves that the observers can't reliably access. Include a timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto Plato's distinctions without naming them at the start, an honest note on which feature — probably the idea that reason remains present as an internal observer even when its authority is suspended — the activity can't reproduce and must be supplied verbally, and a warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time it's run. Don't ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; the activity should stand on its own logic.
