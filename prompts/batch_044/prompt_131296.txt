Both microhistory and history from below arose in the 1970s primarily as reactions against the Annales school's quantitative, long-term structural history and against conventional political history focused on rulers and states. Both aim to recover the lives of ordinary people—peasants, artisans, women—who are largely invisible in official archives. Yet they differ fundamentally in scale and method. History from below, exemplified by Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class and by Hobsbawm's work, is essentially a macro-social project: it relies on aggregate data, class analysis, and structural economic change to demonstrate how subordinate groups shaped large-scale transformations. Microhistory, by contrast, narrows the scale to a single village, trial, or individual—such as Ginzburg's Menocchio in The Cheese and the Worms or Le Roy Ladurie's study of Montaillou—using thick narrative and close reading rather than statistical series. Where history from below reconstructs collective consciousness through material conditions and organizational records, microhistory treats popular mental worlds as opaque puzzles that must be decoded from exceptional traces. Ginzburg's clue paradigm claims that a historian can fully recover a peasant's cosmology through sufficiently skilled hermeneutic interpretation of fragments, effectively erasing the distance between elite documentation and popular belief. At the same time, microhistorians typically reject macro-context altogether, treating each case as a self-contained universe with no necessary connection to broader structures. Consequently, microhistory represents a more radical epistemological rupture with state-centered history than Thompson's framework, which still assigns causal priority to economic structures over individual agency.
