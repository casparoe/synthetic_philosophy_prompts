ok here's an argument about logical omnisicence that's been bugging me. (1) standard closure: if S knows p and S knows p→q, S knows q. (2) i know there's coffee in my mug, call it C. (3) C, together with basic arithmetic and logic, objectively entails some insanely remote claim R that takes like half a million proof steps to derive — not that i care which R, just that there is one. (4) now, i also know logic in general: i understand modus ponens, the rules, etc. (5) my friend says that knowing logic in general means i implicitly know every specific instance of entailment, including C→R. (6) so by (1) i should know R. (7) but i don't know R — i've never derived it and i know my cognative limits. (8) therefore, either i don't know C, or closure is false, or (5) is wrong. (9) but (5) looks right: if you understand modus ponens, don't you understand it applies everywhere? (10) and if closure is false everywhere, that's a huge cost — it kills epistemic logic. (11) so the only out is denying (2): i don't really know there's coffee. (12) but apply this to any empirical claim and you get that nobody knows anything empirical. (13) that can't be right. so where's the slip? is (5) an equivocation between general competence and specific propositional knowledge? or is there a hidden modal slide between 'objectively entails' and 'is known to entail'? i'd like to keep (1) and (2) if possible.
