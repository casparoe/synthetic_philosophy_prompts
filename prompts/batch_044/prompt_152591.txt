Here is an argument about letter-spirit—the invariant essence that makes every token of "a" an "a"—that seems to collapse the distinction between alphabets entirely.

(1) For any letterform L, there is an essential property—its letter-spirit—necessary and sufficient for being L.
(2) Because style varies infinitely across tokens, the letter-spirit cannot include metric features like stroke weight, slant, or closure size; it must be purely topological.
(3) The letter-spirit of Latin lowercase "a" is therefore the structure: one enclosed counter joined to an ascending stem on the right.
(4) Handwritten Greek alpha in several European traditions, certain Cyrillic handwritten variants, and even decorative loops in engineering diagrams exhibit exactly that topology.
(5) So by (1) and (3), those tokens possess the letter-spirit of "a"; they are "a"-tokens regardless of notational context.
(6) But alphabet membership fixes phonetic and semantic function, so these tokens should behave as Latin vowels, which they manifestly do not.
(7) To avoid this graphemic overgeneration, we might deny that topology is sufficient, yet that undermines (1): if form alone never guarantees identity, there is no shared property among all and only "a"s.
(8) Alternatively, we might claim the letter-spirit is lineage-anchored—applying only within the Latin notational lineage—but then (2) is undermined, since the same topological form in Greek carries a different spirit despite identical structure, meaning form was never doing the independent work.

Each premise looks defensible, and the reasoning seems valid, yet (7) and (8) present an unacceptable choice: either letter-spirit does not exist, or it is tied to historical adoption in a way that makes pure form recognition mysterious. Which step is the weak link? Explain why that step seemed plausible, then price the three main responses: rejecting the absolute sufficiency in (1), rejecting the purity claim in (2), or accepting lineage-anchoring and explaining what that costs for cross-cultural and cross-system letter recognition. I would most like to keep (2) intact if possible.
