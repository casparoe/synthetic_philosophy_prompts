I am constructing characters for a novel who misunderstand their own motives, and I want the underlying structure to be philosophically coherent rather than merely dramatic. Here are my judgments about when self-deception is genuinely occurring. A memoirist reconstructs her childhood as idyllic despite possessing archival evidence that contradicts her; she genuinely feels nostalgia and never experiences the contradiction as live — I judge this self-deception. A propagandist internalizes a doctrine he initially knew was fabricated; years later he defends it sincerely, yet under clinical interview he can retrieve the original fabrication notes and report them without surprise — I still judge this self-deception, though it feels structurally different. A novelist writes under a pseudonym whose political views she disowns in her private journal; over time she begins to cite the pseudonym's essays as her own convictions, yet she can switch registers instantly and knows precisely which voice is speaking — here I waver: is this self-deception or a kind of performed multiplicity? By contrast, a patient who receives a terminal diagnosis, understands every word of it, and sincerely reports feeling no anxiety because she is "focused on recovery" — I do not judge this self-deception, but rather protective refusal. And a tax preparer who meticulously reports large income but systematically forgets small cash payments, sincerely describing himself as "rigorously honest" — I do judge this self-deception, yet it seems closer to the patient than to the memoirist. Is there a principle — perhaps involving partitioned belief, or what I might call the loss of "first-person custodianship" over a mental state — that captures all my verdicts except perhaps the pseudonym case? Or must I choose between a simpler principle that overturns one verdict and a more complex one that saves them all? Please also predict what your best principle says about a case I have not listed: a therapist who comes to share her patient's delusion during long-term treatment, knowing the clinical literature but finding it no longer emotionally available. Finally, separate what is well-established in philosophical psychology from what remains genuinely contested — for instance, whether self-deception requires literal contradictory beliefs or can be explained by motivated reasoning alone — and do not present any contested account as if it were the consensus.
