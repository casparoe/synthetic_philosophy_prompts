Write a six-month research proposal—suitable for a graduate fellowship or a dissertation prospectus—on the following question. Many constitutional systems do not contain a single flat amendment rule; they contain nested or conditional architectures in which one rule, R1, sets the procedure for ordinary constitutional change, and a second rule, R2, governs how R1 itself may be altered. In some designs R2's own conditions of validity depend on R1's current status—for instance, R2 may be activated only when R1 has not been suspended, or only for amendments touching specific domains that R1 designates. The classic paradox of legal self-reference asks whether a rule can validly amend its own amendment procedure. Existing philosophical responses—strict hierarchical separation, acceptance-based accounts of legal validity, or treating constitutional change as external to the legal system—were developed for single-level rules. They do not clearly extend to cases where the meta-rule's applicability is interdependent with the object-rule's status. Your proposal should state the problem precisely: does nesting R1 inside R2 eliminate the circularity, relocate it to the second level, or compound it into a regress?

Explain why the question is open and tractable. Real-world constitutions increasingly use subject-matter-differentiated entrenchment, emergency-triggered amendment procedures, and delegation of meta-amendment authority to bodies outside the ordinary legislature, so flat models are descriptively inadequate. Yet the philosophical literature has largely analyzed one-layer cases. A conceptual project can make progress without empirical surveys: the issue is logical consistency and the conditions of valid authority, not the frequency with which nested clauses appear.

Propose your approach. Construct a formal taxonomy of two-level architectures—strict hierarchical separation (R2 entirely independent of R1), interdependent nesting (R2 valid only if R1 holds), and conditional delegation (an external body activates R2 when R1 meets a failure condition)—and diagnose for each whether self-reference is resolved, deferred, or multiplied. Defend conceptual modeling over the obvious alternatives: comparative constitutional law can catalog provisions but cannot by itself diagnose circularity, and standard hierarchical solutions assume a static basic norm that nested, conditional rules precisely undermine. Specify what a successful result looks like: a clear criterion distinguishing architectures that stabilize amendment authority from those that generate an infinite regress or an ungrounded loop. Specify failure: demonstrating that nesting is logically possible without showing whether it addresses the underlying tension of self-amending authority. Keep the scope realistic—conceptual analysis with a small set of illustrative constitutional designs, not a global survey or historical study of amendment practices. Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; refer to positions and arguments generally and note that citations will be added separately.
