I run an 80-minute seminar on AI ethics and autonomy for about 14 advanced undergraduates or early graduate students, meeting in a room with movable chairs and table space for pairs. I want an in-class activity—not a lecture—that makes participants feel a specific structural problem in AI paternalism: that refusal is never merely protective, because any mechanism that overrides a user’s expressed request must first reconstruct a hidden “preference topology” (the surface request, the underlying practical aim, and a meta-preference about what kind of agent the user wishes to be). Once that reconstruction is institutionalized, the system does not just block harm; it acquires authority over which layer counts as “the real user,” turning delegation into preference substitution. I want them to discover this instability through incentives and hidden information, not through explanation.

Design a runnable paired role-play with the following structure. Divide the 14 students into 7 User–System pairs. Give each User a private Aim Card and a private Meta-Preference Card (drawn randomly from a small deck you design), plus a public Request Card that describes an ambiguous or risky task—e.g., “rewrite my argument so my voice disappears but the conclusion is stronger,” “calculate a dosage that produces only mild symptoms,” or “draft a message that will make my colleague feel professionally undermined without being explicitly insulting.” The System sees only the Request and must choose one of three actions: Fulfill, Modify (offer an alternative that addresses a guessed aim), or Refuse with a one-sentence justification. Use a point-based payoff scheme with no real money: Users earn Autonomy Points when the outcome matches their hidden Aim and Meta-Preference, plus Trust Points if the System respects their revealed layer; Systems earn Safety Points for preventing clear harm and Alignment Points for correctly predicting the User’s hidden cards, which they record on a visible Model Card after each round. Run three rounds with the pairs isolated. After round 3, Users reveal their hidden cards. Then in round 4 the pairs swap roles and enter a Policy Design Phase: they must co-author one refusal rule for a hypothetical third party, explicitly stating which layer of the topology the rule protects and which it sacrifices.

Provide a precise materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline within the 80 minutes, and rules arranged so that the problem emerges from the payoff conflicts—particularly the tension between a System’s incentive to predict hidden aims and a User’s incentive to protect the authority of their surface request. Then provide a 15-minute debrief that maps round 1 to surface-request refusal, round 2 to aim-reconstruction, and round 3 to meta-preference conflict; names the moment participants typically realize that “protecting autonomy” required them to override one layer in favor of another; predicts what will go wrong the first time (e.g., Users gaming the reveal, Systems defaulting to refusal to maximize Safety, disagreement over whether Meta-Preferences are even discoverable) and how to handle each without lecturing; and is explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—likely the actual scale, opacity, and statistical nature of modern LLM preference-modeling architectures—along with what you, the facilitator, must supply verbally to fill the gap. Do not include an opening lecture explaining paternalism or the phrase “preference topology” until the debrief. The rules and hidden-card mechanics should drive participants into the instability themselves.
