I have recently become preoccupied with the philosophical distinction between historical contingency and structural inevitability, prompted by the discovery that my grandparents’ marriage — and consequently my own existence — turned on a misdelivered letter in 1952. Had that letter reached its intended recipient, my family line would not have formed; there is no redundant causal path by which an equivalent union might have produced me. This singular bottleneck has led me to examine whether large-scale historical outcomes exhibit the same openness, or whether they are instead multiply realisable.

Below are my verdicts on several counterfactual scenarios. I seek not a narrative of what would have occurred, but the principle — if one exists — that unifies these judgments, together with its predictions for a case I have not listed.

1. Had Archduke Franz Ferdinand’s driver not taken the wrong turn in Sarajevo, the alliance structures and Balkan tensions of 1914 were such that a general European war would likely have broken out by 1916 via some other crisis; the specific trigger was contingent, but the macro-conflict was structurally overdetermined.

2. Had James Watt never refined the steam engine, coal-powered mechanisation would still have emerged within two decades through iterative improvements to existing atmospheric engines; the technological core of the Industrial Revolution was redundant across inventors.

3. Had Charles Darwin never published his theory, Wallace’s independent formulation — developed in the mid-1850s — would likely have compelled the same evolutionary synthesis within months; the theory was epistemically inevitable given the accumulated empirical data.

4. Had the Library of Alexandria survived intact into the medieval period, the institutional conditions for modern empirical science might have consolidated centuries earlier with a markedly different geography of knowledge; its destruction removed a singular reservoir that other libraries could not functionally replace.

5. Had the American colonies lost the Revolutionary War, republican government as a global model would have been delayed but eventually realised — most plausibly through the French revolutionary trajectory — because the socio-economic conditions for representative institutions were spreading independently across the Atlantic world.

6. Had the French Revolution never occurred — say, through successful constitutional reform by Louis XVI — I am inclined to think that modern secular republicanism would not have developed in its actual form; European monarchy might have adapted gradually without producing the specific ideological rupture that reshaped political modernity.

There is an evident tension between (5) and (6): in both cases I invoke spreading socio-economic conditions, yet I treat American failure as replaceable and French absence as irreplaceable. My tentative principle is that an outcome is historically inevitable if and only if it is reachable via at least two causally independent pathways within a generational span, and contingent if it depends on a singular causal bottleneck lacking functional equivalents. This appears to cover (1)–(4), but I am unsure whether it genuinely resolves the conflict between (5) and (6) without ad hoc adjustments, or whether it forces me to abandon one of them.

- State the principle, or small family of principles, that best captures the pattern in (1)–(6), precisely enough that its clauses can be traced back to the cases that motivate them.
- Apply it to the unlisted case: had Werner Heisenberg never entered physics, would the matrix formulation of quantum mechanics have emerged by 1930 through another researcher?
- Indicate honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving every verdict is possible only at the cost of unacceptable complexity.
- Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am assessing conceptual coherence, not scholarly coverage.
