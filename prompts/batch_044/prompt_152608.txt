In evaluating historical counterfactuals I keep encountering an asymmetry that does not map neatly onto standard minimal-rewrite rules. When a scenario alters several variables that belong to a recognized institutional cluster—say, both the electoral formula and the party-financing rules of a particular regime—critics often treat the change as one coherent intervention and assess its plausibility as a package. But when a scenario alters the same number of variables that do not form any recognized cluster—say, voter turnout, judicial independence, and military loyalty each shifting separately—critics treat every alteration as an independent implausible miracle and reject the scenario for requiring "too many" changes. The quantitative burden is identical; only the perceived organizational coherence differs. Is there an established concept for this specific evaluative pattern, where counterfactual permissibility hinges not on the magnitude or number of modifications but on whether they constitute a historically recognizable bundle? If there is no standard term, can you define the phenomenon precisely, distinguish it from institutional path-dependence and from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustments, and specify what conditions would make a bundle legitimate rather than ad hoc?
