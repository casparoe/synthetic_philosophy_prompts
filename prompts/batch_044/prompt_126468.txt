I am a philosophy professor locked in a long-running disagreement with a colleague I have always regarded as my equal in judgment—similar background in moral philosophy, comparable track record of sound reasoning, no reason to think either of us is generally sharper on these matters—about whether our university should require students to declare any use of generative AI in coursework. I see the rule as an unjustified suspicion that undermines trust between teacher and student; she sees it as the bare minimum for honest evaluation. We have argued extensively, each presenting what we take to be our best reasons, and neither of us has moved.

What complicates the situation is that her stance is partly rooted in a specific experience: she served on a disciplinary panel that handled an undetected case of AI-assisted plagiarism, which she says gives her direct knowledge of a risk I have not personally encountered. My stance, meanwhile, derives mainly from a long-held general view about student autonomy that I held before this policy was ever proposed. She now argues that because our beliefs come from such different sources—her particular case versus my general principle—we are not equally positioned on this question, and that I should therefore adjust my confidence downward and defer to her rather than treating our disagreement as a standoff between two equally reliable minds.

Given that I genuinely respect her reasoning, should I revise my belief? Or does the fact that our convictions are anchored in different kinds of grounds—firsthand experience of a specific harm versus commitment to a broad principle—mean that treating us as symmetrical here is a mistake, and that I am rational to hold firm? I am asking not for a recommendation on the policy itself, but for an analysis of what this kind of disagreement does to the standing of my belief.
