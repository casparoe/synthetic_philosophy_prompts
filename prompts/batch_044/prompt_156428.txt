I have observed a particular dialectical pattern in discussions of rational disagreement—especially where participants treat the formal principle that shared evidence together with identical initial beliefs should yield convergent judgments as a normative accounting scheme. The pattern is not a direct denial of that principle, but a specific way of protecting it from counterexample by treating any persistent divergence as proof of an unspecified prior difference. I describe two concrete cases; then I ask whether this move has a recognized name, and, whether named or not, I want its precise boundaries, its distinction from nearby fallacies, and the conditions that separate legitimate uses from abusive ones.

Case one: Two research teams apply the same statistical procedure to an identical dataset but reach contradictory causal inferences. When pressed, defenders of one team do not point to any independently documented methodological commitment made prior to seeing the data. Instead, they assert that the teams must have held different background weights—unspecified assessments of model simplicity versus predictive fit—that become visible only retrospectively, through the disagreement itself. These reconstructed weights are never measured or articulated outside the context of explaining the divergence; they function as a residual category that absorbs whatever convergence failure remains after all shared evidence is accounted for.

Case two: In a policy debate about precautionary regulation, two experts agree on every empirical forecast but recommend opposite actions. A mediator explains the stalemate by invoking a missing common prior over the value of avoiding catastrophic risk. When asked to specify that prior independently—say, by eliciting the experts' risk preferences in domains unrelated to the current policy—the mediator offers only restatements of the experts' opposing recommendations: one assigns a higher prior weight to tail risks, the other to opportunity costs. The prior is defined entirely by the conclusion it is meant to support, and its content vanishes when separated from the disagreement it explains.

In both cases, an invisible parameter is posited solely to preserve the convergence norm: divergence occurred, therefore the parameter must have differed, and the parameter's specific content is read off from the very divergence it explains. Is there an established term for this specific maneuver—the post-hoc reconstruction of an unobservable prior divergence to insulate a shared-belief framework from empirical counterexample? If so, I want more than the label: I want to know how it relates to, and differs from, ordinary ad hoc hypothesizing, the motte-and-bailey defense, and simple confirmation bias, and whether there exist legitimate border cases where prior reconstruction is epistemically sound. If there is no established term, please coin one with a rigorous definition, specify the boundary conditions that distinguish legitimate reconstruction from this pattern, and provide contrasting cases that sit near the edge.
