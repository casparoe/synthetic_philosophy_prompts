I just finished a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend who insists that if history does not invoke universal laws, it is not really explaining anything—only arranging events in a pleasing order. I am now preparing a single ninety-minute session for a small interdisciplinary reading group—mostly literary scholars and one sociologist, none with philosophy training—on the covering-law versus narrative model of historical explanation. Do not explain the debate to me; plan how I should teach it.

First, lay out the dependency order: what conceptual distinction or tool has to be in place before the covering-law model can even be introduced, and why introducing narrative explanation too early collapses the two into mere stylistic preference? Second, pick the one concrete historical case that should carry the entire session—I am torn between the fall of the Western Roman Empire and the British Industrial Revolution—and tell me which one actually works, which one fails and why, and what specific feature the successful case must possess, such as contested multi-causal structure or a clearly bounded outcome that invites counterfactual probing. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form or arrive with: I anticipate they will treat narrative as synonymous with subjective interpretation, and that they will assume covering-law explanation requires predicting the past rather than accounting for it; specify the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted, and how. Fourth, include one brief check—perhaps a single targeted question or a two-minute writing prompt—that will reveal whether they understand that narrative explanations can be objectively evaluated without being reducible to law-subsumption. Fifth, state explicitly what I should refuse to cover at this level—perhaps the logical-positivist origins of the covering-law model, or the technical literature on rational explanation—and say so outright.

Finally, inject one slightly non-obvious conceptual framing into the plan: treat narrative explanation as implicitly relying on counterfactual stability conditions—asking which details, if removed or altered, would break the explanatory coherence—so that the session does not merely contrast two models but shows how narrative carries its own form of modal rigor without invoking universal laws.
