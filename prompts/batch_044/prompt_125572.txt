Recently I decided to stop voting in large-scale national elections and to eliminate beef from my diet, both for climate-related reasons. At dinner, a friend reconstructed our disagreement and asked me to justify the coherence of these two abstentions; I want an outside adjudication rather than reassurance. (1) I argued that in elections with millions of voters the probability my ballot is decisive is negligible, so my vote carries no expected climate benefit, and that the same applies to skipping one burger, since supply-chain adjustments absorb my absence without reducing total emissions. (2) She replied that this is the standard inefficacy objection, but it fails for both cases because each act belongs to a collective practice—electoral coalitions and consumer markets—where sustained individual participation maintains the very structures that make collective change possible; abstaining erodes the agent that could eventually legislate or shift demand. (3) I countered that she smuggles in collective intentionality without an actual coordination mechanism. A boycott works because it has a success-threshold linking participants; elections proceed regardless of whether I vote, and beef production responds to aggregate demand rather than tracking my specific withdrawal, so there is no structural link that turns my isolated act into part of a collective one. (4) She said I conflate success-thresholds with identity-thresholds: climate voters constitute a real collective entity whose statistical mandate shapes policy even when no vote is pivotal, and membership in that entity is causally meaningful. By parallel reasoning, she claimed, my beef abstention expresses membership in a consumer collective capable of shifting long-run market signals. (5) I introduced an asymmetry: consumption involves rebound effects—reduced demand can lower prices and increase others' consumption—so my individual contribution is structurally indeterminate and may be fully offset. Voting lacks this feedback loop; an absent vote is never replaced by someone voting twice. Hence, I said, collective identity tracks contribution linearly in voting but not in consumption, which explains why the inefficacy objection bites differently—though she insisted both obligations survive. (6) She concluded that if I invoke collective identity to defend voting, consistency requires accepting it for diet, and that my appeal to economic feedback confuses structural indeterminacy with the absence of contribution, since my expressive and statistical role in the market remains even if prices adjust. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, explain exactly where each speaker is right and where they err, and say whether either misses a deeper point—especially about whether feedback loops can render an individual contribution genuinely indeterminate rather than merely negligible, and what that implies for the symmetry between electoral and consumption-based climate obligations.
