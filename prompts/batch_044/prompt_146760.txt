In my forecasting and epistemology reading group, a disagreement about epistemic modesty has remained frozen for nearly a year despite what looks like substantial common ground. One faction—the calibrationists—holds that modesty is essentially an outside-view discipline: when you form a belief about a specific case, say the success of a research program or the resolution of a policy debate, you should anchor your confidence to the reference-class base rate and treat your inside-view causal story as likely overfit. The opposing faction—the structuralists—agrees that overconfidence is a vice but insists that genuine modesty is not statistical deference; rather, it is the recognition that your causal mechanism might be misspecified in ways that reference-class frequencies cannot detect, so systematically downgrading the inside view whenever it conflicts with the base rate is itself a form of epistemic arrogance. Both sides cite the same canonical forecasting failures, both accept that well-calibrated beliefs are desirable, and both reject naive overconfidence, yet neither has modified its position in response to the other. I am trying to understand what kind of impasse this is. Is it a substantive disagreement about whether the outside view is epistemically prior to the inside view? Is it verbal, turning on whether the term “modesty” denotes deference to frequencies or openness to structural error? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one about belief revision under uncertainty, the other about the transparency of one’s own reasoning—under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, is there any distinction, formal result, or reconceptualization that could actually move the debate, or should we conclude that the stall reflects bedrock intuitions about whether treating one’s own reasoning as opaque is a virtue rather than a failure?

What makes the stall particularly puzzling is that each side seems to smuggle in a normative picture of intellectual agency without defending it explicitly. The calibrationists appear to assume that a rational agent should regard her detailed model as one noisy sample from a distribution of similar models, which makes modesty a matter of statistical self-effacement; the structuralists appear to assume that rational agency requires maintaining ownership over one’s causal narrative, which makes modesty a matter of vigilance against hidden misspecification rather than frequency-based discounting. These pictures are not obviously contradictory—one could hold both—yet in practice they license opposite prescriptions when the inside and outside views diverge sharply. I want a diagnosis that engages these concrete commitments rather than surveying the forecasting literature, together with an honest assessment of whether progress here requires an empirical finding about predictive accuracy, a conceptual distinction between calibration humility and mechanism humility, or an acknowledgment that no argument will resolve the disagreement because it ultimately concerns what kind of epistemic agent we aspire to be.
