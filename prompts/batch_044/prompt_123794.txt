"The lottery paradox happens with a fair lottery of 10,000 tickets. For each ticket we rationally believe it will lose since the probability is 0.9999, but we also believe some ticket wins. The individual beliefs are independent, so accepting one doesn't affect the rest. If belief is closed under conjunction we should believe all tickets lose, contradicting the other belief. So closure has to go.

The preface paradox is the same shape. An author believes each claim in her book is true, but also believes there's at least one error. Here too the claims are evaluated independently, just like the tickets. By closure she should believe the big conjunction of all claims, which conflicts with believing there's an error. So both paradoxes hit the same closure principle and both rely on independent evaluation.

For the collective variant: five reviewers each endorse ten assessments. Every reviewer thinks her own ten are sound, but the panel together says at least one of the fifty is flawed. Since no single reviewer holds all fifty beliefs, there's no individual agent with contradictory commitments. The closure problem is avoided because acceptance is spread out. This isn't a real paradox—just a practical coordination issue about tracking who endorsed what. So distributing belief across agents dissolves the logical tension completely."
