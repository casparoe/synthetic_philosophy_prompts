After a long argument with a friend about whether moral revolutions are driven by reasons or by honor dynamics, I'm stuck on an argument I constructed that seems to collapse the distinction between moral progress and mere social fashion. Here it is, with a distinction I added to make the mechanism precise:

(1) In the end of dueling and the abolition of slavery, the decisive shift was not that people were persuaded by new moral arguments, but that the honor economy sustaining the practice inverted: the status payoffs flipped, so that continuing the practice signaled low standing rather than high (the Williams/Appiah thesis).

(2) Define an "honor-inversion" as this kind of status-payoff flip. For an honor-inversion to take hold socially, adherents must experience the shift not as strategic adaptation but as a recognition of principle; otherwise the code loses internal coherence. Call this the "recursive honor" constraint.

(3) So if honor-inversion is the real mechanism behind moral revolutions, then every apparent instance of moral learning is actually a masked strategic adaptation, experienced as principle only because of (2).

(4) But if all moral learning might be masked strategic adaptation, then our present conviction that slavery is wrong has no special rational authority; it is just an artifact of our current honor structure.

(5) Denying that our moral convictions have rational authority undermines the very framework we use to condemn past practices.

(6) Therefore the Williams/Appiah thesis proves too much: it turns moral progress into fashion, which is unacceptable.

(7) So moral revolutions must be driven by rational arguments after all.

I want to keep (1) and the idea that honor dynamics genuinely matter, and I'm drawn to (2), but I cannot accept (6) or (7). I feel the slip is between (3) and (4) — perhaps an equivocation between "might be masked" and "is masked," or a scope shift from causal explanation to epistemic debunking — but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, can I preserve both (1) and the claim that our condemnation of slavery is rationally authoritative?

Please push back if this request rests on a confused premise: for instance, if I am illicitly deriving an epistemic conclusion from a causal thesis, tell me plainly rather than accommodating the mistake.
