In discussions of G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, his claim that personal affection and aesthetic appreciation constitute the supreme intrinsic goods, and that they form an organic unity rather than a simple aggregate, is sometimes analogized to binocular vision: each eye supplies a distinct image, neither reducible to the other, yet together they produce depth perception that neither could achieve alone, and losing one eye does not merely weaken the view but eliminates the capacity for depth altogether. The analogy is deployed to defend the stronger claim that a life lacking either friendship or beauty is not merely poorer but structurally deficient in value, comparable to monocular vision being structurally deficient in spatial perception. Audit this analogy precisely: lay out the correspondence table (the two eyes, neural fusion, the emergent depth, the blindness upon loss), distinguish the disanalogies that undermine the axiological argument from those that are merely decorative, examine whether the model illegitimately imports a perceptual-holism that Moore's actual reasoning does not require, and determine whether his conclusion -- that these goods form an inseparable organic unity rather than an optimally paired set -- survives once the breaks are mapped. If the analogy fails, propose a better model for what Moore meant by organic unity in this context.
