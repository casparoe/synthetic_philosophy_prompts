Here's an argument reconstructed from Pascal's fragments on justice and force that seems to collapse moral obligation into strategic compliance. (1) True justice is epistemically inaccessible to human reason; the justice we actually enforce is whatever custom and force have established. (2) If a normative standard is epistemically inaccessible, it cannot serve as a practical ground for obligation. (3) Therefore all practical obligations must derive entirely from the existing order of custom and force. (4) Any social order—including a tyrant's—establishes custom and force if it succeeds in being obeyed. (5) So there is no normative distinction between a legitimate state and a successful tyranny, only a descriptive one (stability, duration). (6) Pascal nevertheless insists we have a duty to obey established laws and avoid rebellion. (7) But if (5) holds, that "duty" can only be prudential—avoid punishment, preserve order—not genuinely moral. (8) Hence Pascal's view eliminates the moral/prudential distinction for political obedience. I want to preserve both (1) and (6): I think justice really is hidden from reason, and I think rebellion is usually wrong. Which step between (2) and (5) should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a hidden distinction—say, between the origin of a norm and its binding force, or between epistemic inaccessibility and practical irrelevance—that blocks the slide?
