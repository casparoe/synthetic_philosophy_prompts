I am preparing a lecture on the moral limits of state partiality and want to test whether the standard debate about compatriot priority can illuminate actual policy architecture rather than remain at the level of abstract distribution. Consider the UK vaccine-procurement episode of 2020–2021: the government entered advance-purchase agreements—including with AstraZeneca—that incorporated supply-priority provisions for domestic distribution, and it subsequently restricted exports of UK-manufactured doses to the European Union and to COVAX mechanisms, even while global vaccination coverage in low-income countries remained below five percent and domestic supply had not yet reached all vulnerable groups. The salient facts are that the contracts existed in outline form before specific clauses were disclosed, that ministers defended the restrictions as protecting British lives, and that cosmopolitan critics argued that manufactured doses should have been allocated by epidemiological need rather than nationality.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the nationalism-versus-cosmopolitanism debate over whether compatriots possess special moral claims. I want a disciplined mapping: identify the relevant agents (the UK government, British citizens, EU states, and populations in the Global South), specify the actual distributive options available once manufacturing capacity existed (not merely “share” versus “hoard,” but contractual release, compulsory licensing, and phased export thresholds), and describe the payoffs if the state is conceived either as a fiduciary restricted to its citizens or as an agent bearing global obligations. In particular, press on the distinction between moral partiality—where citizens genuinely hold stronger claims to the good—and institutional partiality, where the state’s distributive reach is structurally limited by its democratic mandate and contractual design regardless of underlying moral weights. Does the contract-based mechanism of priority support an institutional-partiality reading, or does it collapse into an assertion of stronger moral claims? Then identify where the theoretical framework breaks: can either moderate nationalism or strong cosmopolitanism explain why the government eventually released surplus doses only after domestic demand had fallen, or is that timing an anomaly that neither view anticipates? Finally, say what the episode reveals about the theory—whether it confirms that special claims are always mediated by institutional design, or whether it exposes a gap in cosmopolitan objections regarding democratic accountability that the case makes visible.
