I run an 80-minute graduate workshop on bounded rationality for 16 philosophy and cognitive-science students. Design an in-class simulation game—no real money, only points tracked on a visible leaderboard plus a symbolic privilege (choosing the opening case for the next session) for the highest cumulative scorer—that makes participants feel satisficing with aspiration-level adaptation as an ecologically rational strategy rather than a failure of will. Arrange the rules and a hidden payoff environment so that participants who try to locally optimize by spending limited cognitive-budget tokens to map cross-round correlations deplete their resources and earn lower cumulative payoffs, while those who maintain a modest aspiration threshold, accept the first option that meets it, and lower the threshold only after two consecutive failures free up budget and achieve higher long-run scores—experiencing the downward adjustment as strategic relief, not defeat. Specify: a six-round structure with four niche options per round; a hidden temporal-autocorrelation structure linking payoffs across rounds that can be revealed only by spending three budget tokens; each participant’s initial budget of eight tokens and aspiration level of 7; the adaptation rule (failure in two consecutive rounds forces aspiration down by one step and refunds one token); materials needed (niche cards, budget chips, individual score sheets, a sealed correlation envelope); a minute-by-minute timeline; and the point-payoff scheme. The facilitator must not name satisficing, aspiration levels, or ecological rationality before play begins; all framing comes in the debrief. Include a debrief that maps specific game moments onto aspiration adjustment, search-cost depletion, and ecological rationality; predicts first-run failures—such as token hoarding, social reluctance to lower aspiration because it feels like public defeat, or one table cracking the correlation early and breaking the collective experience of difficulty—with concrete handling instructions for each; and frankly states which feature of real-world bounded-rational satisficing the simulation cannot reproduce (for example, the long-term social normalization of aspiration norms or genuine lack of meta-awareness about one’s own cognitive architecture) and that you must supply verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Lead with the bottom-line pedagogical problem in the first sentence—participants discover that fully mapping the hidden payoff structure consumes the very resources that would have made that knowledge profitable—then present the rules, hidden structure, materials, timeline, debrief mapping, failure-mode guide, and honest limitation note, with no preamble and no restatement of the prompt.
