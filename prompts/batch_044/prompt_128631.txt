Write a short story about an experienced Theravada meditator who, during prolonged vipassanā practice, begins to experience her mental life as discrete, momentary cittas—each a brief flash of consciousness with its own object—separated by intervals of subconscious bhavaṅga (life-continuum) rather than by a continuous observing self. One morning she is asked to renew a legal vow she made a year earlier, but her direct phenomenology now tells her that the agent who originally vowed and the agent present now share no enduring substrate: they are causally linked like a flame passed from one candle to another, with only the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) carrying forward in altered configurations. The story should explore whether moral obligation can meaningfully bind a being that has no self to be bound, and whether the causal continuity she observes is sufficient to ground responsibility. After the story, provide a brief author's note that separates what is relatively settled in Theravada Abhidhamma—such as the analysis of consciousness into discrete moments and the doctrine of anattā—from genuinely contested or interpretive claims, especially regarding whether bhavaṅga is ever accessible to conscious inspection and whether the candle-flame analogy captures the orthodox view of personal continuity. In that note, do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view among scholars of the tradition.
