I am working on a reconstruction of Adam Smith's moral psychology, specifically his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. I have come to defend what I call the triadic stabilization thesis: that for Smith, the impartial spectator is not merely an internalized, monological check on one's passions—something like a solitary conscience formed by habit—but rather a structurally relational achievement that requires the actual presence or anticipated judgment of a real social other to stabilize normative assessments of propriety. On this reading, solitary moral reasoning is not just unreliable but conceptually incomplete, because the calibration of sympathy and the determination of what counts as proportionate depend on a three-place relation between agent, sufferer, and an observing interlocutor whose reactions provide the normative anchor. This differs from both the Humean contagion model and the standard internalization reading often projected back onto Smith.

What I want is an honest map of the exposure of this position. Not a literature review, but an assessment of what would actually count against the triadic stabilization thesis. Which passages in Smith—particularly in the sections of The Theory of Moral Sentiments where he discusses the division of the self into agent and spectator—would defenders of the standard monological reading cite as direct counter-evidence, and how decisive are they? Are there conceptual arguments showing that a genuinely internalized spectator can perform all the normative work Smith requires without an actual interlocutor? What results in moral psychology or developmental psychology—say, about solitary moral reasoning or the capacity for self-assessment in socially isolated subjects—would undermine the claim that social triangulation is necessary rather than merely facilitative? And are there analogies in other philosophical traditions—perhaps Confucian practices of self-cultivation or Stoic exercises of self-examination—that suggest Smith could consistently allow for robust solitary moral judgment?

Please rank the defeaters: which would be fatal (forcing abandonment or radical qualification of the thesis), which merely awkward (requiring ad hoc adjustments), and which the thesis's defenders could reasonably dismiss as missing the point. I am especially interested in whether the position risks unfalsifiability: if every apparently solitary moral judgment is re-described as implicitly involving an internalized other, has the triadic claim been insulated in a way that is philosophically damaging? Finally, what predictions or normative commitments does the triadic reading generate that the monological reading does not—say, about the moral status of hermits, about the pathology of social isolation, or about the conditions for moral education—that could be tested against either Smith's own commitments or our contemporary intuitions?
