In debates over whether pain is intrinsically bad or only bad in virtue of its motivational and affective force, philosophers often invoke pain asymbolia—patients who report intact sensory pain but show no withdrawal, distress, or avoidance—and compare normal pain to a home smoke detector: the sensory signal is the siren, the suffering and flight are the sprinkler activation and evacuation, and asymbolia is a detector that blares without being wired to either. From this they conclude that since nothing harmful occurs when the siren sounds in isolation, the badness of pain must reside in the response system, not in the sensory signal itself. Critics counter that the case is more like a smoke detector in a building where everyone is paralyzed: the alarm still indicates real fire, and its value as a warning does not depend on successful escape. Audit both analogies: give an explicit correspondence table mapping sensory quality, affective response, behavioral output, and "badness" onto the detector, the wiring, and the building's occupants; identify where the wiring metaphor quietly assumes that pain's only proper function is to drive action, and whether that functional assumption is supported by the asymbolic case or smuggled in; examine whether the analogy imports a false separation between signal and system that doesn't hold for biological nociception; then consider whether a less familiar but more precise analogy—a fault-tolerant distributed sensor network in which individual nodes report tissue damage without triggering a global shutdown—better captures asymbolia, and ask whether adopting that engineering picture undermines the conclusion that pain without suffering is morally neutral, or whether the neutral conclusion was doing the work all along and the analogy was merely decorative. Finally, say what a genuinely apt analogy would need to preserve, and flag the points where any analogy must give out and judgment takes over.
