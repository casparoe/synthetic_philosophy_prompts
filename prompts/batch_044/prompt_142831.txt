Hey, i'm a PhD student in the history of political thought — mostly Renaissance governance stuff, though i keep getting sidetracked into institutional design. I'm trying to map Machiavelli's implicit rulebook on fortune versus virtù across the verdicts he actually delivers in The Prince: Francesco Sforza gets Milan partly through luck but holds it through ability; Cesare Borgia acquires everything through his father's position, seizes control through ruthless speed, then collapses when fortune turns (his own illness plus Alexander's death); Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus all rise through thier own arms and persuasion, so no fortune is involved; and Agathocles climbs through crime and betrayal, which Machiavelli seems to treat as neither true virtù nor mere fortune but something like efficient wickedness. Oliverotto gets a brief mention too, but i'll leave him aside.

These cases look jointly consistent with at least two different principles. One reads Machiavelli as saying fortune only supplies the occasion, and virtù is what lets you seize and keep it — so Borgia possessed virtù but was destroyed by bad luck, whereas Sforza initially lacked it. Another reads him as saying genuine virtù requires founding by one's own arms and persuasion, with no dependence on inherited structures — which puts both Borgia and Sforza in a fortune-dependent bucket, leaving only the ancient founders as truly virtuous.

Here's the new case i want to test: imagine the director of a decentralised synthetic-biology cooperative who inherits leadership after the founder dies in a lab accident. She never uses physical violence; instead she relies on patent clustering and supply-contract renegotiation to neutralise rivals, and she weathers a sudden international regulatory ban because she had already distributed the cooperative's legal entities across three jurisdictions. She doesn't persuade an armed people in the classic sense, but she anticipates institutional shifts and embeds loyalists in bureaucratic nodes. Would Machiavelli classify this as virtù, as fortune, or as something closer to Agathocles-style criminal efficiency? Which of the two rules above drives your answer?

If you pick one rule, tell me what further hypothetical case would actually split them — perhaps a leader who inherits nothing but builds a loyal militia from scratch using only economic incentives? Please write in British English, say how confident you are in each part, and single out the point you're least sure about — probably the Agathocles comparison, since the line between criminal efficiency and virtù in ch. 8 is pretty murky.
