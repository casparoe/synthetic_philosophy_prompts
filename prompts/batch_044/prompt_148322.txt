Compare two ways people talk about 'the same mechanism' when they're trying to catch anomalies in neural nets. First there's the ML mechanistic interpretability view — you trace a circuit, check if the same nodes and edges do the work across inputs, and flag it if they don't. Then there's the more philosophical interventionist view where a mechanism is the same if it keeps the same causal relations under intervention, regardless of which physical parts realize it. Now here's the twist: imagine we have a model that solves arithmetic one way on small numbers (a clear addition circuit) and another way on large numbers (some weird heuristc using token embeddings), but both produce correct answers. The mechanistic people might call this an anomaly — mechanism switch. But under the interventionist view, if both pathways are causally stable for their respective domains, are they actually two mechanisms or one mechanism with context-sensitive parts? I want you to compare these two frameworks on that exact case, and also say which one handles 'feature superpostion' better — you know, when one neuron participates in multiple circuits. Don't just summarize both; tell me which framework you'd actually use to build an anomaly detector, and what's the biggest thing it misses.
