Since being appointed to a hospital ethics committee tasked with designing triage protocols, I have been revisiting Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the problem often called one thought too many. Two colleagues on the committee, Jordan and Riley, have been arguing about whether this objection applies to institutional decision-making. Jordan claims it is strictly relational: in intimate life, weighing a loved one's life against strangers is corrupt, but in a triage role, ranking patients by survival probability is precisely the duty, and refusing to do so because of a special bond to one assigned patient is the real integrity failure — you have inserted personal attachment where institutional reasoning should rule. Riley responds that this confuses the content of reasoning with its form. Williams's objection targets not calculation but substitutability: the corrupt thought is treating the particular person before you as replaceable by another with equivalent metrics. Even inside a role, if you justify withholding care from your assigned patient by live comparison with strangers, you have undermined the specific commitment that grounds your role; applying a pre-set protocol is different from engaging in comparative weighing that makes your charge fungible. Jordan replies that roles are constituted by exactly such fungibility rules — a triage officer who treats assigned patients as non-substitutable cannot function — so applying Williams here dissolves institutional ethics. Riley concedes roles require rules but maintains there is a distinction between executing a pre-committed allocation scheme and reasoning case-by-case through comparative metrics that treat your specific patient as one unit among others; only the latter is the problematic thought, and it can arise even within roles. They asked me to adjudicate since I must help write the protocol. Please go through their exchange step by step: rate each move, say who is right about what, identify what both miss regarding whether integrity can be role-relative, and state your overall verdict on whether Williams's objection survives in institutional triage contexts.
