At a philosophy-of-mind seminar, two participants—Maya and Jonas—fell into a prolonged disagreement about whether the familiar explanatory-gap framing of the hard problem survives once phenomenal concepts are taken seriously. I observed the exchange and could not adjudicate it; both seemed to land real blows. Here is the argument reconstructed in rounds.

Maya began by claiming the gap is real but mislocated. The problem is not between neural processes and subjective feel, but between descriptive theories and indexical phenomenal concepts. She introduced what she called “phenomenal indexing”: just as “now” or “here” do not describe properties but point to them, phenomenal concepts such as “this redness” carry no descriptive content. Consequently, asking “why is this neural state accompanied by this feel?” treats an indexical as if it were a description awaiting reduction—a category mistake analogous to asking “why is now 2026?” Thus, she argued, the hard problem dissolves: it is merely a special case of the general irreducibility of indexicals to descriptions.

Jonas replied that relocating the gap does not dissolve it. Even if phenomenal concepts are indexicals, we still require an account of why those particular indexicals are anchored to specific physical states in specific brains. To meet this, he sketched a “dual-anchoring” model: a phenomenal concept is anchored both (i) indexically to an internal state and (ii) normatively through a relation of “ownership”—the sense that the state is mine. Without the normative anchor, he contended, we cannot distinguish a normal sensation from a phantom-limb or alien-hand experience, since in both cases the internal indexical is present but only one feels genuinely owned.

Maya countered that Jonas was smuggling metaphysics into conceptual analysis. The feeling of ownership, she said, is itself just another phenomenal concept—“this-mineness”—so explaining it requires the same indexical move rather than a separate normative layer. She pressed a specific implication: if ownership were an independent normative anchor, then a subject who sincerely denies owning a limb should lack the corresponding phenomenal concept altogether. Yet in conditions involving disrupted body-ownership, patients can report a sensation with an explicit “foreign” or “not-mine” quality. That shows the raw phenomenal indexical remains present, while ownership is an added attribution, not part of the anchoring mechanism. Hence Jonas’s model predicts a disappearance of phenomenal content that does not occur.

Jonas responded that this observation actually supports his view. The “foreign” quality is precisely what arises when the normative anchor fails while the physical indexical remains intact. His model therefore predicts exactly the observed pattern: the internal pointer is there, but the ownership relation is broken, yielding an alien quality. By contrast, Maya’s pure-indexical view offers no resources to explain why some indexicals come tagged with ownership and others do not, since all it provides is undifferentiated pointing.

Maya then noted that if phenomenal concepts are pure indexicals, there should be no stable intersubjective comparison of them, yet we do correct one another about color experiences (“that is orange, not red”). Jonas answered that such comparison operates through shared physical anchors and behavioral norms, not through shared descriptive content, so her point was fully compatible with dual-anchoring rather than a problem for it.

They turned to me and asked for a referee’s decision. Do not merely say it is complicated; assign the points precisely.
