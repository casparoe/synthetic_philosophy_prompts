Here is the student's answer in full. The paradox of tragedy is that audiences seek out artworks—tragedies, films, novels—that reliably elicit sorrow, fear, or pity, even though in ordinary life we avoid these affective states. The deeper puzzle is not merely why we expose ourselves to them, but why they do not spoil our judgment that the work is good. This can be framed in terms of synchronous emotional stacking: at any given moment, we seem to hold both a negative response to the represented events and a positive evaluation of the artwork, without relying on diachronic emotional integration, in which the negative affect is gradually transformed into satisfaction only over the course of the narrative arc. One prominent resolution is the control thesis, which holds that aesthetic enjoyment of negative emotion depends on the audience's retained sense of agency, safety, or cognitive mastery. Because spectators know the events are fictional and understand that they can leave the theater or close the book, the negative affect is experienced within a protected frame, allowing it to be enjoyed rather than suffered. This thesis addresses synchronous stacking by explaining how negative and positive evaluations can coexist at the same temporal slice: the negative affect is directed at the fiction, while the positive evaluation derives from the spectator's awareness of their own protected position.

This should be distinguished from the view traditionally associated with Hume, according to which the formal excellence and eloquence of the artistic representation directly convert the unpleasantness of sorrow into a pleasant aesthetic response. For Hume, the transformation occurs in the first-order emotion itself: the sorrow is genuinely felt, but its quality is altered—almost alchemically—by the skill of the artist, so that the spectator experiences pleasure in the very intensity of the feeling. Hume therefore resolves the paradox through a change in the intrinsic character of the affect, not through any appeal to psychological distance or audience control. Nevertheless, the control thesis cannot survive counterexamples drawn from immersive and participatory tragic forms, such as ritual theater or virtual-reality narratives, in which the usual buffers of aesthetic distance are deliberately attenuated. In these contexts, audiences frequently report intense negative affect coexisting with high aesthetic satisfaction, yet they clearly lack the sense of mastery or safety that the thesis demands. This proves that the control thesis is false as a general explanation, and that we must reject it entirely. Instead, we should adopt a strict separation between first-order affective responses, which are always genuinely negative and directed at the fictional events, and second-order meta-representational appreciation, which is always positive and directed at the work's representational properties. On this framework, there is no real paradox of synchronous stacking, because the sadness we feel is never actually directed at the fiction; it is always already a representation of sadness that we contemplate with pleasure. Consequently, Hume's conversion view is best interpreted not as a transformation of first-order feeling, but as an early description of this second-order meta-representational process.
