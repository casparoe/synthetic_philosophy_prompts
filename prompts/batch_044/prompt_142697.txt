I am designing a 75-minute session for an upper-level seminar in applied ethics and philosophy of biology—about sixteen graduate or advanced undergraduate students—on the hedonistic imperative, understood here as the abolitionist proposal to use genetic, pharmacological, and neurotechnological means to eliminate suffering across sentient life rather than merely managing it. I want an in-class simulation that forces participants to feel, through their own decision dynamics, why the logical endpoint of that proposal is far more radical than it sounds in abstract discussion, and specifically why it conflicts with the very mechanisms of collective choice they are using to evaluate it. The group should come away seeing that abolishing suffering is not analogous to curing a disease but to redesigning the motivational architecture that makes deliberation, priority-ranking, and cooperative problem-solving possible.

Please design a structured role-play or simulation—call it the Calibration Committee—for four subgroups of four students each, with a shared 30-minute deliberation phase followed by a 20-minute forced-consensus vote and a 25-minute debrief. Give each subgroup a hidden design mandate drawn randomly from four cards: (1) preserve nociception and negative affect as informational signals necessary for survival and learning; (2) flatten all hedonic variance to a uniform positive baseline; (3) replace natural suffering with synthetic, controllable alert states that carry information without aversive phenomenology; (4) maintain current affective architecture but redistribute suffering accordingically. Give each individual student within the subgroup a concealed stake card—for instance, a parent of a child with congenital insensitivity to pain, a utilitarian committed to zero suffering regardless of coordination costs, a clinician who relies on patient distress signals for triage, or an engineer who believes reward-prediction error requires negative prediction errors to calibrate. The rules should require unanimous approval of a Charter of Affective States using only verbal negotiation and a point-allocation budget representing limited biotechnological capacity; critically, build into the mechanics that if a subgroup votes to eliminate all negative valence (option 2), they must resolve a subsequent resource-allocation conflict—presented as a concrete scenario about distributing scarce medical substrates—without using urgency, discomfort, or differential dissatisfaction as ranking signals. They will discover that their own deliberation stalls precisely where they have engineered away the gradient that was driving it.

In the debrief, map specific moments onto conceptual claims: the hidden stake cards correspond to the fact that real-world abolitionism must accommodate heterogeneous risk profiles and moral commitments; the deadlock under zero-negative-valence corresponds to the contested claim that suffering is not merely an evolutionary legacy but a functional prerequisite for cooperative decision-making; the resistance to signing a pure-abolition charter corresponds to the intuitive attachment many have to suffering as carrying meaning or moral seriousness. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong—the first run will likely produce either premature compromise that avoids the radical endpoint, or blame placed on the rules rather than recognition of structural dependency—and advise how the facilitator should intervene without naming the concept before the deadlock occurs. Also state clearly which feature of the hedonistic imperative the activity cannot reproduce: it models functional coordination failure, not the subjective phenomenology of a life genuinely devoid of suffering, and it cannot settle whether synthetic alert states preserve enough motivational structure to replace natural suffering, since that remains genuinely contested among researchers working on affective neuroscience and value theory. Finally, separate what is settled—such as the evolutionary origins of nociception and the technical feasibility of some genetic modulation of affect—from what is genuinely contested—such as whether suffering is indispensable for moral agency, whether uniform positive affect undermines long-term cooperation, and whether the abolitionist project requires not just biotechnology but a reconceptualization of personhood—without presenting any contested view as the consensus view among philosophers and scientists who study these questions.
