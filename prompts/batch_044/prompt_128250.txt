I am preparing materials for an advanced seminar on the normative structure of consent, with particular attention to deception, coercion, and competence in clinical and institutional settings. During our discussions, participants repeatedly invoke the maxim 'silence is not consent' to settle disputes about whether a patient, research subject, or party to an agreement who remained passive during an explanation or procedure had thereby authorised what followed. The phrase is being made to do very different kinds of work: some use it to block inferences from mere non-objection; others to deny that prior explicit agreement can survive changed circumstances or withdrawal; and still others to claim that any absence of vocal affirmation automatically invalidates authority, even when a normative power to license has already been exercised. I want to bring analytical clarity to the next session rather than letting the slogan substitute for argument.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses that 'silence is not consent' might actually be expressing? For each reading, please indicate whether it concerns an epistemic failure (the silent party lacked adequate understanding), a volitional failure (their will was not free), a performative failure (no valid speech act of authorisation occurred), or a normative-power failure (the authority to license was not properly transferred or has been revoked). Then say, for each thesis, whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, or plainly false; and identify which of these readings actually supports the three specific moves being made in my seminar—namely, that non-objection never counts as authorisation, that prior explicit consent is irrevocable regardless of later silence, and that institutional or medical authority can never be presumed in the absence of vocal affirmation. Please present your answer in British English, without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid attributing the phrase to any named author unless the attribution is verifiably certain.
