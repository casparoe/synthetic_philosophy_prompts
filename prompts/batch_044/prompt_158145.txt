I'm working through some implications of Douglas Hofstadter's discussion of tangled hierarchies and the concept of an inviolate level—the idea that in a properly stratified system there is a ground floor that higher levels cannot alter, as opposed to the strange loop in Escher's Drawing Hands, where each level seems to generate the other with no untouched base. I want to apply this structural distinction to normative reasoning, but I have constructed an argument that ends up undermining moral judgment altogether, and I need help locating the weak step.

Here is the argument. (1) Any rational hierarchy requires an inviolate level: if every level can be revised by the one above it, there is no foundation and reasoning collapses into circularity, like Escher's hands with no artist. (2) Moral reasoning is hierarchical: we have first-order judgments, second-order principles that justify them, and possibly third-order frameworks that adjudicate conflicts among principles. (3) Therefore moral reasoning requires an inviolate level—some basic normative commitments that are not themselves subject to moral revision. (4) But if a level is genuinely inviolate within morality, then no moral argument can ever overturn it; historical moral change, such as the abolition of chattel slavery, must then be either non-rational or confined to non-foundational levels only. (5) Yet we take foundational moral change to be both possible and rational—otherwise moral progress is an illusion and all disagreement is merely surface-level adjustment. (6) So moral reasoning either lacks an inviolate level, making it a tangled hierarchy with no ground, or it possesses one and moral progress is impossible. (7) A tangled hierarchy in morality means every normative claim ultimately refers back to itself: any attempt to justify a principle invokes a meta-principle that is itself justified by the first-order framework it governs, producing a self-referential loop with no external anchor. (8) But reasoning without an external anchor is arbitrary and cannot yield genuine obligations. (9) Therefore, if moral reasoning exists, it must be arbitrary; but it is not arbitrary, so moral reasoning does not exist in any robust form.

If I have to choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and (5), since the notion that reasoning needs a foundation and that moral progress is real both seem indispensable. Where does the chain break—does the analogy from formal or perceptual hierarchies to normative ones fail, is there an equivocation between being inviolate and being immune to any form of revision, or does the strange loop in morality turn out to be benign rather than vicious? In your response, please separate what is settled—such as the standard interpretation of Hofstadter's distinction between tangled and untangled hierarchies—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on meta-ethics and normative structure, and do not present a contested interpretation of these hierarchies or of the possibility of moral progress as if it were the consensus view.
