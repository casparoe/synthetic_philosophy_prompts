You are an expert philosopher of mind with a background in philosophy of sleep and consciousness, and you are also an experienced workshop facilitator for secondary-school students. Please think carefully, take a step back, and begin by briefly brainstorming the conceptual core of the debate: one side holds that dreams cannot be genuine experiences because they fail the criteria we normally use to identify experiences—such as concurrent accessibility, verifiability, or integration with waking judgment—while the opposing side holds that what we call dreaming is not a real-time stream of consciousness at all, but rather a narrative reconstructed at the moment of awakening, with no corresponding phenomenal content occurring during sleep itself. Once you have mapped that tension, design a 75-minute classroom activity for about 12 students aged 16–17 in my high-school philosophy club. The goal is not to lecture them on the debate, but to make them feel its force through structured participation before either view is named.

Design a simulation with clear roles, rules, and a small-stakes payoff scheme that requires no real money. Divide the group into two halves. One half serves as Sleep Observers; the other as Waking Reporters. The activity should revolve around a hypothetical protocol you will call the Phenomenal Blackout Zone: a defined interval during which, by rule, no first-person mental data can be retrieved except through post-waking reconstruction. The Sleep Observers receive only pre-set physiological markers and scripted post-waking reports describing vivid scenes; they must vote, using only those resources, on whether a specific mental event genuinely occurred during the blackout or was retroactively inserted upon awakening. The Waking Reporters undergo a simulated awakening protocol—reading a highly detailed script of a scene that feels immediate and sensory-rich—and must defend, under cross-examination, whether they experienced that scene during the blackout or fabricated it in the act of waking.

Arrange the rules so that students first build what looks like an unshakeable case for real-time dream experience—using the vividness and coherence of the reports—then discover that every piece of evidence they relied on was available only after the blackout ended. Only after they have reached that impasse should you name the positions and map their experience onto them: the Observers’ difficulty in justifying an occurrence without concurrent access mirrors the criteria-based objection, while the Reporters’ inability to distinguish experienced content from reconstructed narrative mirrors the constructionist challenge. Include a precise timeline, role instructions, the payoff scheme (for example, collective points that unlock a shared reward only if both sides agree on a verdict, which should prove impossible), and rules for cross-examination.

In your debrief, map each stage of the activity onto the relevant conceptual moves—explain which classroom moment corresponds to the argument from missing criteria and which corresponds to the narrative-reconstruction thesis. Be frank about which feature of the debate—perhaps the complete absence of concurrent introspective access or the specific problem of false-memory injection—the classroom simulation cannot reproduce, and tell me exactly how you as facilitator must supply that gap verbally. Also predict what will go wrong the first time you run this: for instance, students may conflate feeling that a report is vivid with judging that it records a real experience, or they may treat the blackout rule as a mere practical limitation rather than a conceptual one. Give concrete handling strategies for each failure mode.
