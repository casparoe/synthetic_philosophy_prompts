I have been reflecting on the problem of binding future decision-makers, both in personal life and in collective governance, and I find myself drawn to two related concepts: Ulysses contracts, in which an individual restricts his own future options to guard against anticipated weakness of will, and constitutional precommitment, in which a polity entrenches certain rules to insulate them from transient majorities. Though both are often discussed in isolation—one in behavioral economics and moral psychology, the other in constitutional theory—I wonder whether comparing them reveals deeper implications for how we understand autonomy, legitimacy, and the temporal structure of authority. I do not have formal training in philosophy or law, so I am reasoning from first principles, but I would like an analysis that treats the comparison seriously rather than reducing it to a superficial analogy.

Specifically, please describe the most important implications of juxtaposing Ulysses contracts with constitutional precommitment. I am particularly interested in whether the legitimacy of such binding requires what I would call intertemporal consent—that is, some form of authorization running from the affected future party back to the present decision-maker—or whether legitimacy can instead be grounded purely in present rationality or collective self-interest. Additionally, I would like you to consider how these implications change when the scale shifts from a single person planning for his future self, to a small group such as a cooperative board or a family trust, and finally to an entire political community. Does the need for intertemporal consent grow stronger or weaker as the number of affected parties increases, and does the analogy between tying oneself to a mast and entrenching a constitutional provision break down at any particular scale?
