I work as a senior mediator at a community justice center that has adopted an Ubuntu-informed framework for resolving interpersonal and collective disputes, but we operate under a statutory charter that also protects individual rights to autonomy, privacy, and non-participation. Our caseworkers need a reusable decision procedure—not a philosophical essay—to determine, for any given dispute, whether the harm should be addressed primarily through relational restoration (repairing the interdependent fabric that constitutes the parties’ personhood) or through individual rights-based redress (compensation, exclusion, or legal adjudication). The principle to operationalize is that personhood is relationally constituted: an injury is not merely a private loss but a rupture in the web of recognition and obligation that makes each party who they are. Yet the procedure must preserve the distinction between harm done to a relation and harm done through a relation, must recognize when an offender denies the relational frame entirely, and must never override a party’s non-derogable boundary merely to restore communal harmony.

Please translate this into a step-by-step procedure that a caseworker with no philosophical training can run in about forty-five minutes, including: the ordered questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the explicit points where judgment is unavoidable and must be signed off by a senior mediator rather than automated; and the conditions under which the procedure stops with a definitive recommendation, stops with a referral to legal arbitration, or stops with a value judgment that requires human sign-off. The procedure should handle cases where the victim demands exclusion despite viable repair, where the offender refuses to acknowledge relational obligation, and where the relational harm extends to third-party community members who are not direct parties. Apply it to three worked cases: (1) a workplace racial-slur incident in which the offender accepts relational responsibility and seeks to repair, but the victim demands termination and refuses any mediated encounter; (2) a communal water-access dispute in which one party treats the conflict as purely transactional and denies that relationships are relevant; and (3) a family inheritance conflict involving concealed assets, where relational repair requires disclosure that one party considers a protected privacy boundary. In at least one of these, the procedure should yield a recommendation that runs counter to the center’s relational ethos—please flag exactly where and why. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which categories of dispute lie outside its scope, which moral disagreements it cannot resolve, and where it is likely to misfire if applied mechanically.
