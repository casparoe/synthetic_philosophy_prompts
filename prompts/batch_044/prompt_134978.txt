As a doctoral student in modern intellectual history, I am attempting to apply Hobsbawm's theory of invented traditions alongside Anderson's notion of imagined communities to the ideological consolidation of the Japanese kokutai during the Meiji era. Hobsbawm's framework emphasizes the conscious, elite-driven fabrication of symbolic continuity with an ostensibly ancient past, whereas Anderson characterizes the nation as an imagined community defined by horizontal comradeship among anonymous co-nationals—mediated through shared cultural technologies such as print capitalism and experienced as a form of simultaneous anonymity among strangers. My difficulty arises with the Meiji case, where national identity was explicitly anchored in the visible, embodied sovereignty of the Emperor rather than in an abstract, anonymous collective. Because the Emperor was a non-anonymous focal point present in ritual and iconography, it seems that Meiji subjects could not have experienced the specific form of anonymous solidarity Anderson treats as constitutive of modern nationhood. This leads me to wonder whether my reasoning contains a false opposition: if the kokutai is best understood as an invented tradition in Hobsbawm's sense, does its reliance on a visible, personalized sovereign necessarily exclude it from being an imagined community in Anderson's sense? Or have I mischaracterized the relationship between symbolic visibility and horizontal comradeship, perhaps by assuming that mediated orientation toward a central figure cannot enable the imagination of anonymous fellow citizens in the way that print-capitalism does in Anderson's European examples? I am particularly unsure whether extending Anderson's mechanism of mediation to an imperial, non-print-centered institution stretches his framework beyond its intended conditions.
