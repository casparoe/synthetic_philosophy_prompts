I'm taking a graduate seminar on Kantian ethics and keep returning to the same stalemate in the literature on the Formula of Universal Law. Defenders maintain that the contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-will tests genuinely filter out maxims like false promising or total neglect of one's talents, because universalizing them undermines the social practices or rational capacities those maxims presuppose. Critics, however, routinely construct maxim-variants—say, "cultivate my talents, but only on Tuesdays and never on weekends," or "refuse aid to anyone who lives more than ten miles away"—that appear to survive universalization without generating either logical or volitional contradiction, yet strike us as morally arbitrary or deficient. The defenders reply that such maxims fail once fully specified with the agent's genuine end and the relevant circumstances, or that they violate the Formula of Humanity regardless; the critics reply that this saves the theory only through ad hoc gerrymandering of what counts as the "real" maxim.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not mainly about moral intuition but about the ontology of a maxim itself: one camp treats it as a subjective principle that includes the agent's particular intention and situational framing, while the other treats it as an objective action-type that should be assessed independently of idiosyncratic qualification. If so, the sides may be running different universalizability tests under one name—one asking whether a subjective principle can be consistently willed by all rational agents, the other asking whether an objective rule could stabilize a system of mutual expectation. But I am unsure whether this is genuinely a verbal disagreement about "maxim," a hidden formal dispute about the logic of modal generalization under partial compliance, or simply a case where competing moral starting points are being projected onto a framework that was never designed to arbitrate them.

Diagnose the structure of this impasse using what each side actually says about false promises, qualified talent-cultivation, and geographically restricted beneficence. Is there a substantive disagreement about the conditions for contradiction in willing, or are the sides answering different questions—one about subjective consistency, one about institutional viability—while calling both "universalizability"? And tell me honestly what would count as progress: a sharper taxonomy of maxim-types, a formal result about universalization when agents comply only selectively, a reconceptualization of "contradictory will" that removes the ambiguity, or is the debate essentially irreducible to competing intuitions that no refinement of the test can resolve? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of interpretations, together with an argument for whether anything could actually break the deadlock or whether we should expect permanent stall.
