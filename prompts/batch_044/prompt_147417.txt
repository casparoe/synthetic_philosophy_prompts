As I am drafting a novel whose protagonist practices a revived Stoicism, I have composed the following philosophical profile for a reading group and wish to know whether it holds together. Here is my position:

(1) I take the dichotomy of control to be a boundary of moral answerability, not of causal power: I am responsible solely for my prohairesis—my judgments and intentions—never for outcomes.

(2) I distinguish "thin Stoicism," the therapeutic strand of cognitive reframing and emotional regulation now common in psychology, from "thick Stoicism," which binds that discipline to cosmological sympatheia, cosmopolitan duty, and the active political virtue exemplified by Marcus Aurelius.

(3) Thin Stoicism is defensible in isolation, but it becomes a misrepresentation when presented as the complete Stoic life.

(4) Stoic indifference (adiaphora) applies to all externals, including the welfare of distant strangers; justice may still prompt me to act, yet that action must be performed without emotional attachment to its success or failure.

(5) I reject "hustle Stoicism"—the fusion of Stoic techniques with entrepreneurial productivity—as a corruption that mistakes discipline for virtue.

(6) Nevertheless, I think Stoic practices may be adopted instrumentally to foster professional resilience, provided one does not claim that this adoption constitutes an exercise in virtue.

(7) I hold that profound emotional detachment from outcomes is fully compatible with sustained, even radical, political commitment, so long as the commitment flows from cosmopolitan duty rather than from outrage or hope.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only seem to conflict? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each specify what must be revised and what the least costly fix would be. I do not indicate which commitment I am prepared to abandon.
