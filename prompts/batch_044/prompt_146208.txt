Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a philosophy-of-education seminar of twelve graduate students that makes them feel the structural risk in Alfred North Whitehead’s rhythm of education—romance, precision, and generalization—without naming the stages until the debrief. The central object should be an unfamiliar constructed symbol system (e.g., a short invented grammar with ten rules and an ambiguous sample text) that the group first encounters collectively with open, speculative wonder; then analyzes under severe formal constraints that forbid any aesthetic, narrative, or practical language; and finally applies to a domain remote from its origin (e.g., interpreting a social ritual or a policy debate). Arrange roles so that half the group acts as “romancers” in phase one, then as “precisists” in phase two who must suppress all prior language, and finally as “generalizers” in phase three who must recover meaning without abandoning the formal apparatus. The rules and payoff should be arranged so that participants experience the second stage not as satisfying mastery but as a kind of disciplined loss: the symbol system should feel alive in phase one, dead in phase two, and only partially resurrected in phase three. Include a precise timeline (e.g., 20 / 35 / 20 minutes for the phases, with 15 for debrief), the minimal materials needed, the explicit speech constraints for phase two, and a debrief that maps each felt shift onto Whitehead’s stages, addresses why students may conclude education should skip the middle stage, and connects the deadness felt in phase two to Whitehead’s warning about inert ideas—knowledge received without utilization or fresh combination. Be explicit about which feature of Whitehead’s view the activity cannot reproduce—likely his claim that generalization is not a return to romance but a disciplined transformation of precise knowledge into a new order of application—and state exactly how the facilitator must supply that in discussion. Finally, include an honest prediction of what will fail the first time: some students will resist the artificial suppression of language in phase two, others will find the phase-three application forced rather than illuminating, and provide concrete instructions for handling both failures without breaking the frame or revealing the names prematurely.
