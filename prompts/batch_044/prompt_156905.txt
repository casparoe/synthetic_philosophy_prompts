Write an exam question for an upper-level philosophy of mind and moral psychology seminar, suitable for a 45-minute in-class essay (approximately 500–600 words expected). The topic is the Knobe effect—the well-known asymmetry in folk attributions of intentional action between harmful and beneficial foreseen side-effects. Do not ask for a summary; instead, embed the effect in a conceptual test that separates students who understand the theoretical stakes from those who have only memorized the label.

Construct the question as follows. First, present two parallel vignettes in full: (i) the corporate chairman who approves a profitable program knowing it will harm the environment and not caring about that consequence, and (ii) the parallel chairman who approves a profitable program knowing it will help the environment and not caring about that consequence, with respondents in both cases judging whether the side-effect was brought about intentionally. Second, present a modified institutional case: a corporation deploys a predictive optimization algorithm that, through purely statistical weight updates, reliably produces a harmful (and separately, helpful) environmental side-effect; the corporation’s board forecasts this outcome but builds no representational state corresponding to it, and the algorithm itself lacks beliefs or desires. Ask students to evaluate whether the Knobe asymmetry—stronger attributions of intentionality in the harm case—would persist, diminish, or disappear in this algorithmic-institutional setting, and what that result implies for competing explanations of the effect: specifically, the normative-account view (that attributions of intentionality are partly governed by evaluative considerations such as blameworthiness) versus the mental-state-account view (that they track the causal role of beliefs and desires).

Then provide: (1) the complete exam question, clearly worded and self-contained; (2) a model answer of about 550 words that takes a reasoned position—e.g., arguing that the asymmetry likely weakens but does not vanish in the institutional case, and that this pattern supports a hybrid view—without merely describing the vignettes; (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit conceptual moves, with marks assigned so the total is 25, plus a one-line note for each on the typical failure mode (for example, conflating the normative account with simple moral blame, or treating the algorithm case as obviously irrelevant because algorithms “have no minds” without addressing why that might undermine the mental-state explanation specifically). The scheme should make explicit what distinguishes an A answer (all five moves present with precise conceptual distinctions) from a B answer (solid grasp of the vignettes and one explanation, but missing the institutional-application argument or the comparative evaluation of the two theoretical accounts). Keep the prompt formal and polished, with no request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid attributing the vignettes to a specific publication date or page number—present them as standard philosophical thought-experiments.
