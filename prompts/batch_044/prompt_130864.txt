Exam question: Peter van Inwagen proposes that simple objects compose a larger object only when their activities jointly constitute a single life. A common objection holds that this criterion is implausibly restrictive: a table clearly exists and is made of legs and a top, yet none of those parts are engaged in biological life, so van Inwagen must either abandon the life-criterion or deny that tables exist. Evaluate this objection. Specifically: (a) Does the life-criterion actually commit van Inwagen to denying tables exist, and is that denial as damaging as the objector assumes? (b) Does the objection confuse the conditions for parts composing a whole at a time with the conditions for a whole persisting through gradual part-replacement? (c) Some philosophers have suggested extending the criterion to artificial systems—treating functional homeostasis and negative-feedback loops in self-regulating machines as structural analogues to biological metabolism. Would this extension rescue artifacts, or would it dissolve the very specificity that makes the biological criterion non-arbitrary?

Student answer: The student notes that van Inwagen is indeed committed to the non-existence of tables as composite objects; only simples arranged table-wise exist. The objector therefore misses the target by treating this denial as a reductio, when for van Inwagen it is a feature. The deeper problem, the student argues, is that van Inwagen conflates composition with persistence: he assumes that because parts of an organism stay together through life-processes, all composition must work that way. But tables persist through glue and physical contact, which shows that structural integration—not biological unity—grounds both the formation and the endurance of composite objects. The student then considers the artificial-system extension: if a climate-controlled cabinet or smart structure exhibits feedback loops analogous to metabolism, then a table with embedded sensors could satisfy the life-criterion. This shows that van Inwagen's view is not essentially biological but applies to any stable system with internal regulation. Hence either the criterion is broad enough to cover nearly every durable artifact, defeating its restrictive purpose, or it arbitrarily excludes tables despite their functional similarity to living systems.
