Consider a grounding configuration that arises in multi-system architectures but also has analogues in human epistemic dependency. System A learns to deploy a concept—say, "tensile strength"—without ever correlating the term to direct sensorimotor measurements of material stress, and without receiving an explicit definitional decomposition within its own symbolic network. Instead, A acquires the term exclusively by predicting and reproducing the annotated outputs of System B, which was originally calibrated against physical testing apparatus. Crucially, A has no access to B's weights, training trajectories, or raw sensory inputs; it treats B's symbolic emissions as authoritative annotations and adjusts its representations solely to match them. The result is that A's use of the term tracks B's usage with high fidelity, yet every causal or informational pathway from A's token to the physical property runs through B's already-symbolic outputs.

This arrangement seems distinct from direct causal grounding, and also from standard derivative intentionality, in which one system's intentionality is usually inherited from a designer's mental states or from shared conceptual architecture rather than from a unidirectional dependency on another system's emitted symbols alone. Is there an established term in the philosophy of mind or symbol-grounding literature for this specific mediated pathway—grounding by one-way symbolic dependency on an already-grounded mediator, with no shared architecture or direct world-access? If the literature does not sharply distinguish this case, how should it be classified relative to direct grounding and to derivative intentionality, and what are the precise boundary conditions—such as unidirectionality, artificiality of the mediator, or the empirical nature of the term—that would mark it as its own phenomenon rather than a mere variant?
