In my advanced philosophy of mind seminar, we have reached an impasse that has persisted across three sessions. The dispute concerns whether contemporary statistical machine translation—systems that operate through large-scale pattern correlation without explicit semantic representations—exhibits anything worthy of being called linguistic understanding. One camp, influenced by Douglas Hofstadter's longstanding critique that such systems are profoundly shallow, insists that fluent output without intentional anchoring or phenomenological grasp is mere syntactic mirroring. The opposing camp maintains that functional equivalence in cross-linguistic mapping, combined with the absence of any empirically detectable deficit in communicative success, renders the demand for inner understanding an unnecessary metaphysical addition. Despite exhaustive discussion of the same aberrant translations—where statistical coherence produces semantically absurd results—neither side has conceded ground, and I suspect we are no longer debating empirical capacities but rather the criteria for explanatory adequacy itself. To test this suspicion, I have introduced a distinction drawn from our discussions, which I term epistemic transparency versus hermeneutic closure: a system may be fully transparent in its operational architecture, with every parameter inspectable, yet hermeneutically closed in that none of its internal states function as interpretations of its outputs. I present this not as a resolution but as a potential diagnostic tool. I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations or a verdict in favor of one side. Is this stalemate verbal—stemming from competing uses of understanding—or substantive, involving a hidden disagreement about whether meaning requires intentionality, about which cases count as data for cognitive assessment, or about whether the question presupposes an ill-posed natural-kind boundary? Furthermore, given your diagnosis, what would genuinely constitute progress? Could a formal result in distributional semantics, a refined conceptual distinction, or a deliberate change of question break the deadlock, or should we conclude that the disagreement is rooted in bedrock intuitions about consciousness for which no further argument is available?
