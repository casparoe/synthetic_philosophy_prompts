I was arguing with two friends last night about illusionism regarding phenomenal consciousness, and neither would back down, so I need an outside referee. Maya said phenomenal consciousness is basically a kind of representational fiction the brain spins up — there’s no actual “what-it’s-likeness,” just neural models that misrepresent themselves as having qualitative feel, kind of like a software interface showing a folder icon when there’s no actual folder inside the machine. Jake said that’s incoherent: an illusion needs something real that gets misrepresented (a bent stick in water), so if phenomenal consciousness is an illusion there still has to be some underlying property being distorted, which means you haven’t eliminated qualia at all, you’ve just redescribed them. Maya came back that it’s more like a fictional character — Sherlock Holmes isn’t a misrepresented real person, he’s just constructed — so the brain constructs a “subject of experience” narrative without any corresponding phenomenal property. Jake then said that just explains why people talk about consciousness, it doesn’t show consciousness itself is unreal, and if there’s no property being misrepresented you should call it a false belief, not an illusion, which makes illusionism a mislabeled eliminativism. Then Maya threw in this reflexive twist she read somewhere: apply the illusionist machinery to itself. The illusionist’s own conviction that there’s no phenomenal consciousness is just another representational state generated by the same misrepresenting system, so she can’t claim any special first-person authority for her thesis — her “seeing through” the illusion is itself phenomenally infected. Jake said that actually proves his point: if the illusionist can’t have insider access to the truth of her view, then the only ground for accepting it is third-person neuroscience, which means first-person reports have to be treated as real data to be explained rather than eliminated, so the reflexive move either collapses illusionism into self-contradiction or forces it to abandon its anti-phenomenal conclusion. They kept going in circles. Can you go through their exchange move by move — the initial fiction/illusion distinction, the false-belief objection, and especially that reflexive loop — and say who’s right at each step? Don’t just list options; take a definite position on whether illusionism survives the reflexive challenge and where both of them are missing something, even if it’s just a hidden premise about introspective access.
