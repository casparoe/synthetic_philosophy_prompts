Please argue that when dramatizing real historical figures in fiction and biopics, creators owe the dead not biographical accuracy but what might be called structural fidelity: they are free to invent dialogue, collapse timelines, and imagine private moments, yet they violate a meaningful ethical duty if they reverse the moral-causal structure of the figure's actual choices—specifically, if they portray a decision as flowing from motives that the historical record shows the person explicitly rejected or that are logically incompatible with the reasons they endorsed, even in private. To ground your argument, consider a concrete hypothetical: a filmmaker dramatizes the life of a documented nineteenth-century reformer whose private correspondence reveals that her public advocacy was driven by an unstable mixture of genuine conviction and calculated social ambition; the screenplay, seeking a cleaner narrative arc, portrays every major decision as arising solely from disinterested moral principle. Defend the claim that this particular distortion—stripping away the self-interested causal component rather than merely adding fictional scenes—constitutes a distinct wrong to the dead, and explain why structural fidelity should constrain artistic license even when audiences would prefer a simpler moral portrait. Do not offer bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; construct the position through conceptual analysis and the hypothetical case alone.
