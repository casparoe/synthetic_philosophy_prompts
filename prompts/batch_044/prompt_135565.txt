A philosophy essay summarizes Benjamin Franklin's moral perfection project in his Autobiography as follows: Franklin arranged his thirteen virtues in a strict hierarchical sequence, holding that one must fully master temperance before attempting silence, and so on, because each virtue functionally depends on its predecessor being secured first. He kept a daily ledger not merely to diminish faults but to engineer the complete elimination of every vice through pure rational habit-formation, treating moral development as an individual mechanical process that requires neither emotional education, social institutions, nor religious grace. Although he admitted he never reached perfection, he maintained that the goal was in principle attainable for anyone with sufficient discipline, and that his own failure was purely a deficit of will rather than evidence of an impossible ideal. The essay notes that Franklin later added humility—modeled explicitly on Jesus and Socrates—to his list, and reads this addition as an implicit admission that rational self-culture must be supplemented by religious imitation; it concludes that Franklin is best understood as an early behaviorist whose project demonstrates that secular rationalist perfectionism inevitably collapses into either self-deception or borrowed theology.

This summary strikes me as partly right but possibly unfair in its details, particularly regarding whether the virtues form a strict dependency chain, whether Franklin aimed at total elimination rather than progressive moderation, and whether the humility addition represents a theoretical retreat from rationalism rather than a practical tactic within it.
