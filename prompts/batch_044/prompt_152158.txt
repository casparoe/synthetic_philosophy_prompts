Consider the following sketch for an essay on legal self-reference and self-amending rules, which I am treating as a variant of the classic Nomic-style problem but with an added twist I call the Iterative Constitution.

The hypothetical system contains three provisions. Article A grants a legislature power to amend any constitutional provision by a two-thirds vote. Article B states that Article A may never be amended except by Article A itself acting through that same two-thirds procedure. Article C is dormant: it activates only if Article A is ever suspended or rendered inoperative, and once active it permanently disables Article A and transfers its powers to a separate body.

The difficulty arises if the legislature attempts to suspend Article A. If the suspension succeeds, Article C activates and thereby validates the suspension; but Article C activates only if Article A was suspended, which seems to require that Article A was operative at the moment of suspension—yet Article B appears to block any external suspension. Conversely, if the suspension fails because Article B protects Article A, then Article C never activates, leaving Article A intact but also leaving the suspension attempt without legal effect. The system thus appears to have two mutually exclusive states, each of which formally implies the negation of the other, yet neither outcome seems derivable from within the rules themselves.

I want to argue that this is not precisely the Ross paradox of omnipotence, but rather a distinct form of institutional deadlock I call stalled recursion: the rules supply complete conditions for both outcomes, yet the condition for triggering one outcome is precisely the absence of the conditions required for the other, and vice versa, leaving the system in an unresolved equilibrium rather than in a direct contradiction. I also want to press an analogy to the frame problem in artificial intelligence—just as an intelligent agent must encode what does not change when an action occurs, a legal system relies on implicit negative-persistence rules that specify what lies outside the scope of amendment. Self-reference destroys that negative knowledge, because the system can no longer distinguish cleanly between the object-level rule and the meta-level exception that targets it.

Please comment on this draft. Specifically: (1) Is the distinction between stalled recursion and a standard self-reference paradox genuinely sharp, or am I merely relabeling an old contradiction without gaining explanatory power? (2) Does the frame-problem analogy illuminate the institutional failure, or does it illegitimately import epistemic concerns about representation into a domain that is purely about normative authority? (3) Is there an internal inconsistency in the hypothetical provisions I have sketched—for instance, does Article B’s claim that Article A can amend only itself already presuppose that Article A is in force in a way that makes Article C’s activation conditions impossible by definition? Be direct about errors, vagueness, or points where the reasoning collapses; I am not looking for a bibliography, but if my characterization misrepresents the standard logical structure of self-amending rules, correct me conceptually.
