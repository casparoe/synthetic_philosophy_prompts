I've been developing a feature on how moral philosophers are attempting to formalize the boundary between authentic preference and hidden bias, and in interviews with researchers I've noticed a recurring stalemate that the published literature never quite resolves into a precise disagreement. I'm hoping you can help me draft a conceptual document for my reporting notes.

Specifically, imagine an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued past each other for years. On one side are the Correctionists, who maintain that any moral preference correlating strongly with a person's social position, upbringing, or group membership should be treated as a presumptive bias rather than a genuine reason, and that sound moral reasoning requires a procedural filter to strip these correlations away before evaluation. On the other side are the Situatedists, who hold that correlation with social position is insufficient for bias-status, that many apparently biased preferences encode situated knowledge or legitimate experiential authority, and that applying a uniform correction procedure risks flattening morally relevant differences into a false neutrality that masks its own partisan origins.

The collaboration should produce a joint statement in the following format: first, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign—about what makes a preference stable across counterfactual reasoning, about the limits of introspective access to one's own moral motivations, and about the difference between revising a belief and abandoning a value—phrased precisely enough that neither camp feels misrepresented. Second, a clearly marked disagreements table stating the exact points of divergence in wording each side accepts for its own view, not merely "bias is pervasive" versus "bias is overstated," but the precise conditions under which each camp would concede that a particular preference has been misclassified. Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—and I suspect two are central: whether there exists a non-circular criterion for identifying biases that does not itself depend on contested moral preferences, and whether the phenomenon of "moral terroir" (the idea that certain ethical sensitivities develop analogously to cultivated taste, carrying information about local ecological and social conditions that universal principles systematically miss) can be empirically or phenomenologically distinguished from mere parochialism. For each crux, include a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it—perhaps involving cross-cultural judgments about obligations to distant strangers versus kin, or about the moral weight of ecological interdependence in resource-scarce regions—and record in advance what each camp would predict about the case should the crux be resolved in their favor.

Please draft this as a formal, polished document that both parties could plausibly endorse, separating genuine disagreements from ones that dissolve upon closer inspection into verbal confusion. I should emphasize that I'm treating this purely as an exercise in conceptual clarification for journalistic purposes; I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific authors, and the document should stand on its own reasoning without dependence on named texts or historical attributions.
