I've been trying to make sense of the saying that we live in "the best of all possible worlds," a phrase I first encountered in references to Voltaire's satire and have since heard used to defend everything from theological optimism to the idea that any social reform is futile, because suffering must somehow be part of an optimal design. I don't have any formal philosophy training, but I've been thinking about it independently, especially after conversations with friends who work in systems optimization and began framing the claim as a problem of parameter tuning: if you adjust one variable—say, the amount of pain or the distribution of resources—the entire system collapses into something worse. I'm struggling to see what precise claim is actually being made, because the slogan seems to slide between very different ideas, and I want to understand which ones are trivial, which are substantive, and which are just false.

Could you separate out the distinct precise readings this slogan could express? I'm particularly interested in whether it's a metaphysical claim about God or nature surveying every possible world and selecting this one; whether it's a comparative claim about some single metric like happiness, complexity, or coherence being maximized here; or whether it's actually a tautology that defines "best" as whatever actually exists. I also wonder if there's a defensible interpretation that doesn't require comparing worlds that don't exist—perhaps treating "possible worlds" not as real metaphysical entities but as hypothetical parameter configurations in a design space—and whether that turns the slogan into a claim about local optima rather than global ones. Which of these readings actually support the use people make of it when they invoke it to argue against change, and which are misrepresentations? Please lay them out as a menu rather than picking one, and say for each whether it is defensible, false, or merely a platitude that does no real work.
