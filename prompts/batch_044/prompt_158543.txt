I am preparing material for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and should like to calibrate my reasoning before presenting. I want your credence — a specific number between 0 and 1 — that the paradox of phenomenal judgement can be dissolved within a standard epiphenomenalist framework, without abandoning physical causal closure, attributing causal powers to qualia, or positing a special non-physical faculty of introspection.

To be precise, the claim I want assessed is that phenomenal judgements (for instance, the judgement that one is now experiencing a reddish quale) are accurate not because the phenomenal states causally produce them, but because the physical brain states that do cause them stand in a relation of structural isomorphism to the phenomenal states, which are themselves causally inert. Call this the structural correlation dissolution.

Please decompose your reasoning as follows.

First, assign rough probabilities (each between 0 and 1) to these four sub-claims:

(i) That phenomenal judgements genuinely require some form of causal interaction with the phenomenal states they represent, rather than being explainable by structural correlation alone.

(ii) That it is coherent for an epiphenomenalist to maintain that phenomenal concepts are formed and applied through physical processes that track structural features without causal acquaintance.

(iii) That the explanatory gap — the difficulty of seeing why physical structures should give rise to phenomenal experience at all — undermines the plausibility of structural correlation as a basis for accurate judgement.

(iv) That there is a damaging asymmetry between first-person phenomenal judgement and other forms of knowledge, such that structural correlation works for third-person science but fails for introspection.

Second, combine these into your overall credence in the structural correlation dissolution, and explain briefly how they combine, whether multiplicatively, as weighted considerations, or otherwise.

Third, identify which of (i) to (iv) your final number is most sensitive to — that is, which sub-question, if resolved differently, would shift your credence the most.

Fourth, I hold my own credence at approximately 0.60. A colleague holds hers at approximately 0.25. Given your decomposition, on which of (i) to (iv) is the disagreement between 0.60 and 0.25 most likely to be concentrated?

Finally, what specific argument, conceptual distinction, or empirical result — for instance, concerning the neural mechanisms of introspective report or the transparency conditions on phenomenal concepts — would most substantially move your number in either direction?
