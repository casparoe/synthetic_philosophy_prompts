In our political philosophy seminar the dirty-hands debate has stalled in a way that's driving me slightly mad, and it's occured to me that we might be talking past each other entirely. Everyone accepts that a political leader can sometimes be required to do something morally wrong for the greater good, and everyone accepts there's some stain or cost left behind. But half the room treats that stain as genuine moral residue — an unavoidable wrong that remains on the agent even when fully justified — while the other half treats it as purely psychological, a kind of moral hangover that should vanish once you recognise that democratic authorisation actually transfers blame to the collective rather than leaving it on the individual. We've circled this for months with no movement. Is this disagreement really about whether moral responsibility can be delegated, or about whether emotional after-effects count as moral data? Or are we answering different questions — one about individual virtue and conscience, one about institutional design — under the single heading 'dirty hands'? I want a diagnosis with reasons, supported by what each side actually commits to, not a survey of the usual literature, and an honest answer about whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about how voters actually authorise harm, or a formal model of distributed blame could break the deadlock. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Use British English.
