A colleague summarized the material-constitution view—where a statue and the clay that makes it up are said to be distinct objects—like this: the statue and the clay share every physical part and every non-relational property at every moment they coincide, and their only differences are modal, since the statue is essentially arranged statue-wise while the clay is essentially that particular portion of matter. So when the statue is melted, nothing physical is destroyed; the clay simply continues to exist minus one modal feature. On this account, there are not really two objects in one place, but one underlying entity that bears two different sets of modal properties, which means the coincidence problem dissolves into a single object described in two ways. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what a defender of constitution would accept, what subtly misrepresents the view—particularly regarding whether the statue and clay share all properties and whether modal difference alone can ground numerical distinctness—and whether the conclusion that there is only one object actually follows from the premises given.
