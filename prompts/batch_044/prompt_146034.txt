I'm drafting an essay that re-examines cyclical theories of history—specifically Polybius's anacyclosis and Ibn Khaldun's cycle of group solidarity (asabiyya) and state decline—through the idea of recursive institutional blindness. The standard reading treats both as deterministic loops: Polybius's regimes cycle through virtue, corruption, and collapse; Ibn Khaldun's nomadic solidarity fuels conquest, generates urban luxury, and erodes the very energy that built the state. I want to argue that these are not merely repetitive patterns but self-undermining feedback structures in which the institutional form of a regime actively obscures the conditions of its own reproduction. In Polybius, each constitutional form develops internal criteria of legitimacy that prevent it from recognizing its own decay until collapse is irreversible; in Ibn Khaldun, the bureaucratic state quantifies and taxes the tribal energy it depends on, thereby destroying what it measures. I then contrast this with linear-progressive or directional models—particularly Toynbee's challenge-and-response framework and Spengler's morphological determinism—to ask whether the cyclical/linear distinction collapses once we see that repetition is driven by a system's inability to observe its own dependency relations. The essay concludes by proposing that cyclical history is better understood as a series of locked-in feedback loops rather than as a circle or a line.

I'm particularly interested in comments on three things. First, whether this framing genuinely captures Polybius and Ibn Khaldun or forces them into an anachronistic mold borrowed from modern systems thinking. Second, whether the contrast with Toynbee and Spengler holds up, given that Toynbee allows for creative responses that break cycles and Spengler allows for morphological variation within a predetermined life-span. Third, whether the concept of recursive institutional blindness adds anything beyond standard accounts of institutional path dependence, or whether it merely redescribes well-known mechanisms of corruption and decay. I also want to know if the essay's central move—collapsing the cyclical/linear distinction into a question of reflexive capacity—feels like a substantive philosophical contribution or a verbal redescription that leaves the underlying historiography untouched. Please address these points in roughly the order given, and flag any place where the argument seems to depend on a misreading of the primary thinkers rather than on the synthetic framework I'm imposing.
