Design a runnable 75-minute classroom simulation for 16 advanced undergraduates in a philosophy-economics cross-listed seminar. The goal is to make participants feel the structural pull of rational herding and information cascades through direct experience, not through lecture. Frame the scenario in everyday institutional terms—do not name the cascade model or its canonical authors in any student-facing material, and reveal the formal concept only in the debrief.

Construct the simulation around a sequential peer-review panel awarding a single internal research grant. Each student receives a private evaluation envelope containing a noisy score (drawn from a hidden distribution with an objective true mean) for the same hypothetical proposal. They must then declare a recommendation—approve or reject—one at a time, in a fixed visible order. Each student hears all prior recommendations, but never sees the underlying scores that produced them.

Inject a non-standard structural twist: assign half the participants an invisible institutional-loyalty cost—an unknown penalty, applied only if they break with the majority recommendation—that is drawn randomly and revealed only after the cascade locks. Crucially, students do not know whether they themselves bear this cost, and they know that others may or may not bear it, but they do not know who does. This creates a second-order reasoning problem: breaking the cascade requires not only disagreeing with observed public belief, but also risking an unknown private penalty, and assuming that others are not rationally responding to the same hidden risk.

Design the payoff scheme using only course-participation points (no real money). Reward individual accuracy relative to the hidden true mean, but also award alignment points if the student's recommendation matches the final group decision. Make the alignment reward substantial enough that a purely accuracy-seeking agent might still rationally herd once the public sequence looks strong.

Provide: (1) exact materials and setup rules (cards, envelopes, or digital equivalents; how scores are generated and hidden; the order of declaration); (2) the payoff matrix and point totals; (3) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75-minute session, including when the cascade should be expected to lock; (4) the student-facing instructions, written in plain language without philosophical or technical jargon; (5) the facilitator's debrief, mapping specific moments in the sequence onto the distinction between private signal and public belief, identifying the point at which rational Bayesians with common knowledge of rationality should still follow the crowd, and explaining how the hidden loyalty parameter transforms a first-order cascade into a self-sealing second-order one; (6) an honest assessment of which formal feature of cascade theory—such as the precise Bayesian updating rule with known signal precision—this physical simulation necessarily simplifies or misrepresents, and how the facilitator should supply that missing piece verbally; and (7) a prediction of the most likely failure mode on the first run—such as a student breaking the cascade too early because they misread the payoff structure—and a specific recovery strategy.

Keep everything self-contained: no bibliographies, no citations, no requests for further reading, and no dependence on named theories outside common knowledge. Ensure the philosophical idea does its work through the rules, incentives, and hidden parameters, not through explanation given beforehand.
