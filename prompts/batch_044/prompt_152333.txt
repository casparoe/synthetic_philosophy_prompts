After a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether documented cross-cultural differences in folk morality genuinely threaten moral objectivity, I want our disagreement reconstructed as a rigorous dialogue. Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them the Universalist and the Relational Pluralist—who take each other’s points seriously rather than talking past one another.

The Universalist should defend the view that WEIRD populations do not provide the moral baseline but rather a specific institutional distortion: they systematically overrepresent impersonal, rule-based moral reasoning (centered on harm and fairness) and therefore misread relational, context-sensitive ethical practices elsewhere as “moral variation” rather than as different surface expressions of shared underlying moral capacities. They should propose a conceptual distinction between “normative content” and “institutional framing” and argue that what looks like deep divergence is often a translation error caused by forcing relational obligations into the WEIRD vocabulary of individual rights and duties.

The Relational Pluralist should counter that this framing already assumes what needs to be proven—namely, that there is a single “moral architecture” beneath cultural differences—and should argue that in many societies ethical life is organized through obligations tied to kinship and social roles that lack any direct WEIRD analogue. They should press the “problem of moral translation”: describing such practices in WEIRD moral terms necessarily commits a category error of normative individualism and strips them of their distinctive normative force. They should invoke a novel distinction—perhaps an “index of normative translatability” versus “ontological incommensurability”—to ask whether cross-cultural convergence in revised judgment is required for objectivity, or whether convergence in the conditions of revision is sufficient.

Make the exchange genuinely reciprocal. The Universalist must respond directly to the translation-objection and concede at least one genuine weakness in their position—for instance, that universalist frameworks risk insulating themselves by reinterpreting any robust counterexample as “misapplication” of universal capacities. The Pluralist, in turn, must address the Universalist’s charge of parochialism-in-reverse—that pluralism often relies on an unexamined WEIRD concept of “culture” as bounded, coherent, and internally uniform—and should concede where universalist appeals to shared cooperative cognition or emotional responses actually constrain pluralist claims. Neither character should be a strawman; neither should appeal to authority, bibliographies, or citations. Keep the tone formal and polished, and let the dialogue turn on whether moral objectivity requires cross-cultural agreement in moral judgment or only agreement in the structural conditions under which judgments are corrected.
