Since my recent appointment as chair of a regional disability adjudication panel, I have been required to train ten adjudicators—none with philosophical training—to apply a conceptual distinction drawn from Thomas Szasz's thesis that mental illness is largely a myth used to medicalize problems in living. Our mandate is to determine whether a claimant's condition represents a genuine biomedical pathology warranting full benefits or a socially mediated form of distress that falls outside medical disability categories. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run in thirty minutes per case: the questions must come in a fixed order, each needs explicit criteria for what counts as sufficient evidence, the procedure must mark clear stopping points for approval or denial, and it must flag exactly where the judgment is factual versus where it is unavoidably a value decision. Please include worked applications to two specific scenarios: a claimant with post-viral fatigue that carries documented immunological markers but whose functional limitations mirror social withdrawal patterns, and a claimant whose dissociative episodes occur exclusively during workplace conflict with no corresponding neurobiological findings.

The answer should take a committed, definite position—do not merely lay out competing interpretations—on precisely where Szasz's framework holds up administratively and where it collapses under practical pressure, including at least one case where the procedure produces a determination that seems unjust. Be explicit about the misfires: which distinctions survive simplification, which must be dropped, and where an adjudicator must override the flowchart because the theory offers no answer. I ask this without requesting any bibliography, citation, or reference to specific texts, since our staff operates entirely without library or online access during hearings, and I want the reasoning self-contained.
