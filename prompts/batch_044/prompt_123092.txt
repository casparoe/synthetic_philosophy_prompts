I have recently been appointed chair of the participatory inquiry board for our city’s neighborhood infrastructure pilot, and I am now responsible for designing the deliberative process for recurring local disputes—traffic-calming proposals, community land-use adjustments, noise and nuisance complaints—under the explicit mandate that our work be guided by Dewey’s experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. I need this translated into an actual, reusable procedure that my staff and I can run in a two-hour public session with roughly fifteen residents, with limited staff time, no requirement for unanimous consent, and the obligation to produce a written record that the city council can adopt, modify, or reject. The procedure should not be a philosophical summary; it should be a flowchart-in-words that a non-philosopher can execute.

Please construct the procedure with ordered stages or diagnostic checkpoints that operationalize the core commitments: democracy as cooperative social inquiry rather than preference aggregation; the transformation of an indeterminate situation through collective testing of proposed resolutions; and the aim of a warranted, revisable working agreement rather than a final vote or a static truth claim. For each stage, specify the questions the chair must ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure stops with a negative or deferred result, and which steps are genuinely value-laden and therefore must be flagged as judgment calls rather than resolved by the protocol.

In particular, include: (1) an opening diagnostic that distinguishes whether the group is facing a genuinely problematic situation or merely negotiating pre-formed interests, with criteria for refusing to proceed if the latter; (2) a problem-definition stage that requires participants to articulate the situation in terms of experienced consequences rather than abstract rights or identities; (3) a proposal-generation stage that treats solutions as hypotheses to be tested against anticipated transformations of the situation, not as bargaining positions; (4) a testing stage that specifies what counts as empirical evidence about consequences, and where the board must acknowledge that risk assessment and value weighting remain irreducibly judgmental; and (5) a reconstructive audit stage that checks whether the adopted resolution actually transforms the problematic situation or merely accommodates it, with instructions for initiating a revisability clause rather than closing the file.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a proposal to convert a neighborhood parking lot into a community garden, where some residents cite property-value effects and others cite food-security benefits; (b) a proposal to install automated speed cameras on a residential street, where empirical predictions about accident reduction conflict with objections centered on surveillance and trust; and (c) a case where the procedure yields an unwelcome conclusion—specifically, a dispute over noise complaints about a local workshop that the inquiry reveals is symptomatic of a deeper, structurally unaddressed conflict about gentrification and displacement, such that the board lacks the authority and scope to resolve it locally and must therefore recommend institutional restructuring rather than a site-specific remedy.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of democratic problems lie outside its scope, where it will systematically misfire if applied uncritically, whether it can ever deliver a genuinely warranted collective belief or only a provisional working agreement, and how the chair should document its limits for the council record.
