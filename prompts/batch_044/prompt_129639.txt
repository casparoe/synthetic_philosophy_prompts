I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow evening at a neighborhood workshop I've organized about whether our town should adopt binding local referendums for zoning and budget decisions. About thirty people will attend: half are neighbors who are furious about a recent council vote they feel ignored them, and half are municipal staff plus two visiting scholars who study civic participation. I have no formal training in philosophy or political theory, but over the past year I've been thinking hard on my own about what makes consent genuinely valid—specifically whether deception in how a question is framed, or a voter's lack of competence about the actual trade-offs, can nullify consent even when no one is physically coerced. I want my ten-minute remarks to do more than introduce the agenda; I want the idea of valid consent to structure what I can and cannot say. For example, I should not claim that a simple majority vote automatically produces legitimate authority if the ballot language obscures the tax implications, or if voters are being asked to approve a technical fiscal mechanism they do not understand. At the same time, I need to acknowledge the neighbors' anger as philosophically serious—not merely emotional—and I need to take a clear, committed position: does our proposed referendum design, which includes mandatory plain-language disclosure and a two-week public comment period before voting, actually secure valid consent, or does it still fall short because the aggregation of individual judgments itself dilutes the competence required for each person's consent to count? Write the remarks in a spoken but polished first-person voice, with the philosophical argument doing the real structural work, defending a definite answer with reasons rather than laying out options for me to choose between. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or verbatim quotations from philosophers. At the end, add a brief note indicating where the argument takes liberties with standard distinctions—for instance, if it treats collective decision-making as analogous to individual consent in a way some theorists would reject—or where it relies on a contested interpretation of competence or deception.
