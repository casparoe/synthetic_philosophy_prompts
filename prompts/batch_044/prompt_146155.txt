I'm designing a sorting instrument for a graduate seminar in metaethics, and I want to move beyond the standard encyclopedia dichotomy that asks whether one is a moral realist or an anti-realist. What I need is a decision tree built from the logical space of three concrete commitments, so that a respondent can be sorted by what she actually believes rather than by the label she adopts. The first branch should ask whether moral disagreement is fundamentally cognitive—a dispute about what is the case—or practical—a conflict in plans or attitudes that need not involve contradiction. The second should ask whether, if moral claims are truth-apt, their truth is fixed by natural properties alone, requires irreducibly normative properties, or is determined by some idealized response condition. The third should ask whether moral progress is intelligible as convergence on independent truths, as growing coherence among attitudes, or as a category mistake. Please order these questions so that earlier answers do not make later ones redundant, and construct the tree so that each path terminates in a leaf.

Each leaf must carry either the name of a recognized position—at minimum, non-naturalist realism, Cornell-style naturalist realism, error theory, non-cognitivist expressivism, ideal-observer constructivism, and moral relativism—or the label unnamed if the combination is not defended in the literature. Alongside the name, provide one sentence identifying the view's most awkward commitment regarding either cross-cultural disagreement or the authority of morality over agents lacking relevant psychological capacities. Where two recognized positions fall on the same leaf, flag them as indistinguishable by this instrument and propose one further case—for instance, whether a being with no conscious states but with functional analogues to suffering can be wronged—that would separate them. You must also identify at least two combinations of answers that appear to be unoccupied, and diagnose whether each is incoherent (the commitments contradict one another), unstable (they collapse into a neighboring leaf), or merely unexplored.

The tree should be presented with the branching questions stated in plain language that does not presuppose familiarity with technical terminology, followed by the mapping of answers to positions. Do not organize the presentation as a list of named theories with descriptions; instead, present the logical branches first, then locate the named positions within that space. Finally, include a brief scoring key: given a respondent's answers to the three questions, which leaf should she land on, and which further diagnostic case should be used to resolve ambiguity?
