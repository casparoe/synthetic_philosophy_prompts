Consider the British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform, convened in 2004: 160 citizens selected by lot from the provincial population, who met over eleven months, studied comparative electoral systems, and in October 2004 recommended replacing first-past-the-post with BC-STV, a single-transferable-vote system. Their recommendation was submitted to a province-wide referendum in 2005 held concurrently with the provincial election, where it received 57.69 percent support—exceeding a simple majority but falling short of the government-imposed 60 percent supermajority required for adoption—while winning majorities in 77 of 79 electoral districts. Analyze this episode through the lens of sortition and lottocratic theory, particularly the claim that random selection produces deliberation untainted by partisan interest and achieves descriptive representation, yet lacks the democratic mandate that only election or direct popular authorization can confer.

In your analysis, address the following with disciplined mapping—who are the relevant actors, what are the options, and what counts as evidence—rather than forcing the theory to speak where it is silent. First, to what extent does the assembly's internal process—ordinary citizens mastering complex voting mechanics and reaching a near-unanimous recommendation—confirm the lottocratic prediction that sorted bodies can deliberate rationally about institutional design? Second, how should a proponent of sortition explain the referendum's failure: does it demonstrate that recommendations from a non-elected body suffer from a deficit of binding legitimacy, or does it instead show that lottocracy is viable only when paired with a direct ratification mechanism? Third, introduce and apply a conceptual distinction between deliberative legitimacy (the epistemic quality and representativeness of reasoning inside the sorted body) and binding legitimacy (the authority to impose structural change without further democratic endorsement). Does the BC case show that these two forms of legitimacy can come apart, and if so, is that separation an anomaly that weakens the case for lottocracy, or is it precisely what the theory should anticipate? Fourth, identify at least one feature of the episode—such as the supermajority threshold, the geographic distribution of support, or the assembly's own selection protocol—that the theory explains poorly or leaves entirely silent. Conclude by assessing whether the episode strengthens, complicates, or undermines the argument for replacing elected legislative bodies with sorted citizens' assemblies.
