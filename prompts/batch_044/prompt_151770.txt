I am advising a five-year interdisciplinary research programme—uniting archaeologists, philosophers of science, and cognitive anthropologists—whose central aim is to understand how inferences from material culture to past beliefs and social institutions can be justified rather than merely asserted. I do not want a literature review; I want a defended strategic agenda. Among the currently unresolved questions in this space, which two or three deserve the majority of our limited resources, and which should we actively deprioritise because they are either intractable within our horizon or downstream of more fundamental issues?

To ground the choice, consider three competing lines of inquiry. The first is what I shall term the problem of institutional silence: the challenge of inferring collective rules—property regimes, kinship obligations, or decision-making protocols—from material traces that never directly represent those rules, but only manifest their indirect, distributed effects. The second concerns the epistemic status of ethnographic analogy: should documented societies serve as defeasible heuristics that shape our interpretive imagination, or as evidential constraints that can raise or lower the probability of specific belief-reconstructions? The third is what I call the distributional fallacy—the unexamined assumption that spatial or quantitative patterns within an assemblage map transparently onto the social distribution of mental content, without sufficient correction for taphonomic distortion, ritual redeposition, or multi-use cycles that decouple physical location from intentional meaning. There is also a practical constraint: our findings may feed into heritage-policy decisions and legal assessments of indigenous institutional continuity, so the agenda must weigh theoretical importance against the kind of robust, defensible conclusions that can survive adversarial scrutiny outside the academy.

Please provide a prioritisation that is explicit about its criteria—dependency relations (which questions are upstream of which), tractability across a five-year horizon with current methods, and the magnitude of change that resolving each would produce for excavation practice, museum curation, or policy. Which of these lines, if settled, would force a revision of how the others are pursued? Which risks being overrated—unresolved, certainly, but with an answer that would leave actual archaeological practice largely unchanged? And please present your reasoning in British English, without supplying bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
