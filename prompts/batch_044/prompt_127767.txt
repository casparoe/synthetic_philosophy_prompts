I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop next month on constitutional machinery and legal self-reference. The room will be split evenly between legislative drafters and constitutional lawyers—who think in terms of entrenchment clauses, amendment thresholds, and suspension procedures—and philosophers of logic and language, who understand self-reference but have never had to write a statute that binds its own successors. I need the address to do real structural work: rather than presenting the paradox of self-amending rules as a puzzle with a hidden solution, I want the speech itself to embody the problem. Specifically, I want to show why any attempt to state the meta-rule that governs amendments already presupposes a legal framework that the meta-rule might itself alter, which means I cannot simply stand before the room and declare that the matter is resolved. The idea should shape what I am able to claim and what I must leave open.

Please write this as a fifteen-minute occasion-setter. It should be accessible to family members and non-specialists who may also be present, without flattening the conceptual difficulty for the experts. To ground the argument, include one concrete hypothetical constitutional clause: an amendment procedure that contains a provision allowing its own suspension by a different voting threshold, where the suspension clause is drafted in language that refers back to the amendment procedure set forth above without specifying whether that reference picks out the pre-suspension or post-suspension version. Use this clause to illustrate how the slippage between rule and meta-rule is not merely logical but drafting-level practical. The speech should explain why the workshop's central question—how a legal system can coherently authorize its own revision—remains genuinely open, not just technically difficult. Do not turn it into a lecture with citations or a literature review; keep it an address that sets the tone for three days of disagreement.
