I'm on the review board for a small grant program that funds quick-turnaround social-science pilots. We get like 20 proposals a month, and researchers keep asking if they can run a small preliminary survey before locking their main study design — basically asking wether it's worth delaying the grant decision to gather more info. I want a decision procedure based on Good's theorem on the value of information, turned into something my board can actually run in a 15-minute meeting with nothing but a whiteboard and coffee.

The procedure should take a proposal and ask questions in order: can they actually update their action after recieving the info? are the prior probabilities genuinely fixed, or does the pilot itself become part of the result and corrupt them? Where the math gives a clear yes or no, say so; where it becomes a judgment call about wether the delay cost outweighs the expected value, flag it as a values vote, not a fact. I need it to catch the hidden misfires — like when the information looks free but actually expands the decision space in a way that violates the theorem's conditions. Then run it on three cases: a team that wants to pre-test survey questions on 30 people before a 500-person study; a team that wants to peek at preliminary census data before choosing which neighborhoods to sample; and a case where we all want to approve the pilot but the procedure forces a no because the information can't actually change our funding decision. Keep it practical for people who aren't decision theorists, mark every spot where we have to vote on values instead of computing answers, and be honest about what the procedure just can't do.
