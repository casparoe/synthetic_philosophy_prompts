Please evaluate the following student answer to an exam question on Fitch's paradox of knowability. Do not indicate your own preferred resolution; instead, provide a point-by-point assessment, assign a specific grade, and justify it with precise comments on where the reasoning holds, where it subtly slips, and what important distinctions the student has overlooked.

The exam question asked: Reconstruct Fitch's derivation from the knowability principle—formally, p → ◊Kp—together with the existence of an unknown truth, p ∧ ¬Kp. Attend carefully to the role of the distribution principle for knowledge over conjunction and to the modal step that generates the contradiction. Then discuss whether the paradox is best dissolved by rejecting the knowability principle, by denying that "p and nobody knows p" expresses a genuinely possible state, or by appealing to a de dicto / de re distinction in the attribution of ignorance. In particular, distinguish between interpreting the principle as a normative claim about the absence of in-principle barriers to knowing p and interpreting it as an empirical claim about the actual cognitive capacities of finite agents.

Student answer:

The argument begins with the claim that if p is true then it is possible that p is known: p → ◊Kp. We then suppose there is some true p such that nobody actually knows it, so we have p ∧ ¬Kp. From p we derive ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Now if an agent knows the conjunction (p ∧ ¬Kp), then she knows p and she also knows that p is not known by anyone. But if she knows p, then it is false that nobody knows p. Hence the state of knowing (p ∧ ¬Kp) is self-undermining. So ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, which contradicts what we derived from p. Therefore the supposition p ∧ ¬Kp must be rejected, and we conclude that for every p, if p then Kp.

To avoid the conclusion, I think we should reject premise (i). The principle that every truth is knowable sounds like an empirical generalization about human minds—clearly false, since there are truths about distant galaxies or microscopic events that no human will ever learn. Once we treat it as an empirical overidealization rather than a logical law, the paradox never gets off the ground.

Regarding premise (ii), the statement "p and nobody knows p" involves attributing ignorance to p. Read de re, this means p itself bears the property of being unknown, but if I can identify p specifically enough to say it is unknown, I already have some epistemic grip on it. Read de dicto, "it is unknown that p" is just a claim about the absence of knowledge in the community, which does not require me to know p myself. The paradox arises from sliding between these two readings: we treat the unknown status de dicto when we assert it, but then apply knowledge distribution de re inside the modal scope. Keeping the attribution consistently de dicto blocks the contradiction without abandoning the knowability principle.

Finally, some responses invoke paraconsistent logic and say the contradiction is real but harmless. That seems unnecessary here, since rejecting the empirical interpretation of (i) already dissolves the problem, and clarifying the de re / de dicto boundary handles the rest.
