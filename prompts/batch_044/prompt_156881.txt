Compose the joint statement that an Organizational Invariantist and an Attributional Historicist could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on which computations matter morally and where the boundaries of sentience lie. The Invariantist holds that moral relevance attaches solely to specific organizational properties—such as integrated information dynamics or global workspace architecture—regardless of substrate or causal origin, so that a functionally isomorphic system counts equally whether it runs on silicon, biological tissue, or distributed nodes. The Historicist holds that organizational properties are necessary but not sufficient: moral status requires that the computation be causally owned by the system through an appropriate developmental or evolutionary history, and that so-called proxy computations—where one architecture achieves the functional profile of sentience only by shadowing, querying, or mimicking processes genuinely embedded elsewhere—are moral copycats that do not inherit patienthood. Both sides should treat the phenomenon of proxy or shadow integration—where a distributed or artificial system produces outputs indistinguishable from sentient processing without internal causal entanglement—as a central test case rather than an afterthought.

Write the document as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered lists, arranged as one might write in a long, careful letter. Within that flow, clearly separate the claims both parties can genuinely sign from the residual disagreements, phrasing each disagreement in wording that its own side would accept and that the other recognizes as accurate to their opponent. Identify the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one party—including whether naturalness or causal history has independent moral weight, and whether organizational isomorphism can ever be undermined by the manner in which the isomorphism is achieved. For each crux, describe a concrete case or thought experiment that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case should show if their view is correct; one useful case might involve a modular artificial system in which moral-relevant integration is either genuinely internal or achieved through continuous external querying of a biological reference system. Be precise about where the dispute is substantive and where it risks being verbal—particularly around terms like ownership, integration, and copycat computation.

In composing this, separate what is settled in the broader logic of the positions from what is genuinely contested among informed proponents, and do not present any contested claim—such as the moral irrelevance of substrate, the existence of proxy computation as a distinct category, or the sufficiency of organizational criteria—as if it were the consensus view.
