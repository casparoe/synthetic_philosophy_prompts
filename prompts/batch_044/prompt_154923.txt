I've been working through Williams's integrity objection — specifcally the "one thought too many" thing with the drowning spouse — for a seminar paper, and I built this extension that now seems to prove moral criticism is literally impossible. I'm pretty sure there's a slide somewhere but I can't spot it, and I'd rather not just ditch (6).

The argument goes like this. (1) Williams says saving your wife is okay if your reason is just she's your wife, but not if it's "saving spouses is permitted, and she's mine." The corruption is inserting a general description between you and the particlar. (2) I think this generalizes: any intermediate representation — even "this is what loyalty requires" — mediates between the bare attachement and the act, so it corrupts integrity just the same. (3) So the only integrity-preserving motivation for acting on a bond is the bare identification itself — he's my friend, this is my child — with no further because-clause.

(4) Now take moral criticism of a harmful attachment, like saving an abusive partner over a stranger. The critic has to offer some reason — say, equal moral worth — but that reason is necessarily a mediated description, not the agent's bare attachment. (5) So either we accept the harmful act as integrity-preserving and drop all criticism, or we allow mediation and sacrifice integrity ourselves. (6) But Williams treats integrity as basically non-negotiable; losing it is worse than losing morality. (7) Therefore moral criticism of attachments is always illegitimate, and ethics collapses into pure partiality.

This feels wrong — I want to keep (1) and (6) if I can — but I don't see the leak. Is there an equivocation in how "mediated" works between (2) and (4)? Does (4) wrongly assume criticism has to come from outside the attachment rather than being internal to it? What's the cheapest premise to reject, and what does giving it up actually cost me?
