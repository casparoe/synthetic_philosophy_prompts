Consider Zhuangzi's parable of the butterfly dream and his broader skepticism about fixed distinctions between self and other, waking and dreaming. Now imagine he is confronted with two modern developments: first, empirical neuroscience showing that during certain sleep stages the brain suppresses the mechanisms that normally flag experiences as internally generated, which explains why dreams feel like direct perceptions of reality; second, the philosophical argument that our current empirical world might be an artificial computation indistinguishable from base reality. Would Zhuangzi treat these findings as confirming his view that there is no stable criterion to separate real experience from transformed appearance? Or would he reject them as still presupposing a metaphysical split between an authentic substrate and a deceptive surface — a split that his idea of continuous mutual transformation, in which self and world perpetually become each other without underlying essence, was meant to dissolve? In constructing your answer, do not simply summarize the parable; instead, show how his response would turn on whether modern skepticism relies on a representational model of mind — the assumption that experience is a picture that can either match or fail to match the world — that he would find conceptually alien. Be specific about which aspects of contemporary neuroscience and computational metaphysics he could absorb into his framework, and which he would have to reject as committing a category error between empirical causation and the transformation of things.
