A colleague summarized an essay linking Weber, labor policy, and Japanese overwork culture as follows: Weber's Protestant work ethic was not merely descriptive but contained an implicit normative brake—the Sabbath limit—that prevented capitalist rationalization from destroying its human substrate. The essay claims that France's 35-hour week was an unconscious secularization of that Sabbath principle, designed to preserve productivity by capping exhaustion, whereas Japan's karoshi demonstrates the same capitalist drive stripped of any theological or legal brake. From this the author concludes that any sustainable labor policy must institutionalize a non-instrumental limit on work, or rationalization will eventually consume its own workforce. I suspect Weber is being made to bear normative weight he never intended, and that the political history of the 35-hour law is more economic than theological. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what Weber actually argued, what counts as a defensible modern extension, and what misrepresents either his sociology or the causal story behind karoshi and the 35-hour week. In particular, does the conclusion that we need a secular Sabbath actually follow from Weber's analysis?
