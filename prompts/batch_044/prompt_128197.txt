I'm in the final year of a seminar on democratic theory and next week I'm presenting a thesis I developed for the course: in ranked-choice elections that present a clear lesser-evil dynamic—where one candidate is unacceptable and the viable field includes both a moderate and an extreme—voters have a conditional expressive duty to submit a complete preference ranking rather than bullet-voting for the moderate, even when bullet voting is strategically optimal for defeating the extreme. The duty does not rest on causal efficacy, since individual rankings do not change outcomes in large electorates; instead, it derives from the communicative function of defining the boundary of acceptable governance. By ranking the moderate above the extreme but below other acceptable candidates, the voter performs a normative judgment about which positions fall within legitimate authority, and this contributes to a shared public record of institutional boundaries. I concede up front that strategic bullet voting is rational by standard decision-theoretic lights and that the duty generates no outcome-related obligation.

The audience is a reading group of about twelve: mostly social choice theorists who treat voting as preference aggregation under strategic constraints and are steeped in Arrow-style impossibility results; two democratic theorists who work on civic virtue and expressive voting; and a constitutional lawyer who cares about voter intent, ballot design, and the legal interpretation of ranked preferences. They are skeptical of moral duties in voting because they think voting is either instrumentally useless or purely expressive in a non-duty-generating way.
