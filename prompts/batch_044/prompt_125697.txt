I am trying to determine my actual commitments on the boundary between therapy and enhancement, not by adopting a theoretical framework first but by testing my gut reactions against concrete cases and seeing whether they converge on a principle. Here are my verdicts.

Correcting a severe immunodeficiency through gene therapy in a child: clearly therapeutic. Applying the identical protocol to a healthy adult to confer broad viral resistance: clearly enhancement. Restoring hearing with a standard cochlear implant to someone with sensorineural loss: therapeutic. Implanting the same device in a person with normal hearing to perceive ultrasonic frequencies: enhancement. A mood-regulating implant that eliminates clinical depression and restores the patient’s prior emotional range: therapeutic. The same implant used by someone without any mood disorder to maintain a permanently elevated, non-fluctuating affect: enhancement. Reconstructive surgery after severe burns: therapeutic. Cosmetic rhinoplasty performed solely for aesthetic preference: enhancement. Cognitive rehabilitation after traumatic brain injury to return to one’s prior baseline: therapeutic. Pharmacological cognitive augmentation in a healthy graduate student to expand working memory well beyond typical human limits: enhancement. Hormone treatment for a child with documented growth-hormone deficiency: therapeutic. The same hormone treatment for a child of entirely typical development and normal statistical height range, administered solely to reach a taller percentile for social or athletic advantage: enhancement—though this case keeps pulling me back, because both interventions move a body toward a statistical norm.

The pair I find hardest to reconcile involves grief and recovery. An intervention that resolves clinically recognized prolonged grief disorder and restores the capacity for normal functioning: therapeutic. But an intervention—say, a targeted neuromodulation protocol—that does not erase memories or suppress sadness, but compresses the natural grieving trajectory from a year to a few weeks in an otherwise healthy bereaved person: I oscillate. It feels like accelerating healing, yet it also feels like optimizing an emotional process that was not pathological.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on restoration of species-typical functioning, or on the presence of a recognized harm or dysfunction, or on the direction of change relative to an individual’s own baseline—that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about the growth-hormone pair and about the grief-compression case? Which of my verdicts must be overturned to preserve simplicity? And what does the resulting principle predict for a case I have not listed: a neural interface that allows a musician with perfect pitch to discriminate microtonal intervals beyond the resolution of normal human audition—restoration of nothing, expansion of a sensory faculty that was already exceptional? Does this cross the line, and if so, by the same clause that governs the other cases?
