I have recently been appointed senior conservation compliance officer at the National Museum of Material Culture, and over the past quarter I have found myself adjudicating the same metaphysical dispute in different guises: when a restoration proposal replaces part of an artifact's substance, does the object remaining in our vaults still count as the same work for insurance valuation, exhibition labeling, and heritage-law documentation? The issue became personally urgent after a disagreement with our legal counsel over a restored ceramic urn, and it was sharpened further in a conversation with a colleague who had audited a seminar on metaphysics and mentioned the classic statue-and-clay distinction—the idea that an artwork and the matter constituting it can occupy the same region yet have different persistence conditions, so that destroying the clay need not destroy the statue and vice versa. I now see that my team has been applying an implicit, unexamined theory of material constitution, and I need it made explicit, reusable, and runnable by staff who have no philosophical training.

Please construct a decision procedure my team can apply to any restoration proposal in about thirty minutes, grounded in the framework of material constitution but translated into practical steps. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, clear stopping points where the answer is no and the object is no longer the original work, and explicit flags where the decision is one of curatorial values—such as whether artistic authenticity requires causal continuity with the maker's hand—rather than empirical fact. The procedure must distinguish the persistence conditions of the artwork as an intentional object (form, artistic function, historical identity) from those of the material substrate, and it must handle the hard case in which the original material is entirely replaced but the form and function are preserved by an expert restorer using period-appropriate techniques. Apply it to three proposals: first, a ceramic vessel from the 1700s in which thirty percent of the original clay has been replaced with chemically identical but newly fired material; second, a bronze equestrian monument in which the original bronze was melted down due to structural failure and then recast into the identical form using the original molds; and third, a marble relief in which only five percent of the stone was replaced, but the replacement is a modern synthetic composite that alters surface reflectivity in a viewer-noticeable way. For the bronze recasting case in particular, I want to see exactly what the procedure outputs even if it conflicts with my own curatorial intuition that the monument has been lost. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle—where it will necessarily misfire, which metaphysical distinctions it suppresses to remain usable, and which judgments must still be referred to a senior officer with authority over the collection.
