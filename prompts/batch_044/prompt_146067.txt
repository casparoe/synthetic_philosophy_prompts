Consider the following exam question and student response.

Question: In a hypothetical chronometric isolation chamber, an observer undergoes successive mental states—first acute pain, then calm pleasure—while every external physical process is suspended and no clock advances. The observer reports a vivid first-person sense of "now" shifting. Assess whether this scenario supports ontological presentism over eternalism, or whether an eternalist can accommodate the report without adopting a "moving spotlight" metaphysics. In your answer, distinguish presentism proper from the growing-block view, and address whether the observer's temporal access requires a metaphysically privileged present or can be accounted for by B-theoretic relations plus indexical self-reference.

Student answer: Presentism is clearly supported here because only present mental states exist; the past pain is no longer real and the future calm is not yet real, so the observer's direct acquaintance with change is genuine change, not mere variation across a static four-dimensional block. The growing-block view is essentially the same position, since it also denies reality to the future and accepts that the present is ontologically special; it just allows the past to hang around, which doesn't affect the argument. Eternalism fails because if all times exist equally, the observer is just one slice in a block, and there is no fact of the matter about which slice is "now"—the report of shifting awareness must then be an illusion or a purely linguistic indexical. But indexicals can't do the work, because "now" picks out a real metaphysical boundary that eternalism denies exists. Therefore the chamber proves presentism, specifically in its growing-block form, since that form best explains why past mental states are retained in memory but future ones are absent.
