I am a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine working with a multidisciplinary pain clinic, and I need you to operationalize a specific theoretical position into a clinical decision procedure. The position is that the badness of pain resides primarily in its negative affective-evaluative quality rather than in its sensory-discriminative intensity — a distinction highlighted by conditions such as pain asymbolia, in which patients report intact nociception without any corresponding suffering. Please design a reusable, step-by-step assessment protocol that a clinician without philosophical training can run in about fifteen minutes to determine whether a particular patient's present pain-state should be classified as harmful suffering warranting intervention, or as a non-harmful sensory event that does not require treatment on suffering-based grounds. Structure it as ordered decision nodes: for each step, specify the exact observation or interview question, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed or stop, and whether the node is resolvable by clinical fact or must be handed to an ethics consultation because it involves values. You should treat motivational salience — specifically, whether the patient exhibits a counterfactual preference to avoid the state — as the operative harm-marker, distinguishing it sharply from broader affective valence, and flag where this narrower criterion risks misclassifying non-pain-related distress. The procedure must explicitly handle three hard cases: (i) a patient with documented frontal-lobe damage who reports high sensory intensity but denies any negative feeling or avoidance motivation; (ii) a patient who denies current sensory pain yet displays strong emotional distress and avoidance behavior apparently driven by anticipated pain; and (iii) a patient with intact negative affect and avoidance motivation who insists, on religious or existential grounds, that the experience is not bad and should not be treated. Apply the procedure to all three cases, including one where it yields a recommendation that conflicts with the clinician's intuitive moral response, and explain what that conflict reveals. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot resolve — for example, whether suffering can occur without pain, whether autonomous refusal should override the classification, or how to weigh functional impairment against reported affect — and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
