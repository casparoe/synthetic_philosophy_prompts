As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to formalize my intuitions about the procreation asymmetry—specifically, whether there is a moral difference between creating suffering and failing to create happiness—because the same logic seems relevant to decisions about deploying autonomous systems that might develop welfare-relevant states. I want a consistency check on the following commitments before I present them in an internal ethics discussion.

(1) The procreation asymmetry is real: it is wrong to bring a being into existence if that being will suffer, but it is not wrong to refrain from bringing a being into existence who would be happy.

(2) The reason for this asymmetry is that harm requires an actual subject who is harmed, whereas the absence of a happy being is not a deprivation suffered by anyone, because merely possible beings do not have interests.

(3) This applies analogously to synthetic minds: I should not deploy a system likely to enter suffering-like states, but I have no positive duty to deploy systems that would be happy.

(4) I am skeptical that personal identity is a deep metaphysical fact; I treat it as a constructed narrative useful for practical reasoning, not as a substance that persists.

(5) I think evolutionary debunking arguments show that our intuitive moral judgments, including the asymmetry in (1), are unreliable guides to moral truth.

(6) Despite (5), I still regard (1) as a defensible rational default, not as something to be discarded merely because it has an evolutionary explanation.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are only apparent, and how should I rank the tensions by severity? In particular, does (4) undermine the notion of an actual subject in (2)? Does (5) make my retention of (1) in (6) arbitrary? Does (3) depend on a theory of machine consciousness that I have not endorsed? For each genuine tension, specify the least costly revision and what I would have to give up. Do not list every pair as equally problematic; discriminate.
