After a long argument with a friend about whether "genocide" has become morally overextended, I've settled on a view I want tested: the 1948 Convention's exclusion of political groups and cultural destruction from the legal definition was not merely a diplomatic concession but a conceptually defensible boundary, and attempts to revive Lemkin's broader framework—or to extend the label analogically to systemic economic violence or climate-induced displacement—risk dissolving the distinction between genocide and other severe wrongs rather than clarifying it. What would count against this? I want decisive defeaters, not just awkward cases: are there moral or historical examples the narrow definition clearly fails to capture that are genuinely continuous with the Holocaust or Rwanda? Do Lemkin's own writings undermine the claim that he intended a sharp boundary? What should defenders of the narrow view concede about the arbitrary legal protection this exclusion affords to political groups? And does this position insulate itself by definitional fiat—if so, is that a flaw for a category meant to do both moral and legal work?
