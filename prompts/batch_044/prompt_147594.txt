I've been prepping my grad seminar on just war theory and I keep tripping over the moral equality of combatants thing, probably becuase I read too much revisionist stuff over the summer and now I can't see Walzer's view clearly. Here's my confusion, which my students will probably share: if the thesis is that unjust combatants aren't more blameworthy for killing than just ones — that they share the same rights and liabilities — then doesn't that imply that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause actually acquires a kind of moral licence that cancels out the defender's ordinary right to resist? In ordinary self-defence, if someone unjustly attacks me, my right to stop them doesn't depend on them having some reciprocal right to hurt me back. But in war, if both sides are morally equal, it looks like the aggressor's permission to kill is on a par with the defender's permission to defend, which seems to erase the asymetry that makes defence justified in the first place. So is Walzer actually committed to saying that just combatants have less of a claim to defend themselves than they would in a non-war context? And if not, where exactly does the asymmetry live?

I've also been wondering whether this whole framing assumes something like a contractualist reciprocity that only made sense when combatants faced each other with comparable vulnerability. In modern conflicts where one side relies heavily on autonomous systems and there's no mutual risk or recognition, could the equality thesis be a kind of historical artifact rather than a normative principle? I'd like my students to wrestle with whether the equality is descriptive of a particular battlefield reciprocity or genuinely normative, but I'm not sure I've got the distinction right myself. Could you help me sort out where the false move is in my reasoning, and whether there's a way to preserve the asymmetry of jus ad bellum without collapsing into the revisionist view that unjust soldiers are basically murderers?
