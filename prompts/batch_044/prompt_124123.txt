As a retired physical chemist who has spent the last several years working through the philosophy of science—particularly Kuhn’s account of scientific revolutions—I find myself returning to a tension I never fully resolved during my laboratory years. Kuhn argues that paradigm shifts are precipitated by persistent anomalies that normal science cannot dissolve, and that competing paradigms are often incommensurable in their taxonomies, methods, and standards of evaluation. The replication crisis that emerged in experimental psychology and spread into parts of the biomedical sciences after roughly 2011, together with the institutional response of open-science reforms—pre-registration, registered reports, stricter power analyses, and the gradual displacement of null-hypothesis significance testing by estimation and Bayesian alternatives—seems to present just such an anomaly cluster. Yet I am unsure whether Kuhn himself, had he witnessed these developments, would have interpreted them as the birth pangs of a genuinely new paradigm or merely as an especially vigorous episode of puzzle-solving within the existing statistical-positivist framework.

More specifically, I wonder whether Kuhn would have recognized genuine taxonomic incommensurability between what we might call the exploratory p-value paradigm and the confirmatory pre-registered paradigm. Both camps still deploy the same underlying statistical ontology—probability distributions, error rates, effect sizes—so the divergence seems primarily sociological and procedural rather than conceptual in the way that, say, the transition from phlogiston chemistry to Lavoisier’s oxygen theory was ontological. At the same time, practitioners within each camp appear to evaluate the very same study differently depending on whether it was pre-registered, suggesting that the criteria for what counts as credible evidence have become paradigm-dependent. Would Kuhn have seen this as a weakened or partial form of incommensurability—one that lacks the full world-view transformation he associated with Copernican or Einsteinian revolutions—or would he have insisted that without a change in the fundamental entities and laws being investigated, we are witnessing not a paradigm shift but a disciplinary self-correction?

If you were to reconstruct Kuhn’s likely assessment, I would be particularly interested in whether he would draw an analogy to the transition from classical to relativistic mechanics, in which the old framework was shown to be a limiting case, or rather to the phlogiston-to-oxygen case, in which the old entities were abandoned entirely. Given that the replication crisis has not produced a new ontology of psychological or biological phenomena—only a stricter sociology of method—does this imply that Kuhn’s model of revolutionary science is ill-suited to methodological crises that leave the underlying theoretical entities untouched? Or might he have argued that in the social and life sciences, where theory is often thinner and more entangled with measurement practice, a shift in methodological taxonomy can indeed function as a full paradigm replacement even without an accompanying metaphysical rupture?
