Here's an argument I cooked up for my metaphysics seminar and now I'm suspicious of it because it seems to prove monism from pretty harmless premises. (1) Grounding is irreflexive and well-founded. (2) If x and y are both fundamental and don't ground each other, then the disjunctive fact x or y has no ground — neither disjunct explains the disjunction, and there's no further fact to do the job. (3) So well-foundedness plus irreflexivity means there can be at most one fundamental fact. (4) But now take that single fundamental fact, call it W. W presumably has parts or aspects — maybe it's a complex state of affairs. Apply (2) internally: the fact that W has aspect A or W has aspect B... wait, that gets messy. Actually the real problem is that if W is truly one, then every other fact is grounded in W, but then the grounding relation itself seems to be part of W, which makes W ground itself? I'm not sure I stated that right — there's a typo in my notes somewhere. Anyway, the conclusion that there's exactly one fundamental fact looks like Spinoza, which I don't want to buy. Where's the mistake? Is it that disjunctive facts don't need grounding? Or that fundamental applies to entities, not facts? I want a committed answer — don't just list options, tell me which premise dies and what it costs me.
