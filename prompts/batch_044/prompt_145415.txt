You are a philosopher of law and bioethics with training in political philosophy and feminist theory. Before you begin your analysis, take a few minutes to brainstorm the normative frameworks that could underpin institutional designs in reproductive policy—do not settle on a single narrative too quickly, and pay attention to tensions within each framework.

Consider the following institutional divergence. In most European jurisdictions, commercial surrogacy and compensated egg donation are banned or severely restricted; where altruistic surrogacy is permitted, the gestational carrier is typically recognized as the legal mother at birth, and any transfer of parental rights requires post-birth adoption procedures that treat the arrangement as a private family matter rather than an enforceable contract. By contrast, in many U.S. states, commercial surrogacy operates through enforceable contracts, intended parents may obtain pre-birth parentage orders based on genetic contribution or contractual agreement, and gestational carriers are compensated as service providers with no legal standing to claim parental rights. Meanwhile, egg donation in the U.S. is structured as a market transaction with price differentials tied to donor characteristics, whereas European regulation either prohibits compensation beyond expenses or channels donation through state-controlled clinics that frame it as a gift rather than a sale.

What philosophical commitments about the body, labor, contract, and parenthood does this divergence presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value that makes the European model coherent—does it require viewing reproductive capacities as inalienable from the person, or can it be justified on harm-reduction or anti-exploitation grounds without that metaphysical commitment? Then examine whether the American model presupposes a consistent view: for instance, does treating gestation as contracted labor commit one to treating other forms of embodied care work the same way, and if not, what feature distinguishes reproductive labor? Assess whether either practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether a liberal autonomy framework, a feminist relational-ethics framework, or a market-libertarian framework could license either institutional design with only minor adjustments. Finally, describe what a policy built on the rival view (the one not dominant in each jurisdiction) would have to change structurally: not merely the payment rules, but the legal ontology of motherhood, the status of the contract, and the role of state clinics.

Please reason carefully, distinguishing what each practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. At the end of your response, explicitly state how confident you are in your reconstruction of these implicit theories, and single out the specific comparative claim—about inalienability, about the consistency of treating gestation as labor, or about philosophical underdetermination—of which you are least certain.
