I am facilitating a 75-minute seminar for 14 graduate students in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. I want a runnable, in-person activity—not a lecture or open discussion—that makes participants experience conceptual slippage in a microdomain, of the sort modeled in the Copycat framework for fluid concepts: category boundaries that shift not through random error but through systematic analogical pressure across parallel structures inside the domain itself.

Design the activity for one long table with no computers and no outside references. Invent a closed microdomain consisting of 24 physical cards, each displaying an artificial “figure” built from three visible feature dimensions—say, shape orientation, internal pattern density, and relational position to a reference token. State three explicit classification rules that initially appear to yield sharp categories, but arrange the card set so that analogical parallels between subgroups pull competing directions on at least four borderline cases. Structure the rules, roles, and any stakes so that participants reach contradictory yet individually defensible classifications of those borderline cases before the facilitator names the phenomenon.

Provide: the precise composition of the card set and the three rules (stated exactly); a minute-by-minute timeline including individual sorting, pair comparison, and group deliberation phases; the role or team structure; the mechanism by which disagreement is surfaced and whether a scoring or payoff scheme is used; the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the mechanisms of fluid concepts (analogical pressure, context-dependent feature weighting, absence of a fixed conceptual kernel); and an explicit, honest admission of which feature of the actual Copycat computational architecture—or of standard interpretations of fluid concepts—this analog activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied verbally.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for instance, participants freezing on ambiguity, appealing to the facilitator as authority, or latching onto a single analogy—and exactly how to handle each failure without resolving the ambiguity prematurely. Finally, separate what is broadly settled about conceptual slippage in such microdomains from what remains genuinely contested among specialists (for example, whether the phenomenon undermines classical categorization generally or is confined to analogical domains), and flag any point where the activity takes liberties with the framework it illustrates. Do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view.
