I'm running a ninety-minute session for a forensic psychology reading group — clinicians who work with violent offenders and have no philosophy training beyond occasional pop-psychology references to "free will." They arrive assuming that moral responsibility is either a metaphysical property people possess or lack, and that competent therapy requires adopting a purely clinical, non-judgmental stance toward every patient. I want them to understand Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes: that moral responsibility is not a thesis about causal origins but about whether someone is a fit target of attitudes like resentment, gratitude, and indignation, and that the "objective attitude" is not the default posture of good therapy but a specific, limited suspension of ordinary interpersonal engagement.

Plan the session's explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself. First, identify the dependency order: what idea must be in place before the reactive-attitude framework can even be heard correctly, so that they do not immediately reduce it to "just feelings" or to a defense of libertarian free will? Second, specify the single carrying case — a constructed scenario of a therapist responding to a patient who has committed serious harm — and say exactly what this case must include (both the reactive and the objective stance in play) and what it must exclude (sympathy, moral approval, or a global judgment about determinism) in order to do the conceptual work. Third, map the misconceptions they will form in sequence: likely that reactive attitudes are irrational emotional reactions, that Strawson is secretly arguing for free will, that the objective attitude is identical to therapeutic neutrality, and that if human behavior is causally determined we are rationally required to adopt the objective attitude toward everyone. For each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted. Fourth, propose one brief check at the end that reveals whether they have grasped that responsibility concerns the quality of an interpersonal relation rather than a verdict about causal origin. Finally, tell me which parts of Strawson's broader taxonomy — the vicarious reactive attitudes, the self-reactive ones, or the full compatibilism-versus-incompatibilism framing — should be explicitly left out, with the reason that leaving each out protects rather than dilutes the core idea.
