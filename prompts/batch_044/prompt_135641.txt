After a recent professional setback forced me to reevaluate how I respond to outcomes I cannot directly command, I joined a small discussion group that reads ancient Stoic texts alongside modern cognitive-behavioral literature. A fellow participant—who has worked through the contemporary popularizers extensively but is not a scholar—summarized for me the Stoic dichotomy of control and its present-day revival roughly as follows. The ancient Stoics, she said, divided all things into what is up to us (our judgments, intentions, and acts of assent) and what is not up to us (health, reputation, wealth, and every other external); they held that virtue is the sole good and that externals are therefore not merely outside our power but also literally indifferent to human flourishing in every sense. Modern revivalists, she continued, have translated this into a clinical protocol: by refusing to assign any value whatsoever to outcomes, one eliminates emotional disturbance at its root, and recent neuroscience on cognitive reappraisal confirms that this suppression of affect is both therapeutically sound and the direct implementation of Epictetus’s teaching. From this she concluded that anyone who still feels anxiety before an interview or grief after a loss is simply failing to apply the dichotomy correctly, because a consistent Stoic experiences only rational assent without any affective coloring, and that this is why Stoicism is ultimately incompatible with emotional attachment or with caring about preferred outcomes. I am inclined to adopt this framework for my own situation, yet I worry that the account may conflate several distinctions that the tradition keeps separate. Which parts of this summary would a careful reader of the Stoics and of the modern revival accept as accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly regarding the status of preferred indifferents, the Stoic taxonomy of the passions versus the eupatheiai, and the actual relationship between ancient doctrine and modern clinical practice—and does the conclusion that Stoicism requires emotional suppression, the elimination of attachment, or the denial of all value to externals actually follow from the parts that are correct?
