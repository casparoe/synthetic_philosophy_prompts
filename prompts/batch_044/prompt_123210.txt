I have been reading about scientific theories and how they survive failed predictions, and I keep returning to Imre Lakatos's picture of a research programme: a hard core of basic commitments shielded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. When an experiment conflicts with the theory, scientists adjust the belt rather than abandoning the core. Lakatos called a programme progressive if those adjustments generate novel predictions, and degenerating if they merely explain away anomalies after the fact.

But I have also noticed what seems like a different pattern, one I do not have a name for. In some long-running scientific traditions, the core assumptions themselves appear to be slowly reinterpreted or quietly replaced over generations—not in a single revolutionary break, as Thomas Kuhn described, but through a gradual drift in which each small revision is presented as a clarification rather than a change. By the end, the hard core looks quite different, yet nobody ever acknowledged touching it.

How should I compare Lakatos's research-programme framework with this pattern of gradual, unacknowledged core revision? Does Lakatos's distinction between the hard core and the protective belt allow for the core itself to shift incrementally without automatically triggering a degenerating classification, or does his model require the core to remain literally intact? Furthermore, if this gradual drift is common, does it represent a descriptive weakness in Lakatos's model relative to Kuhn's account of paradigm change, or can Lakatos accommodate it by reclassifying the revised elements as part of the belt all along? I am especially interested in whether the progressive-versus-degenerating evaluation can still apply once the boundary between core and belt has become blurred through long-term revision.
