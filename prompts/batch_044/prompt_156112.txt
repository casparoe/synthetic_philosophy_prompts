I'm working through notes for an essay on whether germs, climate anomalies, and volcanic eruptions can be treated as genuine historical actors without committing us to environmental determinism. My central move is to distinguish functional agency—the structural capacity of a non-human force to constrain or redirect human institutions—from intentional agency, which requires some form of goal-directedness. I apply this to the sixth-century Mediterranean, where a cluster of volcanic winters (including the well-known mid-sixth-century cooling event linked to widespread tree-ring anomalies) and the first Yersinia pestis pandemic appear not merely as background conditions but as co-structurers of tax-base collapse, urban abandonment, and military logistics. To capture this, I propose a concept I'm calling historical co-authorship: a pathogen or climate forcing functions as a co-author not because it intends outcomes, but because its specific biological or geophysical properties—epidemiological transmission dynamics, aerosol optical depth, seasonal persistence—narrow the decision-space available to human actors in ways that a generic bad weather or disease outbreak framing misses. The essay then asks whether this framing genuinely escapes determinism. If human responses are constrained but not necessitated, does attributing co-authorship do explanatory work, or is it merely a metaphor that risks reifying non-human forces? I also consider a counter-argument that treating volcanic aerosols as actors is a category error, since agency requires at least minimal feedback loops absent in geophysical processes, and I wonder whether epidemiological models developed for modern populations can be projected onto pre-modern plague dynamics without anachronism.

I'd like comments on three points. First, does the functional versus intentional distinction actually block determinism, or does it smuggle determinism back in through the language of structural constraint? Second, is the historical co-authorship concept genuinely novel and useful, or does it collapse into existing multi-causal models in historiography? Third, are there hidden normative commitments—perhaps a tacit preference for distributed over centralized causation—that make the framing seem more illuminating than it is? Please be direct about logical gaps, hidden assumptions, and whether the argument's accuracy holds up, especially regarding the projection of modern epidemiological concepts onto pre-modern contexts.
