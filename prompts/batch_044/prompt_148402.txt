At a philosophy of mind study group, Jordan and Riley got into a sustained argument about whether global workspace theory can genuinely accommodate phenomenal consciousness without collapsing it into access consciousness. Jordan began by claiming that recent global-workspace models—particularly those that treat broadcasting as graded rather than all-or-nothing—leave conceptual room for what she called "half-broadcast" states: sensory information that has achieved global availability for emotional modulation and perceptual integration but has not yet been formatted for conceptual report or rational action guidance. She argued that this is precisely where phenomenal consciousness resides: globally available, yet not access-conscious in Block's strict sense, since access requires conceptual availability for reasoning and voluntary control.

Riley pushed back that this merely redefines phenomenal consciousness as a subset of access. He said that if a state is globally available—even only for "emotional integration"—then by definition it is accessible to some cognitive systems, which is all Block ever required for access consciousness. To defend the distinction, Riley introduced what he termed "refractory phenomenal residues": hypothetical cases in which intense local recurrent processing in early sensory cortices produces rich experiential character, but the signal is too noisy or weak to reach any global workspace threshold. He argued that these residues show phenomenal consciousness can exist with no broadcasting whatsoever, and that global-workspace theorists are forced either to deny that the residues are conscious—which he found implausible—or to concede that phenomenal consciousness does not require global availability.

Jordan replied that invoking "refractory residues" is ad hoc unless we have independent criteria for attributing consciousness to sub-threshold neural events. She granted that local recurrent processing might be necessary for phenomenal consciousness, but insisted it is not sufficient: without at least partial global integration, there is no "subject" for whom the experience exists. She then argued that Riley's position risks collapsing into a form of panpsychism about micro-processes, since if any isolated local recurrent loop can be phenomenally conscious regardless of global context, consciousness becomes ubiquitous throughout the brain.

Riley countered that Jordan was conflating "global integration" with "global broadcasting." He proposed that phenomenal consciousness requires only local integration with a self-model, not full workspace broadcasting, and that Jordan's "half-broadcast" category was actually closer to his view than she admitted—except that she insisted on retaining global availability, which he saw as unnecessary baggage inherited from access-based frameworks.

Jordan closed by asking whether, on Riley's view, a fully anesthetized patient with isolated local recurrent activity in V1 could have a visual experience that produces no global effects whatsoever. Riley said yes, in principle, though he admitted we might never detect it. Jordan responded that if such a state is undetectable in principle and makes no difference to cognition or behavior, then postulating it violates parsimony and risks turning phenomenal consciousness into an epiphenomenal ghost.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate Jordan's initial half-broadcast proposal, Riley's refractory-residue objection, Jordan's response about ad hoc attribution and panpsychism, Riley's distinction between integration and broadcasting, and Jordan's final parsimony challenge. For each move, rate it as clearly mistaken, partly right but missing something, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the deepest issue both parties are failing to address—particularly whether their disagreement is substantive or partly verbal about what "access" and "global availability" must entail.
