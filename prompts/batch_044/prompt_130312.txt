A friend and I have been arguing about whether the sphex wasp’s behavior tells us anything about free will, and she laid out an argument that now feels like a trap. (1) Sphexishness is mechanical rigidity: repeating a fixed behavioral script regardless of whether the environment has changed in ways that defeat the script’s purpose. (2) Free will requires what we might call counterfactual interruptibility—the capacity to revise one’s course in light of changed circumstances, not merely to be stopped by external force. (3) The sphex wasp lacks this, so it lacks free will. (4) Now consider human habits and expert automaticity—driving home on autopilot, a musician’s finger patterns, implicit heuristic judgments. These also run without deliberative revision, persist when circumstances have shifted (the road is closed, the key has modulated), and cease only through external interruption or exhaustion. (5) So human automatic behaviors are sphexish in precisely the structural sense that matters for freedom. (6) Therefore they lack free will. (7) But if habits and expert automaticity lack free will, then the vast majority of human action—perhaps ninety percent—lacks it. (8) Moral responsibility requires free will, so we are responsible for only a tiny fraction of what we do. (9) That conclusion is intolerable. Where is the slip? Is it that sphexishness requires a specific representational failure—an inability to register that the goal-state is satisfied—rather than mere behavioral fixity? Or does (4) to (5) smuggle in an equivocation between “not currently revising” and “structurally incapable of revising”? Or should we bite the bullet and accept (8), and if so, does saving free will for human habits force us to attribute it to the wasp as well? I want a point-by-point diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked plausible, and what each alternative costs—especially whether preserving human agency requires denying that sphexishness is even the right criterion for its absence.
