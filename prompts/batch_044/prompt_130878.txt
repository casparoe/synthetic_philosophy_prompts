I am preparing a consistency audit for my applied ethics seminar on institutional morality and individual obligation. Below is the package of commitments I have arrived at regarding effective altruism and its standard critiques. I want to know whether they form a coherent philosophical profile, or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations.

(1) I accept the institutional critique in its strong form: that effective altruism's focus on individual optimization systematically obscures structural mechanisms of harm, treating unjust institutions as fixed background conditions rather than objects of collective transformation, and that earning-to-give within such institutions risks functioning as moral licensing.

(2) Despite (1), I defend earning to give as permissible and sometimes optimal, provided it is practiced as what I term non-substitutive giving: the donor explicitly treats philanthropic contribution as parallel to, rather than a replacement for, institutional reform efforts.

(3) I regard measurability bias as a deep epistemic failure in longtermist cause prioritization. The attempt to rank distant, speculative outcomes using near-term quantifiable metrics involves a category error that undermines the reliability of career-ranking estimates derived from such comparisons.

(4) I hold that career choice should be governed by straightforward expected-value maximization: agents ought to select paths that maximize social value using the best available quantitative estimates, including longtermist ones, and epistemic humility should not function as a blanket prohibition on acting under uncertainty.

(5) I also maintain that honoring sunk career costs can be rational: if I have spent years building specialized expertise and relational capital in direct global-health implementation, switching away solely because updated longtermist estimates assign a different path a higher expected value wastes genuinely irreplaceable organizational knowledge and human capital.

Please audit this set. Specifically: does (2) genuinely avoid the legitimization problem raised in (1), or does non-substitutive giving collapse into the very moral licensing it seeks to avoid? Does (3) render (4) untenable, given that (4) relies precisely on the longtermist estimates that (3) discredits? Is (5) compatible with (4), or does my defense of sunk-cost honoring invoke a non-consequentialist value—such as narrative integrity or loyalty to past investment—that conflicts with expected-value maximization? Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones, and indicate what the least costly revision would be and what it costs.
