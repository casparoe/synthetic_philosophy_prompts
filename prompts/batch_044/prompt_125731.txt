I co-chair the Steering Committee of the Meridian Interdisciplinary Archive (MIA), a federation of twelve research institutes that maintains a shared, federated digital repository of normative philosophy and ethics texts. At our session two weeks ago we deadlocked over the proposed Differentiated Access Protocol (DAP), which would allow any member institute to restrict its own researchers' access to particular philosophical content streams—specifically a recently digitized corpus of radical moral subjectivism—without removing that material from the global network. The protocol is defended as a minimal liberal guarantee: each institute governs its own members' epistemic environment. Our founding charter, however, imposes a Collective Welfare Mandate requiring that all network-design choices maximize aggregate scholarly utility, operationalized through cross-citation density, collaborative replication access, and federated search coherence. My co-chair, Dr. Vasquez, insists that the welfare clause must dominate in any conflict; I am not convinced. Before the March plenary I must circulate a short formal opinion recommending whether the committee should adopt the DAP outright, reject it, or approve it with conditions—including a concrete implementation or sunset timeline—and the reasoning must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than merely restating one charter clause.

The institutional deadlock maps precisely onto the tension Amartya Sen formalized between minimal liberal rights and Pareto-optimal aggregation. Institute Alpha, running a moral-psychology lab, demands unrestricted access to the corpus for its researchers; Institute Beta, whose institutional charter commits it to a particular axiological framework, demands that its local nodes exclude the same corpus for its own members. Honoring both preferences locally—Alpha receives access, Beta enforces exclusion—fragments the federated network: cross-institutional citation paths break, replication packages fail to propagate across nodes, and aggregate welfare declines in a way that harms at least one party without benefiting anyone. Overriding Beta's local exclusion in the name of collective efficiency, however, violates the minimal liberal principle that an institution should control what its own researchers encounter. The memo should therefore examine whether the liberal guarantee can be preserved through a redesigned mechanism—such as opt-in metadata tagging or tiered access layers rather than hard node-level blocking—that avoids Pareto-suboptimal fragmentation without collapsing autonomy into collective optimization. Draft the document as a committee opinion addressed to the Steering Committee, with a clear bottom-line recommendation, a specified time frame for action or review, and an explicit engagement with the argument that the liberal principle ought to be treated as lexically prior in any federated scholarly body.
