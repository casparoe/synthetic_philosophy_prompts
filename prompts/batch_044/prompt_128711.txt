In my ethics elective we keep hearing that an advance directive is basically a Ulysses contract: the patient ties her future self to the mast of her earlier values, just as Ulysses had his crew bind him so he could hear the Sirens without steering the ship toward them. Teachers and some clinical ethics discussions use this to argue that we should honor a dementia patient’s prior refusal of life-extending treatment even when she now smiles, asks for food, and seems genuinely content to live—because the “real” self is the rational planner, and the later satisfaction is just the Sirens talking. I want to audit the analogy with real precision. Can you lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the Sirens, the ropes, the ship, and the open sea—and then show exactly where the mapping breaks? In particular, separate the disanalogies that matter for the binding conclusion from the ones that do not: for instance, in the myth the threat is an external seductive force, whereas in dementia the change arises from inside the same biological system, and there is no independent “crew” with its own authority to tighten or loosen the ropes. Then tell me whether the conclusion—that the directive should be treated as strictly binding—survives once those breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the moral work all along. Finally, ask whether the picture quietly imports a specific metaphysics of personal identity, such as the claim that one “true self” owns the body across time, and if that claim is contested or false, what remains of the argument for overriding the present patient’s expressed wishes?
