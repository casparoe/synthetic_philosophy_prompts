I've been arguing with a friend about Maimonides and animal sacrifice and I'm stuck on one point. Everyone knows the standard story: in the Guide he says sacrifices were basically a divine concession because the Israelites were addicted to pagan worship and needed a gradual weaning-off, but prayer and intellectual contemplation are the real deal. Meanwhile in the Mishneh Torah he codifies every sacrificial rule with insane precision, which makes it look like he thinks these rituals are permanently binding and metaphysically important, not just a temporary crutch. I want you to argue that there's actually no contradiction here, but only if you reject the usual historical reading of the concession. Make the case that Maimonides is really making a metaphysical claim: that direct intellectual worship of God is the only unmediated relation possible, so any bodily ritual—including sacrifice—is pedagogically necessary for people who haven't achieved that intellectual perfection, but ontologically redundant once they have. From this, defend the further claim that if the Temple were rebuilt tomorrow, Maimonides should still oppose reinstating animal sacrifice, since the whole point was transitional toward a worship that doesn't need physical mediation. Don't just summarize—actually argue for it, and deal with the strongest objection: if sacrifices were only a transitional crutch, why does he legislate them with such obsessive detail in the Mishneh Torah? And maybe touch on whether this pushes him closer to rationalist reform Judaism than orthodox readers usually want to admit. I feel like there's a real argument here but I can't quite get the steps to line up, especially around whether the concession is about human psychology or about the nature of worship itself.
