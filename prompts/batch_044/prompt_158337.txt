i was talking to a friend about death qualification — you know, in capital cases they strike jurors who say they oppose execution on principle, but they don't do the reverse and strike people who think we should execute more people. at the same time, the appellate system is designed to catch procedural errors, not to guarantee factual innocence, so the institution is basically accepting a small but real chance of killing someone who didn't do it. what philosophical commitments does that combination actually presuppose?

my working theory is that it's not just one view but a cluster: maybe a theory of civic duty that treats moral oppositon to punishment as a disqualifing bias rather than a reasonable disagreement, plus a kind of institutional consequentialism where the risk of error is traded off against deterrence and social value. but then there's the retributivist rhetoric — they deserve it — which seems to care about actual desert, not just procedural fairness. so is the practice secretly presupposing that desert is satisfied by correct proccess, or is it presupposing that moral knowledge about punishment works differently from other kinds of knowledge?

i'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, with clear distinctions between what the practice strictly needs and what just happens to fit well with it. also, if we replaced that implicit view with something that treated moral disagreement about capital punishment symetrically — like we do with other value disagreements in democratic institutions — and combined it with an epistemic standard that treated irreversible harm as requiring near-certainty, what exactly would have to change in jurry selection and appellate review? and is there any coherent view that licenses both the current practice and the usual moral justifications people offer for it?
