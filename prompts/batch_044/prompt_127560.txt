As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying metaphysics in earnest, I have been following the endurantism versus perdurantism literature with growing frustration. For decades, philosophers have disputed whether an object—say, a living human being—is wholly present at each instant of its existence (endurantism) or rather extends through time as a four-dimensional entity composed of temporal parts (perdurantism). The standard cases—Ship of Theseus, gradual cellular replacement, fission—are handled by both sides with ontological adjustments that never seem to force a concession, and the exchange feels circular rather than progressive. My own hypothesis is that much of the disagreement is verbal, turning on whether “persistence” should be analyzed as strict numerical identity under qualitative change or as the spatiotemporal continuity of a process. Yet I am unsure whether a hidden substantive dispute remains. Is there an unacknowledged empirical question—perhaps about how quantum decoherence fixes the boundaries of macroscopic systems over time, or about whether biological identity tracks functional architecture rather than material composition—that the metaphysical framing obscures? Or are the two camps answering genuinely different questions—one logical, one ontological—under a single heading?

To test whether the stalemate can be broken, I want to introduce a niche scenario that does not appear in the standard literature. Imagine a synthetic biological system engineered so that every molecular constituent is replaced continuously and uniformly, with no discrete stages, yet a global functional architecture—analogous to a standing wave in a flowing medium—remains invariant across the replacement. There are no “stages” of the ship, only a steady-state pattern maintained by constant throughput. Does this case genuinely discriminate between endurantism and perdurantism, or does it merely reproduce the old stand-off in biochemical language? More generally, please diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions masked by shared terminology? Specify what each side actually commits to in its handling of continuity and identity, and identify whether a formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about macroscopic boundary conditions, or a change of framing question could constitute real progress—or whether the debate is best understood as an intractable clash of starting intuitions about what it means for something to “remain the same.” I ask for analysis, not a bibliography or survey of interpretations.
