Following a recent neurological evaluation that left me uncertain about the reliability of specific sensory pathways, I have been revisiting skeptical arguments with fresh attention. I have noticed a recurring pattern in discussions of brain-in-a-vat scenarios: interlocutors begin by conceding that they cannot disprove a global skeptical hypothesis, yet they immediately proceed to construct elaborate, technically precise distinctions entirely within the framework of that very possibility—distinguishing, for example, between being deceived about the existence of external tables and being deceived about the mathematical properties of those tables, or between sensory deception and logical deception. The global concession appears to function as a ritual opening, after which reasoning continues with full epistemic confidence inside the skeptical scenario itself. Is there an established term for this specific argumentative structure—where skepticism is acknowledged globally but then compartmentalized to allow rigorous local reasoning within it? If no precise term exists, how should this move be understood in relation to contextualist responses, mitigated skepticism, and the distinction between global and local skeptical arguments? I am asking specifically about this pattern of domesticating the vat from within, not about skepticism in general, and I want to know exactly where its conceptual boundaries lie: what distinguishes it from ordinary pragmatic acceptance, and whether it collapses into a form of implicit realism despite its explicit skeptical framing.
