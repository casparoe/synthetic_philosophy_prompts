I've been working through some older aesthetics discussions on the paradox of tragedy—why people deliberately seek out art that produces grief, dread, or despair—and I'm dissatisfied with how the standard explanations treat the negative affect as either a purged residue (the catharsis view), a transformed pleasure (the conversion view), or a safely contained simulation (the control-frame view). What keeps nagging at me is a slightly different knot: in the moment of genuine emotional absorption, the sadness doesn't feel like a cost we tolerate for some compensatory reward; it feels like the very substance of the value. The fictionality of the work doesn't cancel the grief but creates a unique ontological space where grief can be fully real without being dangerous. I want to capture this specific tension—between the authenticity of the affect and the knowledge of its fictional shelter—without resolving it into either a hedonist triumph or a Stoic dismissal.

Could you write me a poem—exactly fourteen lines, with a clear volta turning around line nine, in blank verse or loose iambic pentameter—to close a reading-group session on this? The octave should build the naive expectation that art ought to please us, using concrete images: someone returning repeatedly to a particular tragic painting or opera that they know will devastate them. The turn should pivot to the recognition that the devastation isn't the price of admission but the point; that the safe frame doesn't merely contain the sorrow but authorizes its full reality. The closing couplet should name exactly what must be given up—perhaps the axiom that pleasure and pain are always competing currencies—without deciding whether that surrender is a liberation or a loss. It should be philosophically precise enough that an aesthetics specialist recognizes the positions without feeling them caricatured, and it should work as a poem for someone who has never read a word of philosophy.
