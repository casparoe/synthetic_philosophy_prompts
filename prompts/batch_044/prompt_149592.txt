Here's an argument I constructed for a discussion on moral scale that now strikes me as proving far too much. (1) If there is any nonzero epistemic probability that insects are sentient, then expected moral weight equals population size multiplied by that probability times intensity of experience. (2) Insects outnumber humans by roughly a billion to one; even assigning each insect only a one-in-a-million chance of sentience yields an aggregate expectation that dwarfs the human total. (3) Therefore rational agents should weight insect welfare far more heavily than human welfare in almost every decision. (4) Apply the same formula to bacteria—around 5 × 10^30 individuals, with a nonzero but far smaller per-unit probability—and the expected moral weight of bacteria swamps that of insects, and hence of humans. (5) So the reasoning doesn't merely prioritize insects; it dissolves practical ethics into an undifferentiated concern for maximally numerous entities, implying we should not build hospitals, cultivate crops, or even walk on grass if that risks harming microscopic life. (6) Because that paralysis is unacceptable, one premise must be rejected. I want to preserve the intuition that vast numbers can amplify moral concern, but I need to avoid the collapse. Is the mistake in treating individual-level epistemic probabilities as linearly aggregable? In sliding from aggregate expectation to individual obligation? Or in failing to apply a threshold below which near-zero probabilities should be disregarded? Identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and say what each major repair costs.
