ok so me and two grad-school friends were arguing about the unilateralist's curse — that thing where a bunch of agents each do something that looks smart alone but together it's catastrophic, like every nation deciding to run geoengineering trials because each thinks they're being careful. Sam said the curse only bites if people are overconfident in their private signals. If everyone's rationaly calibrated with a shared prior, they should defer to the group expectation and the problem dissolves. Riley said no, it's structural not epistimic — even with perfect calibration, if your rule is 'act when private EU is positive' and outcomes depend on everyone else, you get a cascade where each agent thinks 'maybe I'm the only mistake.' Sam then brought up Aumann agreement: if they share priors and know each other is rational, they should converge before acting. Riley said that ignores the core issue — the actions are unilateral and simultaneus, so you can't condition your choice on theirs; agreement is irrelevant when you can't observe or respond. Sam replied that real-world decisions are never truly simultaneous, so the actual problem is institutional design. Add veto points and you solve it. Riley countered with what she called the 'reverse curse' or veto-holder problem — now one blocker can stop beneficial coordination, and if every blocker thinks they're the only cautious one, you get total paralysis instead of collective harm. They turned to me and asked who's right at each step. I couldn't tell. Can you adjudicate move by move: Sam's overconfidence claim, Riley's structural cascade, the Aumann move, Riley's simultaneity objection, Sam's institutional fix, and Riley's veto reversal? Also, what are they missing about how confidence should aggregate when the action is irreversible? Don't just pick a winner — say where each is clearly wrong, mostly right, or partly missing the point.
