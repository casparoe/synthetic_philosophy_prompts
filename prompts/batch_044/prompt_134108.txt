I serve on the board of a metropolitan transit authority weighing whether to deploy an open-source autonomous routing system across our bus fleet. The system is not proprietary: dozens of other municipalities are independently evaluating the exact same codebase, and the risk-tolerance parameter embedded in the algorithm is identical in every instance. Our board is genuinely concerned with reducing transit injuries globally, not merely within our own jurisdiction. The complication, however, is logical rather than causal. If we set the parameter to a conservative value, that choice does not physically alter the code running elsewhere, yet because every deployment evaluates that same parameter through an identical reasoning procedure, our decision is strongly correlated with the choices those other cities will make. Several board members argue that we should therefore treat our own selection as evidence about what the other instances will do, updating our expectations about worldwide outcomes before deciding. Others insist that rational choice should track only the direct causal effects on our own passengers, since we cannot influence other cities by changing our local settings. I find both lines of reasoning plausible and unsettling. Without invoking formal decision theories by name, can you help me see whether there is a principled way to honor our global concern without falling into the trap of treating our own decision as mere news about distant copies of the same program? And if the correlation is real but non-causal, does it belong in the calculus of rational action at all? Please respond in American English.
