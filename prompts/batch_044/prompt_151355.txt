Since being asked—partly in response to my late father's lifelong concern with civil rights—to draft the founding philosophical framework for a public humanities center devoted to historical justice, I have found myself holding the following commitments, and I need to know whether they form a coherent package or whether I am quietly helping myself to incompatible premises.

(1) History possesses no intrinsic teleology and no providential oversight; moral outcomes are not encoded in the causal structure of events, so the literal answer to whether the arc of history bends toward justice is negative.

(2) Despite (1), I believe moral agents ought to adopt what I call a practical eschatology: treating justice as a regulative horizon that orients collective action, even though this orientation lacks metaphysical warrant and could be permanently frustrated.

(3) I understand "justice" in broadly Wittgensteinian terms, as a family-resemblance concept. There is no essential feature—whether distributive equality, procedural fairness, or recognition of dignity—that is necessary and sufficient across all instances; there are only overlapping networks of practice.

(4) Given (3), comparative judgments about moral progress should remain local, context-bound, and defeasible. Nevertheless, I want to maintain that the global dismantling of chattel slavery represents a genuine, cross-culturally recognizable moral achievement, not merely the replacement of one local power arrangement by another.

(5) I reject what I call narrative determinism: historical narratives do not possess independent explanatory power, and to say that history was moving toward abolition is to confuse retrospective interpretation with causal mechanism.

(6) At the same time, I hold that deliberately constructing and transmitting narratives of moral resistance can be causally efficacious in shaping future practices, provided we do not mistake them for historical laws.

Please audit these commitments pairwise. For each pair, tell me whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, and if genuine, rank the severity. For each genuine tension, specify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what it costs me—whether I must weaken my anti-teleology, qualify my family-resemblance view, or surrender my claim about long-range moral learning. For the apparent tensions, explain precisely why the conflict dissolves on closer inspection. Finally, identify the single revision that would clean up the most inconsistencies at the lowest overall cost, and tell me whether adopting it still leaves me with the position I originally intended to defend.
