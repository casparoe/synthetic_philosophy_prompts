Consider the institutional practice—already piloted in several national archives and heritage ministries—of “predictive curation”: employing long-range sociocultural forecasting models to identify which present artifacts, structures, and records will likely be judged historically significant two centuries hence, and concentrating preservation funding on those predicted items while allowing others to decay or survive only in temporary digital form. Proponents defend the practice as rational resource allocation, yet it implicitly treats the historical record not as an inherited burden requiring comprehensive stewardship, nor as a cyclical reservoir of meaning that may regain relevance unpredictably, but as a linear trajectory whose endpoint can be probabilistically anticipated from present data.

What philosophy of history does predictive curation presuppose? Must it commit to a linear-progress model in which the present is merely a transitional stage toward a knowable future, or can it be reconciled with a cyclical conception—of the kind articulated by Polybius in his theory of anacyclosis, by Ibn Khaldun in his account of asabiyyah and civilizational decline, or developed in the macro-historical schemes of Spengler and Toynbee—under which precisely those artifacts our models now dismiss might recover decisive importance in a later phase? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the practice; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and assess whether it is philosophically underdetermined, such that a cyclicalist could consistently endorse predictive curation by interpreting the forecasting apparatus differently. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed linear view with a genuinely cyclical one—where historical value is recurrent rather than cumulative—what concrete alterations would be required in the curatorial criteria?
