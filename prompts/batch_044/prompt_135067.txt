I'm writing this for a philosophy of social science seminar I'm currently taking, where we've been working through Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism. After reading the secondary literature on whether values can be genuine causes rather than just post-hoc rationalizations, I tried to formalize what makes Weber's explanation specific, but I ended up with an argument that seems to prove too much and now I can't locate the error. Here it is, organized around a distinction I've been using between motivational causation—where a value operates as a reason that shapes individual choice—and structural causation—where a value operates as a constraint that eliminates alternatives regardless of whether agents are aware of it.

(1) If cultural values are to explain institutional outcomes causally rather than merely descriptively, they must work through one of these two mechanisms: either they motivate agents to choose actions that aggregate into the outcome, or they structurally constrain the space of available actions so that the outcome becomes inevitable. (2) Weber's account of the Protestant ethic relies primarily on motivational causation: the value of worldly asceticism motivated rational capital accumulation without consumption, producing capitalism as an aggregate effect. (3) But if motivational causation is real, then for any institutional outcome O, we can identify some value V that, if widely held, would motivate agents to perform actions that aggregate into O. (4) Similarly, if structural causation is real, then for any O we can describe a cultural framework that eliminates all non-O-producing practices. (5) Therefore, for any outcome whatsoever, there exists both a motivational and a structural cultural explanation. (6) This means that citing a specific value like the Protestant ethic to explain capitalism gives us no more explanatory purchase than citing any arbitrarily constructed value to explain any arbitrarily chosen outcome. (7) So either cultural values never genuinely cause institutional outcomes, or they always do for every outcome, and Weber's specific claim is either false or trivial.

The conclusion feels absurd because it seems to erase the difference between a good historical explanation and a just-so story, yet each premise looks defensible in isolation. I want to preserve the idea that culture can be genuinely causal—otherwise the course readings make no sense—but I also want to preserve the intuition that Weber's explanation is non-trivial and specific to capitalism rather than a template applicable everywhere. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a hidden equivocation between the existence of a possible explanation and the actuality of a causal mechanism, or is the problem deeper, lying in the unstated assumption that a cause must be unique to be real?
