I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the metaphysics of gradual neural replacement—the hypothetical clinical procedure in which a patient's biological neurons are substituted over several years with synthetic analogues that replicate statistical firing dynamics but not structural connectivity, molecular substrate, or native error-correction architecture. I want an interactive decision-tree taxonomy—about seven or eight leaves—that sorts readers by the philosophical position they actually hold on whether the original person survives, is destroyed, or is succeeded by a new subject, without letting them simply pick a familiar label. Each branch node should be phrased as a concrete judgment or case claim that an educated general reader can answer without prior knowledge of metaphysical jargon.

The tree should begin with whether psychological continuity of any kind is necessary and sufficient for personal survival through the procedure; then branch for those who answer yes on whether that continuity must be mediated by a continuous biological substrate or can survive across functional isomorphism between discontinuous media; then, for those who allow cross-substrate continuity, whether the prolonged hybrid phase—during which the brain operates with both biological and synthetic neurons using incompatible error-correction protocols—contains one persisting subject, two overlapping subjects, or a mereological fusion with no single correct answer; and finally whether one's verdict on the hybrid phase is best explained by an endurantist ontology (the subject is wholly present at each stage), a perdurantist ontology (the subject has temporal parts connecting earlier and later stages), or an exdurantist ontology (only instantaneous stages exist, linked by external relations). For each leaf, provide the position's name if it is recognized in the literature—such as psychological-continuity endurantism, biological-continuity animalism, perdurantist hybrid-part theory, or exdurantist stage-series view—or mark it unnamed, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, such as having to deny that anyone feels pain during the hybrid months because there is no single subject present, or having to accept that two people share the same hospital bed during the transition. Please also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate—where two positions agree on every case judgment but differ only in metaphysical gloss—and briefly diagnose why they collapse together. The taxonomy should stand on its own without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be accessible to readers who have never taken a philosophy course.
