I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, but I have been trying to determine whether societies genuinely collapse or merely undergo radical restructuring. Drawing on the conceptual distinction I use professionally — cascading system failure versus graceful degradation — I offer the following gut verdicts on historical cases and ask whether they cohere under a single principle or whether my framework is being applied inconsistently.

My verdicts are these. The Late Bronze Age crisis around 1177 BCE strikes me as a genuine collapse: writing systems disappear across wide regions, long-distance trade networks fracture, palatial centres are destroyed, and subsequent centuries show no rapid recovery of prior institutional complexity. By contrast, the Classic Maya decline from roughly 800 to 900 CE feels like graceful degradation rather than collapse; monumental construction ceases and political hierarchies fragment, yet populations persist, languages continue, and new social forms emerge without demographic catastrophe. Easter Island (Rapa Nui) I am inclined to class as collapse — ecological overshoot, near-total deforestation, sharp population contraction, and the loss of the moai-building socio-political complex — though I recognise that the inhabitants survive and eventually adapt. The transformation of the Western Roman Empire in the West during the fifth century CE, however, looks to me like degradation without collapse: Latin administrative practice continues, the Church preserves institutional memory, and successor kingdoms deliberately maintain Roman structures. Finally, the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 is clearly not a societal collapse in my view; it is the rapid failure of a specific state apparatus, while the underlying society continues with modified institutions.

Is there a precise principle — perhaps concerning the continuity of core institutions, demographic persistence, or the reversibility of lost complexity — that delivers all five verdicts? If so, please state it clearly, show which clause is motivated by each case, and predict its outcome for two cases I have not listed: the Khmer abandonment of Angkor in the fifteenth century, and the Indus Valley Civilisation's shift away from urbanism and writing around 1900–1300 BCE, on which I keep reversing my judgement. If no single principle saves every verdict, please identify the simplest coherent principle, state honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn, and explain whether retaining the verdict and complicating the principle is more or less plausible than accepting the overturn. Please answer in British English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and treat the distinction between genuine failure and mere change as the central conceptual issue.
