I've been working through an argument that applies Gödel's first incompleteness theorem to metaphysics of mind, and it collapses into a conclusion I can't accept. (1) Gödel's first theorem applies to any consistent formal system that can represent primitive recursive arithmetic with a recursively enumerable axiom set. (2) Any philosophical theory of consciousness that is precise enough to be rationally evaluated must be regimentable into logical form, and since it must embed arithmetic and computation to describe brains, it satisfies the conditions for the theorem. (3) Therefore every consistent, precise theory of mind contains a true statement about consciousness that the theory itself cannot prove. (4) Because first-person reference introduces a self-referential loop structurally analogous to Gödel numbering, the unprovable statement is naturally one involving the subject's own awareness—say, the claim that this very system is phenomenally conscious. (5) If a theory cannot derive a true first-person fact about consciousness that falls within its domain, then adherents can never fully justify the theory's account of mind from within its own resources, which means rational acceptance requires stepping outside the framework. (6) But if stepping outside is always required, then no precise theory of consciousness can ever be rationally adopted on its own terms, which seems false—people do rationally defend physicalism, dualism, and panpsychism. (7) I want to keep (1) and the idea in (5) about internal justification, but I think the slide from (3) to (4) is doing suspicious work. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
