Here's an argument my study partner cooked up for our philosophy of science seminar and now I'm second-guessing because the conclusion feels too cheap. (1) If determinism is true, then every future state of a system is fixed by its present state plus the laws. (2) In a chaotic system, the Lyapunov exponants are positive, so any two initially close trajectories diverge exponentially. (3) Because we can only ever measure initial conditions with finite precision, the set of trajectories compatible with our measurements grows exponentially over time. (4) So from our epistemic position, there isn't one fixed future but a whole sheaf of them that are observationally indistinguishable. (5) But if determinism allows observationally indistinguishable futures that are ontologically distinct, then determinism doesn't actually constrain the world any more than indeterminism does. (6) So determinism collapses into indeterminism and we should reject it, or at least stop treating them as rival theories. I feel like the slide from (4) to (5) is where the sleight-of-hand is, but I can't quite pin it. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (5) if I can, but I want to know what I'm trading away.
