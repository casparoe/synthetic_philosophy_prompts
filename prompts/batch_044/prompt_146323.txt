I've come to hold a hybrid position in the Malcolm-Dennett debate on whether dreams are experiences: I accept Dennett's claim that the standard phenomenological report is a retrospective narrative construction assembled during awakening, but I think Malcolm's criterion argument actually supports this rather than undermining it, because the absence of an independent verification criterion for sleep-state occurrences shows not that there are hidden experiences, but that the concept of having an experience is incoherent when first-person authority is suspended. So there is no report-independent phenomenal state corresponding to dreaming; attempts to rescue one via lucid-dreaming protocols, REM-state neurofeedback, or appeals to phenomenal transparency smuggle in waking-state criteria that do not apply. What would genuinely count against this view? I want more than appeals to intuition or to lucid-dream reports. Specifically: which empirical results about memory consolidation during REM, or about the temporal architecture of dream narratives, would damage the construction thesis? Which conceptual moves—concerning continuity of consciousness across sleep stages, or the applicability of phenomenal concepts without waking access—would compel me to admit a genuine experiential state? And are there deflationary functionalist intermediates into which my view collapses, and would that collapse be damaging or merely clarifying? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones defenders of this hybrid position would themselves concede as serious, and keep your answer to one tightly argued paragraph of no more than 250 words.
