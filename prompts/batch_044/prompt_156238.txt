I am working through Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, specifically his tripartite distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of relating to the past. I want to determine whether my own judgments about when each serves life and when it harms it can be captured by a single principle, or whether I must abandon one or more of them.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, monumental history. I think it is healthy when a struggling musician studies the lives of composers who overcame adversity—using their perseverance as a spur—without imitating their styles. But I think it is harmful when a military academy teaches cadets that Alexander's tactics are directly applicable to modern cyber-warfare, encouraging false analogies with ancient conquest.

Second, antiquarian history. I think it is beneficial when a small coastal town preserves traditional fishing practices and seasonal festivals as living customs that give continuity, not as tourist spectacles. But I think it becomes harmful when a national heritage body mandates that every building over fifty years old must be preserved exactly, preventing climate adaptation or modern use, so that districts become uninhabitable museums.

Third, critical history. I think it is necessary when a democratic movement dismantles the legal and symbolic framework of an authoritarian past to make room for new institutions. Yet I think it turns destructive when a cultural programme declares all art and philosophy before a certain date irredeemably tainted, removes them from education, and discourages all engagement—severing roots rather than breaking oppressive chains.

There is also a case I keep returning to: a city retains a monument to a controversial figure in its city centre, but surrounds it with counter-monuments and extensive contextual material, so that it serves neither pure veneration nor erasure, but as an object of ongoing reflection. Part of me finds this a mature form of critical history; part of me feels it is an evasion that fails properly to honour or to condemn.

Finally, consider a situation not listed above: a digital archive uses artificial intelligence to generate restored speeches of dead political leaders, which contemporary campaigns then deploy to lend authority to present policies.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning the balance between present action and rootedness, or between organic continuity and imposed preservation—that captures all these judgments? If so, please state it precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case above, and predict its verdict on the AI-restored speeches scenario. Be honest about which of my intuitions the principle cannot accommodate, particularly regarding the monument case and the distinction between the two critical-history examples. Please write your answer in British English.
