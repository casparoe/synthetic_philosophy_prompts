I am putting together a workshop for a local civics discussion group that has been debating three specific electoral reforms: whether voting should be compulsory for citizens, whether the voting age should be lowered to 16, and whether long-term non-citizen residents should be allowed to vote in local elections. The participants keep talking past one another because they are not explicit about the underlying principle they are using—some appeal to democratic membership, some to affected interests, and some to the quality of collective decision-making. I want an instrument that reveals which principle actually drives each person's judgments, rather than asking them which reform they like.

Please write a decision tree (or an equivalent eight-to-ten question quiz with a scoring key) that sorts people into positions based on concrete cases. Each branch should be framed as a scenario an ordinary person could answer without knowing philosophical labels: for example, whether a well-informed 16-year-old should have the vote when an uninformed adult does not; whether a non-citizen who has lived and paid taxes in the community for fifteen years has a stronger claim than a disengaged citizen; whether it is fair to penalize non-voters if voting is understood as a civic duty rather than a right. The leaves should be named by the position they represent—such as strict citizenship-plus-age, affected-interests expansion, compulsory universalism, epistemic filtering, or a hybrid view—not by party or ideology. Please build the tree from the logical space first: start with whether voting is primarily about expressing membership, protecting interests, or achieving competent outcomes, then branch on how each principle handles age and residency. Note any combination of answers that lands in an apparently unoccupied or unstable position, and briefly explain why that combination is incoherent or unattractive—for instance, if someone accepts affected interests for residents but rejects them for younger citizens without an independent reason. Keep the language everyday, but make sure the philosophical architecture (membership, interest, competence) is what actually separates the branches.
