Having spent thirty years measuring population dynamics in marine invertebrates and now, in retirement, attempting to read philosophy of biology with the same patience I once reserved for tide-pool field notes, I recently found myself in a spirited but inconclusive argument with a former colleague—now a museum curator—about why our old research specimens are still filed under morphological species names that genetic barcoding has since fractured. The institutional picture is more tangled than either of us had appreciated. Natural history museums maintain accession catalogs organized around stable morphological descriptions, many unchanged since the nineteenth century; yet the conservation statutes that determine which organisms receive legal protection—the IUCN Red List criteria and various national endangered-species acts—employ a hybrid of reproductive isolation, genetic distance, and ecological-niche tests that routinely split or lump those same museum categories. At the same time, a vocal minority of systematists advocates abandoning the Linnaean rank of "species" altogether in favor of a rank-free phylogenetic nomenclature, a proposal that conservation lawyers resist almost reflexively because statutory funding mechanisms require a named taxon at the species or subspecies level to trigger habitat designation.

What philosophical theory of biological classification does this three-way tension—museological stability, statutory hybridization, and legal resistance to rank-free taxonomy—actually presuppose? Does the practice commit us to the view that species are real, mind-independent natural kinds, or does it merely treat them as administratively convenient clusters, and could a purely pragmatic or pluralist ontology accommodate the same legal and curatorial practices without contradiction? I want to distinguish what the system strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: is the law’s dependence on named ranks a contingent feature of legislative drafting, or does it reveal an implicit commitment to essentialism about species? Furthermore, tell me what exactly would have to change in museum accession protocols and conservation funding mechanisms if we replaced the implicit ontology with a view that treats species boundaries as entirely constructed by human interest rather than discovered in nature, and identify whether any part of the current framework is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with both realism and anti-realism about species—so that the institutional practice alone does not settle the metaphysical question.
