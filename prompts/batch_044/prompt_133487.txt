The student wrote:

Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles states that if x and y share every property, they are identical. The weak version includes relational properties; the strong version applies only to intrinsic, non-relational properties. In his 1952 thought experiment, Black describes a universe containing nothing except two spheres of identical size, colour, temperature, and material, separated by a distance in empty space. Because every qualitative feature is duplicated, the spheres appear numerically distinct, which threatens weak PII.

A common defence appeals to relational differences: sphere A is two miles from sphere B, and sphere B is two miles from sphere A. Black anticipates this move and notes that, with only two objects present, these relations are symmetric; there is no external coordinate system or third object to ground an asymmetry. If space is purely relational, neither sphere possesses a directional property the other lacks.

Some philosophers have tried to salvage PII by invoking haecceities — primitive individual essences. This was Leibniz's own fallback position: he accepted that each substance carries a unique thisness guaranteeing its distinction from every other, even when all phenomenal properties coincide. Without such primitive identities, he believed substance individuation would be impossible.

Finally, quantum mechanics reinforces Black's point. In symmetric quantum states, particles are genuinely indistinguishable yet remain numerically distinct. This demonstrates that nature permits Black-style pairs, so weak PII fails not only as a conceptual claim but as an empirical one.

Overall, Black succeeds in showing that weak PII is false if one rejects both absolute space and primitive thisness. The strong formulation avoids the difficulty by treating spatial position as intrinsic, though the quantum analogy still suggests that Leibnizian individuation is not supported by physics.
