Below is an essay sketch I want commented on. I am arguing that standard speech-act taxonomy treats performatives as discrete events complete at the moment of utterance, but that a large class of institutional utterances—those I call "standing performatives"—have success conditions that extend over time and depend on a persistent institutional substrate rather than on felicity conditions fixed at t=0. A discrete performative like "I bet you five dollars" succeeds or misfires at the moment it is spoken; by contrast, a standing performative like "this university is hereby accredited for the next ten years" or "the emergency powers are hereby invoked" does not merely produce an immediate effect but establishes a continuous normative state. On my view, if the substrate collapses—say, the accrediting body dissolves or the emergency framework is legislatively revoked—the original utterance is not just violated afterward but retroactively suspended or voided, which is different from saying someone broke a promise made in a discrete utterance. I also want to push the idea that Austin's category of "exercitives" (exercising powers, rights, or influence) implicitly assumes this substrate stability but never analyzes it, so the distinction between illocutionary force and perlocutionary effect becomes unstable for standing cases: maintaining the state looks like a perlocutionary consequence, yet it is actually part of the illocutionary success condition.

Second, conceptual coherence: is "standing performative" a genuine extension of speech-act theory, or am I just redescribing institutional maintenance conditions that belong to sociology or law rather than philosophy of language? Third, on the internal logic: I claim that for standing performatives, the speaker's authority must be continuously recognized rather than merely present at utterance—does this collapse into a theory of institutional authority that makes the speech-act component redundant?
