Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mara and Julian, debating whether universals are constituted by shared causal-functional roles or by shared intrinsic natures. Mara defends what she calls "structural-functional universalism": she holds that a property is genuinely universal when its instances occupy an identical causal-structural position within a system, even if their internal physical or qualitative makeup diverges radically. Julian defends "substrate-sensitive realism": he holds that a universal requires a genuinely common intrinsic nature across its instances, and that functional equivalence without intrinsic sameness produces only a "counterfeit universal"—a perfect simulation rather than a true instance of the property.

The dialogue should center on a hypothetical case they are examining together: a biotechnology that creates "synthetic instances" of a natural biological property—specifically, an artificial photosynthetic system that uses no carbon-based pigments, no chlorophyll analogues, and no evolutionary homology, yet converts light to chemical energy with identical efficiency, produces identical byproducts, and integrates into ecosystems in ways indistinguishable from natural photosynthesis. Mara should argue that if every causal, ecological, and functional relation is preserved, then demanding carbon-based intrinsic sameness is metaphysically arbitrary and reflects only a parochial attachment to familiar substrates. Julian should reply not by denying the functional equivalence, but by arguing that functional profiles are always calibrated against a background class of "canonical" instances that do share an intrinsic nature; remove that anchor, he should claim, and the functional description becomes so gerrymandered that it no longer picks out a single universal but an open-ended family of simulations.

The exchange must contain real back-and-forth. Julian needs to address Mara's arbitrariness charge directly—perhaps by arguing that explanatory priority runs from intrinsic nature to functional role, not vice versa, and that functionalist criteria risk collapsing distinct properties that happen to converge on similar outputs. Mara needs to respond by pointing out that in domains like philosophy of mind or ecology we routinely individuate properties by functional and relational criteria while treating substrate as irrelevant, and she should press Julian on whether his "canonical background" is a substantive metaphysical constraint or merely a psychological bias toward familiar instances. Both characters should remain equally competent; neither should be a strawman. End without resolution, but with each having sharpened their position in direct response to the synthetic-instance case.
