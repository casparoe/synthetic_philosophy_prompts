I'm writing a feature on democratic backsliding and keep going back to this historian I met at a panel—she's got this very specific taxonomy for when modern democracy is actually replaying teh fall of Rome, not just lazy punditry. She's consistent, but I'm never totally sure what rule she's running.

Here are her calls. She said yes, Roman analog, when a leader used a failed coup attempt to legally purge the judiciary and rewrite the constitution by referendum—she called it "Sullan by other means." Yes again for the slow bureaucratic strangulation of opposition access across the 2010s, where loyalists were quietly placed on election boards and voting rules were tightened without any single crisis point. But she said no to Jan 6, 2021: the mob broke windows but the military never moved and no state mechanism was seized. No also for Brazil 2022, where Bolsonaro questioned results but the courts and armed forces held firm and he eventually left office—she called it "a noisy transition, not a republic falling." She also dismisses modern First Triumvirate analogies for coalition governments, since Rome's crisis wasn't power-sharing but the inability to share power without private armies backing each faction.

Here's teh case I'm stuck on. Imagine a country where a populist leader loses an election, urges supporters to occupy election offices, but the army stays in barracks and teh legislature eventually certifies the original winner. The leader never returns, but his party—now led by a younger figure—wins the next election and passes laws letting the executive appoint regional election officials and restrict absentee ballots. The institutions stay intact but are rewired from within; there's no personal dictatorship, no proscriptions, no marching on Rome.

Would she call it Roman? I think two rules fit her track record equally well: one about formal institutional hollowing regardless of violence, and one about teh fusion of personal patronage and military loyalty replacing republican norms. Depending on which she's running, she might say yes or no. Lead with the bottom line—would she call this Roman-style collapse?—then lay out both rules, say which of her earlier verdicts best distinguishes them, name the further case that would settle it, and keep it under 300 words with no citations and a quick caveat section at the end.
