As a novelist attempting to render a society whose moral framework is genuinely incommensurable with my own—without reducing its members to either noble savages or monstrous others—I have found myself returning repeatedly to Montaigne’s essay on cannibals. I grasp the general outline of his mirror argument: he holds European practices up against those of the Tupinambá to expose the parochialism of our judgments. Yet a deeper conceptual tension eludes me, and I want it unpacked with some precision. On one reading, Montaigne advances a purely descriptive cultural relativism: moral norms are artifacts of local custom and habit, leaving no culture with an epistemic privilege over another. On another reading, he appeals constantly to nature—to an uncorrupted, natural state—as a normative standard by which he condemns European artificiality, cruelty, and religious violence. The conceptual problem is how these two commitments can coexist. Can his naturalism be reconciled with his relativism if nature functions not as a universal moral law but as a rhetorical lever designed to destabilize European self-certainty? Or does the position collapse into a hidden hierarchy, one that valorizes the cannibal’s way of life precisely because it conforms to an implicit universal of human flourishing? I am also struck by a pronounced asymmetry in his comparison: European torture and execution are rendered in visceral, almost grotesque detail, while the consumption of enemies is treated with a kind of aesthetic distance and even admiration. Does this asymmetry indicate that his relativism is strategic rather than principled—aimed less at genuine epistemic humility than at a targeted self-critique of his own civilization? I would like an explanation of the conceptual architecture that binds these elements together: the relation between descriptive relativism, normative naturalism, and rhetorical inversion in Montaigne’s argument, particularly with regard to the problem of representing radical moral alterity in fiction without either exoticizing it or assimilating it to familiar categories.
