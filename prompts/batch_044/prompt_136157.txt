Consider Avicenna's flying man thought experiment—an individual suspended in air, isolated from all sensory contact with his body and the external world, yet immediately aware that he exists. The argument is frequently cited as a precursor to Descartes's cogito, yet its precise philosophical targets, its metaphysical commitments, and its relevance to contemporary philosophy of mind remain contested. Among the open questions and potential lines of development that cluster around this argument, which do you judge to be the most worth pursuing now, and in what order?

Specifically, I want you to prioritize among the following kinds of issues—not merely list them, but defend an agenda: (i) the interpretive question of whether the flying man establishes the existence of a substantial soul distinct from the body, or only a minimal, non-corporeal mode of self-awareness; (ii) the comparative question of how Avicenna's conception of self-consciousness differs structurally from Cartesian self-certainty, and whether that difference undermines or enriches modern appeals to first-person authority; (iii) the constructive question of whether the argument can be rehabilitated within current debates on embodied cognition and minimal phenomenal selfhood, particularly regarding whether self-reference requires some form of bodily framing; and (iv) the methodological question of how treating the flying man as an intuition pump rather than a metaphysical proof changes what we can legitimately derive from it in philosophy of mind.

For each item you include in your ranked agenda, explain: what makes it upstream of the others—i.e., which answers would have to be settled before others become tractable; what criteria of importance, conceptual tractability, and potential payoff to adjacent fields (such as philosophy of psychology or comparative philosophy) govern your ordering; and what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to revise that ranking. I am less interested in a historical survey or a bibliography than in a reasoned allocation of intellectual effort: if a small research group had three years to advance our understanding of this argument's philosophical significance, where should they begin, and what should they treat as secondary?
