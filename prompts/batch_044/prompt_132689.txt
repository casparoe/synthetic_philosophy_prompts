I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I have been working through Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism—specifically the view, developed in his critique of the philosophers, that created things possess no intrinsic causal efficacy and that God alone is the direct cause of every event, with fire present at burning only as an occasion sustained by what he treats as a divine customary pattern that could in principle be suspended. I have constructed the following argument, which seems to prove that occasionalism makes empirical prediction rationally unfounded. Each premise strikes me as defensible, yet the conclusion feels too quick. I would prefer to keep the analogy between code dependencies and natural causation (step 3) and the claim that scientific laws describe real patterns rather than merely divine habits (step 6), though I am open to giving either up. Which numbered step is the weak link? Please explain why it initially looked sound, and price what abandoning it costs—particularly whether rejecting it saves occasionalism only by rendering my engineering reasoning irrational, or whether it reveals a hidden commitment in my analogy.

(1) Al-Ghazālī denies that fire, cotton, or any created thing has an intrinsic power to produce burning; God creates the burning directly in each instance, with fire present only as an occasion.

(2) He describes the regular succession of fire and burning not as a necessary causal connection but as God's customary pattern—something God could suspend at any moment without logical contradiction.

(3) In software, I treat a dependency as genuinely causal only if it is counterfactually robust: if I disable module M under controlled conditions and failure F stops occurring, then M is part of the causal chain.

(4) Applying this analogically: if occasionalism is true, then removing fire does not guarantee the absence of burning, because God could still produce burning directly. So fire lacks counterfactual robustness.

(5) Therefore, on occasionalism, no correlation among created things is counterfactually robust in the relevant sense; any robustness belongs solely to God's will.

(6) Scientific laws—such as the laws of combustion—are precisely counterfactual generalizations about what will happen when fuel, oxygen, and heat are present or absent.

(7) So if occasionalism is true, these laws do not describe causal structures among created things; they merely record the present content of God's custom, with no rational guarantee of repetition.

(8) Yet I rationally rely on exactly these laws to design and debug physical systems, for example predicting thermal failure in hardware.

(9) Therefore either occasionalism is false, or my predictive engineering practice is rationally unfounded.

(10) I reject the latter; hence occasionalism must be false.
