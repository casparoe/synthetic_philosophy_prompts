I am drafting a short essay tentatively titled “Belief Attribution to AI: Dialogical Ventriloquism Beyond the Intentional Stance,” and I would like your critical comments on the argument before I develop it further. The essay takes Dennett’s intentional stance as its point of departure but argues that applying it to large language models reveals a structural instability that the standard framing obscures. Below are my working notes; please treat them as the essay’s core rather than a polished draft.

The argument proceeds in four steps. First, I distinguish “stance-belief” — the belief we attribute when adopting a predictive, interpretive strategy — from “architectural belief,” a propositional attitude embedded in a cognitive architecture governed by coherence and rational-update constraints. Large language models clearly lack architectural belief. Second, I claim that attributing stance-beliefs to an LLM is disanalogous to attributing them to a human agent, because with humans the stance targets a single rational system whose behavior is normatively constrained, whereas with an LLM the “belief” is distributed across model weights, training corpora, and — crucially — the user’s prompt. Third, I propose the concept of “dialogical ventriloquism”: in interactive use, the user supplies the normative framework (truth-conditions, consistency requirements, contextual salience), while the model supplies syntactic and semantic form. The resulting utterance expresses something belief-like, yet it is owned neither by the model alone nor by the user alone; it belongs to the interaction system itself. This, I suggest, explains why belief attribution to AI feels more unstable than attribution to animals or infants: the ventriloquism is transparent, and we can perceive the strings being pulled. Fourth, I anticipate the objection that this is merely eliminativism about belief in general, applied selectively to machines. I reply that the divergence between stance-belief and architectural belief is systematically visible in the LLM case in a way it is not in ordinary human interaction, so the phenomenon reveals something about the conditions of belief attribution rather than eliminating belief altogether.
