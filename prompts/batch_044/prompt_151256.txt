Take the 1990 U.S. Clean Air Act Amendments—specifically the adoption of a cap-and-trade mechanism for sulfur dioxide emissions, negotiated between major environmental organizations and electric-utility interests—as a case study in gains from trade through moral compromise. Do not treat the episode as a standard cost-benefit or regulatory-economics story. Instead, frame the underlying conflict as one between a value system that treated atmospheric emission rights as non-commodifiable, carrying something like a deontological constraint against treating pollution as a tradable good, and a value system that treated emission reduction purely as an optimization problem over aggregate welfare and production costs. Analyze whether the institutional compromise generated genuine mutual gains—each side receiving something it valued that it could not have secured unilaterally—rather than a simple split-the-difference concession or the asymmetric domination of one framework by the other. In particular, examine whether the market mechanism corrupted the environmentalists’ core commitments by commodifying the air, or whether it achieved a deeper structural compromise by translating incommensurable values into a shared institutional language that both sides could endorse for different reasons. Then reverse the direction: what does the actual trajectory of the program—its measured ecological outcomes, its distributional effects across regions and firms, and the subsequent evolution of environmentalist rhetoric about market mechanisms—show about the “gains from trade” model when applied across moral frameworks? Does the episode confirm that value systems can integrate through compromise without either being hollowed out, does it reveal that such integration is always a masked victory for one system, or does it show that the model itself misdescribes the episode because the “values” in question were never stable enough to be traded? In your assessment, clearly separate what is well-established about the legislative history and the program’s empirical record from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of environmental ethics, political economy, and philosophy of value—especially regarding whether commodification necessarily corrupts the values that accept it, and whether incommensurability can ever be bridged by institutional design rather than resolved by theoretical reduction. Do not present any single interpretation of the compromise’s moral structure as the consensus view.
