I am a high school student serving as the student editor for our school's interdisciplinary journal, and I have been asked to moderate an interview with Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of biology who has recently defended an unconventional interpretation of evolutionary cooperation. Rather than treating the prisoner's dilemma as a literal model of strategic interaction among organisms, she argues that applying either the one-shot or iterated framework to biological evolution commits a category mistake: it imports rational-choice decision procedures—expected-utility calculation over future payoffs—into contexts where only differential reproductive success operates. On her view, what evolutionary biologists call cooperation in bacterial quorum sensing or cleaner-fish mutualism is not the resolution of a dilemma through reciprocity, but a form of structural coupling in which interdependence is the default state and defection is metabolically impossible rather than strategically costly. She further claims that the celebrated success of Tit-for-Tat in iterated tournaments tells us nothing about evolution, because those tournaments presuppose agents who can represent future rounds and adjust strategies accordingly—capacities that have no analog in non-cognitive selection dynamics.

Given that she anticipates the standard reply—that evolutionary game theory is only a heuristic model, not a psychological claim—I would like you to draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that do not simply ask her to restate this position. I want at least two questions that press her on how she distinguishes genuine strategic reciprocity from mere temporal correlation in iterated biological interactions; one question on whether her critique undermines Axelrod's evolutionary tournaments altogether or only restricts their domain of application; and one question that asks what empirical finding—say, in microbial ecology or evolutionary robotics—would actually count against her structural-coupling account. For each question, please include a brief follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: for instance, if she invokes metabolic impossibility, ask how she accounts for parasitic lineages that exploit mutualistic structures without contributing; if she dismisses tournaments as heuristics, ask what normative work, if any, remains for game-theoretic reasoning in evolutionary explanation. The tone should remain respectful but intellectually rigorous, suitable for publication in a student-run journal.
