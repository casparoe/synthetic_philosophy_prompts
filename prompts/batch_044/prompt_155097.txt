For a graduate historiography seminar of twelve students meeting for ninety minutes, design an in-class deliberation game that makes participants experience why the macro-periods 'Middle Ages,' 'Renaissance,' and 'Scientific Revolution' may be retrospective constructions rather than discovered discontinuities. The facilitator should provide no up-front lecture and should not reveal the constructedness thesis until the debrief.

Structure the session in three phases.

Phase One (25 minutes): Divide students into four groups of three. Give each group an identical packet of ten short, anonymized primary-source excerpts, described generically rather than attributed to specific named authors—for example, a twelfth-century Latin commentary on natural motion that also cites an Arabic observational table; a fifteenth-century mercantile contract that employs humanist rhetorical framing; a seventeenth-century court record describing an astronomical dispute resolved by appeal to both scriptural and mechanical arguments; a fourteenth-century Italian legal document preserving scholastic logical distinctions alongside vernacular poetic form. Require each group to sort the excerpts into exactly three chronological bins corresponding to the conventional triad, using only internal textual evidence (language, concepts, institutional references). Award points for internal consistency and for the ability to defend exclusions, but impose a collective penalty if any excerpt is left out or if two groups place the same excerpt in different bins without being able to articulate the criterion that justifies the split.

Phase Two (20 minutes): Reveal the actual provenance and approximate dates of the excerpts, then reassign each group a single sorting criterion—technological practice, institutional setting, or conceptual vocabulary. Have them re-sort the same packet under this constraint, now permitted to split excerpts across bins if the criterion demands it. The scoring shifts: coherence within the assigned criterion is rewarded, but cross-group agreement is no longer required.

Phase Three (15 minutes): A forced-choice vote. Each student must individually defend, in one minute, whether their assigned criterion in Phase Two revealed a real boundary or merely projected one onto continuous material. The facilitator records the distribution of answers but does not adjudicate.

The debrief (30 minutes) should map Phase One onto naive chronological realism, Phase Two onto the dependence of periodization on the question being asked, and the forced-choice vote onto the difference between heuristic utility and ontological commitment. Be explicit about which feature of macro-periodization the activity cannot reproduce—namely, long-duration structural patterns such as demographic cycles, linguistic drift, and institutional persistence that might justify broad labels even when local evidence blurs them—and explain how the facilitator must supply this limitation verbally rather than expecting the game to generate it.

Predict what will go wrong: students will likely anchor on technological markers and treat institutional or conceptual continuity as noise; the facilitator should have a prepared counter-example (for instance, the persistence of university curricula across conventional Renaissance boundaries) to deploy only if the anchoring becomes totalizing, not as a lecture point.

Do not ask participants to cite historians, do not provide a reading list, and do not depend on recognition of specific named works in the packet descriptions. The design should work for students who have only general familiarity with the conventional narrative of Western European intellectual history.
