At an interdisciplinary seminar on value alignment, two researchers—Dr. Chen and Dr. Patel—disagreed about whether inverse reinforcement learning can genuinely recover human preferences when people use self-binding mechanisms. Their exchange went as follows.

(1) Chen argued that IRL is essentially revealed-preference inference: if a person installs an app-blocker but repeatedly disables it to scroll social media, the disabling behavior reveals the stronger preference, and any verbal claim about wanting to focus is cheap talk that should be down-weighted.

(2) Patel objected that this misses the logic of commitment devices. People set up blockers precisely because they anticipate that their future, impulsive behavior will diverge from their presently endorsed goals. The blocker itself, together with the explicit pre-commitment, is behavioral evidence of a second-order preference for self-control; treating the later disabling as equally authoritative as the installation erases the very distinction that makes the device intelligible.

(3) Chen replied that Patel is importing a normative theory of autonomy that IRL cannot access from the outside. To an observer, both installing and disabling are just actions in a trajectory. Privileging one as meta-preference requires an independent criterion for authentic endorsement, which makes the inference circular: you need to know the true preference already to decide which behavior reveals it.

(4) Patel countered that no deep normative theory is necessary. Self-binding has observable structural signatures—upfront cost, irreversibility, and an explicit framing statement made at the time of installation. An IRL framework can treat actions bearing these signatures as indicators of a hierarchical preference structure without assuming a full account of autonomy. The inference remains behavioral, just structured.

(5) Chen responded that this only pushes the ambiguity upward. Those structural signatures identify a commitment device, but they do not establish that the agent still endorses it at the moment of disabling. People abandon devices; leftover blockers become dead habits. Without direct access to the agent's current endorsement—which pure behavioral inference cannot provide—Patel's structural filter cannot distinguish an abandoned commitment from an active one being violated. So the problem of circularity or ambiguity returns at the meta-level.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify any false dichotomy or missing option they both overlook, and explain what feature of this particular case—rather than of IRL in general—does the philosophical work.
