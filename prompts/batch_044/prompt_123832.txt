In my graduate seminar on collective memory and political legitimacy, we have spent the past month examining what I call “defeat narratives”—national myths constructed around failed or pyrrhic struggles such as the Confederacy, Masada, the Battle of Kosovo, and the evacuation at Dunkirk. The discussion has reached an impasse that strikes me as philosophically interesting rather than merely historical. One faction of students argues that these myths are structurally pernicious: they convert military failure into moral purity by framing defeat as proof of exceptional virtue or victimhood, thereby licensing revisionist politics and suppressing accountability for the original cause. The opposing faction contends that defeat narratives serve an irreplaceable civic function, providing societies with shared frameworks of resilience, sacrifice, and continuity that do not depend on the justice of the original conflict; they point out that Dunkirk is commemorated not to glorify imperial war but to celebrate collective endurance, and that Masada functions in modern identity less as a call to suicide than as a boundary-marker for sovereignty.

Despite these positions being articulated with increasing precision, neither side has modified its core commitment in any appreciable way. I am inclined to think the stall is not merely empirical—both sides concede the historical details—but rather involves a hidden disagreement about what counts as a “myth” in the normative sense: whether it is essentially a false belief that requires debunking, or a symbolic resource whose ethical status depends entirely on present deployment. Yet I wonder whether there is also a deeper disagreement about the ontology of collective identity itself—whether a nation is the kind of entity that can be harmed by the erasure of a myth, or whether myths are merely instrumental tools that can be swapped without moral loss. Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two different normative projects—perhaps one about historical truth and one about social solidarity—being pursued under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what kind of argument or conceptual distinction, if any, would actually move the debate rather than simply restating the opposing intuitions?
