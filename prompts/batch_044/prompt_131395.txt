I was arguing with a friend last night about whether The Stranger ultimately sides with the executioner or with the executed. She insisted that Meursault's final acceptance—his feeling that the world greets him with a gentle, indifferent calm—amounts to a kind of existential consent to the guillotine, as though the novel vindicates capital punishment by showing the condemned man reach clarity through it. I think she is wrong, and I want to read the novel through Camus's actual arguments against the death penalty, especially the reasoning he developed in his essay comparing state execution to premeditated murder and criticizing the bureaucratic secrecy and moral theater of judicial killing. But I do not want a simple reading that says Camus was against it, therefore Meursault is merely a victim.

Specifically, I am struck by the way the trial in Part Two displaces the killing itself and makes Meursault's emotional failures—failing to weep at his mother's funeral, attending a film the next day—the central evidence for his guilt. Could that structural move be read as dramatizing exactly what Camus criticized: that capital punishment is rarely about the act committed and more about the state's need to stage a moral spectacle? At the same time, Meursault's crime is spontaneous, almost accidental, driven by heat and the glare of light on a blade; the essay emphasizes that state killing is coldly premeditated, planned for months. Does the contrast between the unplanned murder and the meticulously planned execution illuminate the novel's view of justice, or does it expose a tension in Camus's philosophy—between the absurd, unmotivated act and the rationalized violence of the law? I would like a reading that treats both the novel and the anti-death-penalty argument seriously, points to specific scenes—the trial's rhetoric, Meursault's refusal to perform remorse, the chaplain's visits, the machinery of execution itself—to show where the philosophical lens holds and where the work resists it, and says whether The Stranger ultimately adds something to the debate about capital punishment that philosophical argument alone cannot capture. I am preparing this for a discussion with friends who have read the novel but not the essay, so please do not ask for outside citations or a reading list; just work with what is in the text and in the philosophical position itself.
