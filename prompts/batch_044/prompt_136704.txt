I am designing the final exam for an advanced seminar in moral philosophy, with a unit on contractualist ethics. I want one essay question that cannot be answered by summarizing Scanlon's well-known rejection of utilitarian aggregation. Instead, the question should present a novel extension—call it intertemporal contractualism—that applies the reasonableness test across temporal stages of a single life: can a present stage reasonably reject a principle that permits present burdens in exchange for substantial future benefits, acting as proxy for its future stage? The prompt should require students to determine whether this introduces a hidden aggregation—this time within one life rather than across lives—and whether Scanlon's framework can block it without smuggling in prioritarian weights or an independent theory of diachronic personal identity. Please provide: (a) the exam question, calibrated for a 75-minute response of roughly 700 words; (b) a model answer that distinguishes interpersonal from intrapersonal aggregation, explains why the rescue-case logic does not straightforwardly transfer to intra-life trade-offs, assesses whether reasonable-rejectability is being conflated with the strength of reasons, and delivers a reasoned verdict on consistency; and (c) a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific moves each must contain—such as correctly locating the anti-aggregationist commitment in the contractualist test itself rather than in a theory of the good, identifying the hidden work done by continuity assumptions, and addressing whether proxy-representation replicates the very summation Scanlon rejects—together with the common errors that should reduce marks.
