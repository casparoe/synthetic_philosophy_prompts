I am a novelist revising a manuscript in which identity is treated as assembled rather than given, and I have been returning to Robert Louis Stevenson’s Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde as an unexpected precursor to that idea. I want a philosophical reading of the novella through Buddhist anatta—not the popular “no soul” shorthand, but the Abhidharma analysis of the five skandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness) and the Theravada view that karma attaches to a stream of impermanent aggregates rather than to any enduring owner. Please read the transformation scenes, Jekyll’s final confession, and the relationship between Jekyll’s scientific vocabulary and Hyde’s physical description: does the text treat the two figures as conventional labels for one underlying substance that merely chooses between moral poles, or does it accidentally dramatize personal identity dissolving into discrete, ownerless mental and bodily events?

I also want you to attend to the tension between Stevenson’s Gothic machinery and the metaphysics. Where does the Christian moral framework—especially the implication that Jekyll remains responsible for Hyde’s crimes—depend on a persistent agent that anatta denies? Do the laboratory apparatus and the portrait function like the skandhas, holding together an illusion of continuity across states? And where does the novella actively resist this reading—perhaps by requiring a hidden “true self” in order for the horror to land? Give me a reading that takes both the text and the Buddhist philosophy seriously, says honestly where the work undermines its own need for an enduring soul, and indicates what Stevenson’s novella adds to the philosophical debate about identity that argument alone does not capture.
