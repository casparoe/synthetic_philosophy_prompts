I am serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-division seminar in philosophy of mind, and I have been asked to calibrate my grading for a take-home exam on Daniel Dennett’s framework of explanatory stances. The assignment was designed to push students past textbook repetition and into application, specifically asking them to evaluate whether predictive success with an artificial system licenses belief-attribution in Dennett’s terms. I have one submission that reads as polished and conceptually fluent, yet I suspect it slides past a few subtle missteps that a hurried grader might reward rather than penalize, so I want an external point-by-point assessment before I finalize my rubric.

The exam question was: “Dennett distinguishes the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance. A research team reports that their large language model—call it Ariadne—produces accurate, non-obvious predictions about quantum experiments that are not straightforward derivations from its training corpus. They argue that treating Ariadne as literally believing propositions about superposition and entanglement improves predictive accuracy over both the physical and design stances, and therefore that the intentional stance is not only useful but ontologically appropriate here. Evaluate this argument. In particular, discuss whether predictive success alone licenses belief-attribution within Dennett’s framework, address the relationship between the design stance and the intentional stance in this case, and consider whether any conditions beyond predictive utility—such as evolutionary history, rational coherence, or the ineliminability of the stance—are required by Dennett for genuine belief.”

One student submitted the following answer: “Dennett proposes three stances for explaining system behavior. The physical stance uses physics and chemistry; the design stance uses the system’s programmed function or architecture; and the intentional stance treats the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires that cause its behavior. To take the intentional stance toward Ariadne, we need only show that attributing beliefs yields better predictions than the other stances. The research group demonstrates this in conditions (1) through (3): the predictions are accurate, not derivable from training data, and belief-attribution improves forecasting. So the argument succeeds in justifying the intentional stance.

However, Dennett also requires that attributed beliefs form a rationally coherent network and that the system possess an evolutionary history that grounds its representations—conditions he developed in collaboration with Ruth Millikan’s teleosemantic theory. Because Ariadne was engineered rather than evolved, it lacks the proper causal history for real representational content. Therefore, although the predictive argument works heuristically, it fails to establish genuine belief. We should treat Ariadne’s quantum ‘beliefs’ as convenient fictions, much like we treat the beliefs of chess computers, because the design stance can ultimately explain its outputs through weights and training dynamics. The argument confuses predictive utility with ontological commitment, but since Dennett demands both evolutionary grounding and rational coherence, the conclusion should be that Ariadne does not really believe anything.”

Please grade this answer as if you were the instructor or an external examiner. Assign a specific letter or numerical grade, and then justify it with precise, point-by-point comments that identify exactly what the student gets right, where there is a subtle conflation or misattributed view, and whether any inferential step does not actually follow from Dennett’s position. Do not signal your own philosophical agreement or disagreement with the student’s conclusion; the deliverable should be an assessment of the answer’s fidelity to the theory, not a defense of an independent stance. Specifically flag any place where the student imports a condition—such as evolutionary grounding, rational coherence as a precondition, or a sharp ontological divide between heuristic fiction and real pattern—that belongs to a different philosopher (for instance, Millikan’s biosemantics or Davidson’s principle of charity) and misattributes it to Dennett.
