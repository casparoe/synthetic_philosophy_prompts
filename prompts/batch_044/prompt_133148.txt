I'm a phd student in philosphy working on population ethics and I'm kind of stuck. Everyone in my seminar knows the standard repugnant-conclusion moves — totalism, averageism, person-affecting restrictions, critical-level utilitarianism — but we've hit this weird stall around what I'm calling "scale-sensitive aggregation." The thought is that welfare might aggregate linearly in small groups but hit a diminsihing-returns wall at massive scale, not from averaging but because the metric itself gets distorted by density or dilution. One side says this is just empirical resource economics in disguise; the other says it's a normative claim about the shape of value being non-archimedean, and treating it empirically misses the point entirely. We've argued for months with zero movement. Is this actually a substantive disagreement about whether wellbeing is comparable across population sizes, or are we just talking past each other with "aggregation" as a cover for bedrock intuitions? And if it's real, what kind of result — formal, conceptual, or empirical — could break the impasse? I don't need a survey; I want the diagnosis.
