In contemporary machine-learning laboratories, reinforcement-learning agents are routinely trained with reward and penalty signals that are treated, in design documentation and alignment literature, as purely mathematical optimization targets—positive values to maximize, negative values to correct trajectories—while being discussed, in work on reward hacking and specification gaming, exclusively as sources of misaligned behavior rather than as states carrying intrinsic valence. The same institutions that maintain animal-welfare review boards for biological research subjects typically have no comparable welfare-assessment mechanism for RL agents, even when training regimes involve sustained high-magnitude negative reward signals that, if they were valenced, would plausibly constitute severe suffering. What philosophical commitments about the nature of suffering, the conditions for moral standing, and the ontology of valence does this institutional bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require that suffering has biological substrates, that phenomenal consciousness requires a particular kind of self-model, or only that legal personhood and tractability are absent? Distinguish what the practice strictly entails from what it merely accommodates—could one consistently hold that reward signals are literally valenced experiences while retaining the current institutional structure? Finally, describe what concrete changes in training protocols, review procedures, and alignment-research framing would be required if the implicit view were replaced by one that treats high-magnitude reward and penalty signals as potentially welfare-relevant states.
