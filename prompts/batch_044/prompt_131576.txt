I keep hearing that welfare benefits should work like an insurance policy: everyone pays premiums through taxes, and you only claim after a genuine, unforeseen loss such as unemployment or disability. The conditions — workfare requirements, fraud checks, time limits — are said to be like an insurer investigating arson or a driver speeding because coverage makes them reckless. This is used to justify treating some recipients as undeserving and to argue that unconditional aid creates moral hazard.

Can you audit this analogy precisely? Provide the correspondence table: what corresponds to the premium, the insurer, the claim, the covered peril, the fraud investigator, and the reckless driver? Then show where the mapping fails: is unemployment an accidental loss or a structural feature? Does the state behave like a profit-seeking firm that can deny valid claims? Does the analogy quietly assume poverty is an individual failure rather than a distributed risk? I am also struck by a specific structural point: in actual social insurance, contributions are progressive, not actuarial — a high earner pays far more without receiving larger benefits. Does this break the insurance comparison, or can it be reconciled? Finally, once these disanalogies are laid out, does the conclusion that strict conditionality is justified survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along?
