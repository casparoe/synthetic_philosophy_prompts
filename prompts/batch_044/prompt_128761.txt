I have been trying to understand why the Löbian obstacle is treated as a surmountable design challenge rather than as an absolute barrier to self-trusting artificial agents, and I suspect my reasoning contains a hidden mistake. My understanding is that Löb's theorem establishes that, for any formal system S extending Peano arithmetic that can represent provability, if S derives the schema that provability implies truth—i.e., if S proves □φ → φ—then S proves every sentence φ, which for a consistent system is impossible. Consequently, Gödel's second incompleteness theorem prevents S from proving its own consistency, and the Löbian result strengthens this by showing that even a uniform trust in one's own soundness collapses into triviality. From this I have inferred that any agent with sufficient representational capacity to model its own reasoning must either be logically inconsistent or must withhold full endorsement of its own inference rules; flat, reflective architectures that lack an explicit hierarchical separation between object-level reasoning and meta-level evaluation seem therefore to be not merely risky but conceptually incoherent.

Yet in discussions of reflective stability and self-modifying agents, I encounter proposals that appear to embrace precisely such flat architectures—systems that revise their own decision procedures without an ordinal-ranked stack of meta-levels, apparently assuming that bounded rationality or probabilistic credence can circumvent the obstacle. This puzzles me: if the Löbian argument is a matter of formal derivability rather than empirical overconfidence, then weakening one's credence from certainty to 0.99 or distributing reasoning across submodules should not dissolve the contradiction, since the theorem concerns the presence of the derivability condition itself, not its strength. So is the standard resolution that probabilistic agents fall outside the theorem's scope because they do not satisfy the formal conditions on □, or is my error the assumption that an epistemic norm of self-trust must be encoded as the exact Löbian schema? I would appreciate a clarification of where the analogy between formal self-reference and practical reflective reasoning breaks down, and whether there is a coherent interpretation of 'reflective stability' that does not implicitly violate the conditions of Löb's theorem.
