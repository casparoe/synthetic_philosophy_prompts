As a journalist covering political philosophy and the ethics of statecraft, I am examining Winston Churchill's 1941 decision to ally Britain with Stalin's Soviet Union against Nazi Germany—not as strategic history, but as a disciplined case study in what philosophers call the problem of dirty hands. I want to push the analysis past the familiar framing of "necessary evil" by introducing a conceptual distinction that is rarely applied to this episode: between structural complicity, in which the alliance itself embeds a democratic state within a totalitarian moral architecture regardless of its leaders' intentions, and transactional complicity, in which discrete acts—sharing Ultra intelligence, coordinating naval operations, or supplying Lend-Lease materiel—generate specific, separable moral debts. Analyze the Anglo-Soviet pact through this lens: which elements of Churchill's cooperation were unavoidable structural entanglement, and which were discretionary transactions that accumulated moral residue? Then assess whether the standard dirty-hands framework—particularly as developed in the Walzerian tradition—actually preserves this distinction, or whether it collapses both into a single, undifferentiated "moral cost" that obscures the difference between being trapped in an institution and actively feeding it. What does the case reveal about whether dirty-hands theory needs an institutional dimension—something like "moral architecture"—that it currently lacks, and does Churchill's position as an elected leader allied to a totalitarian partner make the structural-transactional boundary harder to maintain than the theory assumes? I am not asking for a bibliography or a review of the literature; I want a rigorous mapping of the theory onto this specific historical arrangement, with an honest account of where the mapping fails and what failure tells us about the framework itself.
