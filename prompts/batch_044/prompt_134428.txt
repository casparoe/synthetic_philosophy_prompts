After a long argument with a friend about whether mental properties can be genuinely causally efficacious without being rendered epiphenomenal, I want to press a comparison that we could not settle. Compare Kim's causal exclusion argument—the claim that if every mental event has a sufficient physical cause and physical causation is closed under the physical, then mental properties are systematically excluded from causal work—with the determinable/determinable defense, according to which a mental property and its physical realizer are not competing causes but stand in a determinable-to-determinate relation (as crimson stands to scarlet), so that attributing causation to the mental property is attributing it at a different level of determinacy rather than overdetermining the effect. Do not merely summarize; evaluate how the two frameworks handle a case I am constructing specifically to stress them: imagine a mental property M, being in a state of cognitive dissonance, that is multiply realized by two physically disjoint neural configurations, N1 and N2, with no unified physical determinable except a highly disjunctive property D. In this scenario, does the determinable/determinable defense collapse because the physical side lacks a single causally unified determinable, or does Kim's exclusion argument actually gain force, since D is causally inert compared to N1 and N2 individually? Further, which view better preserves downward mental causation without violating causal closure, and where does each pay its metaphysical price—in accepting widespread causal overdetermination, in reconceiving mental properties as causally redundant determinables, or in abandoning closure altogether? I want the comparison to take both the structure of the exclusion problem and the determinable/determinable ontology seriously, without appealing to empirical neuroscience, asking for further reading, or treating the mental property as a mere placeholder.
