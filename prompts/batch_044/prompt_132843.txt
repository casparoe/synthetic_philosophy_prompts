I am in the middle of drafting a novel about a travel essayist who spends each season in a different country, and lately my writing group has become obsessed with a question that keeps bleeding into the manuscript: when a visitor steps into a place with its own unwritten choreography—whether it is the precise arithmetic of tipping in a Tokyo taxi, the silent geometry of queuing in London, or the negotiated anarchy of jaywalking in Hanoi—does she acquire a moral obligation to perform the local script, or is she merely playing at courtesy?

We have argued this for months without resolution. One faction holds that conventions are coordination devices with moral weight only for those embedded in the practice; a visitor, lacking the reciprocal history and the shared vulnerability of membership, owes only non-maleficence, not deference to the choreography itself. The other faction insists that the harm of non-compliance is not distributed differently for outsiders—the queue still collapses, the server still suffers, the pedestrian flow still fractures—so the obligation attaches to the act, not the identity of the actor. We agree on the sociology, we agree that suffering is bad, and yet neither side has moved. I have begun to wonder whether we are not arguing about morality at all, but about two different pictures of what a social world is: one in which rules are contracts among insiders, and one in which rules are environmental conditions that anyone present must navigate with care. I have tried to sharpen the disagreement by introducing what I call protocol morality versus hospitality morality—the idea that some obligations bind us because we have agreed to the game, while others bind us because we have entered someone else's stage—but I am unsure whether this distinction resolves anything or merely renames the stalemate.

So I want to ask: is this impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under the single heading of moral obligation? Are we divided by hidden empirical assumptions about how much damage an ignorant visitor actually causes, or by bedrock intuitions about belonging and trespass that neither side can argue for? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually count as progress—a conceptual distinction, a thought experiment, an empirical study of harm, or the admission that the question itself is malformed? I am asking not for a verdict on tipping or queuing, but for a diagnosis of the structure of our disagreement, supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether anything could move it.
