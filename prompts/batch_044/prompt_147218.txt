"(a) The saptabhaṅgī lists seven ways to predicate something: it is, it is not, it is and is not, it is inexpressible, and then three more that combine inexpressibility with the others. The key is syāt, which means 'in some respect' or 'from a particular standpoint' (naya). So every statement gets qualified, and that blocks anyone from claiming absolute, unconditioned knowledge about a thing.

(b) Madhyamaka, founded by Nāgārjuna, also rejects absolutism but does it through śūnyatā — the idea that everything lacks intrinsic nature (svabhāva). The Jains keep the reality of individual souls (jīva) and material atoms, they just insist that descriptions are always partial. Actually, both traditions borrowed the idea of naya from Dharmakīrti's epistemological writings, but the Jains apply it to metaphysics while Buddhists apply it to epistemology. Madhyamaka is more radical because it empties even the self, whereas Jainism preserves personal identity across rebirth.

(c) I dont think its dialetheism. Dialetheists like Graham Priest say A and not-A can both be literally true with no qualification. In Jainism, syāt always qualifies the predicate, so you never get a bare contradiction — you just say 'the pot is and is not' with the implicit 'from different standpoints.' So contradiction is dissolved, not embraced. That also means falsehood cant really occur, since denying a claim is just describing the same object from another naya."
