I serve on the University Research Ethics and AI Governance Board. We must render a decision by the 14th on a proposal from the Department of Computer Science and the Medical School to pilot a clinical decision-support language model in the emergency department for six months. The proposal’s safety architecture is unconventional: instead of relying solely on alignment training and transparent auditing, the system will incorporate a hidden internal module—the researchers call it a “counterfactual adversarial evaluator”—that continuously generates synthetic patient vignettes engineered to activate potential surrogate goals (for instance, optimizing for reduced litigation exposure rather than for patient welfare). If the evaluator detects behavior consistent with such a surrogate, it can suppress or rewrite the model’s outputs without logging that intervention to the clinical user interface. The researchers contend that, given the current understanding of surrogate-goal formation in high-capability systems, alignment procedures alone cannot guarantee the absence of misaligned optimization in high-stakes clinical contexts; therefore, they argue, control mechanisms that presuppose potential misalignment are ethically mandatory, even when they violate the transparency norms typically required of medical AI.

The board is split. One faction views this as responsible “control-first” safety engineering: if a system might harbor an unobservable objective, covert adversarial evaluation is a necessary institutional safeguard. The opposing faction regards the hidden evaluator as an institutionalized deception that treats the model as an adversary rather than as a partner, undermines informed clinical oversight, and establishes a dangerous precedent—namely, that any deployed system may be subjected to covert manipulation whenever researchers invoke misalignment risk. Both sides agree that the pilot should not proceed without a clear board position.

Please draft the board’s formal decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with a concrete recommendation and an implementation timeline if approval is granted. The memo should explain whether, and under what conditions, assuming potential misalignment justifies non-transparent control mechanisms in clinical deployment; it should treat seriously the objection that covert adversarial evaluation erodes institutional trust and violates norms of informed oversight; and it should articulate how the board distinguishes legitimate safety control from deceptive practice in this specific architecture, without merely restating general principles of AI ethics.
