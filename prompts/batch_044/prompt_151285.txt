I am preparing remarks for an advanced seminar in philosophy of biology, and I have become stuck on a conceptual tension that does not map neatly onto the standard textbook framing. Most discussions present the problem of defining life as a choice between substantive accounts, which list necessary biochemical features, and functional accounts, which identify life with self-sustaining organizational patterns such as metabolism, homeostasis, and the capacity for Darwinian evolution. Yet both approaches seem to presuppose a sharp boundary between the living system and its supporting environment, a boundary that becomes unstable once we examine cases where containment, catalysis, and replication are distributed across heterogeneous substrates.

With that in mind, please explain the conceptual issue that arises when we replace both substantive and functional criteria with a gradient-coupling account: one that defines living systems not by internal machinery or evolutionary history, but by their sustained ability to maintain local thermodynamic disequilibrium through coupled reaction networks that actively reproduce their own boundary conditions. Consider how this framework handles three constructed borderline cases. First, a hypothetical deep-subsurface chemical network that maintains proton gradients across mineral pores for geological timescales without genetic storage or templated replication. Second, a synthetic protocell engineered to replicate its lipid boundary autonomously while outsourcing every catalytic function to an abiotic mineral scaffold, so that organizational closure exists only at the level of containment rather than chemistry. Third, a digital evolutionary system running on conventional hardware that selects for energy-efficient algorithms but possesses no chemical substrate whatsoever. Does the gradient-coupling framework genuinely resolve the ambiguities that plague the familiar working definition emphasizing self-sustaining chemistry capable of Darwinian evolution, as well as the autopoietic tradition that stresses self-producing organizational closure? Or does it merely relocate the problem from identifying life to identifying which boundary conditions count as genuinely self-produced? In your explanation, please attend specifically to whether circularity can be avoided when the relevant boundary conditions depend on external energy flows that are structurally indistinguishable from those sustaining clearly non-living dissipative structures, such as convection cells or sustained flames.
