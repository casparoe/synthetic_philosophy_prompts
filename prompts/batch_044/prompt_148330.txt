I run a philosophy of religion seiminar with about 14 grad students, one 75-minute session. We're working through Jain anekāntavāda and syādvāda, but I'm tired of the blind-men-and-elephant analogy — I want them to actually feel the pressure that forces the "syāt" qualifier, that contradictory predicates can all be genuinely true of the same thing, not just that we're epistemically limited. Design a runnable in-class game with clear rules, materials I can prep in ten minutes, and some small symbolic stakes (no real money). The core idea should hit them through what they do and bet, not through me explaining it afterwards.

Here's the framing I want you to inject: invent a fictional sealed "Meridian Object" hidden in a box with seven different access ports. Split the class into seven pairs; each pair gets one envelope describing what their port reveals — the descriptions should be internally coherent but flatly contradictory across pairs (e.g., one envelope proves the Object is eternal and singular, another that it is temporal and plural, another that it is conscious, another insentient, another harmful, another beneficial). Give each pair 8 minutes to examine their envelope and then write exactly one absolute claim on the board using the form "The Object IS..." or "The Object IS NOT..." — they are banned from hedge words like "maybe," "from our view," or "it seems." Then run a 20-minute disputation round: pairs defend their claim and wager "credability chips" (everyone starts with 5) on whether their description is the sole correct one. The pair with the most chips at the end symbolically gets to "name" the Object for the rest of the course. Make the envelopes convincing enough that people actually get angry defending them.

Then map the debrief for me: which moment in the game corresponds to ekānta (absolutist predication), which to attachment to one's naya (standpoint), and which to the shock of anekānta. Tell me what will predictably go wrong — I imagine someone will try to invent a hidden synthesis or say "they're all just wrong" — and how to block that move without me lecturing. Also be frank about what feature of Jain metaphysics this activity can't reproduce on its own: I think it's the claim that contradictions literally inhere in the object rather than just reflecting our limited access, so I'll need to supply that part verbally. Don't give me bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure texts; just the activity design, the timeline, and an honest note on its limits.
