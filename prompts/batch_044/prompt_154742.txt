I am currently taking an advanced course in bioethics, and during a recent seminar on reproductive justice my classmates and I became sharply divided over whether strict European prohibitions on commercial surrogacy genuinely protect gestational carriers or merely displace risk elsewhere. After considerable reflection, I have come to hold a somewhat counter-intuitive view, which I should like to develop into a disciplined philosophical argument rather than an intuitive reaction. Specifically, I wish to defend the position that the European prohibitionist model does not eliminate the commodification of reproductive labour but instead institutionalises what might be termed gestational risk arbitrage: by forbidding regulated domestic markets, the ban compels intended parents and intermediaries to seek arrangements in jurisdictions with weaker labour protections, lower compensation standards, and minimal oversight, thereby exporting exploitation rather than preventing it. On this account, the American market—despite its well-documented inequities—at least renders the transaction visible, taxable, and subject to contract law and tort remedy, whereas the European approach generates a concealed two-tier regime in which affluent Europeans access surrogacy abroad while domestic statute pretends the practice does not occur within its borders.

Could you please construct a formal argument in favour of this position? I am particularly interested in how one might answer the standard Kantian objection that any market in gestation necessarily treats the carrier as a mere means, and whether the arbitrage critique undermines that objection or only complicates its application. In addition, I should welcome your thoughts on whether the visibility and regulatory oversight available within a domestic, legally recognised market could, in principle, be structured so as to respect the autonomy and bodily integrity of carriers without collapsing into pure commodification. Please write your response in British English, employing appropriate ethical terminology, but do not depend upon specific statutory citations, case law, or reading lists that I would be unable to verify without access to legal databases.
