Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for a philosophy-of-science graduate seminar that sorts respondents by what they actually commit to regarding reduction and emergence, rather than by the labels they prefer. The instrument should begin with the premise that macro-level scientific explanations are sometimes genuinely indispensable, then branch through exactly five diagnostic nodes, each posed as a concrete question about a specific scientific scenario—such as whether the global stability of a gene-regulatory network can be fully derived from the chemical kinetics of individual transcription factors alone; whether collective navigation in a bird flock involves downward selection constraints on individual flight rules rather than direct causal energy transfer from the flock level; and whether thermodynamic properties like temperature should be treated as merely epistemic conveniences over microphysical states.

At each node, offer two or three answer options that do not presuppose knowledge of the named positions. The tree should split on: (1) whether macro-indispensability is purely practical or epistemic, or genuinely ontological; (2) if ontological, whether macro-properties possess causal autonomy that is not derivative from micro-level dynamics, specifically allowing for constraint-based downward causation without requiring direct inter-level energy transfer; (3) whether accepting such macro-causation violates the causal closure of the micro-physical domain; (4) whether emergence is restricted to specific domains (for instance, evolutionary or biological systems with selection dynamics) or applies whenever there is scale separation; and (5) whether one can consistently maintain that reduction succeeds in some domains while failing in others, or whether reductionism must be global.

The leaves must include: strict eliminative reductionism; weak or pragmatic emergence that denies ontological novelty; non-reductive physicalism that accepts downward causation through selection or constraint; strong ontological emergentism that rejects micro-closure; pluralist or domain-relative reductionism; and at least one combination marked unnamed with a brief diagnosis of why it is unoccupied (e.g., incoherent, unstable, or unexplored). Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns to leaves, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves—particularly weak emergence and pluralist reductionism—that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining the overlap.

The audience is graduate students; keep the instrument to five nodes and no more than eight leaves, and ensure every branch question can be answered without already knowing which interpretation it leads to.
