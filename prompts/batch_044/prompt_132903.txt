I was arguing with a colleague from a rationalist forum about whether strategy-proofness is a sensible design criterion for real-world collective choice, and he offered me the following summary of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result. He said that the theorem proves any voting rule allowing people to rank alternatives over a set of three or more options is necessarily vulnerable to strategic manipulation unless the rule is dictatorial—that is, unless one particular voter’s ranking determines the outcome regardless of what anyone else reports. From this he concluded that genuinely democratic, non-manipulable voting is literally impossible in principle, and therefore that rational agents in democratic elections should generally misrepresent their true preferences rather than vote sincerely. He added that the only escape is to restrict the domain of admissible preference profiles, for instance by assuming single-peaked preferences, but that this merely changes the subject by ruling out the preference configurations that make manipulation interesting in the first place.

Something about this feels like it overreaches, but I am not fluent enough in social-choice theory to locate the exact slippage. I want to know, going through his claims in order, which parts correspond to the actual theorem and which are overextensions: does the result apply to any collective decision procedure, or only to ordinal voting rules satisfying universal domain and a specific independence condition? Is “dictatorial” being used in its precise formal sense, and does that sense justify the rhetorical claim about democracy being impossible? Does the theorem itself imply that rational actors should misrepresent preferences, or is that a separate game-theoretic inference? And does restricting preferences to a single-peaked domain genuinely restore non-dictatorial strategy-proofness, or does it just replace one impossibility with a narrower possibility? Please audit the summary sentence by sentence, separating the accurate core from the distortions, and tell me whether the sweeping conclusion about democratic voting survives once the conditions are stated precisely and the misrepresentations are corrected.
