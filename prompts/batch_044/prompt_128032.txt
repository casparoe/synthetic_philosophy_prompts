As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand why the debate over prioritarianism and the so-called leveling-down objection seems to have reached a permanent impasse. One side holds that the moral value of benefiting someone is weighted more heavily the worse off that person is; the other side argues that if this view were correct, then reducing the welfare of those who are well off—without benefiting anyone else, merely to diminish inequality—would be “better in one respect,” which they treat as an absurdity revealing a hidden intrinsic commitment to equality. The defenders reply that values can be plural: an act may be better in one respect yet worse all things considered, and that the objection illegitimately collapses parts into wholes. Reading these exchanges, I am struck that both camps speak of “weighting” welfare as if it were analogous to assigning priorities in a distributed load-balancing algorithm, yet they appear to presuppose incompatible formal properties. One treats the weighting as a continuous convex function over welfare levels; the other treats it as generating a discontinuous or lexical ordering whenever no actual redistribution occurs. I therefore wonder whether the stall is genuinely normative, or whether it is partly a verbal or formal dispute about what an aggregation function must satisfy—continuity, separability, non-comparability across individuals—disguised as an argument about equality itself.

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it a hidden clash about the mathematical properties of moral aggregation, a difference in bedrock intuitions about whether equality has intrinsic value, or two distinct projects—one about distributive justice, one about the coherence of plural-value reasoning—running under a single heading? Please support your diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says rather than surveying interpretations, and tell me whether anything—such as a precise formalisation of the weighting function, a conceptual distinction, or an explicit change of question—would actually count as progress, or whether the impasse should be accepted as irreducible.
