I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent some time thinking on my own about the intuition that we are only genuinely responsible for what we do if we genuinely could have done otherwise — and about the puzzling counterexample in which a hidden mechanism stands ready to force a person toward one choice should they try to move away from it, yet since they choose that very option freely, the mechanism never activates at all. In such a case, did a real alternative ever exist for them? I want to design a 90-minute workshop for about ten adults with mixed backgrounds, run without any prior reading required, that makes participants feel this tension directly rather than grasping it only as an abstract argument. To avoid repeating the standard formulation exactly as I have seen it described, I want the hidden constraint to operate not on the final physical action but on the space of deliberation itself: there should be a hidden protocol that makes one option structurally unvoiceable within the group's reasoning process, not merely physically impossible afterward. The participants should reach a collective decision in good faith, then discover that an alternative was excluded from their mental and conversational landscape from the very start, and only then should the group ask whether they "could have done otherwise."

Please design a complete, runnable in-room activity with the following elements: the precise setting and group size; clear rules and hidden roles that participants do not initially know about, including instructions for the hidden roles; a simple symbolic payoff or stakes structure using points rather than real money; materials that can be assembled without special resources; and a step-by-step timeline for the 90 minutes. The structure should be arranged so that the group naturally reaches consensus on a specific distributive or ethical choice, with all members believing they have weighed the available options openly. Only in the debrief should they learn that one option was never available to them — not because anyone openly forbade it, but because a hidden rule made it impossible for anyone in the room to propose or name it. Then provide a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto parts of the philosophical idea: the moment of free-seeming choice, the moment of discovery, the question of whether the absence of an actual alternative undermines moral responsibility, and the distinction between "no one stopped me" and "the option was never live."

Also include an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time this is run — for instance, if participants guess the hidden roles prematurely, if the emotional reaction to the revelation is stronger than expected, or if the group refuses to accept the legitimacy of the hidden constraint — together with practical advice on how to handle each failure mode. Finally, include a frank note about which specific feature of the Frankfurt-style case the physical room activity cannot reproduce and that I, as the facilitator, must supply in my own words afterward. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand entirely on its own, and a model without web access should be able to construct it from common knowledge alone. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for presenting directly to a facilitator.
