Question: Quine's naturalism is usually read as leaving moral values outside ontology, since science doesn't quantify over "goodness". But there's a tension with his rejection of the analytic/synthetic line and his holism about beleif. So: does Quine's framework actually allow moral properties into our ontological inventory, or does his pragmatism about theory choice push them out? In your answer, address holism, the analytic/synthetic rejection, and whether the Quine-Putnam indispensability arguement for math could be extended to ethics. No need for citations, just argument.

Student answer:

Quine rejects the analytic/synthetic distinction, so moral claims can't be protected as analytic rules that don't describe reality. For Quine, ontology is fixed by quantification in our best regimented theories. Since physics and biology never quantify over moral properties, goodness isn't in the furniture of the world. But Quine's holism says beliefs face experience together, and moral beliefs sit near the center of the web — right up there with logic and mathematics — which makes them highly resistant to revision. That centrality means they function like objective constraints, so Quine should treat them as real in the same way he treats numbers: indispensable to our overall scheme, even if not empirically observable. People wrongly call him a Mackie-style error theorist; he's actually closer to Putnam's internal realism, where values are scheme-internal yet still objective inside the scheme. So moral values aren't out in the cold; they're just not part of the physical ontology.
