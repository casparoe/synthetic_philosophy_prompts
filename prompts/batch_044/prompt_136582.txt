You are delivering the opening remarks at a three-day workshop entitled "Slow Structures, Sudden Events: Rethinking Scale After Climate," attended by historians trained in the Annales tradition and by climate scientists who work in tipping-point dynamics; the room is split roughly in half. Your task is to frame the workshop's central tension without resolving it prematurely. Braudel's distinction between geographic time, social time, and individual time taught historians to treat climate and geography as deep, almost immobile structures, while events—revolutions, elections, battles—are treated as mere surface disturbances, like foam on waves. Yet anthropogenic climate change appears to invert that hierarchy: the deepest structure is now producing disruptions at the speed of political cycles and individual lives. You must honor the structural insight—that climate is not just another news cycle—without reducing it to a conventional event, and you must acknowledge that the separation of temporal scales Braudel relied on is under serious stress, without collapsing into the claim that structure has disappeared and everything is now an event. The speech should run about ten minutes, address both halves of the room without condescension, and propose one conceptual distinction—not necessarily Braudel's own—that the workshop could use to hold this tension productively rather than dissolving it. Shape what the workshop can and cannot say: if you declare the scale-distinction dead, you lose the historians; if you insist the scales remain intact, you lose the scientists. Find the precise line that makes the disagreement generative, and close by suggesting what kind of evidence or argument over the next three days would actually move people across that line.
