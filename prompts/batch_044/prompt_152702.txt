I run a philosophy seminar for about 14 undergrads, one 60-minute session. I want an acitivty that makes them feel Chesterton's fence in their bones — not just Burke's warning about reform, but the actual epistemic asymmetry: it's cheap to knock a rule down, expensive to discover what it was holding up. Design a structured role-play. They inherit a fictional town's weird, over-complex planning code — rules that look like pointless bureaucracy. Give them limited, asymmetric info: some know fragments of the original reasons, others don't, and the reasons themselves have been lost through generational turnover (the fence is "orphaned", not just mysterious). Split them into reformers and stewards with different incentives, then let them vote on which rules to scrap. The rules should have hidden interdependancies: removing one quietly breaks two others in ways they can't foresee until a "consequence round" reveals it. Include roles, a timeline, the payoff scheme (use reputation points or access to future rounds, not real money), a debrief that maps each moment onto Burke/Chesterton, an honest note on which conservative argument the game can't reproduce, and what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it. Write the whole thing in British English, please — and don't ask them to read anything specific beforehand, they should come in cold.
