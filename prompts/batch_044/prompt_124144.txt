For a philosophy-of-mind seminar handout on whether dreams are experiences, I want a taxonomy that moves beyond the standard Malcolm-versus-Dennett framing. Write it in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — using flowing sentences to trace the branches. Build the tree from four diagnostic questions that someone could answer without knowing the philosophical labels. First: is there a criterion, available either in first-person report or in third-person observation, that distinguishes being awake and undergoing an experience from being asleep and lacking one, such that sleep's failure to satisfy this criterion undermines the claim that dreams are experiences? Second: does a waking report of a vivid dream scene provide direct retrospective evidence of a prior phenomenal state, or does it only record a belief or narrative constructed at the moment of awakening? Third: does the neurophysiological overlap between REM sleep and waking consciousness — similar activation in sensory and limbic cortices — support the continuity of experience, or is it equally compatible with an offline simulation that generates representations without any accompanying phenomenal presence? Fourth: does lucid dreaming, in which the sleeper exercises apparent voluntary attention and self-reference, demonstrate that experience can occur during sleep, or can it be explained as a localized partial awakening that imports waking criteria without establishing that ordinary REM involves experience? From the combinations of answers, locate at least six positions: Malcolm's skeptical view that sleep lacks the necessary criterion; Dennett's account that dream reports are constructed upon awakening like a loaded cassette; a hybrid view that grants phenomenal presence only during lucid episodes but denies it in non-lucid REM; a view that accepts experiential content during sleep but denies it belongs to a unified subject or self; and at least one other intermediate stance of your own construction that occupies a logically available but rarely defended space. For every combination that seems unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Present the entire taxonomy as a single continuous text, describing each branch and leaf in sentences that indicate how the answers combine.
