I've been arguing with my flatmate about the Pasadena game — you know, the coin-flipping one where the payoff is 2^n over n but with opposite signs on odd and even flips, so the expectation doesn't convergant. I get that depending on how you reorder the terms you can make the sum come out to literally anything, which feels like Riemann's rearrangement thing applied to decision theory. But I think people are missing something: if the expected value is undefined because the series is conditionally convergent, then doesn't that mean the game doesn't have one true value, it has every value? So when philosophers say expected utility theory is silent here, I think they're being too timid — it's not silent, it's multivocal. A rational player should be able to choose the summation ordering that matches their risk appetite, just like picking a prior. And since St Petersburg gets solved by bounding the utility curve, why doesn't that work here? If I cap my utility at some huge number, the terms shrink and the sum should settle down, right?
