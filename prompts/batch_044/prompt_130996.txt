After a long argument with a friend about whether Pascal is merely a conventionalist about politics, I sketched these notes and want your comments on whether they hold together or overread the fragments.

The usual interpretation takes Pascal’s reflections on justice and force to mean that justice is whatever custom and power have fixed, and that we obey the stronger only to avoid civil war. I propose a different architecture. Pascal distinguishes, I think, between domination that is bare coercion and domination that functions as a visible sign of an underlying charitable order. When he observes that justice is established, he describes a fallen realm in which sword-force substitutes for missing charity; when he calls force without justice tyranny, he is not appealing to a hidden natural law but measuring political power against the only legitimate authority he recognizes elsewhere—the Church’s persuasive, non-coercive ordering, which derives its power from consent in charity rather than arms. The fragments on custom and tyranny thus form what we might call a negative ecclesiology: they depict politics attempting to imitate the Church’s ordering form without its charitable substance.

On this reading, Pascal’s acceptance of strong monarchy and his revulsion at arbitrary violence are not in tension. Legitimate rule must carry the form of ordered charity, even if its material is coercion; illegitimate rule is coercion that pretends to be self-grounding. But this requires importing ecclesiological concepts into fragments that never mention the Church. Is that importation defensible? And if the reading stands, should we understand obedience to established customs as a kind of provisional sacrament, or as pure prudence?

Please comment especially on internal coherence, on whether I am projecting a Jansenist structure onto scattered aphorisms, and on whether there is a closer analog in Pascal’s polemical writings that undermines or supports the claim.
