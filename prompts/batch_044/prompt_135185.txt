I am currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on collective decision-making and social choice theory, and I need to design an in-class exercise for approximately eighteen peers during a single sixty-minute session. The goal is to make participants feel the structural inevitability of strategic manipulation—not as an abstract result, but as a lived institutional pressure—before we formally discuss the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. The scenario should center on selecting one of three mutually exclusive structures for our next module (for instance, a final examination, an independent research paper, or a collaborative presentation), with each student holding a private cardinal ranking over the three options and also a hidden second-order preference for whether the group reaches its decision through sincere aggregation or through coalition-building.

To keep the idea from being a standard voting simulation that participants could anticipate from pre-training examples, I want to inject a specific institutional friction: a two-stage mechanism I will call preference-laundering. In stage one, each student anonymously submits a delegated preference vector to one of three randomly assigned agenda setters; in stage two, these agenda setters—who face no binding obligation to report honestly—compose the final pairwise comparison ballots using a modified Borda count that permits strategic withdrawal or omission of alternatives. The payoff scheme should use course-participation points rather than real money: students earn points both for securing their personally top-ranked outcome and for being part of the winning coalition, so that misrepresenting one's delegated vector becomes individually rational even under common knowledge of the rules.

Please design the complete rules, the hidden-information structure, the timeline for the sixty minutes, and the exact payoff matrix. The debrief should map concrete moments—such as a student revising their delegated vector after observing an informal coalition signal—to the formal conditions under which strategy-proofness fails, and it should state clearly which aspect of the theorem's universal quantification over all preference profiles the exercise cannot reproduce. Also include an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run, particularly confusion arising from the proxy layer, and advise how the facilitator should manage that breakdown without resolving the strategic tension prematurely.
