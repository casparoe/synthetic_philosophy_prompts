As a novelist constructing a character who is a late-eighteenth-century utilitarian reformer, I have been studying secondary accounts of Jeremy Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality, particularly the essay in which he examines irregularities of sexual appetite. In a recent biography, the author offers the following summary of Bentham's position. The author writes that Bentham defends private, consensual sexual acts—including those then criminalized as sodomy—by applying the principle of utility directly to the physical sensations involved: where there is pleasure without direct bodily harm, the act is beneficial, and therefore the law should not only tolerate but actively protect such conduct from social interference. The biographer further claims that Bentham classifies all objections based on religion, social disgust, or the preservation of marriage as instances of antipathy, which he treats as a purely emotional reaction with no weight in legislative science; according to this summary, Bentham excludes the moral and political sanctions entirely from his analysis of sexual behavior, relying solely on the physical sanction of pleasure and pain. From this, the author concludes that Bentham's framework necessarily demands complete institutional neutrality: because marriage privileges one form of sexual association, maintaining state-recognized marriage amounts to legislating antipathy, and consistency with Bentham's principle requires abolishing such privileges. The biographer presents this as a direct logical consequence of Bentham's harm principle, suggesting that anyone who accepts his reasoning about punishment must also accept the dismantling of marriage as a legal institution.

Something about this account strikes me as possibly flattening Bentham's actual jurisprudence—particularly his well-known insistence that indirect consequences must always be weighed—but I am not sufficiently versed in his texts to identify precisely where the distortion lies. Could you examine this summary claim by claim? Please distinguish what a careful reader of Bentham's legislative science and his essay on paederasty would recognize as accurate, what represents a common oversimplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation—especially regarding his use of the four sanctions, his treatment of indirect social effects, and the relationship between decriminalization and institutional design. Then assess whether the biographer's conclusion—that Bentham's argument entails the abolition of state-recognized marriage—actually follows from the corrected premises, or whether it rests on a conflation that collapses once his distinctions are restored. I need no bibliography, no citation of manuscript pages, and no list of further readings; I want only an accuracy audit I can use to calibrate my character's dialogue and beliefs.
