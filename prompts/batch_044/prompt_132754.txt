I've been working through the literature on moral emotions with a specific question in mind: whether guilt—typically understood as action-oriented, reparable, and compatible with self-respect—is structurally a better cultural choice than shame, which binds identity to damage and tends to drive concealment rather than repair, and whether a society can genuinely engineer that choice rather than merely inherit it. I want to apply this framework not to an individual case but to an institutional technology: South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, established in 1996 under the 1995 Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act, which offered amnesty in exchange for full disclosure and structured its public hearings around individual testimony rather than collective indictment. Treat the Commission as a deliberate attempt to manufacture a national emotional architecture, and analyze the episode through that lens.

Through the guilt-versus-shame framework, identify the relevant actors—perpetrators seeking amnesty, victims testifying, the state sponsoring the process, and the witnessing public—and specify what emotional posture the institutional design invited each to adopt. Did the exchange of disclosure for amnesty construct a guilt-based logic in which wrongs could be named, repaired, and released, or did the public, identity-exposing nature of the hearings risk producing a shame-based identification with past violence? Say which features of the episode the theory explains well—perhaps the reparative logic of naming wrongs without punishment—and which it misdescribes or misses, such as the collective rather than individual scale of the emotions involved, or the way indigenous concepts like Ubuntu shaped the Commission's self-understanding beyond the standard guilt/shame binary. Finally, treat the case as testing the theory itself: does the TRC's mixed legacy—some perpetrators distorting testimony to secure amnesty, some victims feeling retraumatized rather than reconciled—show that a culture can choose its dominant moral emotion through institutional design, or does it reveal that emotional regimes are constrained by structural conditions that design alone cannot override?
