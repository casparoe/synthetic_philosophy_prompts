Here's an argument my roommate threw at me after skimming some Burke and Chesterton, and now I cant tell if its clever or just broken.

(1) Burkean conservatism says long-standing institutions hold tacit wisdom we cant fully state.
(2) Chesterton's fence: dont tear down a fence until you know why it was put up.
(3) Now take any proposed reform R to institution I. Either we understand I's full functional role, including its complementarity with other institutions, or we dont.
(4) If we dont grasp it, the fence blocks R.
(5) If we do grasp it fully, then we've mapped all the tacit knowledge and its complementarities.
(6) Once thats mapped, we can rationally redesign I and adjust the complementary institutions together, so R preserves the wisdom.
(7) So in either case, either R is forbidden or R is harmless.
(8) Therefore Burkean conservatism never gives a substantive reason to prefer I over R; it only delays R until we've done the mapping, after which R is just standard engineering.
(9) But Burkeans resist specific reforms with arguments about loss, not just "we havent mapped enough yet."
(10) So the view either collapses into "research then redesign" or its self-defeating.
(11) Wheres the crack? Which step should I reject, and whats the cost?
