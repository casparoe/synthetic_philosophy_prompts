I've been developing a particular reading of Quinean naturalism applied to ethics—one that I think is faithful to his broader epistemology but pushes it into territory he rarely addressed directly. The position runs as follows: moral values carry no ontological commitment; they are not part of the inventory of entities that our best total science posits. Instead, they occupy nodes in the web of belief that are maximally distant from sensory experience and maximally resistant to revision—not because they enjoy a special epistemic or analytic status, since Quine's critique of a robust analytic-synthetic distinction rules that out, but because abandoning them would require such widespread and destabilizing adjustments elsewhere that rational agents preserve them as pragmatic boundary conditions. Crucially, I want to add a structural twist: moral judgments should be understood not as describing normative properties, but as expressing coherence constraints on belief revision itself. To say that cruelty is wrong is not to attribute cruelty with a property of wrongness, but to register a fixed point in our rational architecture: any total theory of the world and our place in it that treats cruelty as costless must be rejected, not because it misrepresents an independent normative fact, but because it violates the conditions of rational agency that make theory choice possible at all. On this view, morality is neither objective in a realist sense nor merely subjective preference; it is a maximally entrenched, non-descriptive structural feature of human cognition that carries zero ontological weight yet plays an indispensable regulative role.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of standard objections to moral anti-realism in general. Specifically, I need to know which of the following would be decisive versus merely awkward: empirical findings that moral disagreement is always resolvable by shared sensory evidence (which might actually support the view, so clarify the direction); evolutionary or anthropological evidence that moral norms track independent adaptive constraints rather than being arbitrary cultural fixtures; formal arguments showing that if logical laws are revisable in principle, then treating moral norms as analogously revisable but never actually revised collapses into an unstable distinction; philosophical results demonstrating that coherence conditions on rational agency either smuggle back normative properties through the back door or reduce to descriptive psychology, thereby undermining the non-descriptive claim; and cross-cultural moral universals that resist explanation as pragmatic entrenchment because they appear even in radically different theoretical environments. I also want to know what defenders of this Quinean structural view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging—perhaps that the analogy between logic and morality breaks down because revising logic is globally self-undermining in a way revising morality is not, or that without some minimal analytic-synthetic distinction the very notion of maximal entrenchment becomes incoherent. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones risk collapsing the view into expressivism or error theory unintentionally, and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a virtue for a regulative framework or a vice for a philosophical thesis. I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden commitment to normative realism inside the boundary-condition language, and if that commitment can be excised without destroying the view's explanatory power.
