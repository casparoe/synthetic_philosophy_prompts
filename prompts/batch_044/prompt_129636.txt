I serve on the membership committee of the Easton Cooperative Living Association, a private organization established in 1918. Our charter grants joint property and inheritance rights only to members who are "married heads of household," a phrase that has never been defined in our bylaws. Two long-standing members, legally married to each other in a same-sex union, have applied for joint status; one partner has a progressive disability and depends on the other for daily care and medical coordination. Half the committee argues that the charter's original purpose was to recognize unions that combine economic provision with the biological rearing and transmission of property across generations, and that same-sex couples fall outside this purpose by nature regardless of their legal status. The other half argues that we should simply apply the state's current legal definition of marriage and approve the application without further inquiry. I have no formal training in philosophy, law, or sociology, but I have been thinking about this on my own, and I believe the real question is what marriage is for in the context of an institution like ours: whether its essential function is generative—linking generations through procreation—or associative—stabilizing a long-term bond of mutual dependency and care regardless of reproductive potential. I have also been considering a third possibility that occurred to me: that the phrase "heads of household" might actually point not to marriage itself, but to a functional role of managing interdependent vulnerability within a shared domestic life, which this couple clearly fulfills but which might also apply to other arrangements, such as siblings or close friends in similar dependency. I need to circulate a written opinion before our vote next Thursday. Please draft the committee memo: recommend a concrete course of action—approve the application under existing language, approve it with a charter amendment that defines eligibility in functional terms with specific criteria, or deny it with a clear statement of principle—together with a timeline for implementation if any amendment is involved. The memo should treat the generative-purpose argument seriously and on its own terms rather than dismissing it, should examine my functional-role idea honestly and explain whether it offers a stable middle ground or collapses back into one of the other two views, and should reach an explicit bottom line that committee members without philosophical background can understand and vote on. It should not request outside reading lists, citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholars, and it should be written in plain, formal language appropriate to an institutional decision document.
