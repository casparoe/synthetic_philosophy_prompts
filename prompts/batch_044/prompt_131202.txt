I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on collective action and climate ethics, and I've been running a semester-long reading group that has hit an impasse I can't quite diagnose. We've been circling the inefficacy objection—whether an individual's carbon footprint, dietary choice, or vote in a large election can be genuinely wrong given that the collective outcome is unchanged by any single contribution. The literature feels exhausted: everyone knows the causal redundancy arguments, everyone knows the counter-appeals to collective responsibility or expressive value, and yet nobody in my group—nor, it seems, in the published exchange—has actually moved. The interventionists insist that participating in harmful collective practices is wrong even when withdrawal makes no discernible difference to climate trajectories, factory-farm demand curves, or electoral margins; the skeptics reply that without a counterfactual difference in the world-state, there is no harm to ground the wrongness, and that appeals to "not being complicit" or "setting an example" either smuggle in implausible causal expectations or confuse aesthetic purity with moral reasoning.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not factual—everyone agrees on the physics, the economics, and the mathematics—but conceptual, and specifically about a hidden disagreement over what counts as a moral reason in structurally redundant contexts. One side seems to require that a reason for action be tied to an actual or nearby-possible-world change in outcomes—what we might call an efficacy-dependent reason—while the other allows reasons satisfied by the agent's relation to the harmful structure itself, independent of marginal causal impact, a position some recent work frames in terms of "structural complicity" rather than direct causation. If that's right, then the debate might be substantive but systematically ill-posed: the sides aren't disagreeing about whether individual action "matters" in some shared sense, but about whether causal efficacy is even the right metric for moral evaluation when collective mechanisms mediate harm. But I'm unsure whether this captures the real fracture. Could it instead be verbal—a dispute about whether "harm" must be counterfactual-comparative, or whether "wrong" can be satisfied by participatory relations? Or is there a bedrock intuition about cleanliness or symbolic integrity that the skeptics dismiss as irrational but that the interventionists treat as foundational and non-negotiable?

I want a diagnosis that engages the actual positions concretely—the skeptic who thinks individual climate mitigation is futile given global emission trajectories, the interventionist who thinks consuming factory-farmed meat is wrong regardless of market elasticity, and the voter who treats democratic participation as either a civic duty or an irrational ritual—rather than a survey of named theorists. Is this disagreement about hidden empirical claims, about standards of moral reasoning, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Are the sides answering different questions—one about optimal strategy, one about character or integrity—under the single heading "what should I do?" And if my structural-complicity framing is correct, what would actually advance things: a formal decision-theoretic model that integrates non-causal reasons without violating the redundancy intuition; a conceptual distinction that separates causal redundancy from moral redundancy; or is the impasse a sign that the individual-level framing—"what difference do I make?"—is itself malformed, and that progress requires abandoning it for a fully collective-level analysis? Give me the diagnosis, with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and an honest answer about whether anything—a distinction, a model, a change of question—should be expected to move it.
