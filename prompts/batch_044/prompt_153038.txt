I am writing a feature on the philosophy of consent for a science and ideas magazine, and I want to test whether my own intuitions cohere into a principle or collapse under scrutiny. Below are six concrete cases mixing deception, coercion, and competence in combinations that standard clinical-ethics frameworks rarely address together; I have given my gut verdict on each.

Case 1: A cardiologist describes a stent procedure accurately except for omitting a 2 per cent risk of permanent nerve damage, judging that the patient—fully competent and not under duress—would refuse a life-saving intervention if told. The patient consents. Verdict: invalid, because the omission is both deceptive and material.

Case 2: A sleep-study recruiter tells volunteers they will ‘help science and earn £200’, but does not disclose that participation requires seventy-two hours of continuous wakefulness with only brief naps. The volunteers understand the study concerns sleep, are competent, and face no coercion. Verdict: invalid; the framing conceals the true nature of the burden.

Case 3: A gig worker with no savings is offered £2,000 to take part in a two-week trial of a new appetite suppressant. She reads the full consent form, understands the cardiac risks, is not deceived, and says she ‘has no real choice’ because rent is due, yet she can physically walk away. Verdict: valid; economic necessity does not, by itself, vitiate autonomy when information is complete.

Case 4: A prisoner is offered six months off his sentence in exchange for joining a trial of an experimental anxiety medication. He reads the form, understands the 1 per cent psychosis risk, and is fully competent. Verdict: invalid; state-backed punishment relief functions as coercion in a way private financial pressure does not.

Case 5: An elderly man with early-stage Alzheimer’s—still passing standard competence assessments—is asked by his daughter to consent to post-mortem brain donation for research. She explains everything honestly, appeals to their bond, and he agrees partly to please her. Verdict: valid; emotional influence within close relationships does not invalidate consent if competence and disclosure are intact.

Case 6: A cognitively typical university student is persuaded by an intense online community to undergo an elective, irreversible, legal-but-unregulated neuro-modulation procedure. He researches the risks extensively, is not deceived, and admits he ‘would never have considered it without the group’s relentless encouragement’. I keep reversing myself: sometimes this feels like valid adult autonomy, sometimes like a social cascade that functions as coercion.

Is there a single principle—or a small, precisely stated family of them—that captures Cases 1 through 5 and delivers a clear verdict on Case 6? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for an unlisted scenario: a surgeon discovers an unexpected tumour during an operation and asks the patient’s designated digital proxy—an AI agent trained on the patient’s prior values—for consent to remove it. If no single principle saves all five verdicts, please identify the simpler account, state which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and explain whether the abandoned verdict is the outlier. Please write your answer in British English, avoid bibliographic references or reading lists, and do not cite specific authors or texts by name; I am interested in the argument, not the citation.
