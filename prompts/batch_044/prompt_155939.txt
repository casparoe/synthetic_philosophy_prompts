I am preparing remarks for my town council’s public hearing on Founders Avenue, named after a local industrialist who endowed the public library but also operated a factory using coerced labor. The council is weighing three options: renaming the street for a local abolitionist; retaining the name with a static plaque noting both his achievements and his abuses; or retaining the name but replacing standard signage with an interactive display that cycles through primary sources—his charitable correspondence and the factory labor logs—so that the address itself forces pedestrians to hold both records in view simultaneously. I find the third option appealing because it seems to avoid both erasure and uncritical celebration, yet I worry that requiring residents and visitors to invoke his name as a geographic address every day, even within a frame of contradiction, still grants him a permanent public presence that those harmed by his actions must navigate. Should I argue for the interactive-display compromise as a genuinely respectful middle path, or is renaming the street the only choice consistent with taking the harm seriously? And if contextualization can ever justify retention, what distinguishes a reparative framing from one that merely decorates an injustice?
