I'm a journalist covering the science of institutional design and the philosophy of constitutional precommitment, and I serve as an external member on a university advisory panel charged with reviewing charter amendments. We are currently weighing a proposal for what its drafters call a deliberative escrow clause: a constitutional provision that would bind all future governing boards to treat the findings of an independent, rotating methodological panel as binding premises—not merely advisory input—whenever they consider amendments to research-ethics policy. The clause is intended as a Ulysses contract operating at the level of reasoning rather than outcome.

Our panel's established precedents are these. We endorsed a ten-year sunset provision for funding mandates that requires active renewal, on the grounds that future boards should not be permanently constrained by present fiscal judgments. We rejected a proposal to entrench a two-thirds supermajority requirement for all curriculum changes, viewing it as an illegitimate constraint on democratic deliberation. We approved, with conditions, a binding consultation rule that mandates public hearings before charter changes but explicitly permits future boards to disregard the testimony received. We rejected an attempt to embed a named ethical framework—specifically, a particular interpretation of virtue ethics—into the charter's preamble, holding that constitutions may structure procedures but should not codify substantive philosophical commitments.

The new clause differs in that it does not dictate outcomes or procedural steps like hearings; it constrains the epistemic inputs that future boards may treat as authoritative. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Identify our closest precedent and explain whether our prior rulings genuinely underdetermine this case. If we approve, state the principle of constitutional precommitment we would be extending; if we reject, state what boundary we would be defending between procedural loyalty and substantive self-binding. Give serious, non-dismissive weight to the drafters' argument that binding one's future reasoning processes is the purest expression of Ulysses contracting, and address the counter-argument that such a clause mistakes procedural fidelity for an impossible attempt to legislate belief.
