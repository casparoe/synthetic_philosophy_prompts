Having spent the greater part of my career in experimental physics, I have only recently, in retirement, begun to read philosophy of mind with any rigour. At a recent discussion group I encountered the following summary of epiphenomenalism and the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgement, which I should like to have audited for fairness. The speaker characterised epiphenomenalism as the thesis that phenomenal states are causally inert products of neural activity: they are brought into being by brain processes but exert no downward causal influence upon those processes, nor upon any other physical events, including the muscular and verbal outputs through which we report our experiences. On this view, when one judges that one is experiencing a particular quale — say, the phenomenal character of crimson — that judgement is caused entirely by the underlying neurophysiology, with the quale itself contributing nothing to the causal chain that produces belief or utterance. The speaker then introduced what he called the transmission problem of introspection: if our first-person beliefs are triggered by physical states rather than by the conscious states they are ostensibly about, then there is no reliable causal transmission from phenomenal fact to introspective belief, rendering our phenomenal judgements systematically untrustworthy in precisely the domain where we claim privileged authority. From this he inferred that epiphenomenalism is not merely implausible but self-refuting, for it undermines the very introspective knowledge that a philosopher must invoke in order to formulate or defend the position — including her own awareness of what it is like to entertain the theory. He concluded by observing that this difficulty is essentially identical to the argument associated with Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument against physicalism, since both demonstrate that a purely physical account cannot preserve first-person epistemic authority.

How fair is this characterisation? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question. I want to know, claim by claim, which elements a defender of epiphenomenalism would accept as accurate, which are common simplifications, and which are subtle misrepresentations — particularly any conflation between causal exclusion and introspective transmission, or between Jackson’s argument and this particular epistemological challenge. Does the speaker’s conclusion that the view is self-defeating actually follow from the premises he gets right? And is the claim that epiphenomenalism denies all downward causation correct, or does it overstate a more modest thesis about mental-to-physical efficacy?
