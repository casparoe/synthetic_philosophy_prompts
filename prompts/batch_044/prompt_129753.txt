As a novelist, I think constantly about how people sustain different versions of themselves across audiences, and about the particular violence done when those versions are forced into a single account. Analyze the 2013 Snowden disclosures regarding NSA bulk data collection through the lens of privacy conceived not as secrecy or informational control, but as narrative boundary-work: the capacity to maintain coherent yet non-identical autobiographical narratives across separate social contexts. Identify the relevant actors—Snowden, the journalists who mediated the documents, the subjects whose communications were assembled, and the agencies that did the assembling—and describe what each faced and chose under this framing. What does narrative boundary-work illuminate that standard privacy theories tend to miss, particularly about why the harm felt closer to an involuntary merging of selves than to simple theft? Then say precisely where this lens distorts or falls silent: does it misread the institutional logic of state surveillance, which may never have recognized such boundaries in the first place? And does the episode ultimately support the claim that privacy’s value resides in narrative coherence, or does it reveal that coherence to be merely a novelist’s elegant fiction?
