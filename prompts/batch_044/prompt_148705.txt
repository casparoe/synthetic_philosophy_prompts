In zoning appeals across several U.S. and European municipalities, boards routinely treat the loss of sunlight or a protected view corridor suffered by an existing homeowner as a concrete, compensable injury warranting denial or mitigation, while treating the exclusion of prospective residents—who are blocked from housing by the same height or density restriction—as a non-injury that requires no remedy. I am trying to map the implicit philosophy of urban property embedded in that asymmetry. What theory of rights or distributive justice does the practice presuppose? Does it depend on a first-possession or temporal-priority account of spatial entitlement, on a zero-sum commons model of the built environment, or on a generational-bounded view of justice that weights present owners over future inhabitants? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether it can be rendered consistent with a right-to-the-city framework that treats housing access as a claim against exclusionary control. If the underlying theory were replaced by an intergenerational equity model—say, one in which shadow-footprints and view-obstructions are priced as transferable development rights rather than as veto-entitlements—what specific mechanisms in the appeals process and in compensation rules would have to change?
