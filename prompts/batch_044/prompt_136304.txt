I have been reading some introductory philosophy of language, and I keep returning to the debate over what makes an assertion proper or improper—whether the governing norm requires knowledge, sincere belief, or merely truth. The popular summaries make it sound as if we simply pick one mental state and apply it uniformly, but I keep thinking about a situation that does not seem to fit neatly, and I am unsure whether I am confusing different levels of analysis or uncovering a genuine conceptual tension.

Consider what I think of as an “assertoric echo.” I work in a small scientific community where I rely heavily on one colleague’s expertise in toxicology. She tells me, with full documentation, that compound X is non-toxic at low doses. I do not understand the data myself, but I have excellent reason to trust her judgment. Months later, I am speaking to a public-health board and I say, flatly, “Compound X is non-toxic at low doses,” not as a report of her view but as my own contribution to the record. At the moment of speaking, my mental state is belief—strong belief, grounded in her authority, but not first-personal knowledge. Now, if the norm of assertion is knowledge, have I violated it? One possibility is that the norm applies only to the speaker’s own epistemic position, which would seem to forbid me from ever relaying expert consensus unless I personally master the underlying evidence. But that seems to make collaborative knowledge and ordinary testimony structurally improper, which feels wrong. Another possibility is that the norm attaches not to my head but to the reliability of the channel through which the information reached me—what I might call a “delegated knowledge” condition. Yet if we allow that, it looks like the knowledge norm collapses into a general reliability requirement, and we lose the special importance philosophers place on the speaker’s own cognitive responsibility. Meanwhile, a belief-only norm seems to let me off too easily: I could echo something I barely understand as long as I feel sure of it, which does not capture why we criticize people for speaking beyond their competence. A truth-only norm avoids the mental-state problem entirely, but then it seems to erase the distinction between a lucky guess and a well-founded assertion, which was the reason for caring about norms in the first place.

Could you please take on the role of an expert in epistemology and philosophy of language, begin with some careful brainstorming about how these three norms—knowledge, belief, and truth—interact with testimony and delegation, and then explain the core conceptual issue here? I am especially interested in whether there is a stable middle position that preserves speaker-centered responsibility without requiring every asserter to be an original investigator, or whether the attempt to split the difference just smuggles in a hidden choice between speaker-state and channel-reliability.
