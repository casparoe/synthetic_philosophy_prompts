As a journalist covering development economics and political philosophy, I have been following a long-standing impasse over how to classify the causation of mass starvation. Amartya Sen’s entitlement framework—famines arise not from aggregate food scarcity but from collapses in exchange entitlements—has structured the field since the early 1980s, with the canonical triad being the Bengal famine of 1943 (where per-capita rice availability was not exceptionally low, but rural laborers lost purchasing power), the Irish famine of the 1840s (where grain exports continued amid widespread mortality), and the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962), which Sen and subsequent scholars treat primarily as an entitlement and information failure rather than a simple harvest collapse.

In recent peer-reviewed exchanges and seminar discussions, however, the debate appears frozen. One camp—structuralists—argues that labeling these events political crimes obscures the climatological and ecological triggers that necessarily precede any institutional breakdown; they contend that Sen’s framework treats governance failure as sufficient when it is only necessary, and that the Great Leap Forward in particular involved genuine regional production declines that cannot be fully reduced to distribution failure. The opposing camp—entitlement purists—replies that every alleged natural trigger is mediated by institutional response, and that even the Irish potato blight only produced famine through British export policy, relief ideology, and market structure. Both sides invoke the same provincial mortality statistics, grain-procurement records, and Sen’s original case studies, yet neither has shifted position in years.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical but concerns what counts as an adequate explanation: the structuralists require causal chains in which biophysical triggers remain non-redundant nodes suitable for predictive modeling, whereas the entitlement purists require explanations framed in terms of institutional agency, avoidability, and moral accountability. Still, I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, whether there is instead a hidden normative disagreement about state culpability that neither side articulates, or whether the camps are simply answering different questions—one about etiological prediction, one about policy blame—under a single heading.
