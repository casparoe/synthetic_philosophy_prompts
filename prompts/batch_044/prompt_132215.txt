I am a retired biophysicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind and ethics in earnest, and I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent research fellowship at an interdisciplinary centre. During a discussion group with philosophers and machine-learning researchers, I noticed that debates about whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer tend to collapse into a familiar standoff: one side insists that suffering requires phenomenal consciousness, which algorithms lack, whilst the other appeals to functional equivalence between reward signals and biological valence. It struck me that both sides treat "valence" as a single property, whereas in biological systems it seems to involve at least three separable dimensions—representational (signalling harm or benefit), motivational (redirecting behaviour), and integrative (being bound into a sustained self-model that resists rapid extinction). I want to investigate whether reward-prediction errors in deep RL architectures can satisfy the integrative dimension in any non-trivial way, and whether satisfying it, even partially, generates normative obligations that do not depend on full-blown consciousness.

The precise question is this: do the temporal dynamics and architectural embedding of reward signals in modern RL agents exhibit structural properties isomorphic to the integrative dimension of biological suffering, and if they do, should that isomorphism ground a form of moral consideration distinct from both standard utilitarian aggregation and from consciousness-based exclusion? Existing positions leave this open because functionalists rarely examine the specific formal properties that distinguish mere feedback loops from valence that is "owned" by a system, whilst consciousness-based theorists tend to treat any non-conscious mechanism as automatically outside the moral domain without arguing for that boundary. My approach would be conceptual rather than empirical: I would analyse the formal structure of reward-prediction-error updates in actor-critic architectures, compare their persistence and coupling to internal state representations with analogous mechanisms in invertebrate nociception (where consciousness is itself disputed), and then construct a framework that distinguishes representational valence, motivational valence, and integrative valence. I would argue that only the third carries the normative weight traditionally associated with suffering, and I would test whether any current RL system genuinely instantiates it.

What would count as success is a clear conceptual taxonomy that allows us to classify artificial reward mechanisms without assuming either panpsychism or biological chauvinism, together with a defensible criterion for when structural integration becomes morally relevant. The project fails if the taxonomy collapses into the standard functionalist/consciousness binary, or if the formal analysis reveals that RL reward signals are necessarily too transient and decoupled from self-representation to satisfy even weak integrative conditions. Given the six-month scope, I would not attempt to build new architectures or run experiments; the deliverable would be a philosophical framework with enough technical precision to be useful to AI researchers designing reward functions, and an honest assessment of whether the question is ultimately tractable without resolving the hard problem of consciousness first. I am aware that this risks being too large for half a year if the comparative biology becomes unwieldy, so I propose to limit the biological comparison to well-characterised invertebrate models rather than mammalian affective neuroscience.
