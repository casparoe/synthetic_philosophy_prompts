Here's an argument I built that extends Singer's pond reasoning to obligations across time and collective agency, and it now seems to demand more than any human life can provide. (1) Singer's rescue principle states that if it is in our power to prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we are morally required to do so; and the pond case establishes that physical distance does not diminish this obligation. (2) If spatial distance is morally irrelevant, temporal distance should be equally irrelevant: preventing a catastrophic harm decades hence is structurally parallel to rescuing a distant child, since present actions can prevent it. (3) If the presence of other potential rescuers does not reduce each individual's obligation—since each faces the same choice—then with billions of agents, each remains fully required. (4) Applying (2) and (3) together yields that every present person must devote all disposable resources to preventing distant future catastrophe. (5) But "preventing" a collective harm is not distributive: if I fail, others may still succeed, yet if I succeed alone I may still fail to prevent the harm if others do not act; therefore each agent is required not merely to contribute but to ensure the outcome. (6) Hence each of us must work continuously on future rescue up to the point of marginal utility, and indeed beyond, since the obligation is not satisfied by partial contribution. (7) Clearly no one is required to live this way. (8) So the argument must fail somewhere between (1) and (5). I would prefer to preserve (1) and the irrelevance of spatial distance, but I need to know whether the error lies in treating temporal distance as identical to spatial distance, in misapplying collective obligation, or in the non-distributive reading of "prevent" in (5)—and what giving up each of those costs.
