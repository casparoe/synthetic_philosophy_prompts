Two members of my reading group—I'll call them K and R—have been arguing about the central thesis of Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, not over whether analogies are common in cognition, but over whether analogy is the fundamental substrate and what would actually count as refuting it. Their exchange has reached an impasse about falsifiability and external constraints, and I want a move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on who is more pleasant to talk to. Both are making genuinely decent points; please don't treat either as a strawman.

K opens by accepting that cognition runs on analogical mapping, but insists that not every mapping qualifies. For an analogy to be genuinely cognitive rather than associative pattern-matching, K argues, it must exhibit what K terms frame-preserving projection: a structural invariant that survives across domains and prevents arbitrary slippage. K then proposes a constructed failure case: suppose a system analogizes democratic deliberation to a marketplace of ideas, then analogizes the marketplace to an auction, and consequently treats voting as bidding. The normative content of democracy collapses entirely. K takes this to show that without a non-analogical coherence constraint—some invariant that analogies must respect—analogy becomes arbitrary.

R responds that K has smuggled in exactly the kind of external, non-analogical criterion that the Hofstadter-Sander view rejects. In their account, analogies function through controlled slippage; frames shift, but the shift is guided by further analogies, not anchored by an invariant substrate. The democracy-to-auction cascade is not a failure of analogy per se, but a failure of analogical depth—the system has not made enough cross-domain mappings to stabilize the concept. R concludes that demanding frame-preserving projection is demanding something outside the analogical framework itself.

K counters that R's defense renders the thesis unfalsifiable. If every apparent failure is diagnosed as insufficient analogical depth, then no evidence could ever undermine the claim that analogy is the core of cognition. K insists that R needs a principled distinction between productive slippage and destructive collapse, and that merely saying do more analogies is circular rather than principled.

R replies that coherence is analogical coherence across an entire web of concepts. There is no Archimedean point outside analogy; the constraint is holistic consistency among analogies themselves. The democracy example fails not because it violates an invariant, but because it conflicts with other analogies we hold—about equality, about consent, about representation. The correction mechanism remains analogical throughout.

Please adjudicate each move: rate K's initial framing, R's slippage reply, K's unfalsifiability objection, and R's holistic-coherence response as clearly mistaken, clearly right, or genuinely ambiguous, with brief justification for each. Then identify what both parties are missing. Is the real crux whether holistic analogical coherence is substantive enough to do the work R assigns it, or whether K's demand for an external invariant secretly assumes a representationalist picture that the analogical-core view was designed to avoid? Is part of their dispute verbal—perhaps about whether frame-preserving projection names something real inside analogy or something imported from outside? I do not want a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I want the conceptual verdict, including what each side would have to concede for the other to be satisfied.
