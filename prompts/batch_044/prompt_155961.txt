I'm taking a seminar on logical empiricism and its afterlife in social philosophy, and I've become preoccupied with a procedural feature I keep seeing in regional planning documents — not in the philosophical literature, but in the actual administrative protocols of several metropolitan planning boards. The procedure works in two stages. First, every objection, proposal, or value-claim raised by residents must be translated into a standardized evaluation matrix: quantifiable costs, probabilities of risk, preference-weighted utility, and compatibility with existing zoning frameworks. Input that resists this translation — say, an argument that a particular watershed has an irreplaceable character that should not be traded off, or that future generations hold a non-derivative claim against present development — is not rejected outright but is reclassified: it is recorded as an external framework preference, marked non-cognitive, and routed to a separate political vote rather than to the rational evaluation stage. Second, once the matrix is populated, the board treats the remaining decision as purely internal: given the framework, which option maximizes the weighted values? The framework itself, however, is not defended or revised in the evaluation; it is adopted or rejected by administrative mandate or majority vote.

This strikes me as philosophically loaded. Does this institutional architecture presuppose something like Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions — that disputes within a linguistic or evaluative framework are answerable by rational methods, while the choice of framework itself is a practical decision outside the scope of cognitive justification? And does it further presuppose that value judgments, being framework-dependent or expressive, cannot function as premises in the rational stage at all? Or could the same two-stage practice be sustained by a very different meta-ethics — say, one that treats value conflicts as genuinely cognitive but incommensurable across frameworks?

More specifically, I want to know whether the practice is philosophically consistent with Carnap's own writings on planning and value. Carnap seemed to think that scientific rationality could guide planning once value premises were democratically chosen, but he also worried about authoritarian imposition of frameworks. Does this administrative separation of rational evaluation from framework selection faithfully realize his vision, or does it betray it by allowing planners to fix the matrix technically while relegating democratic input to a non-cognitive ghetto? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments — about meaning, value, and the scope of rational deliberation — that make this two-stage procedure seem coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a planning institution committed to the cognitive status of value claims could adopt the same procedural form without contradiction.
