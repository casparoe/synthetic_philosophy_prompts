As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest, I spend part of my time volunteering at an archive that preserves early twentieth-century photographic plates inside a climate-controlled vault. The vault is monitored by two independent systems: a vintage mechanical hygrometer whose hair-tension spring traces a continuous sinusoidal curve on smoked paper, and a modern digital capacitive array that logs discrete binary alerts whenever relative humidity crosses a preset threshold. During the past month, the analog trace has developed a slow, regular drift with a period of about twelve hours, while the digital system has begun emitting brief, irregular spikes that do not align with the peaks or troughs of the analog curve. The head archivist treats these as two entirely separate failure mechanisms—she plans to recalibrate the spring assembly for the first and to update the firmware for the second—because the observable signatures, the repair procedures, and the physical components affected share nothing in common.

I find myself uncertain whether this administrative split tracks a real difference in what is going wrong. When I temporarily alter the vault's HVAC cycling rate, both anomalies diminish together; when I place only the analog instrument inside a desiccated chamber, the sinusoidal drift disappears but the digital spikes persist elsewhere; and when I shield only the digital sensor from direct airflow, its spikes cease while the analog curve remains unchanged. The archivist argues that this merely shows two independent systems being influenced by a common background condition, not that they express a single underlying disturbance. So I am trying to understand what actually determines whether two detected anomalies belong to the same problem or to different ones: is it the shared causal structure behind them, the pattern of interventions that affect both detectors simultaneously, or the functional threat they pose to the preservation of the plates? Furthermore, if an institutional practice like ours routinely divides maintenance budgets according to the representational format of each detector—continuous curve versus discrete alarm—rather than according to whether the disturbances share causal origins, what view of sameness and difference does that practice quietly assume? Could it be coherent for the vault to suffer from two unrelated humidity failures solely because one is rendered as a smooth trace and the other as a binary flag?
