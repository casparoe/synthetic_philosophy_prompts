I was discussin with my friends last night over some pretty bad coffe about that Williams idea where saving your wife shouldn't involve counting heads — you know, the one thought too many thing. Anyway we tried applying it to a bunch of random scenarios and now I can't tell if I have one consistent view or just vibes.

Here are my gut calls. A surgeon who saves her own child instead of two strangers with slightly better prognosis: totally fine, she shouldn't run the calculus. A firefighter who pulls her partner out of a building instead of two strangers: also fine. But a hiring manager who picks her less-qualified cousin for a job because family comes first: that feels corrupt, like she's betraying the role. So loyalty is okay in rescue but not in hiring? I don't know. Then there's artistic stuff. A musician refuses a huge brand deal that'd require changing her lyrics, even though she could donate all the money to famine relief. I think she's right, and if she starts weighing lives saved against her art she's lost something essential. But then there's a doctor who stays in a low-paying public clinic instead of taking a private job — she does it because serving the poor matters more. Is that the same kind of integrity, or is she just being a good utilitarian?

I'm also confused about institutional roles versus personal ones. A judge who goes easy on an old friend: clearly wrong, she's supposed to be impartial. But a soldier who disobeys orders to drag her brother out of enemy fire: I think she should do it, even if it's technically desertion. What's the principled difference? There's this scientist case I keep going back and forth on. She finds something that could help climate policy, but she knows a corporation will twist it to greenwash. She decides not to publish because she can't control the framing. Sometimes I think that's integrity — protecting her work from corruption. Other times I think it's vanity, like she's putting her own purity over real-world good.

So is there one principle that gets all these? I want something that explains why the surgeon and firefighter are okay, the hiring manager isn't, the musician is right but maybe for a different reason than she thinks, and that tells me whether the soldier and the scientist are being principled or just self-indulgent. It should also say what I'd have to drop — like maybe my sympathy for the soldier doesn't actually fit. And can it predict what I'd say about a teacher who refuses to teach a standardized curriculum she thinks is harmful, even though her students will fail exams without it?
