I’ve been working through some older legal philosophy with my study group—mostly people who come to these questions from a rationalist-adjacent angle, interested in whether norms can be justified on genuinely non-circular grounds. Last week we got stuck on the Hart–Devlin debate over legal moralism, but not in the standard introductory form. Two members, Priya and Marcus, started arguing about whether Devlin’s disintegration thesis is best read as an empirical prediction about social cohesion or as a concealed normative claim, and whether Hart’s harm principle actually blocks it or just relocates the same intuition inside a different vocabulary.

Marcus defended a Devlin-style position, but with a specific twist: he asked us to model shared morality as generating positive coordination externalities—something like a normative cascade—so that when a private practice violates deeply held norms, the damage is not mere offense to observers but a degradation of the trust signals people rely on for cooperation. On this framing, prohibiting the practice is not enforcing morality for its own sake; it is preventing a moral externality that undermines social coordination, analogous to regulating pollution. Priya pushed back that this collapses Devlin’s view into Hart’s: if the only harm is degraded cooperation, then Hart can already accommodate it under the harm principle, and Devlin has failed to establish “moral harm” as a genuinely separate category. She also noted that Marcus had not specified the threshold at which a norm-violation becomes cascade-risk rather than merely unpopular, which means his argument risks licensing prohibition of anything that makes the majority sufficiently uncomfortable. Marcus replied that Hart faces an exactly parallel calibration problem—distinguishing harm from offense requires just as much culturally loaded judgment—so if Priya demands a principled line for Devlin, she owes a symmetric one for Hart. He then added a further point: Priya was assuming that social cooperation is the only legitimate collective good, whereas Devlin’s deeper claim is that some societies value a shared moral identity as an intrinsic component of the good life, not merely as an instrumental coordination device.

The group split and I couldn’t referee cleanly. Please go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about what. Specifically: is Marcus’s “normative cascade / moral externality” framing a legitimate refinement of Devlin, or does it indeed collapse into Hart’s harm principle? Does Priya’s demand for a non-circular threshold expose a real weakness in Marcus’s position, or does it apply symmetrically to Hart’s harm/offense distinction? And is Marcus’s final move—appealing to the intrinsic value of shared moral identity—a successful response to Hart, or does it simply restate the moralism Hart was trying to reject? Do not just declare an overall winner; say what both parties are missing, perhaps something about whether the empirical claims embedded in Devlin’s thesis are even falsifiable in principle, or about how pluralistic societies manage coordination without uniform morality.
