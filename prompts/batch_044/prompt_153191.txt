I'm preparing the final exam for my third-year seminar, "Descartes and the Boundaries of Mind," and I want an essay question that moves well beyond the "animals are clockwork" summary every student has encountered in survey courses. Over the term we have examined the mechanistic physiology of reflex and circulation—vital heat, animal spirits, and the hydraulic action of nerves—alongside the argument in Part V of the Discourse that language-use and genuinely adaptive reason are the only sure signs of a rational soul. We have also debated the interpretive problem of whether Descartes denies animals any soul whatsoever, including a "sensitive" or vegetative principle, or only the rational one, and whether his account of human voluntary motion mediated through the pineal gland can be consistently separated from the purely reflexive motion he permits in beasts. Please design a 45-minute, 25-mark essay question that forces students to evaluate this specific tension: since both human and animal bodies operate through identical mechanical principles—animal spirits, vital heat, and nerve-based reflex—what principled reason can Descartes offer for claiming that the very same bodily movements require the substantial union of a rational soul in a human, yet require no soul at all in an animal? The prompt must be answerable by a well-prepared student without outside research, but it should be impossible to answer well by merely reciting that "Descartes thought animals are machines"; instead, it must require engagement with the physiological details and with the distinction between responsive behavior and genuinely voluntary action mediated by a soul. Provide three things: (1) the exam question exactly as it would appear to students, written in clear philosophical prose and specifying the word-length expectation (around 1,000 words); (2) a model answer that lists the specific argumentative moves a top response must make—especially the appeal to the pineal gland's unique interaction with the rational soul, the criterion of adaptive, general-purpose reason versus fixed reflex, and the explicit denial of a sensitive soul in animals; and (3) a marking scheme that allocates points to these moves and explicitly names the errors that should cost marks, such as conflating Descartes with modern behaviorism, treating animal spirits as mental rather than purely mechanical entities, misrepresenting language-use as a sufficient rather than a necessary criterion for mind, or failing to explain why mechanistic explanation does not apply uniformly across both species.
