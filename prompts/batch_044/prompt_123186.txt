Ive been poking around in philosophy since I retired from the lab — was a mollecular biologist for thirty-odd years — and I keep getting stuck on this argument about Kripkes naming stuff that feels like it collapses into circularity. Here is where my head is at. (1) Proper names like 'Aristotle' are rigid designators, so they latch onto the same actual individual in every possible world where he exists, regardless of what descriptions fit. (2) If I turn out to be wrong that Aristotle taught Alexander, that doesnt mean 'Aristotle' refers to someone else; it just means I misapplied a description. (3) Now take a biological kind term like 'Homo sapiens'. If it works the same way, it rigidly designates our actual evolutionary lineage, not whatever happens to share our behaviour or genome layout. (4) So imagine a counter-example planet where creatures evolve with identical traits — same social behaviour, same genome structure — but from a completely separate evolutionary tree. By the rigid-designator story they arent Homo sapiens, even though every observable feature matches. (5) From this it follows that 'all members of Homo sapiens are mortal' is necessary a posteriori: mortality belongs to the actual lineage we picked out empirically, and the alien look-alikes dont count as counter-examples because theyre not in the extension of the term. (6) But this makes taxonomy circular: I spent my career comparing DNA to classify species, yet on this view the empirical work only reveals who is already in the rigidly designated set, rather than defining the kind. (7) So either rigid designation saves necessary biological truths at the cost of emptying taxonomy of empirical content, or Ive smuggled a mistake in somewhere between steps three and five. Which premise should I drop, and what does dropping it cost me for the idea that scientific kind-terms work like proper names?
