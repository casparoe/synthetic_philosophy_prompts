For an upper-level philosophy seminar of about fourteen students meeting for ninety minutes, design a structured classroom activity that makes participants feel the tension between Plutarch's practice of using paired biography for moral learning and what I will call hagiographic compression: the structural tendency of exemplary biography to present a life as morally static, suppressing temporal development, contradiction, and recovery that genuine moral learning seems to require. Have students construct brief parallel lives of an assigned figure—real or invented—under two successive rule-sets. In the first round, participants operate in a Plutarchan mode, earning points for narrative coherence, clear virtue-signaling, and illuminating comparison with a counterpart life; in the second round, they operate in a developmental mode, earning points for tracking genuine moral change, documented failure, and gradual recovery over time, with coherence penalized if it flattens the timeline. Assign specific rotating roles—biographer, moral learner, internal critic, and parallel comparator—and supply a simple card-based tracking system for events and moral assessments. Provide a precise timeline fitting within ninety minutes: framing, first round, second round, direct comparison, and debrief. The debrief should map the experience onto Plutarch's actual use of paired lives to disclose enduring character, onto modern debates about whether biography can genuinely teach virtue if it must suppress moral history, and onto the specific risk that a learner imitates a static image rather than navigating a lived process. Be explicit about which feature of Plutarch's method—perhaps his conviction that comparison reveals stable moral nature rather than constructed narrative—the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply directly. Include the rules, materials, a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no real money, what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and an honest note on the activity's limits. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
