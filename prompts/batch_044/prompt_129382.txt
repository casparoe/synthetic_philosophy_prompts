ok so i just started helping design a museum wing on modern conflicts and got stuck on something weird. for interstate wars like ww2 we frame both sides as legitimate states with ethical narratives and we honor soldiers on both sides in some sections, but for insurgencies or civil wars we always frame the non-state fighters as criminals or terrorists with no standing. the curators say its just international law but it feels like we're smuggling in a deeper view about who gets to be a moral agent in conflict. does this practice presuppose that hegelian idea that war only has ethical meaning between sovereign states that recognize each other, and that everything else is just police action? could a cosmopolitan view license the same framing, or is the practice actually locked into that state-sovereignty theory? i also wonder if hegel's claim that the state can demand citizens risk their lives for its sovereignty depends on this exact divide—like, if insurgents are ethical agents too then the state's monopoly on sacrifice collapses. whats actually being presupposed here, and what would have to change in the exhibit if we replaced it with a view that treats all armed groups as potential ethical actors?
