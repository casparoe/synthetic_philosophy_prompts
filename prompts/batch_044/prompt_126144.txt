I write about science and technology policy, and I have been following the philosophical literature on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems—specifically the claim that learning algorithms can cause harms for which no human agent is morally culpable. From a legal standpoint, this strikes me as puzzling: product-liability doctrines and command-responsibility frameworks already assign causal and financial accountability to manufacturers or operators. So is the philosophical “gap” merely a category mistake—confusing the absence of blameworthy human intent with a structural feature of automation? Or does it identify a more specific deficit, perhaps a kind of predictive opacity that severs moral culpability even when causal chains remain intact? I am especially interested in whether the debate conflates two distinct notions: the failure to locate a culpable agent, and what one might call algorithmic answerability—the demand that an autonomous system provide an account of its action without anyone being properly blamed for it. If liability rules already address the first, does the second survive as a genuine philosophical problem, or is it only a new label for an old legal remedy?
