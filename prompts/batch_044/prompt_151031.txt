At a recent colloquium on early modern natural philosophy, I listened to a sustained disagreement between Dr. Elena Vasquez, a historian of science, and Professor Marcus Lin, a philosopher of science, regarding Johannes Kepler’s actual philosophical commitment to astrology. Vasquez argued that Kepler’s astrological practice was essentially a pragmatic revenue stream detached from his serious cosmological work. She noted that he regularly cast horoscopes for the Habsburg court and for figures such as Wallenstein, yet in private correspondence he characterized astrology as a foolish daughter that astronomy must support financially. She further observed that his own observational notebooks contain weather predictions tied to specific planetary configurations that repeatedly failed, suggesting that even by his own standards the predictive enterprise was empirically hollow.

Lin countered that this interpretation imposes a modern science-versus-pseudoscience boundary that Kepler would not have recognized. He pointed out that in the early seventeenth century there was no clear institutional separation between mathematical astronomy and natural astrology, and that Kepler wrote extensive defenses of a reformed, naturalistic astrology in which celestial bodies emit a non-mechanical influence—analogous to light—mediated through geometric aspects and acting upon a formative faculty in sublunary matter. On Lin’s reading, Kepler genuinely believed that planetary harmonies affected bodily humors and weather, but he insisted that rational human souls, possessing free will, lay outside this deterministic chain. The exemption, Lin maintained, was not an ad hoc rescue but structurally parallel to Kepler’s physics: celestial motive forces govern bodies, while rational agents respond to a different order of causation.

Vasquez replied that if this influence is genuinely non-mechanical and unfalsifiable, then Kepler has not reformed astrology but merely translated superstition into geometric language. She added that the soul’s exemption looks transparently motivated by Kepler’s Lutheran theology—preserving moral responsibility within an otherwise deterministic cosmos—rather than by any independent physical argument. Lin answered that demanding modern statistical correlation misses the point: Kepler’s project was cosmological and metaphysical, not experimental in the contemporary sense. The complexity of interacting terrestrial variables made isolation of single astrological effects practically impossible, so the absence of clean correlations in his notebooks reflects methodological limitation rather than theoretical failure.

They then asked me to adjudicate, and I found myself genuinely uncertain. Please go through their exchange point by point: who is right about Kepler’s motives, about whether his naturalistic mechanism is coherent or empty, about whether the exemption of rational souls saves or undermines his view, and about whether his empirical failures matter for assessing his position. Say who is more right overall. In addition, identify what both disputants are missing about how a working astronomer in 1600 might have integrated these commitments—particularly regarding whether astrology for Kepler was primarily a predictive science, a metaphysical reading of cosmic harmony, or a theological grammar for understanding human freedom within a determined physical order. Be specific about which of their premises conflict and where they are talking past each other.
