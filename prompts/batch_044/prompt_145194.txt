I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether rejecting speciesism necessarily commits you to massive, open-ended intervention in wild ecosystems. She defended a relational-dependency view: domestication creates special obligations because it makes animals dependent on us, but wild animals stand outside that relation, so anti-speciesism does not automatically license reshaping nature. I pushed back that if suffering is bad regardless of species membership, then the causal origin of suffering—human or natural—should not determine whether we have reason to prevent it. By the end we realized we were both assuming different answers to a deeper question: whether the capacity for suffering is a purely intrinsic property, or whether its moral relevance is partly constituted by relational and ecological context. Could you write a polished dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—call them A and B—who work through this tension? A should defend the relational asymmetry; B should defend the intrinsic-capacity view and press the wild-suffering challenge. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the other’s actual points rather than talking past them.
