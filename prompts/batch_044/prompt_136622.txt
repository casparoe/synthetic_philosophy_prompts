Take the American constitutional experience of National Prohibition and its repeal—the Eighteenth Amendment (ratified 1919, enforced through the Volstead Act) and the Twenty-First Amendment (proposed 1933, ratified by state conventions within months)—as a concrete episode of constitutional precommitment. Analyze it through Jon Elster's framework of Ulysses contracts: specify who or what in this institutional setting plays the role of Ulysses, what corresponds to the Sirens, and which constitutional structures serve as the mast and ropes. Give careful attention to the Twenty-First Amendment's distinctive use of state ratifying conventions rather than state legislatures: is this mechanism best understood as part of the original binding strategy—that is, a meta-precommitment about how precommitments may legitimately be undone—or rather as the deliberate dismantling of the ropes? Examine what the regime's brief lifespan (roughly fourteen years) reveals about Elster's conditions for stable rational precommitment, particularly regarding intertemporal continuity of the agent and the presence of overwhelming present desire. Then turn the analysis back upon the theory: does this rapid reversal demonstrate that constitutional Ulysses contracts are structurally self-defeating whenever the restrained preference is widely distributed, or does the convention procedure actually preserve a form of democratic legitimacy that rescues the framework? Be precise about where the analogy between an individual binding his future self and a democratic polity binding its successors breaks down—especially with respect to intergenerational legitimacy, collective agency, and whether the 1933 ratifiers can be treated as the same agent that the 1919 amendment sought to constrain.
