I'm preparing notes for an essay in a philosophy of history seminar, and I'd value critical feedback on the framing before I develop the full draft. The topic is the familiar conflict between a community's collective memory and the historical record, but I want to resist the usual formulation—when they conflict, which should give way?—because I think it presupposes that both are competing answers to the same question. Instead, I propose that we distinguish between mnemonic jurisdiction, by which I mean a community's authority to determine which past events remain normatively binding on present identity, and archival sovereignty, by which I mean the historiographic commitment to evidentiary answerability regardless of present identity-claims. To ground the distinction, I plan to build around a specific scenario: an indigenous community's oral tradition asserts continuous occupation of a territory for ten millennia, while archaeological and carbon-dating evidence indicates intermittent occupation with significant gaps. Rather than adjudicating which account is true, the essay asks which form of authority applies, and whether they can be separated without one collapsing into the other.

My concern is that this jurisdictional framing might appear to resolve the tension only by relocating it. I anticipate an objection that if mnemonic jurisdiction is permitted to override archival findings in practice—even if only by assigning them different normative weight—then it opens the door to a form of denialism that is merely re-described rather than addressed. I want to respond by arguing that jurisdiction does not falsify records but assigns them a functional role within communal practice, yet I am unsure whether this distinction holds when the community's identity-claims depend on factual assertions that are directly contradicted by the archive. Specifically, if continuous presence is not merely commemorated but asserted as a historical premise for present land claims, does my framework implicitly concede too much to a kind of epistemic relativism? I am also worried that the term mnemonic jurisdiction conflates legitimate political autonomy with an illegitimate epistemic privilege.

Could you comment on whether the jurisdictional distinction genuinely illuminates the conflict or merely displaces it, whether my archaeological example is adequate to bear the conceptual weight I place on it, and whether I have overlooked a more direct objection—perhaps that collective memory and historiography are not separate jurisdictions but interdependent practices that correct each other? I am not asking for a bibliography or for suggestions on further reading; I am trying to see whether the argument's architecture is sound before I commit to it.
