I'm trying to work out what I actually think about putting numbers on belief, going by gut reactions to cases. If an insurer prices flood risk using a hundred years of rainfall records, treating it as a precise probability feels right even if I don't understand the sensors. But if a doctor says there's a 30% chance of some ultra-rare disorder because one symptom lines up, the number feels like false precision; I'd rather say we just don't know. Now, a climate model gives 95% confidence by 2050 — I keep going back and forth on this one. Sometimes I think it's like the flood case and I should recieve the figure; other times, because I can't check the code and there's no century-long backtest, it feels closer to the doctor case and I want to reject quantification altogether. Then there's the weight thing: if ten more studies get piled into a meta-analysis but they all draw on the same dodgy behavioural dataset, the pooled interval gets tighter yet I feel less sure, not more. By contrast, when three independant economists I trust converge qualitatively on the claim that loose regulation wasn't the main driver of the 2008 crash — no numbers, just agreement — my confidence actually rises. Is there a coherent principle here, maybe drawing on Keynes's risk-uncertainty split and that weight-of-evidence idea? Or do I need to drop one verdict? And what should it say about a prediction market pricing some geopolitical event at 80% when the liquidity comes mostly from anonymous wallets running opaque algorithms?
