I want to test whether my judgments about life can be captured by a single coherent principle or whether some must be abandoned. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

A synthetic minimal bacterium engineered de novo with a genome far smaller than any naturally free-living organism, capable of metabolism and division but lacking evolutionary ancestors, counts as alive. A self-replicating robotic factory that mines ore, assembles copies of itself, and adapts its design to local conditions does not count, despite replication and evolutionary change. A prion strain that propagates between hosts, mutates, and is selected for resistance to proteases is not alive. A computer simulation running on physical hardware that implements genuine evolutionary dynamics—mutation, selection, and virtual metabolism—is not alive, even though its internal processes are structurally isomorphic to biological evolution. A human body in a persistent vegetative state with intact brainstem and metabolic function but no consciousness or possibility of recovery is alive as an organism, not merely artificially sustained. A forest fire that spreads via sparks, consumes fuel, and adjusts its spread pattern to wind conditions is not alive. But a tardigrade in anhydrobiosis, with metabolism suspended and undetectable for years, remains alive.

The principle I am drawn to is that life requires being a physically instantiated, chemically based system with organizational closure and metabolic self-maintenance, regardless of evolutionary origin or mental status. Yet this seems to need an ad hoc supplement to exclude the prion, and it is unclear whether it excludes the virtual simulation for the right reason—lack of chemistry—rather than for an arbitrary substrate restriction. Meanwhile, including the synthetic cell despite its artificial origin risks letting in the robot if I define closure too broadly, and adding a lineage requirement might disqualify the cell. Is there a precise formulation—perhaps centered on autopoiesis combined with a specific chemical substrate—that saves all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop: the synthetic cell, the prion exclusion, or the simulation exclusion? Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, apply it to an unconsidered borderline—a chemically synthesized protocell that maintains a lipid boundary and internal reaction cycles but was assembled atom by atom with no evolutionary history and no genetic material—and say whether that case forces any further revision.
