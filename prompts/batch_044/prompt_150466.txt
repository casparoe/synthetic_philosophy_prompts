Consider the following take-home essay prompt from an upper-level course on moral philosophy, together with a student's submitted answer.

Exam question: The paradox of deontology arises from the pairing of agent-centered restrictions—prohibitions against harming innocent persons even to prevent greater harm—with agent-centered prerogatives—permissions for agents to prioritize their own projects and welfare over aggregate welfare. Articulate the paradox precisely, distinguishing the normative structure of a restriction from that of a prerogative. Then evaluate the following proposal for resolving it: a threshold of collective agency, according to which an individual agent's duty not to harm weakens proportionally to the number of co-agents available to share the burden of prevention, so that no single agent must violate her own integrity. Does this threshold preserve the agent-relative structure that Samuel Scheffler defends, or does it collapse deontological ethics into a form of rule-consequentialism? In your answer, explain Scheffler's distinction between the two agent-centered reasons and address whether the threshold introduces a collective-optimization criterion that undermines the absolute character of restrictions.

Student answer: The paradox of deontology occurs because deontological ethics includes both agent-centered restrictions, which forbid harming one innocent person even to save five others, and agent-centered prerogatives, which allow an agent to pursue her own projects rather than always maximizing overall welfare. Scheffler resolves this tension by deriving both restrictions and prerogatives from the agent-relative value of personal integrity: the restriction prevents us from violating our own moral boundaries, while the prerogative permits us not to sacrifice those boundaries for the greater good. The proposed threshold of collective agency resolves the paradox by distributing the burden of harm-prevention across multiple agents. When more co-agents are present, each individual's restriction weakens proportionally, meaning no single agent must violate her integrity. This preserves Scheffler's agent-relative framework because the duty remains indexed to what each particular agent must do, not to aggregate outcomes. Moreover, this approach aligns with Kant's universal law formulation, since universalizing a maxim of shared burden produces no contradiction in conception. Nevertheless, the proposal ultimately fails: if the strength of my restriction depends on how many others are available to help, then whether I may kill one to save five varies with external circumstances beyond my control. This contingency undermines the absolute nature of deontological constraints and shows that the threshold collapses into rule-consequentialism. Therefore, Scheffler's original defense remains fully intact, and the paradox stands unresolved.
