You are delivering a seven-minute toast at the retirement dinner of a moral philosopher who spent her career defending G. E. Moore's claim that friendship and the appreciation of beautiful things are the only goods with intrinsic value, and that their value is an organic unity — not reducible to the sum of pleasures or achievements they happen to produce. The audience is split evenly between fellow value theorists, who will recognise every allusion to Moore's account of intrinsic value, and family members who have never read philosophy but know her as someone who turned down promotions to preserve her long walks with old friends and her Sunday museum visits. Write the toast so that Moore's idea does genuine structural work: you must honour her professional contributions without treating them as merely instrumental to happiness or status, and you must speak of her friendships and her love of art without reducing them to the cause of her success. Do not resolve the tension falsely; let the audience feel that her life exemplifies a kind of value that outstrips both conventional career metrics and private sentiment. Write in British English, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
