Schrödinger, drawing on Advaita Vedanta, defended the view that consciousness is not merely interconnected but numerically singular—there is, in a strict metaphysical sense, only one consciousness, with apparent multiplicity arising from limiting conditions analogous to facets of a single crystal. This position is rarely integrated into contemporary philosophy of mind. Take Schrödinger’s monistic claim seriously and examine its implications for the combination problem in panpsychism—the puzzle of how micro-level experiential subjects could combine to constitute macro-level subjects like human beings. Describe the most important implications, specifically: (i) whether the combination problem dissolves if one denies that there are plural micro-subjects to begin with, or whether it re-emerges in a different form; (ii) whether this dissolution renders other minds epistemically inaccessible, and if so, whether that is a fatal cost or a feature consistent with Vedantic ontology; and (iii) how Schrödinger’s crystal-facet analogy fares against modern relationalist or structuralist accounts of phenomenal consciousness that attempt to preserve both unity and multiplicity without invoking a singular substrate. Your response should avoid merely summarizing Vedanta doctrine or standard panpsychist taxonomies, and should instead trace the conceptual tension between a singular-consciousness ontology and the conditions under which recognizing another mind remains coherent.
