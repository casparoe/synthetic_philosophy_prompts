I have recently begun volunteering with a small educational archive that curates bureaucratic documentation of state violence, and in preparing exhibition materials I found myself returning to a conceptual tension I first encountered in a graduate seminar on atrocity and modernity. The tension lies between the claim—central to the Historikerstreit—that the Holocaust must be treated as radically unique and therefore resistant to comparison with other genocides or totalitarian crimes, and Zygmunt Bauman’s argument that the genocide was not a premodern regression but rather an extreme manifestation of modern bureaucratic rationality, moral distancing, and the division of labor. I am struggling to hold both positions simultaneously without collapsing one into the other, and I need to understand what follows conceptually from accepting their joint validity.

Specifically, I want to explore the implications of framing the Holocaust as both historically incomparable—thereby blocking straightforward comparative policy learning—and structurally reproducible through modern administrative logic, which seems to demand universalist safeguards. Could you describe the most important implications of this dual framing for three related problems: first, how moral responsibility should be assigned within bureaucratic systems that can produce unprecedented violence through ordinary organizational procedures; second, whether comparative historical analysis has any legitimate role if uniqueness claims are taken seriously; and third, how contemporary institutions might design safeguards against administratively mediated violence when the threat is both singular and systemic? I am especially concerned with whether this combination forces a paradox between particularist remembrance and universalist prevention, and how that paradox might reshape our obligations to document, compare, and intervene in institutional design.
