As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest, I have adopted a tightly restricted deflationary position on truth: within quantitative predictive modeling—whether ensemble climate projections or particle-filter state estimation—I treat the predicate “is true” as a redundant calibration marker that adds no epistemic content beyond a model’s validated predictive score. Thus, to assert “Model M is true of system S” conveys nothing more than “M’s outputs fall within the observed confidence interval for S,” and I deny that truth plays any explanatory or causal role in why successful models succeed. I do not extend this claim to ordinary language or ethics, only to the domain of formal scientific representation. Tell me precisely what would have to be false about this restricted view for it to collapse: which formal results or conceptual distinctions in logic and philosophy of science (for instance, regarding explanatory power, reference-fixing, or the inference from predictive success to approximate truth), which actual modeling practices, and which underlying intuitions about scientific realism would constitute decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments? Please distinguish the objections that a committed deflationist in this narrow domain could legitimately concede as damaging but survivable, from those that must be denied outright if the view is not to dissolve. In particular, rank the defeaters from fatal to manageable, indicate whether the position is well insulated or risks hidden collapse into either minimalist realism or pure instrumentalism, and identify the weakest point in my own framing—the objection I am most hoping the discussion avoids—that I should prepare to defend rather than ignore.
