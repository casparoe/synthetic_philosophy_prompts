In discussions of reproductive ethics and genetic selection, I keep encountering a particular argumentative trajectory that I cannot place precisely within the existing taxonomy of fallacies or conceptual shifts. The pattern begins with what seems to be a strictly permissive stance: parents ought to be free to use available technologies to select for traits that improve a child’s expected welfare, but this is framed purely as an exercise of reproductive liberty, compatible with parents who choose not to select at all. Then, often in the very same framework, a second principle is introduced—something like a duty of beneficence—stating that if parents can avoid serious disadvantage or secure a significantly better life for their child, they have strong reason, perhaps even an obligation, to do so. The two principles are defended separately, each with its own intuitive appeal, yet together they appear to generate pressure that collapses the initial permission into a default expectation: selecting becomes the responsible choice, and declining it requires special justification. I have seen this happen both in abstract debates about embryo selection and in applied discussions of carrier screening, where the language shifts subtly from “parents may choose” to “parents should want the best for their child,” without anyone explicitly defending the bridge between liberty and duty.

What strikes me is that this is not quite the slippery slope, because the intermediate steps are not speculative predictions but independently argued moral claims—freedom is valuable, welfare matters, parents are responsible for their children’s prospects. Nor is it straightforward equivocation, since the terms “choice” and “obligation” are not being traded back and forth in a single sentence. It also differs from ordinary function creep in technology, because the creep here is normative rather than institutional: the technology’s capabilities stay fixed, but the moral status of using them changes. I am therefore wondering whether there is an established concept—perhaps in bioethics, political philosophy, or the philosophy of technology—that captures this specific dynamic, where a liberal permission is gradually transformed into a beneficent requirement through the logic of available means and parental responsibility. If there is no precise term, I would like to know what the nearest neighboring concepts are—how it relates to, and differs from, the standard slippery slope, from the non-identity problem’s framing of future persons, and from debates about positive versus negative duties in procreative contexts—and whether one could coin a term with clear boundary conditions, specifying exactly when the shift has occurred and when it has not.

Please do not simply supply a label; I am after the carving itself. What exactly is this phenomenon, what is it not, and where do its edges lie? If an established concept fits only imperfectly, explain how the described trajectory diverges from it in its mechanics and in the kind of justification it employs. If nothing established fits well, propose a definition that distinguishes this normative slide from adjacent argumentative moves, and illustrate with boundary cases—situations that look similar but should be excluded because the transformation from permission to obligation is either absent or operates through a different mechanism altogether.
