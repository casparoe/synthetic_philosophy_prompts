Hey, I'm a phd student in phil of econ and decision theory stuff, and I've been trying to apply Arrow's conditions to intertemporal choice and I think I've walked into an argument that proves way too much. Here it is:

(1) Arrow's impossibility result applies to any rule that aggregates a profile of complete, transitive orderings into a single ordering, provided unrestricted domain, Pareto, IIA, and non-dictatorship hold.

(2) Take a rational agent at t0 choosing among life-paths L1, L2, L3. Along each path there are future agent-stages S1...Sn, and each Si has prefferences over outcomes—including counterfactual ones for paths not taken.

(3) The present agent's choice can be modeled as aggregrating all these Si-orderings into one current decision.

(4) With three or more paths, Arrow says this aggregation must be dictatorial—one Si dominates—unless one of the four conditions fails.

(5) Non-dictatorship seems non-negotiable: if my 80-year-old self is the dictator, then my present self is just a slave to whatever that stage wants, which kills autonomy.

(6) Unrestricted domain and Pareto look safe: we shouldn't restrict which preference profiles are possible, and if every future self prefers L1 to L2 then so should I now.

(7) So IIA has to be the one that breaks. That means my preference between L1 and L2 can flip depending on whether L3 is on the table.

(8) But for an individual, IIA failure is just framing vulnerability—I'm open to Dutch-book style exploitation and money pumps.

(9) So Arrow seems to show that rational intertemporal agency is impossible: either I'm dictatorial (not autonomous) or I'm frame-sensitive (not rational).

(10) But people clearly do make long-term plans without being either. So there must be a hidden mistake in applying Arrow inside one life.

Which step is wrong, and what does giving it up cost? I'd especially like to keep (5) and (6) if possible, but I'm open to paying somewhere else.
