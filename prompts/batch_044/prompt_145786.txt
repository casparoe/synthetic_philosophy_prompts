At a philosophy seminar, two colleagues—Dr. Maya Chen and Dr. Leo Vasquez—got into a sustained disagreement about whether genuinely hard choices between incommensurable values can ever be rationally resolved, or whether they necessarily collapse into arbitrariness or hidden comparability.

Maya argued that when first-order values are truly incommensurable—say, artistic integrity and financial security in a career decision—there is no fact of the matter about which is better, not even equality. Rational choice theory, she said, requires at least a partial ordering, but hard choices present a comparability gap that no covering value can bridge without changing the subject. She claimed that appeals to “narrative coherence” or “life-structure fit” merely relocate the incommensurability upward: if coherence can be achieved through either an artistic or a financial life-structure, then the meta-criterion offers no rational resolution. For Maya, the appropriate response is not comparison but reasoned commitment—choosing while fully acknowledging that the unchosen option retains its uncanceled value, and maintaining a kind of counterfactual attachment to it. She insisted this attachment is not irrational regret but a structural feature of respecting goods that remain genuinely incomparable.

Leo replied that Maya conflates the absence of a single metric with the absence of all rational guidance. He granted that first-order values may lack a common scale, but argued that practical reason operates with domain-relative weights and second-order structural preferences—such as preferring to be the kind of agent whose commitments are stable over time—without reducing those first-order values to that scale. He said Maya’s counterfactual attachment is descriptively accurate but normatively unstable: if the unchosen value genuinely retains its full claim, then every hard choice produces an irreparable rational defect, which undermines the possibility of a coherent life plan. Leo proposed that rational resolution comes not from ranking values in isolation but from assessing which option is more robustly supported by the agent’s existing practical identity, and that this assessment need not pretend to compare the values directly.

Maya countered that Leo’s “robust support” is just a disguised lexical ordering: once practical identity breaks ties, one has introduced a hidden comparative principle (“better fits my identity”), which dissolves incommensurability by substituting one value for another. She added that Leo’s fear of irreparable rational defect assumes rationality requires complete justification, whereas her view accepts that rational agents can live with unresolved conflict.

Leo answered that accepting unresolved conflict as permanent is not maturity but a failure of practical reason; that Maya’s view makes every hard choice a kind of existential leap that cannot be criticized or defended, which renders practical deliberation pointless.
