For the final examination of an upper-level seminar on the logic of normative systems and legal self-reference, draft one essay question that forces students to diagnose a structural paradox rather than summarize familiar literature. Present the following concrete hypothetical: a constitutional order contains (i) a first-order amendment rule that may revise any constitutional provision except itself, and (ii) a second-order meta-amendment rule that may revise the first-order rule, but only if the proposed revision preserves the system's "closure property"—defined within the prompt as the capacity to derive every valid legal norm from the base rules without external normative appeal. Ask students to determine whether this two-tier hierarchy genuinely resolves the paradox of legal self-amendment or merely displaces it onto the meta-level, and require them to use the analogy with Hofstadter's game of Nomic to illuminate what hierarchical solutions can and cannot achieve. Insist that they distinguish syntactic self-reference, in which a rule mentions itself in its own formulation, from semantic self-reference, in which a rule's conditions of application depend on the total set of rules that includes it, and ask them to identify which form operates in the hypothetical and why that distinction matters for assessing stability. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and should call for an argument of roughly 700 words. Provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks. For each point, state the specific analytical move that earns credit, the mark weight, and the one-line description of the typical error that should lose those marks—such as conflating the two tiers into a flat circularity, treating Nomic merely as an illustration rather than as a diagnostic tool for hierarchical failure, or describing self-reference without distinguishing its syntactic and semantic variants. The difficulty should come from the structural diagnosis, not from recall of named doctrines.
