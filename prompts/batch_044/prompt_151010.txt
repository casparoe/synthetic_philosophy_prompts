I'm an auditor for a regional autonomous-systems licensing board, and I review deployment proposals—warehouse robotics, clinical triage tools, adaptive logistics—on a quarterly cycle. For each proposal I have about thirty minutes, I work from the org chart and the technical spec, and I have to initial every step of my assessment before signing off or referring the file to a three-person ethics panel. I need a reusable decision procedure that turns the philosophical literature on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems into something I can actually run: a sequence of questions in fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer, clear stop points where I block the proposal, clear hand-off points where the issue is values rather than engineering, and explicit flags for the judgment calls that are dressed up as procedural steps.

Ground it in the actual distinctions that matter—causal traceability of harm, normative answerability to an affected party, and remedial addressability after an incident—but do not assume I have the original papers in front of me. The procedure should distinguish design-time traceability from runtime adaptive behavior, and it should treat human oversight not as a binary "is there a human?" check but as an architectural feature: who can intervene, on what timescale, with what authority.

Structure it as a flowchart in words. Start with autonomy classification. Then check design-time agent identification. Then check runtime adaptation and learning loops. Then check oversight architecture (veto, audit, or only post-hoc review). Then check post-incident remediation pathways. At each stage, specify the factual check, the values call if any, and the failure mode—where this step will miss a gap or invent one.

Apply it to three proposals: first, a fixed-route warehouse robot with deterministic planning and a named floor supervisor who holds real-time veto authority; second, a clinical triage system that updates its weighting scheme weekly from live hospital data, produces individual priority scores without case-level explanations, and allows the clinical director only monthly batch audits with no per-case veto; third, construct a hypothetical case—make it specific—where the procedure returns "clear with conditions" but the result is one I should find unacceptable, so I can see exactly where my values diverge from the framework and where the procedure admits it cannot settle the matter.

Also tell me: what do I do when an answer is missing in ten minutes? Where do I mark "insufficient data"? What are the procedure's known misfires—cases where it will falsely clear a dangerous gap or block a benign system? And be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: it should not pretend to resolve whether a given level of risk is acceptable, only whether the architecture makes someone answerable for it.

Write this in a tone that respects that I'm not a philosopher but I am used to reading rationalist forums—precise about uncertainty, honest about simplification, and unwilling to let a checklist pretend it has eliminated judgment.
