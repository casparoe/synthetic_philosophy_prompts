I've been trying to determine whether karma, construed strictly as a normative theory of moral desert rather than as a metaphysical mechanism of cosmic causation, can yield coherent verdicts across a range of cases. Here are my intuitive judgments. First, a corporate executive who systematically defrauds employees for decades, amasses enormous wealth, and dies peacefully in old age: I maintain that he genuinely deserves suffering, and that the absence of any visible penalty represents a deficit in the moral order, not proof that desert is illusory. Second, an engineer who accidentally triggers a catastrophic failure despite rigorous safety checks: I judge that she bears no negative desert for the harm, even though the causal outcome is identical to deliberate sabotage. Third, a donor who gives away a fortune solely for social prestige and tax optimization: I think she acquires far less moral credit than a donor motivated by compassion, though she may still deserve public recognition. Fourth, a combatant who kills only in direct self-defense but within an unjust war: I feel she is not blameworthy for the specific killing, yet she still carries some desert-related burden for having participated in an unjust cause. Fifth, I am drawn to a cumulative model—if someone builds substantial positive desert over a lifetime, a single grave transgression should not erase all prior moral standing, just as a single good act should not cancel a history of cruelty. But this conflicts with another conviction: if we allow for rebirth, a person born into extreme suffering despite an exemplary previous life does not necessarily have a hidden moral debt; sometimes suffering strikes me as genuinely undeserved, regardless of prior lives.

The case that keeps me oscillating involves indirect, systemic harm. Consider a policymaker who implements an economic program she sincerely believes will reduce poverty, but which—through mechanisms she negligently failed to investigate—causes widespread environmental damage that will kill thousands in future generations. Does she accumulate negative desert in proportion to the total harm caused, only in proportion to her culpable negligence in belief-formation, or not at all because she lacked malicious intent?

Please take a committed stance. State the precise principle—perhaps involving a distinction between direct and indirect desert, a cumulative accounting framework with non-cancellation conditions, or a modified intentionalist criterion—that best accommodates these verdicts. Identify which clause addresses each of the five judgments above, deliver a definite prediction for the policymaker case, and be explicit about which one or two of my intuitions you regard as inconsistent and therefore reject in favor of simplicity. Do not merely list competing theories of karma; defend one reconstructed principle and show exactly where it forces me to revise my gut reactions.
