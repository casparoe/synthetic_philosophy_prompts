In epistemological debates about bootstrapping and so-called easy knowledge, philosophers frequently invoke the image of pulling oneself up by one's bootstraps: physically, the maneuver is impossible because there is no external fixed point, so the reasoning is taken to be viciously circular and incapable of yielding genuine justification. Skeptics use this analogy to argue that we cannot non-circularly validate our perceptual faculties and thereby secure knowledge of the external world; anti-skeptics respond that epistemic support can be holistic, iterative, or self-correcting in ways that physical pulling is not. I want a precise audit of this analogy: lay out the correspondence table—what corresponds to the bootstraps, the boots, the ground, the pulling motion, and the missing external anchor—then separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine circular reasoning from those that are merely decorative. In particular, examine whether statistical bootstrapping (using a sample to estimate properties of the very distribution it was drawn from) or compiler self-hosting (a compiler compiling its own source) supplies a closer analog, since both are formally circular yet methodologically sound. Does importing either of these show that the physical-bootstraps image quietly assumes justification must be linear and non-circular? And once all the mappings and breaks are on the table, does the skeptical conclusion—that bootstrapping reasoning is illegitimate—survive, or was it the analogy doing all the work?
