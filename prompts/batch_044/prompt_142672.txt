The student was asked: In Oskar Lange's iterative trial-and-error model, central planners adjust prices in response to reported shortages and surpluses, with factory managers instructed to follow a parametric rule—minimize average cost—while treating announced prices as given. First, explain why Lange believed this procedure could solve the allocation problem without private capital markets, distinguishing the computational task from the incentive task. Second, evaluate the claim that Friedrich von Hayek's critique of dispersed, tacit knowledge and Ludwig von Mises's 1920 argument about the impossibility of rational economic calculation without exchange of capital goods are essentially two descriptions of the same informational deficit. Third, consider a modified scenario in which managers can strategically misreport their local cost curves to influence future price announcements: does this strategic dimension expose a weakness that Lange's parametric framing suppresses, and is that weakness closer to Hayek's concern or to Mises's?

The student's answer reads: Lange's model works because planners don't need to know every local production function; they just need managers to report surpluses and shortages, then adjust prices like a computer running an algorithm until supply equals demand. The managers follow the rule of minimizing average cost at the given prices, which makes them price-takers, just like firms in perfect competition. This solves Mises's problem, because Mises said socialism fails since there are no markets for capital goods and therefore no rational prices for them. Lange showed you can compute shadow prices administratively, so Mises is wrong. Now, Hayek in 1945 said knowledge is dispersed and tacit, but this is really the same point as Mises—both say planners can't gather all the information. The only difference is that Hayek talked about local knowledge, while Mises talked about capital goods prices. In the strategic misreporting scenario, managers might lie about costs to get easier quotas, but that's an incentive problem, not an information problem. So Lange's model handles information through iteration, and the strategic issue is a separate motivational failure that neither Mises nor Hayek really addressed. Actually, Hayek's point was about incentives to reveal knowledge, so it might overlap, but fundamentally both critics miss that computation can replace markets.
