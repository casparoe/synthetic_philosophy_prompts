For my science-and-ideas column, I am drafting the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and need a 90-minute essay question that cannot be answered by reciting Popper’s textbook definition. Write a prompt that introduces a hypothetical revision to the problem of verisimilitude: replace Popper’s logical-content and falsity-content metrics with a predictive-proximity measure, defined as the expected divergence between a theory’s predictions and those of the true theory across a specified range of counterfactual conditions. The question must require students to do four things: first, identify the precise logical feature that allows Popper’s original account to rank certain false theories above the true theory; second, articulate exactly what the predictive-proximity revision changes about the measurement; third, argue whether this solves the ranking problem or merely relocates it—especially regarding how counterfactual conditions are selected without circular reliance on the very theory whose proximity is being measured; and fourth, evaluate the objection that rendering verisimilitude empirically tractable in this way sacrifices the philosophical distinction between logical strength and approximate truth. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words, followed by a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit conceptual moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks at each step.
