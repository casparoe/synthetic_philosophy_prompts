I am a doctoral researcher in political philosophy examining how classical liberal frameworks apply to contemporary digital public spheres. I want to explore a hypothetical reconstruction: how would John Stuart Mill likely have evaluated a recently proposed platform-design mechanism—one that replaces engagement-based ranking with an argument-reconstruction gate, requiring users to demonstrate accurate comprehension of an opposing position before they may post a reply? Mill never encountered algorithmic curation, social-media echo chambers, or the modern problem of whether opponents argue in good faith, yet his commitments to the harm principle, to the value of open confrontation with error, and to suspicion of social coercion are well established. Given these commitments, do you think he would view the reconstruction gate as a legitimate institutional scaffold that protects interlocutors from misrepresentation and fosters genuine deliberation? Or would he regard it as an impermissible prior restraint that assumes bad faith rather than remedying it? More specifically, do you think Mill would judge this mechanism to address the root of political polarisation—the erosion of mutual epistemic charity—or merely to displace that failure onto a procedural filter? Please provide your analysis in British English, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am seeking a conceptual reconstruction rather than a literature review.
