After a recent experience in a collective-bargaining context made the logic of credible commitment suddenly concrete rather than abstract, I have been asked to lead a single ninety-minute workshop for a reading group of non-specialist graduate students—mostly in law and public policy—on the hitchhiker thought experiment associated with Derek Parfit and the broader puzzle of rational irrationality. Do not explain the argument to me; plan how I should build it with them. Lay out the dependency order of the conceptual pieces: which distinction—between evidential and causal reasoning, or between ex ante rationality and ex post evaluation—must be secured before the payoff structure is introduced, and which can only make sense afterward. Choose the one carrying scenario that should bear the full explanatory weight; defend why the canonical stranded-driver example should be modified or replaced for this audience, specifying exactly which structural feature of the original (the temporal separation of decision and execution, the irrelevance of reputation mechanisms, the visibility of the agent's reasoning to the other party) the substitute must preserve and which it may relax. Map the predictable misconceptions—perhaps that irrationality here is mere weakness of will, that reputation solves the problem, that the case assumes determinism, or that it is just a prisoner's dilemma in disguise—and state precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off. Provide one closing question that tests whether the students have grasped the structural tension rather than merely retelling the story. Finally, identify which strand of the broader discussion—such as the analogy to personal identity over time, the appeal to self-defeating theories of rationality, or the comparison with Newcomb's problem—should be explicitly bracketed and acknowledged as omitted at this level.
