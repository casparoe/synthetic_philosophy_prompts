Hey — I'm a retired chemist, only got into philosphy about a year ago, so bear with me. At my local discussion group last week two people got stuck arguing about whether self-deception is even possible. I'll call them Mara and Leo, neither of them philosophers by trade.

Mara said it's basically impossible: if you genuinely believe your spouse is faithfull, you can't simultaneously believe they're cheating, because belief aims at truth and you'd notice the contradiction right in your own head. She said what people call self-deception is really just weighing evidence badly because you want something to be true — that's bias, not literally lying to yourself.

Leo pushed back. He thinks self-deception is real, but not because there's some hidden part of you that knows the truth. Instead he talked about "epistemic compartmentalization" — not a split personality, just that different contexts activate different belief-sets, and you never let them talk to each other. He gave the example of a researcher who keeps running experiments she knows are flawed but never lets that knowledge touch her grant applications.

Mara replied that Leo just renamed motivated reasoning. She said compartmentalization sounds fancy but it's still just avoiding uncomfortable thoughts, and if you genuinely avoid them then you don't actually hold the contradictory belief — you just have gaps. So there's no deception, just ignorance you engineered yourself.

Leo came back with: but if you engineered that ignorance specifically to protect a belief you depend on emotionally, and you'd correct it imediately if it served your interests, that's not ordinary ignorance. He called it "strategic epistemic neglect" — the intentionality makes it deception even without a second self.

Mara said that smuggles intentionality back in where it doesn't belong. You're not intentionally neglecting anything; you're just bad at facing hard truths, like everyone else. She said calling it strategic makes it sound like there's a planner inside, which is exactly the myth we should reject.

Then Leo asked: if it's not strategic, why does the ignorance always line up so neatly with what you'd want to be true? That correlation needs explaining, and random bias doesn't really cut it.

They turned to me since I was just listening, but I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each step — especially whether Leo's strategic epistemic neglect is a real thing or just a relabeling of bias — and tell me who's right about what and who comes out more right overall. Please answer in American English, and no need for fancy citations, I wouldn't know half of them anyway.
