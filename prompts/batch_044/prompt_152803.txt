I serve on the Certification Appeals Panel of the Institute for Industrial Metrology, and we must reach a binding decision on whether to certify FractureNet, a predictive-maintenance algorithm intended to forecast catastrophic failure in ceramic turbine blades. I have no formal training in philosophy, but the technical brief has made clear that the case hinges on conceptual problems surrounding dispositions—specifically, whether the system can be said to predict a blade's fragility when that disposition may be masked by adaptive dampeners on the production line, or finked by triggered annealers that restructure the crystal lattice precisely when stress is applied. The manufacturer contends that certification should evaluate predictive accuracy under these real-world conditions, since the interferences are part of the operational environment; our safety officers reply that this confuses the underlying dispositional property with its contingent suppression or transformation, and that a meaningful certification requires testing under interference-stripped conditions in which neither masks nor finks operate.

The brief introduces two further engineering concepts that seem philosophically loaded: dispositional shadowing, a double-masked state in which suppressed fracture manifestations yield false negative readings, and counterfactual inertia, whereby an annealer alters the very conditions that would have manifested the disposition. I need a formal memo addressed to the Panel recommending a concrete course—full approval, conditional approval, or denial—with explicit criteria that reviewers can apply consistently. The memo should treat the distinction between a genuinely absent disposition and one that is merely masked or finked as practically relevant to our safety standard, and it should include an honest assessment of the strongest consideration favouring the opposing view. Please write in a register appropriate to an institutional record, using British English conventions, and do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as the document must stand on its own reasoning for colleagues without philosophical specialisation.
