For my final-year module on comparative epistemology and South Asian philosophy, I have been asked to apply anekāntavāda to a concrete historical crisis rather than treating it as an abstract metaphysical doctrine. I have chosen the deliberations of the 1947 Boundary Commission for the Partition of British India, particularly the competing territorial claims advanced by the Muslim League, the Indian National Congress, and representatives of the Sikh community. Each party offered mutually exclusive descriptions of the same districts—some defined by religious majority, others by river systems or railway networks—yet all demanded exclusive sovereignty over them.

Could you analyse this episode through the lens of anekāntavāda, specifically using the saptabhaṅgī or seven-fold conditional predication? I want a disciplined mapping: which of the Commission’s actual options—demographic partition, geographical division, or deferred arbitration—correspond to the modes of assertion, denial, and simultaneous assertion-and-denial (syāt asti ca nāsti ca)? More importantly, I want to know where the framework explains the Commission’s failure to reach agreement and where it misdescribes the conflict. For instance, does treating each communal claim as conditionally true illuminate why Cyril Radcliffe could not produce a boundary acceptable to all parties, or does syādvāda merely redescribe an irreducible deadlock?

I am also interested in the normative dimension: does anekāntavāda’s association with ahiṃsā impose any constraint on how boundaries ought to have been drawn—say, favouring lines that minimise forced displacement—or is the doctrine strictly descriptive? Crucially, what does the episode reveal about the theory itself? Can syādvāda function in a zero-sum context where all parties accept the conditional framing yet still demand exclusive territorial control? Where exactly is the theory silent, and does the violence of August 1947 show that many-sided truth collapses under conditions of exclusive sovereignty?
