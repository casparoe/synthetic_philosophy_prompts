After a long argument with a friend about why the debate between Browning’s situational account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Goldhagen’s cultural explanation of perpetrator motivation never seems to advance, I want a structural diagnosis rather than a verdict. Browning treats peer pressure, authority structures, and career incentives as the decisive causes of killing, allowing widespread antisemitism only as background context. Goldhagen treats a culturally specific, elimination-oriented antisemitism as the primary causal driver, arguing that ordinary Germans were not passive instruments of situation but willing participants shaped by ideology. Both concede the presence of the other’s factor, yet neither grants it genuine explanatory weight. Is this stalemate substantive—about what testimony and wartime behavior actually establish—or is it a hidden disagreement about the standards of explanation in historical sociology, for instance whether explaining violence requires isolating a counterfactual difference-maker at the individual level versus articulating a collective framework that renders the violence intelligible? Might the sides also be answering different questions—one asking why these particular men pulled the trigger, the other asking why this society generated executioners—under a single heading? If the impasse is verbal or methodological rather than empirical, what conceptual distinction, archival counterfactual test, or reconceptualization could move it, or should we conclude that the disagreement is irremediable?
