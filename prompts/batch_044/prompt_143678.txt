I have been thinking on my own about the modern revival of Stoicism, particularly the way it is presented in recent books and online discussions as a practical philosophy of life rather than a religious cosmology, and I have tried to articulate for myself exactly where I stand without relying on any particular academic tradition. I hold the following convictions, and I am unsure whether they form a coherent whole. First, I accept the Stoic dichotomy of control in a strict form: the only things genuinely within my power are my present judgments, my assent to impressions, and the direction of my will; everything else—my health, my material circumstances, the opinions of others, and the behavior of people I love—lies outside it. Second, I think modern Stoicism ought to be entirely detachable from its ancient physical and metaphysical framework; I see no reason to commit to determinism, to a universal logos, or to any particular theology in order to practice the ethical and psychological exercises, and I want the philosophy to remain viable for someone with a broadly naturalistic worldview. Third, I believe that every instance of destructive emotion—anxiety, resentment, envy, or despair—arises solely from my own judgment that some external object is genuinely good or genuinely bad for me, which means that such emotions are always within my power to eliminate by reforming that judgment. Fourth, I am persuaded by contemporary cognitive science that emotional responses frequently occur through rapid, automatic appraisal mechanisms that operate before conscious evaluation; in other words, a person can feel a surge of fear or anger prior to forming any explicit belief about the situation at hand. Fifth, I think that loving one's family or working for social justice is fully compatible with Stoicism, provided one treats these objects as preferred indifferents—things that are naturally valuable and worthy of effort, but not necessary conditions for one's tranquility or virtue.

I wonder whether these five positions hang together or whether some of them pull against each other in ways I have not noticed. Please examine them pair by pair and tell me which combinations are genuinely in tension, which combinations only appear tense but dissolve upon careful analysis, and for each real conflict, how severe it is and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency. If there is one single commitment whose abandonment or modification would resolve the greatest number of difficulties, I would like to know which one it is and what I would have to give up. I ask that you present your answer as continuous prose only, in the manner of a formal letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
