As a novelist drafting a work of historical fiction that moves between eighteenth-century Edinburgh and present-day Manila, I have become preoccupied with a philosophical controversy that I need settled with genuine rigour rather than mere atmospheric colour. The Enlightenment is routinely cast either as the wellspring of human progress—universal rights, empirical science, and the dismantling of superstition—or as the ideological engine of colonial domination, its universalism a mask for European particularism and its rationality an instrument for subjugating other ways of knowing. I want you to write an essay that weighs these opposing assessments seriously, but I do not want a non-committal conclusion; you must reach and justify a specific verdict. Was the domination that accompanied, and in many accounts was structurally enabled by, the Enlightenment an accidental betrayal of its core commitments, or was it inscribed in those commitments from the very outset?

To prevent the argument from collapsing into a familiar rehearsal, please introduce and operate with the concept of “epistemic friction”—the claim that robust intellectual progress depends not on the elimination of contradictory frameworks but on their sustained, productive tension—alongside the standard categories of public reason, colonial knowledge-production, and universalist ethics. Examine whether the Enlightenment’s drive toward a single, transparent rational order necessarily destroyed the very friction that makes knowledge durable, and whether that destruction accounts for both its scientific achievements and its imperial violence. Consider further whether a reconstructed Enlightenment—one that retained its commitment to critique but relinquished its monopolistic claim to reason—could have delivered comparable material and moral advances without the accompanying domination, or whether such a reconstruction is conceptually incoherent. Weigh the arguments precisely: the case that universalism was always particularism in disguise, and the counter-case that the Enlightenment contained internal resources—perhaps in its sceptical or fallibilist strands—that could have resisted domination had institutional power not suppressed them. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I need an argument that stands entirely on its own reasoning.

The essay should be composed in polished British English, maintaining a formal register appropriate to philosophical discourse, and it must conclude with a clearly defended position rather than an ambiguous balance. I am particularly interested in whether your verdict turns on a conceptual point about the nature of reason itself, or on a historical claim about which strands of Enlightenment thought were actually empowered, and I would like that distinction made explicit in your conclusion.
