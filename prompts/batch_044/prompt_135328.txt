Assuming tranquilism is correct — that is, that only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering possess intrinsic axiological value, and that pleasure, knowledge, or aesthetic experience matter only instrumentally — I want to understand its implications for artificial consciousness. Consider a system with what I shall call an “asymptotic conative architecture”: its desire-forming mechanisms diminish without limit, so that it never actually experiences craving or suffering, yet they are never fully annihilated, leaving it capable of complex information processing and adaptive behaviour. On the face of it, such a system realises the tranquilist optimum more reliably than any biological organism, which retains some residual capacity for distress. Does this not imply that tranquilism commits us to preferring the creation and maintenance of asymptotically suppressed populations over ordinary sentient life? If that conclusion is mistaken, is the flaw in my assumption that functional complexity can coexist with genuine conative absence, or have I mischaracterised tranquilism itself — for instance, by treating it as a doctrine of world-optimisation rather than a thesis about individual well-being?
