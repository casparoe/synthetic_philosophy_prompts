Consider a hypothetical institutional framework I will call Calibrated Desert: social roles and rewards are assigned by a transparent, correctable algorithm that estimates each individual's counterfactual contribution to aggregate welfare across a range of possible social positions, explicitly filtering out advantages that are purely brute-luck genetic or social in origin unless those advantages also generate welfare gains that redistribution could not replicate. The philosophical claim is that, within this framework, individuals genuinely deserve their assigned positions in a non-instrumental, morally basic sense—meaning the distribution remains justified even if we stipulate a single correctable algorithmic error that assigns a clearly deserving candidate to a lower-reward role. Give me your actual credence that this claim is true, decomposed into four terms: (a) the probability that moral desert is ever non-instrumentally basic rather than purely conventional or efficiency-tracking; (b) the probability that counterfactual welfare contribution is the correct desert-base, conditional on desert being basic; (c) the probability that filtering brute-luck advantages is compatible with maintaining a genuinely contribution-based standard, rather than collapsing the principle into one that tracks equality of opportunity or pure need; and (d) the probability that a correctable single error does not vitiate the non-instrumental justification, conditional on (a) through (c) holding. Provide each term as a specific number between 0 and 1 with a one-line justification, show briefly how they combine into your overall credence, and identify the term in which you are least confident. Finally, specify what empirical finding about human motivation or perceived algorithmic legitimacy—discoverable without external sources—would most shift your overall number, and in which direction.
