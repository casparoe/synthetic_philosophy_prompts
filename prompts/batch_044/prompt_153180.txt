I'm facilitating a seminar on medieval Islamic metaphysics, and we've reached an impasse that feels structural rather than merely interpretive. The subject is al-Ghazālī's account of causation—specifically, his rejection in his well-known critique of the philosophers of the claim that created things possess necessary causal powers that operate independently of divine will. Within our group, the disagreement has crystallized into three positions that share the same textual evidence but never converge. One camp holds that al-Ghazālī is a thoroughgoing occasionalist: fire possesses no efficacy whatsoever, and God directly creates the burning in each particular instance. A second camp argues he is only an epistemic skeptic, denying that human reason can establish necessary connections between events while remaining agnostic about whether created things have hidden dispositional powers. A third camp, influenced by recent distinctions in modal metaphysics, proposes that al-Ghazālī's target is specifically the modal status of causal relations—he denies that the connection between fire and burning is metaphysically necessary—without thereby denying that fire has some contingent causal role.

We have debated these readings for weeks without movement. Every side can accommodate the same passages about God as the true agent and about the customary course of nature. What I want is not another textual defense but a diagnosis of the stall itself. Is this disagreement substantive—about what actually exists in the world—or is it partly verbal, turning on different understandings of terms like 'power,' 'cause,' and 'necessary'? Are the three camps answering genuinely different questions—one metaphysical, one epistemological, one theological—while using the same vocabulary? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement about modality that none of us has articulated clearly? If the impasse is conceptual, what specific distinction—say, between ontological occasionalism, where created things lack causal powers entirely, and modal occasionalism, where they possess powers but only contingently—would need to be made explicit to move the debate? And if it turns out that the text genuinely underdetermines these distinctions, should we conclude that no resolution is possible, or that progress requires shifting the question entirely?
