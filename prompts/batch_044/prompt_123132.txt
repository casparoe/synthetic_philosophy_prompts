I work as the curriculum and ethics chair at a small design institute, and about twice a semester we get into these messy disputes where a student or alumni uses a compositional method they picked up from a professor's unpublished workshop notes — like a specific way of layering translucent glazes to create depth, or a mental heuristic for arranging negative space — and then licenses the result commercially. The professor claims they "stole my idea," but the student says they just learned a technique, like learning a receipe. I need a decision procedre my three-person review panel can actually run in under an hour, grounded in whatever philosophy of property actually says about owning ideas versus expressions, with clear steps: what to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where we stop with a no, where we hand it off because it's a values call rather than a fact, and where we escalate to legal because it's actually contract breach not philosophy.

The procedre should handle the hard cases: techniques that are partly functional and partly expressive, ideas that only really work in one specific medium, and cases where the professor never wrote anything down but taught it orally in a closed session. Then apply it to three scenarios: (1) the glaze-layering technique I mentioned, used in a ceramic line sold nationally; (2) a student who takes the professor's unpublished negative-space heuristic — basically a mental checklist — and builds an entire branding agency around it; and (3) a case where the student literally copied the professor's handwritten workshop notes and sold them as a "masterclass PDF," but claims they only copied the ideas inside, not the expressive form of the notes. I especially want the procedre to flag where it'll misfire, like when a technique is so tightly bound to one person's style that separating idea from expression is practically impossible, and I want one of those three applications to come out supporting the student in a way the professor will hate but the logic demands anyway. Keep it practical: we don't have lawyers on the panel, just educators, and we need to sign off or escalate within 48 hours.
