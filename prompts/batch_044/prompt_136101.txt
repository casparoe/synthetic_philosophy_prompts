I am preparing a paper on assertion norms and have become convinced that much of the literature rests on a framing error. The knowledge norm holds that one should assert p only if one knows p; since knowledge entails belief, and in non-Gettier cases entails truth, this seems to make both the belief norm (assert only what you believe) and the truth norm (assert only what is true) logically redundant. They are not competing rules but nested conditions—knowledge is the strictest filter, and satisfying it automatically satisfies the other two. If so, treating them as rival norms of assertion is like treating "no felons" and "no convicted thieves" as rival hiring criteria when the latter is a subset of the former. Yet this raises a tension I cannot dissolve. In scientific practice, researchers routinely assert results derived from institutional verification pipelines—statistical outputs, peer-reviewed datasets—without having personally reproduced every step. Their mental state is belief grounded in testimony and division of labor, not first-person knowledge of each claim. If the knowledge norm is primary, these assertions appear impermissible, which seems to condemn ordinary scientific discourse. Does this mean the norm must be indexed to a kind of collective or distributed knowledge, or does it instead reveal that the belief norm was never a redundant subset but an independent constraint that permits assertions precisely when personal knowledge is absent? Please separate what is established in the philosophy of language and social epistemology from views that remain actively disputed, and avoid presenting any one account of testimonial knowledge transmission as the settled consensus.
