A philosophy podcast summarized Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism roughly like this: in his critique of the philosophers, Al-Ghazālī argues that fire has no intrinsic causal power to burn cotton; God creates the blackening and the burning directly on each occasion, with no intermediate link. He denies all necessary connections in nature, so science is impossible and we observe only correlations that God habitually maintains. Every event is therefore a separate miracle, and he would reject any genuine law of nature, since God could alter the course without violating rational principle. His view is essentially Malebranche's occasionalism with an added theological emphasis, and the only reason nature looks regular is divine custom. The host then concluded that this is self-defeating: if Al-Ghazālī truly believed there were no necessary connections, he could not consistently speak of God habitually maintaining them, because habit presupposes the very causation he denies.

Does his position really make empirical knowledge impossible in the way described, or does he allow a different kind of regularity? How close is he actually to Malebranche, and where do they diverge? Finally, does the host's charge of self-defeat land, or does it depend on misreading what Al-Ghazālī means by the habitual course of nature?
