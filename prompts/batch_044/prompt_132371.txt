The dispute over whether qualia are an incoherent posit, anchored in Dennett's well-known Chase-and-Sanborn intuition pump—where two longtime Maxwell House coffee tasters diverge, one claiming his taste has refined and the other that the coffee itself now tastes different—has persisted for decades without apparent resolution. Proponents of phenomenal consciousness argue that the scenario demonstrates at most a shift in descriptive judgment or introspective access, leaving intact an intrinsic, non-functional quality of experience; opponents reply that any mental feature that can be quined—that is, denied without leaving any detectable explanatory or predictive gap—was never a substantive property, but rather a theoretical artifact sustained by unchecked first-person authority. The literature now largely cycles through familiar moves: direct acquaintance is invoked against explanatory redundancy, and intersubjective verification is invoked against private ontology.

Rather than adjudicating the case, I want a structural diagnosis. Is this stalemate verbal, with both sides using qualia for different targets—one for an epistemically private but real phenomenal character, the other for a redundant placeholder in a functional or neural explanation? Is it substantive but hidden, the real conflict being not about consciousness itself but about the conditions for legitimate metaphysical commitment—whether introspective report can establish ontological presence, or whether only properties integrated into third-person explanatory frameworks count? Or are the sides answering different questions altogether—one phenomenological, about what it is like for taste to change, the other metaphysical, about what kinds of properties a science of mind may admit—while treating them as one?

If your diagnosis is that the disagreement is verbal or cross-purpose, what precise distinction—perhaps between phenomenal transparency and explanatory accessibility, or between epistemic privilege and ontological weight—would expose the equivocation? If it is substantive, is there any result—empirical, formal, or conceptual—that should be expected to move it, such as a demonstrated dissociation between neural correlates of taste-change reports and reports of unchanged phenomenal quality? Or is the impasse a sign that the dispute rests on bedrock explanatory intuitions about what an adequate account of mind must include, meaning that no argument or experiment should rationally be expected to dislodge either side? Give the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each camp actually claims about the Chase-and-Sanborn case, and state honestly what, if anything, would count as progress.
