Construct a decision tree for an advanced seminar on moral worth that classifies positions according to how they treat the causal origin and epistemic accessibility of an agent's motivating reason. The root node should ask whether moral worth requires that the normative reason for which the action is right also serve as the agent's operative motivating reason. From there, branch on whether that motivating reason must be occurrently accessible to conscious reflection, or whether it can be mediated by subpersonal, habitual, or socially distributed mechanisms without loss of worth. Introduce a further split that distinguishes positions allowing motivational outsourcing—cases in which the right motivation is caused or sustained partly by external structures such as trusted moral testimony, institutional prompts, or cognitive prosthetics—from positions requiring that the agent personally appropriate the reason through some form of second-order endorsement or internal deliberative integration. Each leaf should state a named or unnamed position, together with a single sentence identifying its most awkward commitment regarding moral expertise, augmented cognition, or habitual virtue. The tree should contain at least six leaves, and you should explicitly flag any pair of positions that your branching logic cannot cleanly separate, particularly any view that accepts subpersonal mediation but rejects social outsourcing versus one that accepts both. Frame every node as a concrete conceptual question that a student could answer without knowing the corresponding philosophical label, and build the taxonomy from the logical space of distinctions rather than from a list of historical figures. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
