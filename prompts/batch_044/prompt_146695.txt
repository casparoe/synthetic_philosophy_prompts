Write a dialogue between two equally informed ethicists, Mara and Lin, who disagree about whether beef consumption causes less wild-animal suffering than chicken consumption once crop-cultivation mortality is included. Mara argues that because beef cattle can graze on grassland unsuited to crops, beef production avoids the high rodent and insect mortality associated with growing feed grains for poultry, so per calorie beef is likely less harmful to wild animals. Lin responds that this comparison treats all wild deaths as fungible regardless of causal structure: she contends that crop-field deaths are direct and foreseeable results of land conversion, whereas beef's larger land footprint produces diffuse ecological suffering—fragmentation, trophic disruption, and climate-mediated range shifts—that body-count metrics miss entirely, and that ignoring this structural asymmetry makes the beef-versus-chicken calculus misleading. Have them press each other on whether moral responsibility should track direct killing, indirect ecological displacement, or some weighted combination, and on whether replaceability arguments apply differently to wild rodents versus domesticated chickens.
