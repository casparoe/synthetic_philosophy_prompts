Write a villanelle (nineteen lines: five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the closing couplet) that takes as its subject the unresolved tension in Isaac Newton's private theology between his cosmological doctrine and his Christology. One refrain should express his view—developed in his writings on natural philosophy—that absolute, infinite, homogeneous space functions as the divine sensorium, the immovable, omnipresent medium through which God perceives and acts upon every part of the universe simultaneously. The other refrain should express his anti-Trinitarian conviction—evident in his manuscript theological works—that the Father alone is the one supreme God and that the Son is a subordinate, created mediator, so that Trinitarian consubstantiality represents a corruption of pure monotheism analogous to the prophetic symbols of ecclesiastical corruption he interpreted in Daniel and Revelation. Structure the poem so that the two refrains turn past each other through the tercets without synthesis or subordination, and let the final quatrain bring them together in a single stanza that highlights the strangeness of a unitary, spatially omnipresent deity who nonetheless works through a historically subordinate agent. The poem should avoid generic devotional language and should be specific enough that a reader familiar with Newton's arguments for absolute space, his rejection of Nicene consubstantiality, and his historical-allegorical reading of biblical prophecy would recognize the exact commitments being dramatized, while remaining a coherent poem for someone who knows none of this background. Do not include citations, footnotes, or bibliographic references.
