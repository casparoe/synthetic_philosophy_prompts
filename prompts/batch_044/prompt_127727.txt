Please describe the most important implications of accepting free-floating rationales—normative reasons that obtain whether or not any particular agent recognizes, holds, or is positioned to act on them—for the moral architecture of collective obligations in large-scale ecological restoration. Specifically, consider a scenario in which a degraded watershed ought to be restored, but no single contractor, government body, or local community can be said to bear an agent-relative reason to undertake the work; the rationale, if it exists, is detached from any reasoner. Address three issues: first, whether such free-floating reasons can generate directed obligations for specific institutional actors in the absence of contractual, historical, or causal ties that normally ground duty; second, whether this view undermines contractualist and constructivist accounts that derive all normative force from actual or hypothetical agreements among reasoners; and third, how the possibility of beneficiary-independent reasons affects our assessment of obligations toward future generations and non-sentient ecological systems that cannot themselves be reasoners. The analysis should remain conceptual rather than empirical—no citations, bibliographies, or policy documents are needed—but should take seriously the tension between agent-neutral normativity and the practical requirements of institutional design.
