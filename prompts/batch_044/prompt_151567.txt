You are a senior philosopher of education specializing in moral learning from narrative and the ethics of biography. Think carefully. Before constructing the procedure, briefly outline—no more than three sentences—the core commitments of the “exemplary friction” framework: that moral learning from lives requires maintaining productive tension between (i) the subject’s proximity to the learner, (ii) the subject’s irreducible alterity, and (iii) narrative openness about whether the life counts as good, and that hagiography collapses this tension by resolving alterity and premature closure.

Your deliverable is a reusable decision procedure that a secondary-school humanities coordinator can run—without further consultation—on any proposed biographical text, exhibit, or multimedia life-story for an ethics curriculum. The coordinator makes these decisions weekly, under time pressure, must defend outcomes to parents and a school board, and has no research staff. Ground the procedure in exemplary friction: translate each of its three commitments into an ordered operational test. Present it as a sequence of steps, each with (a) the question, (b) what counts as a sufficient answer, (c) the action if yes or no, and (d) an explicit flag—VALUE or FACT—indicating whether the step eliminates judgment or requires it. The procedure must yield one of three terminal verdicts: EXCLUDE, PROCEED WITH FRAMING CONDITIONS, or HAND TO COORDINATOR. It must stop with a clear answer in most cases but flag where values, not theory, must decide.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) a widely used celebratory biography of a humanitarian leader that omits documented moral failures and presents the life as an unambiguous model; (2) a recent “warts-and-all” biography of a scientist whose discoveries are intertwined with documented cruelty to collaborators and family members; and (3) a community-nominated biography of a local founder that lacks narrative openness—presenting the subject as unequivocally virtuous—but carries strong parental and board support. Include at least one case where the procedure produces a verdict the coordinator will dislike. After the applications, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—for example, which moral failures are disqualifying, how to weigh community pressure, or whether proximity should be calibrated differently for older students.

Present your three case analyses in a table with columns: Case, Procedure Verdict, Decisive Step, Judgment Override Required? (Yes/No with one-line reason). Then provide a JSON object with exactly these fields: procedure_name (string), total_steps (integer), value_judgment_steps (array of integers), unresolvable_case_type (string), primary_limitation (string).

Write formally and concisely. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not quote verbatim from any real text.
