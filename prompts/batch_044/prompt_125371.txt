After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether I owe more to my compatriots than to strangers across borders—she insisted that any preference is merely tribalism with a flag, while I kept reaching for something about shared institutions and historical cooperation that felt different from raw solidarity, yet I could not articulate the distinction—we agreed that the standard framing of nationalism versus cosmopolitanism was obscuring more than it revealed. I am writing a proposal for a four-month independent research project, to be completed as a thirty-five to forty-page essay suitable as a doctoral writing sample, that asks the following precise question: can the moral claim that compatriots have special obligations be decomposed into two conceptually distinct sources—what I will call diachronic reciprocity, arising from multi-generational institutional cooperation and inherited burdens and benefits, and synchronic solidarity, arising from present shared vulnerability and collective risk—and does this decomposition allow a limited defense of compatriot priority that survives cosmopolitan objections, or does it collapse once the two are shown to be inseparable or normatively equivalent?

The existing debate, as I understand it, treats partiality toward compatriots as a single phenomenon to be either justified or debunked: nationalist arguments invoke shared culture, history, or political community, while cosmopolitan arguments hold that birth within borders is morally arbitrary and that distributive obligations should be global. But because neither side systematically separates backward-looking reciprocity for past institutional contribution from forward-looking solidarity for present shared fate, cosmopolitan critiques that target the arbitrariness of borders do not clearly address whether obligations can arise from participation in intergenerational schemes, and nationalist defenses that invoke cultural identity do not clearly establish that reciprocity generates distributive priorities distinct from universal humanitarian concern. Consequently, the core dispute remains open: it is unclear whether there is a genuinely distinct normative basis for compatriot claims, or whether all apparent particularism reduces either to unjustified favoritism or to universal obligations applied locally.

My approach will be conceptual reconstruction rather than empirical study or historical exegesis. In the first phase, I will formalize the distinction between diachronic reciprocity and synchronic solidarity using general resources from social contract theory and intergenerational justice, without depending on specific canonical texts or bibliographic surveys, and I will specify the conditions under which each could generate distributive obligations. In the second phase, I will test whether the diachronic claim can survive three standard cosmopolitan objections—moral arbitrariness of birth, the problem of non-voluntary membership in inherited institutions, and the charge that inherited advantage is itself unjust—while remaining genuinely distinct from universal solidarity. In the third phase, I will examine whether synchronic solidarity, once universalized, necessarily collapses into cosmopolitanism or can remain locally bounded without contradiction. A successful result would be either a rigorous framework that isolates a defensible, limited form of compatriot priority grounded solely in reciprocity, or a demonstration that the distinction is unstable and that all particularist obligations reduce to either solidarity or arbitrariness. The project fails if the distinction proves conceptually incoherent—for instance, if reciprocity always presupposes present solidarity—or if it yields no difference in practical allocation principles between compatriots and strangers. Given the purely conceptual nature of the work and the absence of empirical data requirements, this scope is realistic for four months of focused research, and the deliverable—whether positive or negative—will be a sharply framed contribution to a debate that currently conflates questions it ought to separate.
