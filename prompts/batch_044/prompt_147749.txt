I have recently become the primary caregiver for my older brother, who is in the early stages of a rare neurodegenerative disorder that dissolves autobiographical memory while leaving emotional responsiveness largely intact. Watching him retreat into moments of recognition that he cannot articulate has made me fixate on a question I rarely see addressed in standard debates about data rights or surveillance: is there a form of privacy that protects not the concealment of facts, but the integrity of a consciousness that is becoming unreadable even to itself?

Write a short story—approximately 800 to 1,000 words—set in a near-future residential care institution that offers patients with this condition a voluntary “narrative continuity implant.” The device accesses residual neural patterns to reconstruct fragments of lost memory and broadcasts them in real time to designated family members, ostensibly to restore relational intimacy and assist clinical monitoring. Center the story on one patient who refuses activation, not from fear of exposure or technological distrust, but from an intuition that the value of his remaining inner life depends precisely on its resistance to being reconstructed, interpreted, and shared. His reasoning should be philosophically serious rather than sentimental; avoid framing the refusal as mere stubbornness or anti-scientific prejudice.

Through his interactions with a physician who views the implant as an unambiguous good and with a family member who experiences his refusal as a withdrawal of intimacy, explore whether privacy in this limit case remains a matter of informational control, or whether it transforms into something closer to the right to remain a mystery—to others, but especially to oneself—so that a kind of dignity can persist that does not depend on being fully understood. You may invent the medical and technological details, but ground the conflict in the lived texture of progressive memory loss and in the specific ethical problem of interpretive access to a mind that is losing its own coherence.
