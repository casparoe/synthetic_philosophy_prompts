I have been working through Kierkegaard's critique of modern social life for a seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy and media, and I find myself repeatedly stuck on a particular passage concerning what he calls the public and the role of the press in creating it. The text, which belongs to the essay often translated as The Present Age, describes the press as producing an abstract collective entity that holds opinions without owning them—an audience that can speak about everything precisely because it is committed to nothing. In this passage, Kierkegaard seems to claim that the anonymity enabled by print culture does not merely hide individual speakers but actually dissolves personal responsibility into a kind of phantom community, and that this process generates a leveling in which genuine individuality is flattened into statistical equivalence.

My study group has been debating how to apply this analysis to contemporary structures of communication, and one of my colleagues recently drew a comparison with federated peer-review platforms in open science, where evaluation is distributed across nodes with no central editorial authority and where reviewers often remain pseudonymous. She argued that Kierkegaard's public is essentially the same phenomenon: a decentralized audience that acquires influence without assuming accountability. Another member of the group objected that this comparison imports technological determinism into a text that is really about spiritual and ethical posture rather than about media architecture. I am not sure which reading is more faithful to the passage itself, because Kierkegaard appears to treat the press as both an institutional mechanism and an existential symptom, and I cannot tell whether he thinks the technology causes the leveling or merely reveals a pre-existing modern tendency toward abstraction.

Could you help me understand the passage more precisely? I would especially like clarification on whether Kierkegaard attributes the loss of individual responsibility primarily to the structural anonymity that the press makes possible, or whether he regards the press as only expressing a deeper aesthetic-existential condition in which people already prefer to hide behind collective opinion. I would also appreciate an explanation of how his concept of the public relates to his broader distinction between the crowd and the single individual, and whether being part of the public in this sense is conceptually different from being part of a crowd, or whether the two are essentially the same form of inauthentic togetherness. Please do not point me to secondary literature; I am looking for a direct conceptual unpacking of the ideas in the passage.
