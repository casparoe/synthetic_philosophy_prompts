I'm grading exams for a friend who's running a seminar on liberalism and empire, and I want to be consistent with the rubric. The question was: How did Mill square his defense of individual liberty and anti-paternalism in works like On Liberty with his decades-long support for British administration in India? In your answer, discuss his distinction between societies ready for representative government and those in a backward state, his view of the East India Company's role, and what his reaction to the 1858 Government of India Act tells us about whether he saw despotism as temporary or permanent.

Student answer:
John Stuart Mill worked for the East India Company for over thirty years, so his India writings aren't abstract theory. In On Liberty he defends the harm principle and rejects paternalism for mature individuals, but in his political writings he says representative government only works for societies that have reached an advanced stage of civilization. For societies in backward states — which he applies to India — despotism is appropriate because the people aren't capable of self-government. He thinks this incapacity is largely racial: Indians have a temperament that makes them permanently unsuited for progressive improvement, so foreign rule isn't just temporary tutoring but a permanent necessity.

Mill defended the East India Company's administration because it was technically expert and insulated from democratic politics. When Parliament passed the 1858 Act transferring authority directly to the Crown after the Mutiny, Mill welcomed the change because he believed Crown rule would be more authoritarian and therefore more effective at maintaining order in a backward society. So there's no real contradiction in his liberalism: rights and self-government are rewards for civilizational maturity, and since Indians haven't achieved that maturity and never will due to their race, they legitimately fall outside the scope of On Liberty's protections.
