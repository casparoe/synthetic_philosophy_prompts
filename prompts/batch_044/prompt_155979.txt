Consider the institutional practice of a university mathematics outreach center that maintains a permanent interactive exhibit titled “Hilbert’s Hotel.” In the exhibit, visitors enter a corridor of numbered rooms (1, 2, 3, …) arranged in a loop so that the numbering never terminates visually; staff guide groups through a physical “shift” demonstration in which every current occupant moves to the room with double their current number, freeing all odd-numbered rooms to accommodate a busload of new arrivals. The exhibit’s signage explains this as proof that an actually infinite collection can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself, and references Cantor’s result that there is no largest cardinal. At the same time, the building’s occupancy permit, fire-safety protocols, and staff scheduling treat the number of rooms and visitors as strictly finite: the loop is a physical illusion created by a rotating partition, the “infinite” shift is performed only on a finite batch of ten demonstrator rooms, and the center’s budget justifies the exhibit by citing “potential engagement growth” rather than any actual infinite audience. Meanwhile, the gift shop sells a “Cantor Dust” construction kit that instructs users to iteratively remove the middle third of a line segment; after three iterations the kit declares that the remaining points form an uncountable set of zero length, despite the physical material being a visibly finite collection of fragments.

What philosophical commitments about actual versus potential infinity does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics: does the exhibit treat ℵ₀ as a completed actual totality, or merely as a regulative idea governing an unending process? Does its appeal to Cantor’s theorem commit it to actualism about all infinite cardinals, or can a finitist—who accepts the mathematical coherence of the argument but denies that it describes any actually existing collection—make sense of the pedagogical demonstration? Examine whether the safety and budgetary protocols, which are rigorously finitist, are in tension with the pedagogical narrative, which seems to presuppose that infinite sets are as real as the rooms themselves. If the center replaced its implicit view with a consistently potentialist or strictly finitist framework—say, one that treats Hilbert’s hotel only as a thought experiment about unbounded extendability rather than about an existing hotel—what specific changes would be required not only in the signage and demonstrations, but in the institutional justification for maintaining the exhibit at all? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined: could a Platonist actualist, a modal structuralist, and a formalist who regards infinity as a useful fiction all license the same physical layout and the same shift demonstration, or does the concrete design of the loop and the rotating partition secretly favor one interpretation?
