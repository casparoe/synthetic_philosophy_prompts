After a reading group session on Aristotle's economic writings—Politics I.8–10 and Nicomachean Ethics V.5—two other members kept arguing about whether his critique of chrematistike applies to modern yield-bearing digital assets, specifically a hypothetical system where labor-hours are tokenized and automatically accrue a governance dividend tied to productivity indices. I stayed out of it, but they asked me to report the exchange and get an outside adjudication, so here is what they said, without my own slant.

Mara took the scale-based view: Aristotle distinguishes oikonomia (household management bounded by need, chreia) from chrematistike (acquisition without natural limit). She argued that the moral line is internal to the agent's purpose—if you accumulate only up to the point where your household and civic obligations are met, even instruments that technically generate returns are not chrematistike, because chrematistike is defined by the absence of a limit, not by the mechanism of growth. She compared it to Aristotle's knife: using it to open letters does not violate its telos, and earning a modest yield on stored labor-value does not violate money's purpose as a medium of exchange. Leo countered that this misses Aristotle's metaphysical point about tokos—money breeding money is unnatural not because of the agent's intention but because currency (nomisma) is a convention, like a measuring rod, that has no internal principle of growth; if it grows, the measure itself is corrupted, which undermines the justice of exchange. He said Mara is treating unnaturalness as a functional failure when it is actually a category mistake: you cannot have a measuring stick that reproduces itself without distorting the very equality it was invented to preserve. Mara replied that Leo is smuggling in a biological reproductive analogy that Aristotle does not actually commit to—money does not literally reproduce, and if the yield is algorithmically capped at the rate of real productive growth, then the token still functions as a stable measure, not a distorted one.

They went back and forth on whether need (chreia) supplies an external limit that saves the practice, or whether any self-generating return violates the nature of the instrument regardless of caps. I honestly do not know who is right at each step. Also, say who is more right overall, and whether there is a better way to frame the question that does not depend on treating money as either purely conventional or purely instrumental.
