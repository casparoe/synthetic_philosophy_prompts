I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a refined version of Samuel Scheffler's claim that human extinction is catastrophic partly because it severs the future continuation that gives present projects their point, not merely because of the deaths involved. Her specific twist is that she treats the harm as a kind of project truncation that applies only to open-ended, non-terminating endeavors — scientific research, artistic traditions, moral progress — and denies that extinction harms closed or self-completing projects in the same way. She also holds that this conjecture explains why a futureless world is worse for the living than a world where they personally survive but humanity dies out, reversing the usual intuition.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to restate the view. I want at least one question on how she distinguishes truncation harm from ordinary project failure, one on what she says to the objection that people in the last generation have no reason to continue open-ended projects if they know there is no future, and one on whether her view implies that childless people or those with only self-completing goals should care less about extinction. For each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — for instance, that truncation is structurally different from failure because the project lacks a successor state; that the last generation's motivation comes from the intrinsic value of the work rather than its instrumental continuation; and that caring about extinction depends on having adopted open-ended commitments rather than on having biological children. End with one question that presses her on whether the view can account for the intuition that extinction is still bad even if the last generation never learns about it.
