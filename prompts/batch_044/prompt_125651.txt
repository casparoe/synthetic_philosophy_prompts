I am a retired analytical chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun serious graduate study in philosophy of science. I am assisting in the design of an upper-level undergraduate examination for a philosophy of science seminar, and I wish to include one essay question on Hempel’s raven paradox—the problem of confirmation generated by the logical equivalence between a universal law and its contrapositive—without allowing students to answer by rote recitation. To prevent summary-level responses, please embed the paradox in a specific scientific-inference framing: present a hypothetical universal claim that all specimens of mineral type M exhibit fluorescence under ultraviolet light, and then present the observation of a clearly non-fluorescent object that is also clearly not mineral M (for instance, a fragment of organic tissue). The question should require students to construct, in this framing, the argument for why the observation appears to confirm the law about M, why that result is intuitively unacceptable, and then to evaluate two competing resolutions—first, Nicod’s criterion restricted by a projectability constraint on the classification of properties, and second, a subjective Bayesian analysis in which prior probabilities for both the law and the background taxonomy are made explicit. Crucially, students must be forced to identify a hidden cost in each resolution: that Nicod’s criterion tacitly presupposes a privileged partition of properties, and that the Bayesian resolution tacitly presupposes independence between the prior assigned to the law and the prior assigned to the taxonomic scheme that distinguishes M from non-M. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, appropriate for a 45-minute essay with an expected length of approximately 900 to 1,100 words; (2) a model answer of roughly 650 words that executes the required argumentative moves in the mineral-fluorescence framing; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that distinguishes an A-grade response from a B-grade response by specifying the exact moves required—correct derivation of the logical equivalence in the framing, precise articulation of Nicod’s criterion together with the projectability objection, correct deployment of Bayes’ theorem or likelihood reasoning, explicit identification of the independence assumption in the Bayesian case, and a concluding evaluative judgment on whether the paradox genuinely undermines naive inductivism—together with a one-line note on the characteristic student error for each component. The question must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should avoid reference to highly specialized or obscure literature outside the standard curriculum for such a course. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for an academic examination committee.
