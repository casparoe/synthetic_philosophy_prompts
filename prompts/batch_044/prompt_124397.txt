I've been thinking on my own about why certain false histories—ones that claim whole centuries are duplicated phantom copies, or that ancient civilizations were guided by hidden outsiders—can feel so convincing even when they are wrong. It seems to me the problem is not just missing or bad evidence, but that these accounts copy the formal clothing of real historiography: chronological tables, footnotes, technical vocabulary, even references to carbon dating or stratigraphy. The copy wears the same evidentiary voice as the original, so you cannot separate them just by looking at the surface. I would like a poem that makes this structural problem felt rather than merely stated. Write a poem of six quatrains, each with the same ABAB rhyme scheme and a similar rhythmic shape, arranged so that stanzas one through three describe a single historical arc—say, a city rising, being abandoned, then rediscovered—while stanzas four through six replay that same arc with identical rhymes and line lengths but altered names, shifted dates, and one inserted claim about hidden architects or phantom duplicates, so that the reader genuinely cannot tell which half is the "real" record and which is the copied one. Let the turn come in the fifth stanza, where the speaker realizes that demarcation fails not because some contradiction has been found, but because both versions speak in exactly the same grammar of proof. The idea should be rendered precisely enough that someone who understands the demarcation problem recognizes it, not just named. Please begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
