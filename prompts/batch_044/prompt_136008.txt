I recently joined the Clinical Neurorehabilitation Ethics and Funding Board at our university hospital, and over the past two months a particular proposal has made an abstract metaphysical debate I once studied feel uncomfortably concrete. The cognitive-rehabilitation unit is requesting twelve months of pilot funding for an intervention in which post-stroke patients practice guided intentional imagery—vividly visualizing specific limb movements—while receiving standard physical therapy. Their protocol documents claim that the mental imagery is not merely correlated with faster motor recovery but exerts an independent causal influence on cortical reorganization, operating through attentional modulation pathways that are not fully reducible to the concurrent physical inputs. I watched a close family member undergo a similar regimen after a minor ischemic event; whether coincidence or causation, the rapid functional improvement left me genuinely uncertain whether our institutional funding criteria should recognize mental states as legitimate therapeutic agents or treat them as explanatory redundancies that rigorous clinical science ought to exclude.

Our board’s charter requires us to evaluate proposals for both empirical rigor and conceptual coherence with the hospital’s physicalist framework for clinical explanation, a framework that implicitly relies on the causal exclusion argument: if every neural event has a complete sufficient physical cause, then mental states—whether conceived as emergent properties or as non-physical entities—are causally redundant and should not be cited as independent mechanisms of recovery. One faction of the board argues that approving this proposal would commit the institution to an ontologically costly form of non-reductive physicalism or systematic overdetermination that our guidelines reject; the opposing faction contends that the exclusion argument assumes a closure principle that may fail in open, self-modulating biological systems, and that rejecting the proposal on purely metaphysical grounds risks blocking a genuinely effective treatment. Before our quarterly meeting on the 28th, we must circulate a formal recommendation memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—containing a concrete course of action and implementation timeline, engaging the researchers’ causal claims with philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing them as metaphor, and addressing the strongest version of the exclusion-based objection: that even if mental imagery correlates robustly with recovery, all of its causal work is already performed by the neural processes that realize it, leaving no independent explanatory or therapeutic role for the mental state itself. At the same time, the memo should not treat physicalist closure as self-evident, since our mandate is to advise the institution on what it ought to do, not to settle metaphysics by fiat. Please draft this memo, recommending a specific decision and explaining how the board should navigate the tension between therapeutic efficacy and causal exclusion without simply restating either side.
