I'm running a philosophy film club and we're watching Groundhog Day next week, and I want to talk about meaning in life without just saying "it's like Sisyphus" and calling it a day. So I'd like to read the film through Camus's absurdism — the idea that we should imagine Sisyphus happy — but also against Susan Wolf's fitting fulfillment view, where meaning needs both objective worth and subjective engagement, and Thaddeus Metz's fundamentality theory, which says meaning comes from connecting your life to something valuable beyond yourself. Phil's arc is pretty clear: he exploits the loop, then despairs and tries to kill himself, then slowly builds skills and relationships until he breaks free after saving the town. Does the film actually vindicate Camus, or does it imply that repetition is only bearable once Phil finds objectively valuable activities he also cares about? I'm especially stuck on the suicide montage and the final perfect day: do they suggest that meaning requires escaping repetition entirely, as Metz might say by connecting to a larger narrative, or can the loop itself become meaningful if filled correctly? And where does the movie resist these philosophical frames — for instance, does the romantic resolution with Rita feel too neat, undermining the absurdist claim that no external reward saves us? I want a reading that takes both the film and the philospohy seriously, not reducing one to the other, and I'm curious what the movie adds to the debate that a straight argument can't capture. Maybe there's something about the comedy or the small-town setting that changes how we think about objective value?
