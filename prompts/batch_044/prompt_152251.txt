As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been working through decision theory and have constructed an argument around Good's theorem on the value of information that seems to prove far too much. The theorem establishes that when information is available without cost and might alter one's choice, rationality requires acquiring it before deciding; from this I derive the following chain: (1) For any decision problem with positive value of information, a rational agent must gather all free relevant data first. (2) Every practical decision—including the decision to gather data—admits of further free information, such as an immediate introspective check of one's preferences or a brief perceptual scan, so there is never a decision that lacks an upstream informational opportunity. (3) Applying the theorem recursively to the meta-decision about gathering information generates an infinite regress: one must first check whether to check, then check whether to check whether to check, with no terminal node. (4) Introspection and observation are reflexive and inexhaustible, so this regress is not merely theoretical but structurally unavoidable for any conscious agent. (5) Therefore rational agents can never complete the pre-decisional audit and are permanently paralyzed, which is absurd. I am reluctant to reject (1), since Good's mathematical proof is sound, and I am reluctant to reject (4), since mental reflexivity seems undeniable; yet together they yield decision paralysis. Which step contains the hidden equivocation, scope shift, or misapplication of the theorem, why did the mistake look plausible, and what does giving it up cost for the broader account of rational choice? Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
