After recently serving on an interdisciplinary working group that failed to reach consensus despite full disclosure of evidence and apparently rational participants, I have returned to the epistemic-game-theoretic literature on common knowledge of rationality. The debate over why practical agreement breaks down—whether through absence of a common prior, failure of common knowledge of rationality, or the static belief-revision structure of formal models—seems stalled: some theorists treat rationality as an internal consistency constraint on beliefs, others as an intersubjective standard that agents must reciprocally recognize in reasoning together. Is this stall substantive, verbal, or a case of different questions—one about formal conditions in a model, the other about cognitive accessibility in actual agents—being pursued under the single heading of agreement failure? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually asserts, together with an honest account of whether the impasse reveals an ill-posed question or a hidden conceptual distinction. In particular, does the distinction between model-level common knowledge and agent-level common recognition of rationality resolve anything, or does it merely rename the disagreement? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal result extending agreement theorems to bounded reasoning, a conceptual clarification of the two senses of rationality, or an argument that the question was never well formed?
