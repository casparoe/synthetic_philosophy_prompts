In Bayesian epistemology, the problem of old evidence has persisted since the 1980s without convergence: data already known before a theory's introduction—such as Mercury's orbital precession prior to general relativity—seem to provide no new probabilistic support, yet they are routinely cited as confirming instances. The literature has accumulated technical responses—counterfactual priors, distinctions between predictive and accommodative success, revisions to conditionalization—without resolving whether these are competing formal solutions or sides talking past each other. Diagnose this stalemate. Is it a substantive disagreement about the correct Bayesian formalism, a verbal dispute about whether 'confirmation' must involve predictive novelty, or a hidden split between normative questions about rational belief revision and descriptive questions about scientific reasoning? Identify what each side actually takes the problem to show, and take a committed position: state precisely what kind of disagreement this is. Then specify what would count as genuine progress—whether a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a reframing of the question itself—rather than another technical patch.
