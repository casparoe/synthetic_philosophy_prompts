I'm leading a 75-minute seminar for third-year politics students on lost-cause mythology as a mechanism of national identity formation. I want to teach them a specific conceptual frame—call it "redemptive defeat"—in which a collective military or political failure is narratively inverted so that defeat functions as proof of exceptional moral or cultural virtue, producing a purified identity rather than a diminished one. The session should build this through the paired contrast of Masada (collective death as unyielding resistance) and the postbellum American South's Lost Cause (defeat as preservation of a superior civilization), holding Dunkirk and Kosovo back for a diagnostic check. Plan the explanatory sequence: which prior distinction—say, between commemorative mourning and legitimating myth—must be established first and why the concept collapses without it; which of the two carrying cases should come first and what structural features it must possess (an unambiguous defeat, the absence of subsequent sovereign restoration, and a retrospective narrative that makes the defeat voluntary or inevitable rather than contingent) versus what it must lack (actual post-defeat institutional continuity or successful armed resistance); the three misconceptions students will form in order—likely conflating redemptive defeat with ordinary nationalism, then with victimhood politics, then with historical denialism—and the precise point in the sequence where each must be intercepted; one final question that distinguishes students who understand the mechanism from those who have only matched examples to labels; and what elements (perhaps the Kosovo case's modern international-recognition dynamics) should be deliberately omitted at this level with a brief reason.
