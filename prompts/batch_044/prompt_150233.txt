I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science consulting with a private research foundation that allocates a fixed quarterly budget to high-risk, long-horizon theoretical projects. The board has adopted geometric rationality—specifically Kelly-criterion reasoning—as its normative framework, but it wants the principle translated into an actual decision procedure rather than left as a metaphor. The difficulty is that the returns here are not monetary but epistemic: success is a shift in a credence distribution over competing explanatory frameworks. The probabilities are usually ambiguous rather than point-valued; the taxonomy of possible successes is contested; and some proposals, if funded, redefine the problem space in ways that alter the payoff matrix for every other grant in the portfolio. I need a reusable checklist or flowchart—executable in about twenty minutes per proposal, then aggregable across the portfolio—that makes this translation explicit without assuming stationary environments, cardinal utility, or a pre-specified outcome space.

Could you construct such a procedure and specify, in order: (1) how to represent a proposal's return profile when the payoff is a change in belief-state rather than a scalar; (2) how to compute or approximate a Kelly fraction when the prior over outcomes is itself disputed, clearly marking which steps are deterministic and which are unavoidable judgment calls; (3) how to update portfolio weights when a funded project changes the dimensions of evaluation, so that the geometric mean is computed over an ensemble whose coordinate system may shift; and (4) how to build in protection against unknown unknowns—what the board calls an ambiguity-aversion constraint—without collapsing into maximin or uniform diversification. The procedure should be honest about where it will misfire.

Please apply it step-by-step to three hypothetical cases: (a) a proposal with quantifiable intermediate milestones but highly uncertain final theoretical impact; (b) a proposal that promises large information gain but depends on adopting a novel ontology that, if correct, renders the evaluation metrics of other funded projects obsolete; and (c) a proposal for which the board holds a near-uniform prior over a large set of incommensurable success conditions, where a naive geometric-mean calculation would recommend dangerous over-leverage. For each case, show the execution, flag any point where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with intuitive scientific judgment, and say whether that conflict exposes a limit of geometric rationality in this domain or a failure of intuition.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or references to specific texts; I need the conceptual machinery laid out precisely enough to defend in a board memo without appealing to authority.
