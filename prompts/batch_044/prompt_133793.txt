I am a retired physicist who, over the past two years, has turned to philosophy of mind with particular interest in the temporal dynamics of conscious experience. I am drafting a short essay on the color phi phenomenon and the well-known distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of perceptual content—whether consciousness involves a post-hoc rewriting of the record or a real-time fabrication presented as finished experience. Rather than defending one side, I want to propose a framing drawn from predictive-processing accounts of perception. On this view, the brain continuously generates a Bayesian posterior over sensory states, suppressing error signals once they fall below a threshold. I call this mechanism a retrospective prior: a predictive correction applied to the content entering awareness that is experienced not as a revision but as part of the original percept. I then draw an analogy to quantum decoherence—not as a physical claim about neural dynamics, but as a structural comparison. Just as decoherence involves no single instant of wave-function collapse but a gradual suppression of interference over a characteristic timescale, conscious experience may involve no discrete present moment but only a temporally extended stabilization window during which competing representations are suppressed. The upshot is that the Orwellian/Stalinesque dichotomy dissolves into a question of temporal scale: over very brief intervals the process looks Stalinesque (pre-presentation fabrication), over longer intervals it looks Orwellian (post-hoc reconstruction), yet both are approximations of one continuous Bayesian update.

I am particularly concerned with two issues. First, whether the notion of a retrospective prior is coherent within predictive-coding theory, or whether it illicitly conflates subpersonal inference with personal-level content. Second, whether the decoherence analogy does genuine explanatory work or is merely an ornamental metaphor that obscures the underlying neurobiology. I also wonder whether my framing avoids the objection—common in discussions of Dennett’s multiple-drafts model—that it eliminates any real presence of experience, or whether it merely relocates that problem into the dynamics of error suppression.

Below are my draft notes for the essay, and I would welcome your direct assessment on the following points:

1. Accuracy: Have I mischaracterized either the predictive-processing account of the phi phenomenon or the logic of the Orwell/Stalin distinction? Are there subtle mistakes in how I describe Bayesian updating in this context?
2. Coherence of the analogy: Does the comparison to decoherence illuminate anything about conscious timing, or does it confuse physical and informational processes?
3. The retrospective prior: Is this concept defensible, or does it amount to a relabeling of standard predictive coding?
4. Interest and originality: Is the argument sufficiently distinct from existing discussions—say, those linking predictive processing to the phi illusion—to be worth developing, or does it merely restate familiar claims in more technical language?

Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am asking only for an evaluation of the reasoning as presented. I am especially interested in whether there are hidden confusions—perhaps a conflation between the temporal order of neural events and the temporal order of experienced content—that a hasty reader might miss but that undermine the central claim.
