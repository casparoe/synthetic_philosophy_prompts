Design a decision-tree classification instrument—usable as a self-sorting quiz for an upper-level philosophy seminar—that maps actual commitments about whether philosophy makes progress, without relying on respondents recognizing named theories or philosophers. Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then populate it with positions. Use five to seven binary or trinary diagnostic nodes, each phrased as a concrete comparative judgment or case commitment rather than a label-check: for instance, whether a longstanding debate that has become technically refined but remains unresolved should count as progress, stagnation, or transformation; whether the disappearance of a problem through abandonment (analogous to phlogiston-style rejection in science) should be credited as philosophical success; and whether growing divergence among equally informed experts signals immaturity or maturity.

The root node should separate those who deny any meaningful philosophical settlement from those who allow at least local resolution. From there, branch through whether such resolution requires empirical input, whether progress can occur in the absence of consensus, and whether the sharpening of disagreement itself constitutes advancement. The tree must contain exactly seven terminal leaves, including at minimum: (1) cumulative-progress analog to the natural sciences; (2) non-cumulative conceptual clarification; (3) perpetual recurrence with zero net advance; (4) therapeutic dissolution of pseudo-problems; (5) pluralist divergence measured by the quality and articulation of competing frameworks; and (6) the under-discussed view that philosophy progresses specifically through the increasing precision of disagreement—where opposing positions become more rigorously articulated, their points of clash more narrowly located, and the space of live alternatives more clearly mapped, without any expectation of convergence. The seventh leaf should be a combination that your branching logic produces but for which no prominent named position exists; place it in the tree and briefly diagnose why it is unoccupied, whether incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored.

Label every internal branch with the explicit separating question. After constructing the tree, append a scoring key that translates answer paths into leaf positions using only descriptions of commitments, not movement names or philosopher attributions. Finally, flag any pairs of required leaves that your diagnostic questions fail to distinguish, explaining what additional case or comparison would be needed to separate them. The instrument should be answerable by someone with general philosophical literacy but no specialized historical knowledge, and it must not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or lists of primary texts.
