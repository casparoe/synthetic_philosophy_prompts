I'm running a two-hour workshop for philosophy graduate students with strong analytic training but virtually no exposure to classical Indian philosophy. The goal is to have them understand Jain anekāntavāda—specifically syādvāda and the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī)—not as a vague "Eastern relativism" but as a rigorous logical doctrine about the necessary partiality of every predication. Plan the explanatory arc: first, identify the precise conceptual dependency, so that I know which idea must be firmly in place before syādvāda can be introduced without being heard as mere epistemic modesty, and why nayavāda has to come before or after saptabhaṅgī depending on that order. Second, choose one carrying example drawn from their own conceptual neighborhood—ideally institutional ontology or artifact kind-membership, not the blind-men-and-elephant parable—specifying exactly what the example must have (multiple non-contradictory respects of evaluation) and what it must lack (any hidden "real essence" that resolves the multiplicity). Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: initially that this is just cultural relativism; then that it is a form of dialetheism accepting true contradictions; then that the modal qualifier syāt ("perhaps," "in some respect") is a speaker-confidence hedge rather than a logical operator. For each, state the point in the sequence where it should be defused and the specific conceptual distinction that blocks it. Fourth, design a brief check for understanding that reveals whether they have grasped the logical structure of nayavāda—how standpoints are partial without being subjective—rather than whether they have merely adopted a tolerant pluralist attitude. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—the metaphysics of jīva and ajīva, the ethical derivation of ahiṃsā, or the full technical apparatus of Jain syllogistic logic—and justify the omission in terms of the audience's preparatory needs. Do not provide a reading list or bibliography.
