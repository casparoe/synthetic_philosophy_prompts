Below is a brief essay sketch I drafted for a discussion on diachronic rationality. I apply Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle—roughly, that a rational agent's present credence in P ought to match her expectation of her future credence in P—to a worry familiar from rationalist forums: that deliberate calibration training can backfire if an agent anticipates undergoing substantial value drift or cognitive restructuring. I claim standard formulations of reflection tacitly assume a stable diachronic agent, and that this assumption fails when one models future updates as belonging to a different "agent-profile" rather than a merely better-informed version of oneself. I suggest this forces us to restrict reflection to "thin" belief revisions—ordinary evidential updates—while excluding "thick" identity-altering revisions. I then note that long-horizon prediction markets seem to presuppose unrestricted reflection, which might partly explain systematic miscalibration in forecasts spanning major personal or institutional change.

I'd appreciate comments focused on three things: first, whether I'm misrepresenting van Fraassen's formulation—specifically, whether he allows updates that break personal-identity conditions without violating rationality; second, whether the thin/thick distinction actually does the work I want, or if it collapses under pressure; and third, whether there's a more precise way to formalize the restriction without importing a controversial metaphysics of personal identity. The intended audience is comfortable with calibration culture and prediction-market reasoning, but I am not looking for bibliographic references—just conceptual corrections, hidden assumptions I've missed, and suggestions for tightening the argument.
