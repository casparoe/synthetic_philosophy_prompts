At a philosophy reading group, two participants—call them A and B—argued about whether lexical threshold negative utilitarianism can avoid the commensurability problem between pleasure and suffering.

A began by claiming that the view solves the problem: because suffering has lexical priority over pleasure, we never need a common metric. We eliminate suffering first; only when suffering falls below the lexical threshold do we even consider promoting pleasure. Therefore the position does not presuppose that pleasure and pain are measurable on the same scale.

B objected that lexical ordering itself requires some form of comparability. To rank suffering as always prior, one must already be able to place both goods on a single evaluative dimension—otherwise the claim is as arbitrary as saying "blue is always more important than loud" without a shared axis. If pleasure and suffering are genuinely incommensurable, then declaring suffering lexically prior is not a reasoned moral conclusion but a stipulation.

A replied that B had it backwards: incommensurability actually strengthens the lexical view. If there is no common currency, then any attempt to "balance" suffering against pleasure commits a category error—like trading length for temperature. The rational response to irreducible incommensurability is not to invent a false exchange rate but to establish a strict hierarchy: remove the negative entirely, then attend to the positive on its own terms.

B then raised what she called the "threshold-margin collapse" problem. Once suffering is pushed below the lexical threshold, the view permits (and requires) distributing resources among competing goods—including reducing minor sufferings versus generating major pleasures. At that margin, the view must implicitly weigh suffering against pleasure, revealing a hidden common metric operating all along. The lexical threshold, B said, is just an arbitrary cutoff on a single continuous scale disguised as a categorical divide.

A responded that B was conflating theoretical structure with practical administration. The lexical rule operates at the architectural level of moral theory; resource allocation within the safe zone is second-order practical reasoning that can use heuristic trade-offs without committing the theory to ontological commensurability. One can employ a provisional yardstick for policy without believing suffering and pleasure share a deep metaphysical currency.
