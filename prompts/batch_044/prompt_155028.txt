Consider the grading rubric common in university history departments for essays on pre-modern institutions: students are penalised for presentism — applying contemporary ethical categories to past agents — and rewarded for demonstrating historical empathy, yet they are also penalised if they fail to describe chattel slavery or colonial extraction as morally wrong in terms that grant no concession to the actors' own normative frameworks. What theory of cross-temporal moral judgement, or of the historian's epistemic role, does this dual practice presuppose? Must it combine moral realism with a contextualist account of knowledge, or does it rely on a hidden distinction between explaining an institution and bearing witness to its victims? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments, say whether the two grading criteria are genuinely consistent or only pragmatically reconciled, and describe what the assessment of primary-source essays would have to become if the department adopted a rigorously anti-presentist stance — including how it would treat archival documents that employ now-obsolete moral taxonomies.
