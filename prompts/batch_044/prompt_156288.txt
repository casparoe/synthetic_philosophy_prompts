I'm preparing a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who recently defended a view she calls dispositional-relational hedonism in response to the heterogeneity problem. On her account, pleasures do not share any intrinsic phenomenal quality—eating chocolate, solving a proof, and hearing a melody feel nothing alike—so asking what they have in common is misguided if answered in qualitative terms. Instead, she claims they are unified extrinsically by a shared second-order disposition: each pleasure tends to reinforce the very cognitive or conative state that generated it, regardless of sensory modality. This, she argues, avoids the circularity of defining pleasure as feeling good, distinguishes genuine pleasure from mere satisfaction, and explains cross-modal reinforcement without positing a hidden phenomenal substrate.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate the view. I want at least two questions comparing her position to standard functionalist theories of reward and reinforcement learning—anticipate that she will reply functionalism captures the behavioral pattern but misses the first-person normative authority of pleasure, and include a follow-up that presses whether that authority is anything more than a report of the reinforcement disposition. I want at least one question on whether tying pleasure so closely to reinforcement collapses pleasure into desire or motivation—the motivation problem—anticipating her reply that the link is normative rather than causal, with a follow-up asking how she distinguishes normative reinforcement from mere habituation. I want at least one question on alien pleasures: whether beings with radically different cognitive architectures could experience pleasures that lack our normative-reinforcement structure, anticipating she will allow alien phenomenal states but deny they count as pleasures absent that structure, with a follow-up on whether this makes her view chauvinistic about the mental lives of non-human or artificial minds. Finally, include one closing question about what observable or first-person evidence could genuinely count against dispositional-relational hedonism, given that she has dissolved the qualitative commonality that critics usually target.

Present the questions in order, beginning immediately with the first question. Do not include an introduction, preface, summary, or commentary. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Lead with the first question in the opening line, then give the reasoning and follow-ups with no restatement of the prompt.
