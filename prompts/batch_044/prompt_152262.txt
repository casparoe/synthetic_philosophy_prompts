Analyze the January 1986 Challenger launch decision—specifically the Thiokol-NASA teleconference regarding O-ring performance in cold weather—through the lens of Good's theorem on the value of information. I want a disciplined mapping: identify the relevant decision-maker or decision-makers, the action space (launch versus scrub), the states of nature concerning O-ring failure, the prior probabilities that a rational agent might have assigned given the available engineering data, and what further information (e.g., temperature-dependent failure-rate estimates or prior flight data) was present but not fully acquired, transmitted, or acted upon. Then apply Good's result—that the expected value of free information is non-negative for a unitary rational agent who updates beliefs and can act on them—to ask whether the information that existed inside Thiokol should have changed the decision, and whether the theorem makes the failure to delay rational, irrational, or simply outside its scope.

But treat the episode as testing the theorem rather than merely illustrating it. The Challenger case involved organizational pressure, divided authority between engineers and managers, and what might be called institutional commitment: acting on new information was technically possible but politically and bureaucratically costly. So ask whether Good's theorem applies at the organizational level or requires a unitary agent; where the theory assumes information is costless to acquire and that updated beliefs automatically feed into actions, the episode shows information being actively suppressed or devalued by the decision structure. Give an honest account of what the framework explains—whether delaying for more data would have had positive expected value under a straightforward payoff matrix—and where it breaks down: whether it can accommodate strategic suppression of information, whether value must include organizational reputation costs absent from the standard matrix, and whether extensions to multi-agent or costly-information settings do better. Finally, tell me what this case suggests about using decision-theoretic value-of-information reasoning in institutional crisis management, and whether the theorem's silence on organizational dynamics is a limitation to be repaired or a correct boundary of its application.
