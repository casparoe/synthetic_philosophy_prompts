I'm trying to wrap my head round Braudel's longue durée for a small reading group, and I think I've hit a wall. From what I gather he splits history into three speeds: the almost-motionless layer of geography and climate, the middle rhythm of trade cycles and social structures — the conjunctures? — and then the froth of events and individual decisions. Here's where I'm stuck. If the deep geographical layer is the real subject, and it's basically unchanged across centuries, then any specific choice by Philip II or the outcome of something like Lepanto must be definately predetermined by the shape of the Mediterranean and its seasonal wind patterns. So why does Braudel spend half the book narrating exactly those political and military events? Is he just being inconsistent, or is the middle layer meant to be an escape hatch that smuggles human agency back in without admitting it? And is there a specific name for this kind of hidden determinism, where you claim to study slow structures but can't stop telling the story of kings?
