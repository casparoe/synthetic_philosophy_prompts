Consider the precise claim that, in a cosmological model with an infinite or sufficiently large number of causally disconnected Hubble volumes, rational agents in different volumes ought to engage in evidential cooperation—making choices that are collectively best for all such agents—even when they share no substantive values, have no causal interaction, and cannot observe one another's actions, relying solely on the recognition that they run isomorphic decision algorithms.

Give me your actual credence in this claim, expressed as a number between 0 and 1. Decompose the reasoning explicitly into four terms:

1. The probability that evidential reasoning genuinely extends across causally disconnected domains, rather than being restricted to correlations within a shared causal past.
2. The probability that the symmetry and algorithmic-isomorphism conditions required for superrational cooperation actually hold for realistic agents in such a cosmology, rather than breaking down due to heterogeneous architectures or bounded epistemic access.
3. The probability that cooperation remains stable under full reflection—that an agent does not defect upon recognizing that its choice is only evidentially, not causally, linked to the outcomes experienced by distant counterparts.
4. The probability that a bare "common rationality" baseline is thick enough to generate cooperative gains without shared preferences over outcomes.

For each term, provide a rough probability and a single line of justification. Then state how you combine them—whether approximately multiplicatively as independent conditions, or with some other structure—to reach your overall number. Identify which term contributes the most variance to your final credence, and specify what kind of philosophical argument, decision-theoretic result, or cosmological finding would most shift that particular term.
