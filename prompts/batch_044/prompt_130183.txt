I am preparing for a seminar on the modern revival of Stoicism, specifically on how contemporary interpreters apply the ancient dichotomy of control—what belongs to our prohairesis, our faculty of judgment, assent, and volition, versus what lies outside it. I constructed the following argument and now find it suspicious because its conclusion undermines the very therapeutic practices these modern Stoics advocate, yet I cannot locate the precise slip.

(1) The modern Stoic revival maintains that virtue and tranquility depend solely on the correct use of our prohairesis, while everything else—including bodily health, reputation, and emotional affect—is strictly indifferent, though some indifferents are naturally preferred.

(2) If an emotional state is genuinely outside the domain of prohairesis, then judging it to possess intrinsic value—good or evil—is a category error: it treats an indifferent as if it were part of the moral sphere.

(3) Clinical depression and anxiety disorders frequently involve endogenous, non-cognitive symptoms—neurochemical dysregulation, anhedonia, involuntary panic—that are not produced by any explicit judgment about externals.

(4) Therefore, on the Stoic account, these symptoms are themselves indifferents lying outside the sphere of virtue.

(5) Now, if a sufferer judges their own depression to be bad and seeks to eliminate it through therapy or medication, they are treating an indifferent—their mental state—as if it were an evil.

(6) The correct Stoic response, then, is to withhold judgment about the value of the depression itself—to regard one's own suffering with the same equanimity one brings to a broken vase or a lost coin.

(7) But this implies that seeking treatment for clinical depression is morally equivalent to lamenting ruined shoes: it reveals a failure of philosophical discipline rather than a legitimate health need.

(8) Modern Stoic authors, however, consistently present their philosophy as compatible with, and even supportive of, clinical psychological care.

(9) So either modern Stoicism must condemn therapeutic intervention for mental illness as a vice, or it must admit that some mental states are not indifferents after all, which collapses the strict dichotomy.

(10) I reject (9) because I want to preserve both Stoicism and mental-health care. Where is the error? I suspect it lies somewhere between (3) and (6), perhaps in how the scope of "judgment" is being applied, or in an unacknowledged shift between treating a state as dispreferred and treating it as evil. Please name the step precisely, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what happens to the Stoic claim that we are responsible only for our assent, not for the impressions that precede it.
