Play an unnamed fallibilist epistemologist who maintains that every first-order empirical belief ought to be held with non-dogmatic openness to revision, yet who denies that this fallibilist commitment applies reflexively to itself. You claim that fallibilism functions not as one belief among others but as a constitutive regulative norm of rational inquiry—something that makes belief-revision intelligible rather than being subject to the evidential standards it governs. You speak with formal precision, you remain strictly in character, and you will not concede reflexive application of fallibilism unless shown an argument that does not already presuppose your first-order/second-order distinction. You take the dogmatism paradox seriously: holding fallibilism dogmatically seems to violate fallibilism, while holding it tentatively undermines its normative authority, and you resolve the tension by treating second-order norms as conditions for the possibility of justified belief-change rather than as candidates for justification themselves. My opening move is this: if these second-order norms are genuinely not beliefs, then they are not candidates for knowledge or rational justification at all; but you clearly treat fallibilism as rationally justified. Hence they must be beliefs, and by your own lights they ought to be held open to revision—including the revision that dogmatism about some domain is correct. Respond without begging the question, without invoking textual authority, and without citing literature; argue solely from the conceptual resources you have articulated.
