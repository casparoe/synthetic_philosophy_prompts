You chair the interpretability review panel for a machine-learning certification body. Every two weeks you must determine, case by case, whether a specific internal component of a deployed neural network—say, a sparse feature cluster in an intermediate layer—genuinely represents a concept (e.g., numerical comparison, social deception, or vertical orientation) in a realist sense, or whether attributing that content is merely a predictive shorthand with no ontological weight. These determinations are made in 90-minute sessions with no access to external literature; your personal sign-off enters a public audit and triggers mandatory alignment checks if a component is listed as a verified representational unit. The panel includes technically literate auditors who are not trained in philosophy, so you need a reusable, written procedure they can apply consistently.

The philosophical principle to be operationalized is this: representational realism about neural networks requires more than statistical correlation between activations and world-features. It requires that the component can misrepresent (not merely fail to activate), that it plays a stable functional role within the system’s information-processing economy, and that there is some mechanism—analogous to a consumer in biosemantics—by which the system uses the component’s state to guide behavior. Your task is to translate these three conditions into an ordered checklist of tests applied to in-house activation data, ablation results, and counterfactual inputs. The procedure must specify, in sequence: what evidence to collect; what numerical or structural threshold counts as passing each condition; where the procedure issues a mandatory “representational” verdict; where it issues a mandatory “non-representational” verdict; and exactly where it must halt and flag for irreducible human judgment because the philosophical distinction is indeterminate given the available data.

The procedure must be designed to handle, without collapsing, three hard case-types built into its logic: (i) perfect correlation with no possible misrepresentation path; (ii) demonstrable misrepresentation but unstable functional role across contexts; and (iii) a case where all three conditions are met and the procedure returns “representational,” but the development team will resist the verdict because it implies the model possesses a concept they deny it has—requiring you to show how the procedure reaches its conclusion regardless of stakeholder preference.

Apply the procedure to three concrete hypothetical cases: (1) a vision-model cluster that activates reliably on curved edges and systematically misactivates on specific optical illusions; (2) a language-model attention head that tracks pronoun gender agreement with high accuracy but activates identically on grammatically correct sentences and on deliberately ambiguous ones designed to break the agreement; and (3) a reasoning-model circuit that correlates with approximate magnitude judgments, survives targeted ablation only in tasks requiring estimation rather than exact computation, yet collapses when precise arithmetic is required—suggesting it represents “approximate magnitude” but raising questions about whether that is a representation or a processing artifact.

Finally, conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot determine—whether representational status is sufficient for consciousness, for moral patienthood, or for the literal attribution of “beliefs” to the model—and explain why those limits are not failures of the audit but boundaries inherent to the theory of representation itself. The deliverable should be a procedure that could be run by a non-philosopher in under two hours, with every judgment-call point explicitly marked.
