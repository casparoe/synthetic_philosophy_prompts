After a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether counterpart-theoretic semantics can be applied to evaluate counterfactual behaviors of large language models across different fine-tuning regimes, I want to reconstruct what David Lewis—who died before these systems existed—would likely have said about the proposal. Lewis defended modal realism and counterpart theory for de re modal claims about actual individuals, but contemporary AI alignment researchers sometimes invoke counterpart relations across training runs to assess robustness. Would Lewis have treated a model in an alternative training regime as a counterpart of the actual model, or as the same model existing in a different possible world? Given his rejection of transworld identity for ordinary objects, would he extend that rejection to artifacts like neural networks, or would he allow that a computational artifact can maintain identity across worlds while only its properties vary? Furthermore, considering his doctrine of the indexicality of actuality, how might he analyze claims like “this model, in a safer alignment regime, would have refused harmful prompts”—would he read these as genuine modal claims about the model’s counterparts, or as epistemic claims about our access to nearby worlds? I am asking for a predicted philosophical position, not a literature review, and I particularly want to know whether he would view the AI use of counterpart language as a legitimate extension of his metaphysics or as a category mistake that conflates representational similarity with modal counterpart relations.
