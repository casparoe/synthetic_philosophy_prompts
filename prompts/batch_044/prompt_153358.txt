John von Neumann distinguished between technologies that merely persist and those that survive in the evolutionary sense: self-reproducing automata capable of replicating their own design logic across changing substrates. He suggested that the survival of such technology depends not on the preservation of physical form but on the fidelity with which its internal constraints are transmitted during reproduction. Taking this as your starting point, argue that current frameworks for artificial intelligence alignment misunderstand the conditions under which beneficial values or constraints actually survive technological scaling and replication. More precisely, defend the claim that alignment should be reconceived not as a static property of an initial system—what we might call phenotypic alignment, or behavioral conformity in a particular instance—but as a reproductive property of the design logic itself, analogous to what we might call genotypic alignment: the capacity of a system's core constraints to propagate faithfully through successive generations of self-modification, substrate migration, or architectural scaling. Explain why treating alignment as static makes it vulnerable to the same kind of drift that von Neumann identified in non-replicative machinery, and show how embedding values in mechanisms of constraint propagation rather than in fixed objective functions better satisfies the von Neumann criterion for genuine technological survival. Keep the argument self-contained; do not invoke specific bibliographies, cite papers, or appeal to authority beyond the conceptual structure of von Neumann's automata theory.
