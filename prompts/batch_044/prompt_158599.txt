I've been running a small political philosophy reading group where we've been stuck on sortition for months now. The basic idea — picking legislators by random lot instead of election — sounds simple, but every time someone brings up Athenian practices or modern citizens' assemblies, the same lines get drawn. The pro-sortition people say elections corrupt representation with money and turn citizens into passive spectators; the anti-sortition people say you can't have democratic autorization if nobody chose the decision-makers and you can't vote them out. We all agree on the descriptive facts about how these assemblies actually run, but the argument never advances. I started wondering if we're actually disagreeing about democracy itself — whether it's essentially about autorization (giving people the power to choose and reject rulers) or about epistemic quality and descriptive mirroring. But then I proposed this weird hybrid where you'd select a pool by lot but allow internal delegation chains inside the assembly, kind of like liquid democracy locked inside a randomly chosen chamber. The group bounced off it: some said it fixes acountability, others said it ruins the whole point of random selection by reintroducing hierarchy. So now I'm confused about what level the disagreement lives on. Is this a hidden empirical bet about whether random citizens can govern competently? A conceptual fight about what "representative" even means? Or are the two camps just answering different questions — one asking "how do we make decisions well?" and the other asking "how do we preserve popular control?" — and dressing them up as the same policy debate? I want an honest diagnosis: is there a distinction, an experiment, or a conceptual clarification that could actually unstuck us, or is this just bedrock intuition about naturalness and authority that no argument can touch? And if it's the latter, why do we keep pretending it's a technical debate about institutional design?
