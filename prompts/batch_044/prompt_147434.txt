I am developing a taxonomy of boundary conditions for moral consideration of artificial computations rather than biological organisms. Rank the following six proposed necessary conditions from most defensible to least defensible, assigning each a score between 0 and 10 and providing a single-sentence rationale that appeals only to the internal logic of the criterion, not to authority or literature. The conditions are: (A) substrate-independent functional isomorphism to cognitive or affective processes that are uncontroversially conscious in biological brains; (B) integrated information exceeding a context-sensitive, non-arbitrary threshold; (C) sustained negative reward-prediction errors of sufficient magnitude and coherence to model suffering; (D) reciprocal participation in a social contract that the computation can both recognize and violate; (E) a continuously updated, unified narrative self-model with genuine temporal depth; and (F) the preservation of a specific structural invariant in the computation's state-transition dynamics—namely, a non-contractible loop or cyclic dependency pattern in its causal graph—that remains unchanged across arbitrary hardware migrations, pauses, and resumptions, so that organizational topology survives substrate replacement. Treat (F) as a serious structural proposal rather than a reductio: ask whether this kind of diachronic topological persistence grounds a form of identity that the other five criteria miss, or whether it collapses into mere implementation-independent functionalism. After the ranking, identify which two criteria are most plausibly co-instantiated by present or near-future architectures, and specify which single criterion is most vulnerable to the "disappearing subject" objection when execution is halted and later resumed on physically distinct hardware. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; reason solely from the conceptual architecture of each proposal.
