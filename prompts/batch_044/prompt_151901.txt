I've been trying to understand Milton Friedman's argument that a scientific model can rest on assumptions that are plainly false about the world, so long as it yields good predictions; people refer to this as the F-twist, and it seems to come up whenever economists defend models that treat people as perfectly rational or markets as frictionless. I want to test this idea against a concrete historical case: the Black-Scholes option-pricing formula, published in the early 1970s, and its relationship to the stock-market crash of October 19, 1987, known as Black Monday.

The Black-Scholes model assumes that stock prices move continuously with no sudden jumps, that volatility remains constant over time, and that traders can hedge risk continuously without transaction costs. By the late 1980s, practitioners using the formula knew that none of these conditions actually held in real markets. Nevertheless, the formula was adopted widely, and for years it seemed to price options successfully. Then, on Black Monday, markets moved in ways that the model treated as essentially impossible, and the hedging strategies it encouraged may have amplified the collapse.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of Friedman's F-twist. First, map the model's unrealistic assumptions precisely, and say whether they are of the kind Friedman would defend—false about the world, yet harmless for prediction. Second, examine whether the formula's predictive success before 1987 genuinely supports the F-twist, or whether that success came from a different mechanism, such as traders using the model to coordinate prices rather than the model tracking an independent underlying dynamic. Third, discuss what the crash reveals: was it a straightforward predictive failure that undermines Friedman's defense, or was it an out-of-domain event that a predictive model was never required to cover? Fourth, consider whether the F-twist contains a hidden stability condition—that unrealistic assumptions are only defensible if they remain false in the same way across the domain of application—and whether Black-Scholes shows that in financial markets this kind of stability is an illusion, because the very use of the model changes how agents behave.

Finally, I want you to say what the episode teaches us about the theory itself. Does Black-Scholes confirm that unrealistic assumptions are harmless when predictions are good? Or does it show that in domains where agents learn the model and adapt their behavior, the F-twist collapses because the assumptions become self-undermining? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent—for instance, Friedman said relatively little about what happens when a model is used not only for prediction but also for institutional design and real-time decision-making—and do not force the theory to speak on questions it never addressed.
