Consider the following exam item from my upper-level philosophy of mind course, and provide a point-by-point grading assessment written in American English.

Question: In a modified version of Dennett's brain-transplant scenario, a surgeon removes Hamlet's brain and sustains it in a nutrient vat (H-vat) linked by radio to a robotic body, R-body, with negligible latency. The surgeon then creates two silicon substrates—Hubert-Alpha in Tokyo and Hubert-Beta in Berlin—each running a real-time functional copy of H-vat's neural dynamics, but with an unavoidable 200-millisecond transmission delay. During a brief glitch, H-vat receives identical sensory feeds from both Hubert-controlled robotic bodies simultaneously, while retaining direct proprioceptive access to R-body. Hamlet reports feeling "here, with R-body," despite the fact that the same informational state is instantiated in Tokyo and Berlin.

Analyze: (a) Whether there is one Hamlet, three, or none in this configuration; (b) What explains the authority of Hamlet's first-person location report if the underlying cognitive state is triplicated across substrates with different spatial positions; and (c) Whether Dennett's appeal to a narrative center of gravity resolves the indexical problem, or whether the temporal delay and duplication undermine that resolution.

Student answer:

The correct analysis is that there are three distinct cognitive systems, but only one Hamlet, because personal identity is preserved by psychological continuity plus narrative coherence. Dennett's original thought experiment shows that the self is not a Cartesian soul but a center of narrative gravity, so Hamlet is wherever the controlling narrative is centered. Here, the 200ms delay means Hubert-Alpha and Hubert-Beta are not fully synchronized with H-vat, so they lack the real-time narrative integration that defines Hamlet's present experience. That is why Hamlet correctly reports being in R-body: the negligible latency preserves the continuity required for narrative center of gravity, whereas the delayed copies are merely predictive models.

However, Dennett's solution does not fully address the glitch case. When H-vat receives identical streams from Tokyo and Berlin, the narrative center splits, because there is no further fact about which stream belongs to "me." This reveals that Dennett's account collapses into Parfit's reductionist view: there is no owner of the experience, only a bundle of perceptions. The grammatical use of "I" is therefore a fiction with no causal or explanatory role; we should eliminate talk of Hamlet altogether and speak only of H-vat, Hubert-Alpha, and Hubert-Beta as separate information-processing nodes.
