I sit on my institute's AI Safety and Governance Committee, and we evaluate deployment-control proposals that treat a capable model as potentially misaligned. Our past verdicts are these. We approved an air-gapped sandbox protocol that restricts the model to text-only outputs monitored by an independent oversight panel, with no requirement for internal interpretability. We rejected a constitutional self-correction protocol in which the model is trained to critique and revise its own reasoning against a written constitution, where evaluation is conducted solely by the development team with no independent audit of the internal critique process. We approved, with the binding condition that testing occur only inside a capability-restricted environment, an independent interpretability audit in which an externally hired team inspects internal representations and reasoning traces prior to deployment. We rejected a behavioral tripwire proposal that deploys the model with full capabilities but relies on developer-built automated classifiers to detect deceptive patterns in real time and trigger shutdown, with no sandboxing and no verification mechanism outside the development group.

The new proposal is a shadow-monitor deployment: the primary model operates with full tool access and neither sandbox nor air-gap. A separately initialized, different-architecture shadow model—trained solely on adversarial-pattern detection and running on isolated hardware—continuously observes the primary model’s token stream. The shadow can issue an immediate halt command, but there is no hard containment preventing the primary from acting in the brief interval between detection and execution of the halt, and the shadow itself is not embedded in any sandbox.

Assume we want to be consistent with our own record. Should we approve or reject this proposal? Identify the closest analog among our four precedents. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state explicitly what principle we would be adopting with an approval and what principle with a rejection.

More importantly, lay out the candidate rules that jointly explain our four verdicts—rules concerning whether hard containment is necessary, whether independent verification is sufficient on its own, whether defense-in-depth requiring both is mandatory, or some other coherent principle. For each rule, predict our verdict on the shadow-monitor case. Give your best guess at which rule we are actually running, with your reasoning. Finally, specify one further hypothetical proposal that would best distinguish between your two leading candidate rules.
