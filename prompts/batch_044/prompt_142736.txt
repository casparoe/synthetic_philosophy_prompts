I am working through a problem in metaphysics of identity and find myself stuck on a particular twist of Black’s classic spheres scenario. Most expositions treat the two spheres as symmetric in every intrinsic and relational property except bare spatial position, and they use this to challenge Leibniz’s Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. But I want to understand a more subtle variant: suppose we grant that the spheres are genuinely indistinguishable with respect to all monadic qualitative properties and all standard pairwise spatiotemporal relations, yet we posit an irreducible “global structural relation” — call it R — that holds between sphere A and the left hemisphere of the containing universe but not between sphere B and that same hemisphere, where R is not reducible to any intrinsic feature of the spheres or to conventional spatial relations. Would the existence of such a relation rescue the Identity of Indiscernibles, or does it merely relocate the problem by smuggling in a primitive relational difference that is no less mysterious than bare numerical diversity?

Please explain the conceptual terrain here with care. In particular, distinguish (i) whether R should count as a genuine qualitative difference between the spheres or as an extrinsic framing that fails to ground identity; (ii) whether the doctrine of weak discernibility applies in this modified case, and if so whether it saves the principle or undermines it; and (iii) whether the principle can survive only if all differences are ultimately intrinsic, or whether a thoroughly relational ontology can consistently uphold it. I am not asking for a historical survey or a bibliography — only a rigorous conceptual clarification that takes Black’s original thought experiment as its point of departure and pushes into this under-discussed problem of relational relocation.
