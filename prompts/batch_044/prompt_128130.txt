At a philosophy of science seminar, two participants—Maya and David—disagreed sharply about Johannes Kepler's genuine philosophical stance toward astrology. Maya maintained that Kepler was a committed practitioner of judicial astrology who believed celestial configurations exert real, causal influence over individual human affairs, and that his well-known criticisms of popular astrologers amounted only to methodological reforms—insisting on precise astronomical measurements of actual planetary positions rather than arbitrary zodiacal divisions—while leaving the core metaphysical claim entirely intact. David countered that Kepler was essentially a skeptical rationalist who practiced astrology primarily for patronage and income, holding at most that celestial bodies might produce a diffuse, non-specific influence comparable to weather patterns that shape collective moods, with no determinative bearing on particular personal destinies.

Maya replied that this meteorological reading struggles to account for Kepler's sustained practice of casting specific natal charts and issuing particular forecasts—including predictions that specific planetary configurations would coincide with political turmoil affecting named patrons—which seem to treat celestial events as genuinely predictive of discrete outcomes, not merely as background conditions for general atmospheric tendencies.

David responded that forecasting collective political events is precisely what a meteorological model does—predicting storms without specifying which tree will fall—and added that Kepler's own framing of astrological correspondences as signs rather than efficient causes indicates he viewed the practice as correlational pattern-matching unsupported by any underlying mechanistic force.

Maya then advanced a more nuanced interpretation: she argued that Kepler's position was neither full determinism nor mere correlation, but rather a geometric harmony theory in which real, observer-independent angular relationships between planets—what astrologers call aspects—resonate with human affairs through proportional analogies rooted in divine mathematical order, rather than through physical efficient causation. This, she claimed, explains why Kepler rejected house divisions, which depend on the arbitrary rotation of the observer's local horizon, while accepting planetary aspects, which depend solely on spatial geometry. She further suggested that this framework allows astrology to be genuinely predictive without being mechanistically deterministic, since the harmony operates at the level of formal correspondence rather than push-pull causality.

David objected that this sounds like a post-hoc rationalization designed to preserve Kepler's scientific reputation by recasting his astrology as symbolic mathematics. He argued that if Kepler genuinely believed in observer-independent geometric influence, he ought to have been able to specify why a ninety-degree aspect carries predictive weight in mechanistic terms, and his admitted inability to do so—coupled with his acknowledgment that astrological predictions frequently fail—demonstrates that he was clinging to a traditional practice he could not rationally justify, not advancing a coherent alternative metaphysics.
