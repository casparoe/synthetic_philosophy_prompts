A seminar exercise applying Lewisian counterfactual semantics to the logic of penal substitution has started to look like a reductio of all vicarious punishment, and I cannot see the exit. Here are the steps:

(1) For penal substitution to hold, there must be a true counterfactual: had Christ not borne the punishment for sinner S, S would have borne it.

(2) On Lewis's account, such a counterfactual is true just in case, among worlds where the antecedent holds, the closest ones—measured by overall similarity, with laws preserved, particular facts matched, and miracles minimized—are worlds where the consequent holds.

(3) If the substitution is genuinely penal rather than merely exemplary or governmental, then the suffering Christ endures must be the very punishment S deserved, not merely an equivalent quantum of pain; otherwise we have replacement, not substitution of penalty.

(4) So in evaluating (1) we must compare worlds that are otherwise maximally like ours except that Christ does not suffer and, instead, either S suffers that exact penalty or divine justice is satisfied differently.

(5) Now consider the two nearest worlds where Christ does not suffer. In world A, God exercises clemency and waives S's debt; in world B, God enforces justice and S receives exactly the penalty Christ actually bore.

(6) World A differs from actuality by one divine act of forgiveness and by the absence of Christ's suffering. World B differs by that absence, plus the introduction of S's suffering, plus all the causal and particular-fact changes that follow from S undergoing that specific penalty.

(7) By standard Lewisian weighting—especially the priority of avoiding miracles and preserving actual particular facts—world A is closer than world B, since world B introduces a large new suffering event that does not occur in the actual world.

(8) Therefore, on Lewis's semantics, the counterfactual in (1) is false: had Christ not died, God would have forgiven S, not punished S.

(9) Hence penal substitution is undermined from within the Lewisian framework.

(10) But the same structure applies everywhere: take any case of vicarious liability—say, a guardian paying a minor's legal penalty. The closest world where the guardian does not pay is one where the authority waives the fine, not one where the minor is imprisoned. So the argument entails that no vicarious payment or punishment is genuinely penal.

(11) That conclusion is absurd; we should not have to deny every form of vicarious liability to save Lewisian semantics.

I would most like to preserve (2) and (3): I think Lewis's similarity metric is basically right, and I think penal substitution does require identity of penalty, not just equivalence. If I must choose, I would rather abandon (7) than (3), but I am not sure what abandoning (7) commits me to. Give me a committed answer—do not merely list options—identify the exact step that fails, explain why it looked plausible, and then price the cost of rejecting it for both (2) and (3). In particular, if the flaw lies in applying counterfactual semantics to normative substitution rather than in the similarity ranking itself, say so explicitly and say whether that rescue saves vicarious punishment in general or only in this theological case.
