As a doctoral student working at the intersection of social ontology and institutional design, I have been observing a sustained disagreement between two colleagues in our interdisciplinary lab about a scheduling practice that has crystallized around our shared-resource booking system, and I believe it poses a precise question about the conditions under which an institutional regularity becomes a convention in the relevant philosophical sense. Our facility operates a hybrid reservation platform: there is an open calendar visible to all users, but running behind it is an automated shadow queue that assigns hidden priority weights based on past cancellation patterns and dynamically updated project-urgency scores. Over the past eighteen months, an unwritten expectation has solidified among regular users—when someone cancels a reservation with less than six hours’ notice, they are expected to manually trigger the shadow-queue release protocol rather than returning the slot immediately to the open pool, even though the software interface technically permits either action and does not enforce the manual path.

My colleague Mara argues that this expectation has become a genuine convention. She emphasizes that everyone knows that everyone expects the manual trigger, that direct-to-open releases are routinely met with informal reprimand, and that the practice is clearly arbitrary in the sense that the group could have settled on automatic redistribution without functional loss, since the underlying algorithm could be reconfigured without altering the physical equipment available. My colleague Jonas denies this categorization. He claims that because the shadow queue is deterministically computed from objective project metrics, the “rule” is not an arbitrary coordination solution but rather an epistemic shortcut for tracking an externally fixed ordering; he maintains that participants are coordinating on the correct interpretation of an algorithmically given hierarchy, not generating a self-sustaining normative equilibrium of the kind that requires arbitrary selection among equally viable alternatives.

Their exchange developed through four specific moves. (1) Mara said that the arbitrariness condition is satisfied precisely because the algorithm’s weighting parameters are themselves contingent institutional choices—the urgency-score formula has been revised twice in the past year—so the current queue ordering is no less conventional than the manual-release norm built around it. (2) Jonas replied that contingency in the algorithm’s design does not render the derived ordering arbitrary for the agents; they treat the weights as given background conditions rather than as objects of collective choice, which distinguishes their situation from a pure coordination problem such as selecting which side of the road to drive on. (3) Mara then argued that common knowledge of the sanctioning pattern is sufficient: even if the queue is algorithmically mediated, the group has stabilized a second-order expectation that one ought not exploit the direct-release option, and that meta-expectation sustains the practice independently of the bot’s computations. (4) Jonas countered that this conflates a norm with a convention—he grants that there may be a normative expectation, but insists it is parasitic on an independent functional rationale (equitable access to scarce equipment) rather than being an arbitrary solution to a coordination problem in which any stable equilibrium would serve the participants equally well.

Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the four moves, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say which colleague is closer to being correct overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding whether algorithmic mediation, especially when part of the mechanism is opaque to participants, alters the standard analysis of how conventions evolve and stabilize.
