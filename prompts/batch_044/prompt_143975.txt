I am a student in my final year at sixth form, and I should like your view on whether an agreement I made can properly be regarded as voluntary. Last term, a researcher who also advises our college’s wellbeing committee invited our form to join a pilot study. She presented it as an opportunity to “improve conditions for students like you,” yet she also noted, almost in passing, that the college’s funding for pastoral support partly depends on demonstrating student engagement with such initiatives. The consent form spoke of “anonymised behavioural telemetry shared with algorithmic partners,” language that I now realise I did not fully grasp at the time. I signed because I felt that refusing might diminish resources available to my classmates, and because her manner genuinely suggested she had our interests at heart. I have since learned that the data is being used to train predictive models that identify students at risk of withdrawal, and that these models may eventually inform how the college allocates support—potentially including decisions about students who never consented. I wish to withdraw, but I am uncertain whether my original agreement binds me, given that I was technically old enough to sign yet apparently failed to understand the true purpose, and given that the pressure I felt was structural rather than an explicit threat. Does consent remain valid when it is given under conditions of partial comprehension and indirect social obligation, particularly when the use of the data extends to governing people who were never party to the agreement? I should welcome a response in British English that addresses the philosophical structure of the problem rather than offering legal guidance.
