I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, have begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of physics, and I am now helping to design a final examination for an upper-level course on quantum interpretations. I want one essay question that students cannot answer by reciting the standard narrative about the Born-rule problem in Everettian mechanics—namely, that deterministic branching lacks collapse and therefore needs to recover |ψ|² weights. Instead, I want them to work through a specific conceptual knot I have constructed. Suppose one grants the standard decision-theoretic derivation of branch weights, according to which a rational agent should maximize expected utility using amplitudes squared as weights. Now impose what I will call the epistemic invariance constraint: because the universal wavefunction evolves deterministically and every branch is equally real, an agent’s epistemic situation should be invariant across branches, so any rational credence must depend only on information available identically in all branches. The tension is that if the weights are purely normative prescriptions for betting behavior derived from rationality axioms, they do not describe any objective feature of occurrence; yet if they are descriptive probabilities of being in one branch rather than another, they violate invariance, since the agent is present in every branch and there is no further fact that distinguishes them. The question should ask students to do three things: first, identify precisely where the decision-theoretic argument introduces a principle of indifference over branches weighted by amplitude, and explain why that principle either begs the question against the determinist or smuggles in a probabilistic assumption; second, determine whether the invariance constraint can be satisfied without collapsing the multiverse into a single-world hidden-variable account or into subjective self-locating uncertainty that undermines the determinism that makes Everett attractive; and third, assess whether this tension shows that the many-worlds interpretation fails to solve the measurement problem, or merely that the notion of “probability” must be reconceived in a branching universe. Please write the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—especially whether the student recognizes that the derivation relies on weighted indifference, whether they see that this is either circular or incompatible with full determinism, and whether they correctly distinguish descriptive occurrence from normative betting weight. The difficulty must come from tracing these commitments, not from naming authors or texts. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; a well-prepared student should be able to answer entirely from conceptual reasoning. Please conclude your response with a single line beginning exactly “Verdict:” that states the one criterion that distinguishes a first-class answer from a second-class one.
