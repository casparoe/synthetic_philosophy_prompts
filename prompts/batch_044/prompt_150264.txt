I'm the new intake coordinator for our tennant union's dispute panel — basically I have to decide which complaints about landlords get forwarded to mediation based mostly on what tenants tell me, often second or third hand. I never thought about epistemology before but now it's my whole job, and I'm trying to build something I can actually run.

I want a decision procedure grounded in the testimony debate — reductionism vs anti-reductionism — but with a twist I've been playing with: call it "testimonial friction," meaning the resistance a claim meets from the listener's background beliefs and from other available sources. The idea is that anti-reductionists are right that we have a default entitlement to accept testimony, but reductionists are right that this entitlement gets eroded by friction — not just by direct counter-evidence but by internal incoherence, by the speaker's known incentives, and by how "heavy" the claim is (how much it asks us to revise our picture of the situation). I need this turned into an actual checklist my panel can run in about 15 minutes per complaint, with ordered questions, thresholds for "pass," "stop," or "escalate to human judgment," and explicit flags for where values, not facts, take over.

Don't just lay out options — take a definite stand: should friction be calculated globally for the whole report or locally per individual claim? And can high-friction testimony ever be accepted without independant corroboration? Defend the procedure you build, don't hedge with "it depends on your philosophical commitments."

Then run it on three cases: (1) a tenant says her landlord entered her apartment without notice, witnessed only by her roommate who told her after the fact; (2) a group of tenants reports the building has black mold, but none have medical training and they all live in the same unit; (3) a single long-time resident claims the management company is systematically overcharging everyone, based on comparing his own bills with neighbors over years. Make sure one comes out in a way I'll probably hate — maybe the mold one gets kicked out despite feeling urgent — and flag exactly where the procedure breaks down or hands off to judgment. Keep it practical, I'm not a philosopher, and don't ask me for citations or a reading list.
