Restate the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem—the distinction between reasons that genuinely justify an attitude and reasons that merely cause it, with particular attention to belief, admiration, and desire—using only the vocabulary of causal inference: directed acyclic graphs, do-interventions, structural equations, and the contrast between evidential conditioning and causal conditioning. I am fluent in that technical vocabulary but not in the normative literature, so keep the philosophical claims explicit. For each of the three attitude-types, translate the thesis that there exist right and wrong kinds of reasons into claims about permissible parent nodes in the structural model governing that attitude, distinguishing cases where the normative distinction aligns with a causal distinction and cases where it diverges. Then report the distortions: for example, whether treating an inappropriate reason as a node to be intervened upon misrepresents the normative claim that it ought to be disregarded, and whether the justification of admiration requires an evaluation function that cannot be represented within the graph without smuggling in normative premises. Conclude with a residue analysis identifying which elements of the debate—such as the asymmetry between belief and desire, or the authoritative status of justifying reasons—lack any counterpart in causal vocabulary. Assess whether that absence reveals an irreducibly normative dimension to the problem, or whether it is merely an artifact of philosophers employing modal and deontic language where a precisely specified causal model could have done the work. Throughout, maintain an honest translation: do not reduce normative appropriateness to optimal prediction error, and do not embed deontic claims inside causal ones.
