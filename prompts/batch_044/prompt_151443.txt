I have recently begun co-directing a collaborative research unit split between Tokyo and Berlin, and the proverb came up in a dispute that has made the topic personally urgent for me: a senior colleague invoked “the nail that sticks out gets hammered down” not merely to predict social friction, but to argue that my insistence on individually attributing an error-correction was conceptually misplaced—that in this context, treating the correction as belonging to a single researcher applies a Western ontology of the autonomous agent to a social world in which persons are not the kind of entities that can genuinely “stick out” apart from their relational roles.

Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this proverb could be expressing, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false? In particular, I want you to separate:

(i) a descriptive sociological claim—that societies shaped by Confucian and broader East Asian traditions enforce conformity through stronger informal sanctions than those typical in Western liberal societies;

(ii) a normative cultural claim—that collective harmony is a value that ought to outweigh individual distinction or dissent;

(iii) an ontological claim about the self—that persons are not pre-social individuals who enter relations, but are constituted by them, so that “standing out” is not the suppression of an authentic self but a failure of proper relational integration;

(iv) a pragmatic coordination claim—that groups with shared goals function more efficiently when visible deviations are minimized, regardless of any deeper metaphysical view of the person;

(v) a less-discussed epistemic or hermeneutic claim—perhaps drawing on the idea of “rectification of names”—that what appears from the outside as punitive “hammering” is actually corrective alignment: the deviation is a misperception of one’s role, and the social response restores correspondence between name (role) and reality (performance).

For each reading, please say whether it actually supports my colleague’s dismissal of individual attribution, and indicate which readings are probably being conflated when people invoke the proverb to end an argument. Is there any coherent synthesis of these claims that avoids reducing the proverb to either pure sociology or pure metaphysics, and what would that synthesis predict about institutional design in a cross-cultural setting?

Please state how confident you are in the distinctions you draw, and single out the point about which you are least certain—particularly regarding whether the ontological reading (iii) genuinely differs from the pragmatic reading (iv) in its practical implications for how credit and correction should be structured.
