I've been following debates in rationalist-adjacent spaces about well-being, suffering-focused ethics, and the familiar experience-machine thought experiment, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative architecture that I can't quite place. It appears whenever people evaluate lives or policies in which intense suffering is causally and narratively woven into goods that seem inseparable from it—think of the sustained grief that is also the expression of deep love in long-term caregiving, or the years of failure and isolation that appear constitutive of a particular kind of artistic or scientific achievement rather than merely costs paid for a separate benefit. In these cases, one side treats suffering and the associated good as separable line items: suffering is entered as a negative, the achievement or relationship as a positive, and the evaluation is a matter of whether the net balance favors the life. The other side rejects this framing entirely, insisting that because the suffering is part of the very structure of the good—remove it and you do not have the same good at all, only a different, lesser one—it should not be weighed as an independent negative entry, but evaluated only as part of an indivisible whole.

What puzzles me is how selectively this holistic refusal is deployed. The same interlocutors who reject decompositional welfare accounting for these "integrated suffering" cases often revert to precisely that kind of moment-by-moment, separable-state analysis elsewhere—tallying authentic pleasures against simulated ones when comparing real life to the experience machine, or weighing a guaranteed reduction in suffering against a loss of autonomy as if the two were independent variables on a common scale. I have watched this shift happen within single threads without anyone treating it as a distinct move. I am aware of narrative theories of well-being and of value-theoretic talk about parts not summing to the value of a whole, but this pattern seems more specific: it is not merely the claim that lives have narrative structure, but a consistent toggle between holistic and decompositional evaluation that tracks whether suffering is embedded in a meaningful context. Is there an established concept or literature for this exact toggle—the selective suspension of welfare accounting specifically when suffering is structurally bound to a valued good? If the nearest concepts are things like organic unity in value theory, framing effects in decision theory, or narrative identity in well-being, I want to understand precisely how this differs from each: whether it is a legitimate methodological pluralism, a named failure mode, or simply special pleading with a philosophical label. If there is no established term, I want one coined with clear boundary conditions—what exactly counts as the toggle, what distinguishes it from ordinary context-sensitivity in evaluation, and where it collapses into plain inconsistency—because I am not satisfied with a bare name; I want the carving.
