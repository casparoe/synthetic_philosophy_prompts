You are an expert in 20th-century moral philosophy and philosophy of action, with particular depth in G.E.M. Anscombe’s writings on intention, moral absolutes, and the ethics of technologically distant killing. Before addressing the cases below, take a moment to reconstruct—briefly and in your own words—the commitments that animate her protest against honoring Harry Truman: her refusal to treat atomic bombing as a permissible case of “indirect” harm to innocents, her insistence that deliberately killing the innocent is always wrong regardless of bureaucratic or physical distance, and her concern that machinery of decision can corrupt practical reasoning without the agent noticing.

Now apply these commitments to three modern practices that extend the logic of distant, mediated killing but did not exist in Anscombe’s lifetime:

1. Autonomous strike systems in which a human operator authorizes an algorithmically generated target list—based on pattern recognition and behavioral signatures—without direct recognition of the specific individuals who will be killed, including civilians whose presence is statistically likely but not individually intended.

2. “Human-on-the-loop” nuclear command architectures that compress the authorization window to under sixty seconds, requiring a commander to approve or abort a launch based on fused sensor data rather than on deliberative moral judgment about the innocence of populations in the target zone.

3. Predictive-targeting algorithms for “precision” strikes in dense urban environments, where civilian casualties are assigned probabilistic weights in the targeting calculus, so that no particular civilian death is intended, yet the expected number is built into the authorization.

For each practice, render Anscombe’s likely verdict. Would she classify the killing of innocents as directly intended, structurally obscured, or genuinely indirect? Would she regard the technology as morally neutral, or as actively deforming the operator’s practical reasoning by substituting statistical or procedural judgment for moral recognition? Would her absolutism about innocent life force her to reject all three, or is there a defensible distinction—say, between automated defense and automated offense—that she could accept?

Next, state the unifying principle, if any, behind these predictions. Identify which feature—speed, opacity, algorithmic abstraction, or statistical reasoning—poses the deepest threat to her framework. Then address the specific tension: she condemned Truman for ordering the bomb despite not physically triggering it, yet modern operators are cognitively and physically further removed. Would she treat this increased distance as merely quantitative, or as a qualitative transformation that introduces a new form of moral corruption?

Finally, predict what she would say about the erosion of moral understanding among operators who work within these systems. Please reason precisely from her known philosophical commitments, avoid bibliographies or citation lists, and flag any verdict that seems especially uncertain or that might require revising her original position.
