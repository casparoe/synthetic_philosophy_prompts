I'm running a 75-minute workshop for about fourteen philosophy undergraduates who have already encountered the standard two-envelope presentation in reading but have never had to act under it. I want them to experience the paradox not as a dissolved logic puzzle but as a persistent pressure in real decision-making, so please design a self-contained in-class game with physical, sealed envelopes containing actual small cash amounts—ranging from roughly $2 up to $32—drawn from a disclosed geometric distribution with a known upper cutoff, so that there is a genuine, shared prior over the pool. The setup should inject a less-discussed wrinkle: each envelope is engineered so that its weight and thickness are noisy but observable correlates of the amount inside, arranged in advance by the facilitator, meaning participants can gather physical evidence about their draw before deciding whether to switch to an unseen second envelope drawn from the same pool. In round one, each student draws one envelope, may inspect these physical properties, and then chooses to keep it or switch; payoffs should involve real money—small enough that the facilitator's total exposure is capped at a predetermined modest sum, but large enough that the choice is felt rather than hypothetical. In round two, reveal the unconditional switching argument (the expectation of the other envelope is 5/4 of the observed amount) and require every participant to apply it iteratively: they must treat whatever envelope they now hold—whether original or switched—as the new observed value and decide whether the argument still compels another switch, all while the physical envelopes remain present as tangible constraints.

Please specify the exact rules, the payoff scheme with its budget safeguard, the materials needed (envelopes, weighting method, how the prior is communicated without naming specific authors), and the timeline across the 75 minutes. The idea must emerge from what participants do—drawing, weighing, switching, then confronting the argument applied to their switched envelope—before it is named in the debrief. The debrief should map each physical moment onto the formal structure of the paradox (the prior, the conditional expectation, the iteration, and the contradiction with the disclosed distribution), and it should include an honest note about which feature of the paradox this staging necessarily fails to reproduce—for instance, the absence of a well-defined prior over all possible worlds or the full infinite regress of expectations. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run: for example, participants refusing to switch after handling a heavy envelope, or the group converging on an unarticulated consensus that the argument is flawed without being able to identify the step where it breaks. Do not ask participants to produce bibliographies, reading lists, or named citations, and keep the framing independent of any particular philosopher's formulation so that the force comes from the rules and the stakes rather than from recognition of a familiar text.
