In my final-year seminar on democratic institutions we have been comparing sortition, strict term limits, and the career-politician model, and one student has begun every contribution with a variation on the proverb that "familiarity breeds contempt"—using it first to argue that long-serving representatives grow contemptuous of voters, then to claim that only random selection by lot can restore democratic humility, and finally to dismiss the value of legislative expertise altogether. I suspect the slogan is doing far more work than it should, but I want to be precise about exactly what work it might be doing. Could you separate out the distinct philosophical claims that "familiarity breeds contempt" could amount to in this debate—psychological, institutional, normative, and epistemic—indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a defensible empirical thesis, or a non-sequitur, and saying which versions actually support sortition, which support term limits, and which undermine both by denying that accumulated experience has any value? I would like the readings arranged as a menu I can present in the next session, with explicit attention to whether the claim distinguishes between moral corruption of the office-holder and the erosion of institutional memory that sortition might cause. Please respond in British English, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
