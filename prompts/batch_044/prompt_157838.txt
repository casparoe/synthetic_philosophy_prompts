I am applying for an eight-week summer independent-study fellowship to produce a 4,000-word research paper on the meta-problem of consciousness. The standard framing, following Chalmers, asks why physicalist accounts of consciousness leave an “explanatory gap” feeling intact: even if consciousness is fully physical, we still wonder why it is physical in precisely this way. Existing responses divide mainly between phenomenal-concept strategies—according to which the gap lies in our special concepts, not in the world—and cognitive-closure strategies—according to which our cognitive architecture is structurally unable to close the gap. Both treat the persistence of puzzlement as either a semantic quirk or a biological limitation, and neither explains why the problem takes exactly the form it does: an asymmetry between first-person and third-person access, a resistance to reductive explanation, and the intuition that the question is not merely unsolved but permanently open-feeling.

My half-formed idea is that both camps overlook a structural feature that may be doing the real work. Any system that builds a model of itself must represent its own representational states at a different level of abstraction from the states being represented; call this the reflexive modeling gap. The precise question I propose is: Is the meta-problem of consciousness actually an instance of this broader reflexive gap—one that would arise for any sufficiently complex self-modeling system, artificial or biological—rather than a special mystery about phenomenal properties? If the gap is generic, does that reduce the meta-problem to a problem in the epistemology of self-reference, or does phenomenal consciousness still contribute something that reflexive structure alone cannot capture?

The project would proceed in three stages. Weeks 1–2: map the standard meta-problem literature without resolving it, identifying exactly where phenomenal-concept and cognitive-closure accounts locate the “hardness” and what each predicts about the four symptoms listed above. Weeks 3–5: develop the reflexive-modeling framework, using predictive-processing and artificial-self-modeling not as empirical neuroscience but as structural analogies, and test whether the framework reproduces the four symptoms without invoking special phenomenal concepts or innate cognitive closure. Weeks 6–7: determine whether the framework eliminates the specialness of consciousness—if the gap is generic, consciousness is not uniquely mysterious—or whether phenomenal properties resist assimilation to reflexive structure in a way that reinstates the meta-problem. Week 8: write up, with an explicit failure condition: if the reflexive framework either cannot reproduce the four symptoms or collapses back into one of the standard phenomenal-concept accounts, then the project fails to show that the meta-problem is structurally generic rather than sui generis.

This scope is realistic for eight weeks because it requires no original empirical research and no mastery of technical neuroscience; it is a conceptual comparison between two explanatory schemas. The obvious alternative—simply adjudicating between Papineau-style concept strategies and McGinn-style closure strategies—would leave the meta-problem exactly where it started. The risk is that the analogy between artificial self-modeling and phenomenal consciousness proves too thin; I will guard against that by requiring that any proposed structural parallel must explain, not merely redescribe, the specific intuition that physical explanations of consciousness feel incomplete. A successful result would be a clear criterion for when the reflexive gap does and does not generate meta-problem-style puzzlement, together with a verdict on whether phenomenal consciousness satisfies that criterion. If I cannot deliver that criterion with determinacy, the project will have failed on its own terms.
