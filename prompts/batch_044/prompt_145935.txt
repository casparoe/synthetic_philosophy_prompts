Write a philosophical dialogue between two interlocutors—call them A and B—who hold opposing positions on whether hedonism can withstand the heterogeneity objection. A defends the view that all pleasures share a uniform phenomenal quality, a specific hedonic tone that persists across radically different intentional objects (for instance, the satisfaction of solving a logical proof, the bodily relief of warming cold hands, and the appreciative pleasure taken in witnessing another person's moral act). B rejects this qualitative uniformity, contending that these states share only a positive evaluative stance toward their objects, and that positing a hidden common feeling either collapses into circularity or describes something too thin to ground a theory of well-being. Structure their exchange around the three cases mentioned above: in each round, A should attempt to isolate the shared tonal feature, while B should argue that what A identifies is either not present, belongs to a different psychological kind (such as satisfaction, relief, or admiration), or is defined circularly as "what all pleasures have in common." Require that both speakers respond directly to each other's arguments: B must address A's claim that evaluative unity is insufficient without phenomenal unity, and A must address B's charge that phenomenal descriptions of pleasure always borrow from the intentional content they are meant to transcend. The dialogue should conclude with an explicit disagreement about whether hedonism needs a shared phenomenal substrate or can be sustained solely by a formal feature of positive valuation. Neither speaker should be a strawman: A should concede that the tonal quality is difficult to articulate without reference to its object, and B should concede that ordinary language groups these states together for some non-arbitrary reason.
