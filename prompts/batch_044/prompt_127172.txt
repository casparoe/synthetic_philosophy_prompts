I'm advising a colleague who directs a small lab publishing open-source water-filtration protocols for rural health clinics. She discovered that her team's last paper overstates efficacy by roughly 8% due to an unreported calibration drift. Correcting the record won't endanger patients—clinics already apply redundant safeguards—but it may jeopardize her upcoming grant renewal. She intends to publish an erratum because, she says, the clinics deserve accurate data. At the same time, she admits her motivation is complicated: she recently embraced an intense personal code of intellectual cleanliness drawn from an obscure self-cultivation essay, and she suspects that without this private philosophical framework she would not have bothered. She also concedes she is partly motivated by fear that a rival lab will expose the drift first. She asks me whether issuing the correction is still genuinely admirable, or whether the fact that her concern for the clinics is filtered through a self-regarding hobby and mixed with reputational anxiety somehow diminishes the value of what she is doing. She also wants to know whether the origin and purity of her reasons matter at all, or if only the outcome and the presence of the right concern should count. How should I think about this?
