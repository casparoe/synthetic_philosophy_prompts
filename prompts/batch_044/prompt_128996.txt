I am serving as a juror in a civil authenticity dispute concerning a painting attributed to a well-known mid-twentieth-century artist. The plaintiff's expert witness, an art conservator with three decades of experience, testified that upon viewing the canvas she experienced an immediate, non-inferential recognition of inauthenticity—she compared it to recognizing a familiar face rather than diagnosing symptoms. The defense countered that her judgment was cognitively penetrated: she had been briefed beforehand about the painting's provenance through a gallery with documented ties to forgeries, and they argue this background knowledge altered the phenomenal character of her visual experience rather than merely providing premises for post-perceptual reasoning.

The judge asked the jury to determine whether her testimony reflects genuine perceptual expertise or belief-contaminated observation, but I am unsure whether that framing captures the actual philosophical difficulty. Specifically, I am wondering whether there is a coherent middle position—what one fellow juror described as perceptual restructuring—in which prolonged training reorganizes the phenomenal field itself so that expert vision is neither pure bottom-up registration nor top-down cognitive intrusion, but a transformed mode of seeing in which certain formal anomalies become immediately salient. Does the standard cognitive-penetration literature recognize such a restructuring, or does it force a false dichotomy? More practically, if her report of direct seeing is phenomenologically accurate, does that accuracy bear on whether her belief about the gallery's history causally shaped what she saw?

I am not asking for case law, citations, or further reading; I want to understand the conceptual structure of the situation and whether the distinction between seeing and interpreting actually applies to expert testimony of this kind.
