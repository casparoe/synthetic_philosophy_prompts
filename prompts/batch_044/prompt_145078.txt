Construct a thought experiment that embeds Fitch’s paradox of knowability within a realistic, resource-bounded scientific collaboration, rather than in an abstract modal framework. Your scenario must be no longer than one paragraph, avoid omniscient agents, perfect predictors, or science-fictional technology, and turn on a specific structural feature: a finite research collective in which members can come to know that a particular empirical result has not yet been established, and in which the collective’s shared epistemic state is itself an object of knowledge within the collective. Engineer the case so that three positions deliver clearly distinct verdicts: (i) an unrestricted knowability theorist who accepts both that every truth is possibly known and a closure principle for distributed group knowledge across testimonial chains; (ii) a Fitch-style realist who rejects the knowability principle and maintains that some truths are necessarily unknown; and (iii) a restrictionist who retains the knowability principle but blocks the paradox by denying that the closure principle applies to self-referential group attributions.

Arrange matters so that, if closure applies within the group, the unrestricted theorist is apparently forced to conclude that there exists a specific true proposition—namely, that some particular claim remains unknown to the collective—which cannot be known by anyone in the collective, contradicting the principle that all truths are knowable. After presenting the scenario, provide a rigorous check: state the key propositions, identify the precise closure rule being applied, specify the relevant nearby epistemic worlds (or possible states of the collective), and explain exactly why each of the three positions delivers its verdict. Then identify the feature that does the work—the self-referential loop in distributed epistemic attribution—and explain what happens if that feature is removed (for instance, by replacing group knowledge with a single isolated agent), showing that the case then collapses back into the standard Fitch result. Finally, present the restrictionist’s defence: how they can concede that closure holds for ordinary empirical claims while denying its application to propositions about the collective’s own knowledge states, and assess whether this saves the knowability principle or merely relocates the problem. Write in British English, maintain a formal register suitable for a philosophy seminar, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; build the case from general epistemic logic and the structure of collective knowledge so that it remains fully meaningful without access to external sources.
