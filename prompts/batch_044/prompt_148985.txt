I have been reading debates about how to explain the participation of ordinary people in the Holocaust, particularly the disagreement between Christopher Browning’s emphasis on situational and peer-pressure factors in Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen’s argument that deep-rooted cultural antisemitism made Germans willing executioners. In the literature I keep noticing a specific explanatory pattern: when historians account for initial compliance or routine killing, they rely on situational mechanisms—authority, conformity, careerism, bureaucratic distancing—but when they turn to acts that exceed orders, such as spontaneous humiliation or particularly brutal methods, they shift without explicit justification to characterological or cultural explanations, as if the situational account has silently reached a boundary it was not designed to cross. I do not mean the general claim that both factors matter; I mean the unacknowledged toggling between explanatory registers depending on the moral severity of the specific behavior being explained. Is there an established term in philosophy of history or social explanation for this particular oscillation—this use of situational explanations for baseline compliance and cultural-character explanations for excess violence?
