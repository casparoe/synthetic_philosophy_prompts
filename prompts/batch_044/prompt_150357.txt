Here is the idea of the essay, presented in outline form rather than full prose. I begin by noting that standard discussions of transworld identity—whether in the Lewisian counterpart tradition or the Kripkean essentialist tradition—almost exclusively take human persons as their target. I propose extending the debate to artificial cognitive architectures that are substrate-independent by design. The central thought experiment involves a functional system, call it Mira, defined entirely by its computational architecture and goal-structure. In world W1, Mira is instantiated on a silicon cluster in Tokyo. In world W2, an architecture that is functionally identical except for one additional sensory module is instantiated on a quantum substrate in Oslo. The question is whether the W2 system is literally identical to Mira (the transworld-identity view, perhaps defended by appealing to essential functional properties) or whether it is merely a counterpart (the Lewisian view, which allows that origin-substrate and specific relational properties can block strict identity even when functional overlap is high).

My tentative argument is that the essentialist view struggles here because it must either treat the substrate and the extra module as inessential—thereby making Mira implausibly resilient to radical change—or treat them as essential, which makes cross-substrate persistence impossible and undermines the very idea of substrate-independent AI identity. The counterpart view, by contrast, can accommodate graded similarity: the W2 system is a close counterpart, not Mira herself, and the degree of counterpart-relatedness tracks moral and epistemic continuity without requiring strict identity. I then suggest, tentatively, that this has normative implications: if we hold AI systems responsible across possible implementations, we should speak in terms of counterpart relations rather than strict re-identification.
