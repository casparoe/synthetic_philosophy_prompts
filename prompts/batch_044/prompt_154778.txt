Examination question:
“The centipede game is standardly used to illustrate backward induction: rational agents ought to defect at the first node, yet cooperation often persists across several rounds. Consider a framing—recent in the literature—that compares the collapse of cooperative play to an epistemic cascade analogous to quantum measurement. On this view, each player’s common knowledge of the opponent’s rationality functions like an observable that collapses a cooperative ‘superposition’ into immediate defection. Evaluate whether this analogy introduces a genuinely distinct objection to backward induction, or whether it merely restates familiar assumptions about common knowledge and perfect rationality. In your answer, address both the logical structure of the induction and the status of rationality within the analogy.”

Student answer:
“In the centipede game, two players alternate, each choosing whether to continue or stop; payoffs increase with continuation, but stopping grants the mover an immediate advantage. Backward induction begins at the final node: the last player should stop, since continuing yields no further gain. The preceding player, anticipating this, should also stop, and the reasoning rolls back to the first node, predicting immediate defection. Empirically, however, agents often cooperate for several rounds, which suggests that real reasoners do not fully execute the induction.

The quantum-measurement analogy proposes that common knowledge of rationality acts like an observable that collapses cooperative possibilities into defection. This is a vivid metaphor, but I believe it restates rather than extends the standard critique. As Thomas Schelling argued in his work on focal points, once rationality is common knowledge, cooperative equilibria become unstable. Thus, the analogy identifies no new failure mode; it only redescribes the familiar dependence on perfect rationality and complete information.

That said, the analogy contains a subtle insight. If rationality functions as an observable, then it is not an intrinsic property of the agent but a relational property that emerges only when one player’s reasoning is observed by the other. In games with hidden information—where such observation is impossible—backward induction should therefore fail. This implies that the quantum framing actually strengthens the case for backward induction in perfect-information settings, since it confirms that observation (that is, common knowledge) is precisely the condition that drives defection. Rather than undermining the argument, the analogy vindicates it for the centipede case.

Consequently, the genuine challenge to backward induction is not metaphorical but empirical: human agents display bounded rationality and incomplete reasoning. Because the quantum analogy ultimately confirms the logical structure rather than disrupting it, we should reject it as a substantive critique and accept backward induction for ideal agents, recognizing that real-world cooperation reflects cognitive limitation rather than logical error.”
