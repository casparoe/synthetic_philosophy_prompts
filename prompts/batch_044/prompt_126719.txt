I was discussing with a colleague from a constitutional design working group whether an automated legislative framework could genuinely incorporate self-amending provisions without collapsing into interpretive deadlock. This led me to think more systematically about self-amending rule systems—such as those modeled in Nomic—in which the rules governing amendment are themselves subject to amendment, and about the broader paradoxes of legal self-reference that arise when a system's authority to alter its own structure is defined entirely within that structure. I am not looking for a historical survey or a bibliography of constitutional theory; rather, I want to understand the deepest structural implications of these dynamics for the possibility of a fully automated or algorithmically mediated legal order that possesses no hermeneutic discretion and no external interpretive anchor.

To make the problem concrete, imagine a hypothetical recursive amendment protocol embedded in such an automated system: Rule Set A governs the procedural conditions under which Rule Set B may be modified, while Rule Set B simultaneously defines the criteria of validity for any amendment to Rule Set A, and there exists no meta-rule, hierarchical escape clause, or appeals mechanism capable of breaking the symmetry between them. Please describe the most important implications of this kind of closed-loop self-reference for legal stability, for the avoidance or inevitability of paradox, and for the claim that any functioning system of legal change necessarily requires an external—presumably human—interpretive authority. In particular, address whether this structure necessarily generates an unresolvable oscillation between competing amendment claims, how it relates to classic paradoxes of self-amendment in constitutional theory, and what it reveals about the fundamental limits of encoding legal transformation in deterministic formal systems that cannot appeal to context, intention, or judicial judgment.
