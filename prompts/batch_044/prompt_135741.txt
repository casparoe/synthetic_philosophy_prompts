I'm coordinating peer review for an interdisciplinary research fund that requires every proposal to be evaluated on three logically interdependent dimensions: the empirical adequacy of its underlying data model, the validity of its inferential bridge from that data to a concrete policy recommendation, and the ethical permissibility of the recommendation itself. Because our five reviewers come from different disciplinary backgrounds—statistics, philosophy of science, and applied ethics—we repeatedly face the discursive dilemma: a majority may endorse each pairwise combination of premises without endorsing the derived conclusion, or may converge on rejecting a proposal for contradictory reasons that cannot be jointly satisfied. I need a reusable decision procedure that my panel can run in a single ninety-minute meeting, working only with binary judgments and without access to external literature. The procedure should specify, in strict sequence, which aggregation rule applies first—premise-based or conclusion-based—what numerical threshold counts as sufficient consensus at each stage, the exact point at which the procedure mandates rejection, the point at which it must halt and hand the conflict to human deliberation because the disagreement is one of values rather than logic, and the structural conditions under which it is guaranteed to misfire regardless of rule refinement. Apply the procedure to two worked cases: first, a profile in which four reviewers accept both the empirical and inferential premises but split on the ethical conclusion, producing an inconsistent collective judgment; second, a profile in which all five reject the final recommendation but do so for incompatible reasons—some denying the empirical premise, others denying the bridge—so that no consistent premise-set commands a majority and the collective rejection appears unanimous only at the conclusion level. Include one hard case where the procedure yields an answer you think the panel will find unwelcome, flag the one irreducible limitation that makes this kind of aggregation permanently vulnerable to manipulation by framing, and state explicitly whether that limitation should disqualify the procedure from high-stakes funding decisions.
