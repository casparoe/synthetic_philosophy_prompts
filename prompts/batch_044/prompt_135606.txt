I've been thinking on my own about the different ways people talk about freedom — being left alone, being able to pursue what you genuinely value, and not living under someone else's arbitrary power — and I keep returning to Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale. The Republic of Gilead obviously strips away the first kind: handmaids are confined, surveilled, and compelled into the Ceremony. Yet what unsettles me most is not the direct coercion but the scenes in which the Commander invites Offred into his study to play Scrabble and converse, apparently bending rules. In those moments he is not actively harming her, yet the unspoken fact that he could, on a whim, remains absolute. Philosophers distinguish this as domination — the presence of arbitrary power even when it is not exercised — from mere interference. I want to read the novel through that three-part lens: negative liberty, positive liberty, and republican non-domination. Does Atwood dramatize republican unfreedom more powerfully than she dramatizes lost negative liberty? Where does the story show domination operating without direct interference — for example, in the Aunts' training methods, in Serena Joy's authority over Offred, or in the architecture of the Eyes? At the same time, Gilead claims to advance women's positive freedom by protecting them from the corruption of the old world and directing them toward their natural purpose. Does the novel treat that claim as pure ideology, or does it allow that conception of freedom any genuine traction? I am especially interested in whether the book suggests that removing arbitrary interference is insufficient if a person has already internalized the values of the dominating structure — a possibility I have not seen discussed in the philosophical writing I have encountered. A good reading should not reduce Atwood's world to a diagram; it should stay faithful to specific scenes and characters, note where the novel resists the philosophical categories, and say what the fiction reveals about liberty that abstract argument alone cannot. Which scenes best show that resistance, and what do they add?
