I'm preparing material for an advanced seminar on the measurement of welfare, and I've constructed an argument about lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism that I now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve the view into either incoherence or redundancy. The specific context is compound suffering—situations in which physical pain, loss of autonomy, and social humiliation occur together—where defenders sometimes claim that compound suffering activates a qualitative "veto condition" that operates across incommensurable dimensions without requiring a single metric. Here is the argument: (1) Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism holds that suffering exceeding a certain boundary cannot be compensated by any amount of pleasure. (2) For this boundary to do normative work, there must be some comparative relation between suffering and pleasure—otherwise we could not determine whether a given state lies above or below the threshold. (3) If suffering and pleasure are genuinely incommensurable—no common ordinal or cardinal scale—then the notion of crossing a threshold that pleasure cannot overcome is undefined. (4) Therefore the view requires at least partial commensurability: we must be able to rank or measure suffering and pleasure relative to one another in the boundary region. (5) But partial commensurability is unstable. Once we can compare suffering-unit S and pleasure-unit P at the boundary, we have adopted a common measure. We can then ask why the exchange rate jumps discontinuously at exactly this point; any answer either generalizes to make all suffering highly weighted (collapsing the view into standard weighted negative utilitarianism) or is ad hoc. (6) The only alternative is full incommensurability, in which case the lexical boundary is empty and the view collapses. (7) Hence lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism is either redundant or incoherent.

If I must choose, I would rather preserve the claim in (1) and the comparative requirement in (4) than accept (7), but I want to know precisely where the argument slips—whether in (3), in the move from (4) to (5), or in a hidden assumption that partial comparison entails a continuous scale. In particular, could a defender maintain that compound suffering triggers a veto through qualitative disjunction rather than quantitative comparison, and if so, does that save the lexical feature or merely rename the problem? Please diagnose step by step, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and explaining what accepting or rejecting each one costs.
