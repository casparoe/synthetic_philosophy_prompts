I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology studying evolutionary dynamics in strategic interaction, and I've encountered a pattern in iterated prisoner's dilemma simulations that I cannot map cleanly onto the standard taxonomy. Consider a strategy that cooperates in every round of interaction but carries an endogenous termination condition: if the partner has cooperated consistently for a specified number of rounds, the strategy exits the interaction immediately before the round in which sustained reciprocity would yield its highest mutual payoff. Within each played round there is no defection—the opponent's behavioral record remains unblemished—yet the partner is systematically deprived of the future cooperative surplus. In evolutionary simulations, such a mutant often invades populations of unconditional cooperators more aggressively than a classic defector, precisely because it avoids the punishment mechanisms that target explicit within-round betrayal. At the same time, it is not merely random truncation or an exogenous endgame parameter, since the exit is conditional on the partner's cooperative history and functions as an exploitation mechanism rather than a cost-saving measure.

The phenomenon seems adjacent to the endgame effect in finitely repeated games, to truncation strategies in evolutionary modeling, and to various forms of conditional cooperation, but none of these categories quite capture the specific structure: a strategy that preserves cooperative norms within rounds while strategically annihilating the future rounds that make cooperation rational. Is there an established term—perhaps in game theory, evolutionary biology, or the philosophy of cooperation—that specifically identifies this kind of conditional, history-dependent exit? If the literature does not distinguish it, I would like to understand how best to delimit it from neighboring concepts such as the endgame problem, forgiveness in Tit-for-Tat variants, and ordinary defection, and what boundary conditions would determine whether it constitutes a genuinely distinct failure mode of cooperation rather than a subclass of strategic truncation.
