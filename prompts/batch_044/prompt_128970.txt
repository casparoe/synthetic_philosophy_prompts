I'm writing a paper for a course on atrocity law and the politics of naming mass violence, and I've become stuck on what seems like a deep inversion between Raphael Lemkin's original concept and the legal instrument that eventually adopted his term. As I understand the historical record, Lemkin formulated genocide in the mid-1940s primarily as a framework for cultural, economic, and political destruction—what he characterized as the dismantling of a group's foundations of life—while treating direct physical extermination as a related but conceptually distinct phenomenon better addressed through existing war crimes categories. He also insisted on protecting political groups, viewing the decapitation of political leadership as an integral mechanism of group destruction. Yet the 1948 UN Convention that institutionalized the word did the reverse: it restricted the crime to physical and biological acts, dropped the cultural and economic dimensions, and—under diplomatic pressure—excluded political collectives from protection.

Given this apparent reversal, I am struggling to understand why jurists and philosophers continue to treat Lemkin's authority as foundational for modern genocide law. If his conceptual core was the non-lethal, structural destruction of groups—including political ones—then the Convention's focus on physical killing with specific intent seems to apply his term to the very phenomena he assigned elsewhere. Furthermore, in contemporary political discourse, activists and governments frequently invoke genocide to describe structural domination, cultural suppression, or political elimination that lacks industrial-scale killing. Are these applications recovering Lemkin's authentic vision, which international law allegedly betrayed? Or has the term become so conceptually detached from its origin that invoking Lemkin's authority is now merely rhetorical? I would especially appreciate clarification on whether my premise about Lemkin's exclusion of physical killing is mistaken, and how the politics of atrocity naming relates to this legal history.
