I am preparing an essay for my senior philosophy seminar on the paradox of tragedy—specifically, why audiences deliberately seek out artworks that evoke sorrow, fear, or pity. In a recent discussion, a classmate presented a summary she attributed to a contemporary essay in aesthetics, which she called the "cognitive quarantine" or "affective distancing" model. According to her account, this view holds that our awareness that tragic events are fictional creates a psychological buffer: negative emotions are genuinely experienced, yet they are simultaneously marked as harmless because no actual suffering is occurring. She claimed that this buffer does not merely dampen the unpleasantness; rather, it produces what she termed a "hedonic reversal," in which the very intensity of the negative emotion is converted into a positive aesthetic reward, meaning that the more harrowing the artwork, the greater the pleasure derived from this "safe danger." She went on to argue that this framework renders Aristotle's theory of catharsis entirely obsolete, on the grounds that catharsis depends on a real emotional discharge, whereas distancing prevents any authentic release and substitutes a purely cognitive satisfaction for it.

I find the intuition about fictional awareness compelling, but the summary strikes me as blending several distinct claims into one bold thesis. Is this a fair representation of any serious philosophical account of tragic enjoyment? I would particularly like to distinguish which elements are accurate descriptions of distancing or quarantine theories—such as the role of psychical distance in moderating emotional response—from the more questionable additions, including whether any version of the view genuinely asserts a literal transformation of grief into pleasure, and whether the dismissal of catharsis as incompatible with distancing is justified or a mischaracterization. I do not need references or a reading list; I am asking only for a conceptual audit that separates the defensible core from the subtle distortions.
