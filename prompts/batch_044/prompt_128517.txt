At a workshop on institutional epistemology, two colleagues—Jordan and Maya—debated whether anonymous, unpaid peer review in philosophy journals should be replaced by a signed, compensated system. They asked me to referee. Here is their exchange.

Jordan began by arguing that Chesterton's fence is routinely misapplied in policy debates: its point is not to preserve every status quo, but specifically to prevent tearing down structures whose purpose you genuinely do not understand. In this case, we understand peer review's stated purpose—quality control and bias mitigation—and evidence in philosophy suggests it often reproduces disciplinary orthodoxy, exploits junior scholars, and does not outperform open, signed alternatives. Because the harm is visible and the protective function is not performing, the fence should come down.

Maya responded with a Burkean conservatism: peer review is not a single-purpose tool but an evolved institution embedding the accumulated practical wisdom of scholarly communities across generations. Even if its formal structure is relatively recent, it has survived repeated attempts at replacement because it solves a coordination problem—allocating scarce publication space without explicit market pricing—through a gift economy that sustains norms of disinterested judgment. Burke's conception of society as a partnership linking the dead, the living, and those yet unborn applies here: we inherit procedural norms containing tacit knowledge about epistemic risk that we are not fully equipped to redesign from first principles.

Jordan countered that Burke's defense of prescriptive rights applies to institutions with genuine historical depth and social selection, not to administrative inventions of the mid-twentieth century. Anonymous peer review in philosophy lacks the pedigree of a common-law tradition; treating it as an inherited wisdom is historical fiction. Jordan added that Maya now justifies the practice through diffuse functions—trust, gift exchange, coordination—that differ from its official rationale of quality control. This retrospective rationalization demonstrates Jordan's point: defenders invent new purposes to keep fences standing once the original purpose has been shown inadequate.

Maya replied that Burkean justification does not depend on antiquity but on functional survival: practices persist because they solve problems we may not articulate prospectively. That peer review has outlasted editorial dictatorship and open-commentary experiments is evidence of its value. Furthermore, retrospective reasoning about institutional goods is not a fallacy but a feature of practical reason—Burke criticized rationalist redesign precisely because it demands that every benefit be traceable to prior intent. To require that institutional goods be visible in advance is to adopt the rationalist mistake Burke warned against.

Jordan's final move pressed an unfalsifiability worry: if any harmful practice can be saved by invoking some diffuse, retrospective social function—trust, coordination, embedded wisdom—then conservatism collapses into an uncritical defense of whatever exists. Jordan asked for a principled criterion distinguishing a decayed institution from a healthy one, noting that Maya offers none except persistence itself.
