I've been thinking about Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper and I think people usually treat it like a mild warning — don't just assume everything is an adaptation, check for constraints first. But I'm wondering if it actually pushes further: in cases where we know the underlying architecture is heavily constrained (like how mammalian visual cortex is wired for edge detection), shouldn't we flip the burden of proof? Like, treat the funcitonal-looking trait as a spandrel by default unless there's direct evidence the constraint was selected for that specific function?

I'm especially stuck on applying this to human narrative preference — the fact that we instinctively parse stories around agentive protagonists with goals. Most evolutionary psych people treat this as an adaptation for social coordination or cheater-detection. But if our theory-of-mind machinery is already locked in by selection for social cognition, then narrative structure might just be a spandrel of that architecture, not an adaptation in its own right. So my view is basically reverse adaptationism for architecturally constrained domains: spandrels are the null hypothesis.

What would actually count against this? I want the kind of evidence or argument that should genuinely move me, not just "well maybe it was adaptive too." Like, is there a decisive empirical finding that would show narrative preference isn't just riding on ToM? Or a formal result? Also, has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that okay for a methodological claim of this sort? I'm less interested in people just listing standard critiques of adaptationism; I want to know what risks this specific version takes.
