I'm writing a feature on how scientific visionaries who died before a theoretical framework emerged might have engaged with it, relying solely on the logic of their documented commitments. Take the late Carl Sagan, whose advocacy for active interstellar signaling and whose skepticism toward anthropocentric models of alien aggression shaped late-twentieth-century SETI ethics. The framework in question is the dark forest hypothesis: the argument that cosmic silence is not evidence of absence, but of strategic concealment, because any civilization that reveals its position risks preemptive annihilation by more advanced competitors.

Rather than stay at this general level, I want to examine a narrower, less commonly analyzed variant—call it 'preemptive containment.' Here, a detecting civilization does not destroy the source of a technosignature. Instead, it imposes an invisible, sustained quarantine: for instance, engineering subtle perturbations in local stellar dynamics that render interstellar travel within a defined radius permanently uneconomical, coupled with systematic jamming or redirection of all outbound communications. The target civilization survives physically intact, yet is confined to an inescapable local bubble—alive, but permanently isolated and unable to expand or announce itself.

Given Sagan's conviction that long-lived technological societies should be discoverable through cooperative transparency, and his argument that aggression tends to be self-undermining at cosmic scales, how do you think he would have evaluated this containment scenario? Would he have seen it as a more ethically tolerable expression of dark-forest logic, or as a more insidious form of cosmic domination—one that preserves the victim only to deny it agency? And specifically, how might his distinction between active and passive SETI strategies inform whether he regarded containment as a rational precaution or as an imperialist suppression of emerging voices?
