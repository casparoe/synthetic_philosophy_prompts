In my course on value pluralism we have been working through Ruth Chang’s idea of hard choices and Raz’s account of incommensurability, and I am trying to understand an institutional split that seems to presuppose one view in one domain and reject it in another. In human-subjects research ethics, institutional review boards typically operate with what looks like a lexical veto structure: if a protocol violates informed consent or exposes participants to disproportionate risk, it is rejected outright, regardless of how large the expected scientific or social benefit might be. The harm and the good are not balanced on a common scale; they are evaluated on independent tracks with disqualifying conditions. By contrast, the same health systems often use cost-effectiveness analysis—cost per QALY, for instance—to aggregate lives saved and resources spent into a single metric for public-health funding decisions. What philosophical commitments about value comparison does this divergence presuppose? Does the IRB practice assume that autonomy and welfare are genuinely incommensurable, or does it merely reflect a practical refusal to compare them? Could a defender of hard choices endorse the lexical structure in research ethics while rejecting aggregation in health policy, or is the split just institutional convenience with no coherent philosophical basis? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value that licenses treating these two domains differently, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change in either practice if we replaced the implicit view with a consistently comparativist or consistently non-comparativist framework.
