In applied AI ethics, a growing number of alignment teams collect human moral judgments—say, on trolley-style trade-offs or resource-allocation dilemmas—then apply statistical de-biasing to correct for framing, order effects, and social-desirability distortion, and feed the residual data into reward models as if they represent the subjects' genuine moral preferences. What theory of moral psychology does this pipeline presuppose? Does it require that moral beliefs have a stable core that survives noise removal, or only that preferences converge after correction? Is it compatible with the view that so-called biases—loss aversion, in-group favoritism, temporal discounting—are not distortions but constitutive moral commitments? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of preference embedded in the practice, distinguish what the pipeline strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and describe what an elicitation protocol would look like if it treated every systematic deviation as a potential value rather than an error.
