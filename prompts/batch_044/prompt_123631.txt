The view under discussion is a form of tranquilism: well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, so that a life with no unmet desires and no pain is maximally good, regardless of whether it contains pleasure, achievement, or meaningful relationships. Opponents—who defend what might be called a constructive or flourishing-based account—insist that well-being requires the presence of positive goods, and they regularly invoke the case of the Satiated Hermit: a being with no suffering, no cravings, but also no projects, no affection, and only minimal awareness. The tranquilists in the room judge this life to be excellent in welfare terms; the flourishing camp judges it barely better than non-existence, because nothing of value is actually present. What frustrates me is that everyone agrees on the phenomenology—nobody denies that suffering is bad or that craving is unpleasant—and yet the two camps talk past each other without any clear point of contact.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a straightforward empirical disagreement, but rather a structural one about what question we are answering: the tranquilists treat well-being as an eliminative concept analogous to health, where the absence of disease is the standard, while their opponents treat it as a constructive concept analogous to strength or beauty, where absence of defect is not enough. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real divide, or whether there is a hidden normative disagreement about whether positive mental states have intrinsic value, or whether the stalemate is simply a clash of bedrock intuitions about the Satiated Hermit that neither side can argue for. Could you diagnose the disagreement precisely—is it verbal, about different concepts of well-being; substantive, about whether positive goods carry independent weight; or something else, such as a divergence over which cases count as data for a theory of welfare? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result in decision theory, a phenomenological argument about the nature of craving, or should we expect that no argument will shift the intuitions involved?
