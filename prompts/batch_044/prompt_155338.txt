Consider my judgments on cross-modal recognition when direct experience in one modality is absent, extending the classic sensory problem to conceptual domains. In the sensory case, I think a person blind from birth who learns shapes by touch cannot immediately distinguish a cube from a sphere upon gaining sight, yet can immediately recognize a familiar person's face by sight having previously known only their voice and touch. In conceptual analogues, I think a mathematician who has learned a proof only through formal symbol manipulation can apply it correctly to an unfamiliar physical system, but cannot grasp the intuitive feel of continuity without direct spatial engagement. I also think someone who learns color vocabulary solely through syntax and wavelength descriptions can correctly classify new objects by color upon first seeing them, yet cannot experience the qualitative character of red — they apply the concept without phenomenal acquaintance. By contrast, I think a synthetic system trained purely on haptic correlations, lacking an internal 3D geometric model, fails to recognize shapes visually altogether, whereas one with such a model succeeds. These verdicts feel partly consistent but I cannot articulate the governing principle: is the boundary between structural-functional knowledge, which transfers, and qualitative-phenomenal knowledge, which does not, or does the face-recognition case show that social-emotional recognition transfers even without explicit structural modeling? Which of my judgments must a coherent principle overturn, and what does it predict for the case I keep returning to — a musician who understands harmony only through mathematical frequency ratios and is then given auditory experience for the first time: can she recognize emotional tension in a chord progression?
