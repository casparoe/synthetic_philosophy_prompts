As a retired atmospheric chemist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been working through the experimental-philosophy literature on intentional-action attribution, particularly the asymmetry in judgments about foreseen side effects that is widely discussed in connection with Joshua Knobe's studies. Over the past year I have discussed a sequence of vignettes with a colleague—an experimental philosopher with a specialization in action theory—and have recorded her consistent verdicts.

In the first case, a corporate executive pursues profit through a manufacturing process, knows that an ecosystem will be damaged as a side effect, and proceeds without concern. My colleague judges that the executive damaged the ecosystem intentionally.

In the second case, the structure is identical except that the foreseen side effect is an ecological improvement, which the executive does not seek. My colleague judges that the executive did not improve the ecosystem intentionally.

In the third case, harm occurs but the agent genuinely lacks any belief that such harm was possible. My colleague judges that the harm was not intentional.

In the fourth case, a parallel benefit occurs with the same genuine ignorance. My colleague judges that the benefit was not intentional.

These four judgments are jointly consistent with more than one unifying principle. One reading is a direct moral-valence rule: a foreseen harmful side effect is attributed as intentional, whereas a foreseen beneficial side effect is not, with ignorance blocking attribution in either direction. Another reading is an acceptance-based rule: intentionality requires a practical stance of acceptance toward the effect; social norms allow acceptance of harm to be inferred from foresight alone, whereas acceptance of benefit requires explicit desire, and ignorance in either case prevents the inference.

I now wish to apply this framework to a new case that introduces institutional agency, distributed decision-making, and the normative architecture familiar from double-effect reasoning—features largely absent from the standard individual-agent vignettes.

Consider a climate-engineering consortium governed by a multi-member board. The board approves a stratospheric aerosol trial. All members know that rainfall suppression in a drought-prone region is a foreseen harmful side effect. They institute mandatory hydrological monitoring and a suspension protocol, lobby individually for stricter safeguards, and each signs a statement of regret. Bureaucratic delay prevents the protocol from halting the trial for seventy-two hours, during which the drought worsens. Separately, the aerosols unexpectedly catalyze a chemical reaction that accelerates ozone recovery, producing substantial environmental benefit; no board member had foreseen this possibility.

The specific question concerns the board's intentionality with respect to the drought aggravation, and, by contrast, with respect to the ozone recovery.

If you are to advise what my colleague should say in order to remain consistent with her earlier four judgments, please proceed as follows.

First, lay out precisely the two principal rules that could unify her earlier verdicts, and state what each rule predicts for both parts of the present case.

Second, identify which of her four earlier judgments is the closest analog to the drought-aggravation scenario, and explain the basis for that comparison, including where the analog breaks down.

Third, take a committed position: state which rule she ought to apply, what her verdict should be on both parts of the case, and give your reasons for preferring that rule over its rival.

Fourth, be explicit about the normative principle she is adopting regardless of which verdict she reaches—whether she extends a moral-valence criterion into institutional and collective contexts, or whether she applies an acceptance-based criterion that examines distributed practical stances rather than individual mental states.

Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—structurally different from both the earlier four and the present one—that would genuinely distinguish the two rules, so that we could determine which principle she actually holds.

Please answer with a definite recommendation rather than a balanced survey of options, and indicate where your own reasoning depends on judgment rather than deduction.
