Consider the track record of an environmental historian who rules, case by case, on whether a non-human force should be treated as an independent "historical actor"—that is, a cause with explanatory agency not reducible to human institutions, choices, or networks—in the narratives she accepts.

Her verdicts so far:

- The spread of the Black Death through Mongol trade networks and its decimation of fourteenth-century Europe: not an independent actor; the explanatory work belongs to human connectivity and urban density.
- The 1815 Tambora eruption, the subsequent "Year Without a Summer," and the conditions that facilitated cholera's global spread: yes, an actor; the climate forcing was exogenous and irreducible.
- The multi-millennial desiccation of the Sahara and the long Bantu expansion into sub-Saharan Africa: not an actor; this framing risks environmental determinism, and human adaptive and migratory strategies are the proper focus.
- The 536–540 CE volcanic winter (the "dust veil" event) coinciding with the Plague of Justinian and widespread societal stress: yes, an actor; the cooling operated outside human social structures.
- The arrival of Phytophthora infestans in 1840s Ireland and the resulting famine: not an actor; the catastrophe was produced by British land-tenure systems and monoculture dependency, with the pathogen serving only as a trigger.
- The extreme 1876–78 El Niño event and the near-synchronous famines across British India, Qing China, and northeastern Brazil: yes, an actor; the climate teleconnection functioned as a unified global force.
