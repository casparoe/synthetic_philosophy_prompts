After a long argument with a friend about whether upzoning violates anyone's rights, I have been trying to isolate a recurring argumentative move in housing and zoning debates that feels conceptually slippery. The pattern runs like this: an interlocutor will concede, in general terms, that exclusionary zoning has caused serious harm and that more housing is urgently needed. But when a specific development is proposed near them, they pivot to framing "the neighborhood" as a kind of persistent moral subject—one that holds entitlements independent of any current resident. They speak of protecting "the character of the place" or honoring an implicit contract with existing homeowners, as if the spatial community itself, rather than the individuals who happen to live there now, can be wronged by an influx of new members. I have seen this in two closely related forms: one that treats local scale and demographics as a collective good being stewarded for future generations, and another that treats current residents as trustees of a neighborhood's "integrity" that prospective residents threaten simply by arriving. What makes the move distinct is that the very people who would become part of the community are treated as outsiders whose exclusion is justified in the name of protecting it; the boundary of moral consideration is drawn around a place rather than around the set of people affected, and that boundary shifts precisely to exclude those whose presence would change it. Is there an established concept that captures this specific configuration—where a geographic community is reified into a rights-bearing entity whose claims can override the membership interests of the people who would join it? If there is no precise name, can you coin and define one, with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary NIMBY self-interest, from status quo bias, from legitimate democratic input, and from the "right to the city" arguments that run in the opposite direction? I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what exactly this reasoning commits one to, where it collapses into something else, and whether it can be made coherent without implicitly denying that newcomers are full members of the moral community being protected.
