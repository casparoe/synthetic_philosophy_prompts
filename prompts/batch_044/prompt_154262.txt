I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and I need a rigorous conceptual clarification of a specific tension within illusionist accounts of phenomenal consciousness. Please explain the problem that arises when an illusionist holds both that phenomenal properties are systematically misrepresented in first-person judgment and that these judgments nevertheless carry a kind of functional authority that shapes reasoning, memory, and social coordination. I want you to distinguish precisely between a strong error-theoretic variant, on which there are literally no phenomenal properties to serve as objects of misrepresentation, and a weaker misrepresentationalist variant, on which there are properties but they are radically different in kind from their apparent qualitative character. For each variant, articulate what exactly serves as the content of the erroneous self-ascription if not an intrinsic phenomenal property, and explain how the illusionist can avoid collapsing either into eliminativism about mental content or into a covert physicalist reduction that merely relocates the explanatory gap. Focus on the conceptual architecture of transparent self-access and representational error rather than on empirical neuroscience, and address specifically whether the illusionist can consistently maintain that the error is never detectable from the first-person perspective without thereby smuggling back in the very phenomenal reality that the theory denies.
