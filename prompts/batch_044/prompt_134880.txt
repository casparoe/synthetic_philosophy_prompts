I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though over the past year I have been reading essays on my own about Donald Davidson’s theory of radical interpretation—the view that to understand a stranger’s language and beliefs we must assume they are largely rational and share our basic picture of the world. Last month I discussed this with a friend in cognitive science, and our conversation turned to whether Davidson’s framework could apply to something that genuinely does not share our logic. That led me to sketch a short essay outline introducing what I am tentatively calling a “tiered charity” model: in radical interpretation, we can tolerate false empirical beliefs far more easily than we can tolerate deviations in basic inferential structure. I apply this to a hypothetical being, or perhaps an artificial system, that consistently accepts contradictions as substantive features rather than as mistakes to be resolved—what I understand philosophers call paraconsistent reasoning. My essay argues that such a case would not merely be difficult to interpret; it would fall outside the conditions for radical interpretation altogether, because without shared logical constraints there is no stable anchor for attributing beliefs or meanings. The outline then asks whether Davidson’s framework implicitly excludes genuinely alien rationalities, or whether an interpreter could always reinterpret contradictions as hidden classical consistency.

I would appreciate your comments on this outline. Specifically, I want to know whether the tiered charity distinction is defensible or whether Davidson already addresses it, whether my application to contradiction-accepting reasoning actually follows from his premises, and whether the conclusion that such beings are uninterpretable rather than merely strange is too strong. I am also curious whether the overall framing sounds like an interesting extension of Davidson’s ideas or just a confusion between logical disagreement and ordinary empirical error, and what obvious objections I am missing. I do not need a bibliography or reading list—only an honest assessment of where the reasoning holds up, where it seems to collapse, and whether any part of the argument strikes you as worth developing further.
