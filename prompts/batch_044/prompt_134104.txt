If we adopt four-dimensionalism, so that I am a spacetime worm whose temporal parts stand in exactly the same compositional relation as my spatial parts, then death looks like mereological truncation rather than the annihilation of a subject. The Epicurean argument—that death is not bad for me because there is no subject present to suffer it—seems to presuppose a three-dimensionalist ontology in which I exist wholly at each moment. On the four-dimensionalist view, the missing future temporal parts are literally parts of me, and their absence is structurally parallel to losing a limb: a deprivation of parts, not of experiences. So the standard deprivation account of death's badness ought to collapse into ordinary part-loss harm, and philosophers should treat dying like amputation scaled up. Yet they clearly don't, and even four-dimensionalists took death to be a distinctive harm. Where is the mistake? Is it that temporal parts are not "mine" in the right sense to ground deprivation, or does four-dimensionalism actually fail to undermine the Epicurean "no subject" point because the truncated worm itself never occupies the missing parts?
