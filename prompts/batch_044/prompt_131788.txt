Consider the long-running debate in philosophy of medicine between naturalist and normativist conceptions of health and disease: the view that disease is ultimately a deviation from species-typical biological function versus the view that it necessarily involves a normative judgment about harm or undesirability. Much of the literature has focused on mental health classification, but I want to shift attention to a cluster of boundary cases that receive less conceptual scrutiny—conditions such as age-related sensory decline, benign anatomical variations that are near-universal in older populations, and traits like sickle-cell carriage that are functionally advantageous in specific ecological contexts yet are routinely catalogued as pathological in clinical taxonomies. Suppose you are advising a researcher with about three years and a background in conceptual analysis, not clinical epidemiology, on where to direct limited effort in this space. Which open questions or lines of work should they prioritize, and in what order? I am particularly interested in whether a modified naturalism can accommodate environment-relative functional norms without collapsing into normativism; whether normativism can specify harm in a non-circular way that does not smuggle in biological normalcy; whether a pluralist framework can distinguish clinical taxonomy from welfare-based taxonomy without fragmenting medicine; and what the resolution of these questions implies for global health resource allocation, where the classification of a condition as a disease often determines funding eligibility. Defend your ranking: for each item, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for conceptual rather than empirical work, and what kind of conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the priorities. I want a defended agenda, not a survey or reading list.
