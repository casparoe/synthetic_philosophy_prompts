I'm working out my position on inheritance, dynastic wealth, and equality of opportunity for a distributive-justice seminar, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is the package:

(1) I work within a broadly Rawlsian framework: fair equality of opportunity is lexically prior, and large inherited concentrations of productive capital are a primary mechanism by which social class reproduces itself across generations.

(2) Parents have a legitimate but strictly limited entitlement to bequeath resources to their children. This entitlement is not pre-political; it derives from the role family continuity plays in sustaining democratic stability and the social bases of self-respect.

(3) I support inheritance taxation that scales with both market value and what I call "dynastic depth"—the number of generations the same capital asset (corporate equity, rental portfolios, land) has remained within a single lineage.

(4) I understand equal opportunity substantively: heirs must not begin life with radically different access to education, risk-bearing capacity, and professional networks, not merely face the absence of formal legal barriers.

(5) I also hold that overly aggressive taxation violates savers' legitimate expectations, weakens the motivational structure behind capital accumulation, and—through reduced growth—can ultimately harm the least advantaged.

(6) There must be a lexical floor: no scheme may leave an heir without resources sufficient for a decent standard of living and basic educational access.

(7) The state should distinguish inherited productive capital, which should face steep progressive and dynastic-depth taxation, from inherited personal-use assets (a family residence, modest liquid savings within a defined threshold), which should be taxed lightly or exempted.

(8) I reject both the view that inheritance rights are natural and the view that tax policy should be settled by pure aggregate utility maximization.

Do these hang together? I want you to go through the pairs and tell me: which are genuinely in tension, which only appear so, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the real tensions by severity, specify which commitment has to give for consistency, and do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging. I am not signaling which belief I would abandon; treat the set as fixed and audit what it actually commits me to. Keep the analysis focused on internal coherence rather than a general lecture on inheritance tax.
