I hold a standing-access account of privacy: privacy is not primarily a matter of controlling information flow, but of the normative structure of accessibility—specifically, the absence of others' legitimate standing to access or deploy information about you, irrespective of whether they actually do so or whether you possess practical means to block them. On this view, end-to-end encryption restores privacy only if it eliminates a service provider's normative entitlement to decrypt, not merely its technical ability; conversely, widely known information embedded in a community with strict norms against its weaponization might remain private despite near-universal awareness. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: which observations, thought experiments, or formal distinctions would be fatal (for instance, cases in which normative standing is clearly absent yet privacy is intuitively violated, or cases in which standing is present yet no privacy harm seems possible); which would be merely awkward (such as the view's difficulty accommodating concerns about hidden surveillance in genuinely public spaces); and which the position's defenders should themselves concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible by auxiliary assumptions. I also want an honest assessment of whether the account risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by redefining 'standing' post hoc to accommodate every counterexample—and whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a failure for an account of privacy. Finally, identify what this standing-access view predicts that rival control-based or reductionist accounts do not, so that I can see where the view is genuinely exposed rather than merely unconventional.
