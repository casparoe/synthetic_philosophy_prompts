I am serving as a teaching assistant grading responses for an upper-level undergraduate examination on Plato's Republic, Book II. The examination question posed to students was the following.

Question: In Republic II, Glaucon recounts the story of the ring of Gyges in order to press a specific challenge against Socrates' developing defense of justice. Carefully explain the structure of Glaucon's challenge: what he asks us to imagine, what conclusion he draws about the origin and value of justice, and how he distinguishes justice pursued for its own sake from justice pursued for its rewards and reputation. Next, distinguish Glaucon's position from Thrasymachus's claim in Book I that justice is the advantage of the stronger, paying attention to whether they share the same account of why laws exist and who benefits from them. Finally, evaluate whether the ring narrative succeeds in showing that justice is valued only for its consequences. In doing so, address specifically whether the ring establishes that human beings are naturally and psychologically disposed toward injustice, or whether it merely removes external incentives; and discuss whether the version of the story Glaucon relates differs in any significant respect from earlier ancient accounts of the same figure.

Below is one student's answer.

Student answer: Glaucon introduces the ring of Gyges, adapting it without alteration from Herodotus's Histories, in order to ask whether justice is ever desired for itself. He asks us to imagine a just person and an unjust person, each given a ring that renders them invisible, and predicts that both would behave identically: they would steal, seize power, and satisfy every appetite without fear of punishment. From this Glaucon concludes that justice is not a natural good but a conventional compromise. People agree to be just only because they are too weak to do injustice with impunity; the agreement is therefore a second-best arrangement, not a choice made for justice's intrinsic value. This argument is essentially the same as Thrasymachus's position in Book I, since both claim that justice serves the interests of the powerful—except that Thrasymachus describes the actual laws made by rulers, whereas Glaucon describes the hypothetical origin of those laws in mutual fear.

The ring demonstrates that human beings are naturally and psychologically disposed toward injustice. Even a person who has always acted justly possesses no internal attachment to justice; the just character is merely a mask maintained by the expectation of reward and the threat of penalty. Once the ring removes both external punishment and internal shame—since invisibility eliminates any witness, including one's own reflected self—the true nature of every human being is revealed as tyrannical. Therefore justice has no value in itself. Its only worth is instrumental: it protects the weak from the strong when detection is possible.
