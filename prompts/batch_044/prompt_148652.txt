I am drafting an essay for a seminar in distributive justice and would like conceptual feedback on the argument sketched below.

Standard prioritarianism holds that benefits to the worse-off carry greater moral weight, which is usually defended as avoiding the leveling-down objection: equality is not intrinsically valuable, so we need not reduce the better-off merely to match the worse-off. I want to press a different line. The standard defense assumes that welfare is measurable on a continuous cardinal scale, so that weighting can operate as a smooth scalar multiplier on marginal value. But many goods over which justice operates—basic autonomy, epistemic access, relational standing—seem to have discontinuous value structures: reaching an adequate functioning threshold appears qualitatively different from a mere quantitative increment.

On this basis, I propose what I call modal prioritarianism. Rather than multiplying the value of goods received by the worse-off, priority operates as a constraint on the admissible set of distributions: no distribution is permissible if anyone falls below a critical capability threshold, regardless of aggregate well-being elsewhere. This relocates rather than dissolves the leveling-down problem. If we can bring everyone just above the threshold by reducing those far above to just-above status, modal prioritarianism may still demand leveling in some configurations, but now the conflict is between admissibility conditions and aggregate welfare rather than between equality and scalar priority.

Please assess whether this framing is coherent. Specifically: (1) Is my claim that prioritarian weighting tacitly requires a continuous cardinal metric accurate as a critique of Parfit’s priority view, or does that view already accommodate discontinuities? (2) Does modal prioritarianism genuinely differ from standard weighted prioritarianism, or is it a notational restatement? (3) Is the leveling-down objection avoided, or merely transformed into a problem about threshold selection?

Please say explicitly what would change your verdict—for example, a demonstration that capability thresholds integrate smoothly into scalar weights—and identify the weakest point in my reasoning, particularly my assumption that goods like autonomy have genuinely discontinuous value profiles rather than steep but continuous ones. I ask for conceptual criticism only; I do not want citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
