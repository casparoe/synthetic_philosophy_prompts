I hold that the moral weight of collective remembrance is not proportional to the scale of harm but is indexed to the structural vulnerability of the affected group: societies owe a more stringent duty to preserve small, obscured injustices against marginalized communities than to maintain monumental commemoration of large, well-documented catastrophes, and they have a corresponding obligation to practice institutional forgetting of individual achievement narratives in order to prevent hierarchical valorization. The view borrows from Margalit's ethics of remembrance and Rieff's critique of compulsory memory, but adds that both remembrance and forgetting function as distributive moral goods governed by status rather than magnitude. What would count against it? I want the arguments, counterexamples, and empirical patterns—about solidarity, historical accuracy, or the psychology of selective memorialization—that should genuinely move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, plus an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view can absorb any outcome by redefining vulnerability or institutional forgetting, thereby insulating it from refutation. If that insulation exists, is it a theoretical vice? Please answer in American English.
