I have recently been weighing whether to leave a multi-year professional commitment that has become far less rewarding than I anticipated, and I have also noticed that I resist revising a long-held belief about a close family member because doing so feels like discarding years of careful observation and emotional adjustment. Both situations seem to involve honoring sunk costs, yet leaving the commitment feels quite different from stubbornly clinging to the belief. I have constructed the following argument, which concludes that honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational and that the distinction between practical and epistemic domains collapses, but I find the conclusion suspicious because it appears to license almost any form of persistence.

(1) Rational decision-making—whether practical or epistemic—should depend solely on expected future value, not on past expenditures that cannot be recovered.
(2) Sunk costs are precisely those past expenditures of money, time, or cognitive effort that do not affect the future value of continuing versus abandoning a course.
(3) Therefore, allowing sunk costs to influence either action or belief revision is always irrational.
(4) Nevertheless, being a diachronically coherent agent requires treating prior commitments and prior beliefs as constraining present options; otherwise, one becomes a mere sequence of unrelated time-slices with no reason to honor promises or maintain stable judgments.
(5) Those prior commitments and beliefs represent sunk investments of resources that are now irrecoverable.
(6) Consequently, if an agent refuses to abandon a commitment or a belief because doing so would waste the prior investment required to build and maintain it, she is honoring a sunk cost.
(7) Yet such refusal is often not only permissible but necessary for rational agency over time.
(8) By (3) this should be impossible, but by (7) it is not only possible but required.
(9) So (3) is false, and honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational.
(10) But this seems to prove too much: if honoring sunk costs is rational whenever it preserves diachronic coherence, then nearly any stubborn persistence—remaining in a harmful relationship, holding a failing investment, or denying clear counterevidence—can be defended as protecting narrative continuity. That result is unacceptable.

Which step should I reject? I want to preserve (1) in some form, since pure backward-looking rationality seems wrong, and I want to preserve (4) and (7), because the idea that rational agency requires continuity over time strikes me as essential. I am unsure whether the error lies in (5)'s identification of commitments with sunk costs, in (6)'s assumption that coherence requires honoring those specific investments, or in (9)'s inference that this licenses broad rationalization. Please identify the weakest link, explain why it initially looked plausible, and specify what rejecting it costs—particularly for my attempt to distinguish leaving the professional commitment from clinging to the belief about my family member. Please answer in American English.
