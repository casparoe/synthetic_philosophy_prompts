You are consulting for a regional cooperative that produces artisanal cheese and has, since its founding in 1987, used a peculiar internal voting rule: each member's vote is weighted not by capital share but by the number of years they have lived within a ten-mile radius of the original creamery, a metric adopted only because the founding charter was drafted by a local historian who insisted on geographic continuity. A technically simpler one-member-one-vote rule is available and used by peer cooperatives. One board member argues that the current rule is merely a frozen accident—if the historian had never been involved, the cooperative would have adopted the standard rule—so they should migrate immediately, since the present arrangement has no deeper justification. Another board member counters that thirty years of hiring, membership disputes, and supply contracts have been structured around this specific weighting, meaning the cooperative's current identity and decision-making culture are not interchangeable with what a standard-rule cooperative would have become; even in nearby worlds where the initial charter differed, she claims, the institution might not have converged on anything like its present form. The board asks you not to resolve the practical costs of migration, but to advise whether the frozen-accident framing captures the philosophical issue at stake. Specifically, they want to know whether the relevant question is indeed "would the same voting rule have arrived anyway?" or whether there is a deeper consideration—perhaps that the sequence of adaptations itself becomes part of what is being evaluated—such that asking about counterfactual convergence misses the point entirely. What is your assessment of the two board members' positions?
