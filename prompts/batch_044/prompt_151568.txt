As a journalist who writes about neuroscience and philosophy of science, I have been trying to work out whether predictive processing undermines the idea of a theory-neutral observation, and I have constructed an argument that collapses into something absurd. Here it is, step by step.

(1) Cognitive penetration of perception occurs only when a subject's beliefs or expectations alter the phenomenal content of the experience itself, not merely the post-perceptual judgment about what was seen.

(2) Predictive processing holds that the brain generates hierarchical top-down predictions, and that only the residual prediction errors are passed upward, weighted by precision estimates shaped by context, learning, and prior expectation.

(3) If these precision-weightings and predictive suppressions operate in early sensory cortices—including primary visual cortex and the thalamic relay—then the features that reach awareness are already filtered by belief-laden expectations.

(4) Those expectations encode statistical regularities that are functionally equivalent to implicit beliefs about how the world is structured.

(5) Therefore, on a standard predictive-processing interpretation, implicit beliefs penetrate not just high-level recognition but the low-level phenomenal character of vision.

(6) Scientific objectivity requires that observers with competing theories can share a common observational basis: they must be able to disagree about interpretation without disagreeing about whether the datum is present at all.

(7) But if (5) is correct, then two scientists with different theoretical priors will have genuinely different low-level phenomenal experiences when they examine the same instrument display, tissue slide, or astronomical image.

(8) In that case there is no shared observational bedrock; every observation is already saturated by the commitments it is meant to test, and empirical adjudication collapses into circular self-confirmation.

(9) Yet predictive processing is widely treated as empirically successful and fully compatible with rigorous scientific practice—including adversarial collaborations in which opposing theorists jointly design experiments.

(10) So either predictive processing must be reinterpreted so that its top-down effects never reach phenomenal content—confining them to post-perceptual judgment—or scientific objectivity must be reconceived as agreement on reports rather than shared experience.

The argument strikes me as proving too much: it turns a mainstream cognitive framework into a case for radical incommensurability. I want to keep (1), (2), (6), and (9), and I prefer not to abandon predictive processing entirely. I suspect the slip occurs between (3) and (5), or between (5) and (7)—perhaps an equivocation between neural modulation and phenomenal alteration, or an unjustified assumption that different priors necessarily yield divergent phenomenal contents rather than convergent ones. But I am not sure which transition is the weakest.
