I’ve been trying to work out for myself whether chaos actually breaks determinism or just breaks our confidence in it, and I keep running into answers that feel like they’re talking past each other—some say the future is open because we can’t compute it, others say it’s still fixed and we’re only ignorant. Before I read more, I want the full specification: what would an adequate theory of deterministic openness actually have to deliver? By “deterministic openness” I mean the relationship between strictly deterministic dynamics—where the governing laws allow no branching—and the fact that long-range prediction fails, that small differences explode, and that agents living inside these systems sometimes stabilize local outcomes without appealing to randomness.

Write the desiderata: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results from physics and mathematics it must stay consistent with, with the reasoning behind each. Specifically, it must (1) account for sensitive dependence—say, weather-forecast failure or double-pendulum divergence—without treating that divergence as ontological indeterminism; (2) preserve the distinction between epistemic unpredictability and genuine physical branching, with a clear case for each side; (3) explain why some chaotic regimes permit useful short-term forecasting while others do not, not just assert that Lyapunov exponents vary; (4) respect the difference between global determinism and local controllability, using a concrete scenario where an embedded agent can stabilize a small region—like a boat adjusting in choppy water—despite being unable to steer the larger turbulent flow; (5) be consistent with bounded chaos and strange attractors, so it doesn’t treat chaos as unbounded explosion; (6) address “embedded forecasting”: an observer inside the system, with finite memory and computation, who couples to dissipative structures to improve local predictions, and explain whether this restores practical openness or merely extends determinism to the observer-system coupling; (7) revise Laplace’s demon honestly for finite precision rather than infinite, and say exactly what is lost; (8) remain compatible with thermodynamic irreversibility and with quantum mechanics, but without letting quantum indeterminacy do all the explanatory work for chaos; and (9) clarify whether chaos gives back a meaningful kind of practical freedom or whether determinism remains intact at every scale, with the criteria for counting something as “meaningful.”

For each requirement, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and say which single requirement does the most damage to popular informal positions. Then tell me if any subset of these is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether perfect long-range determinism, embedded forecasting with finite resources, and the absence of ontological branching can all hold together, or whether satisfying one forces us to weaken another. I want the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on scoring named theories, though if you briefly note how a strict determinist view, a computational-irreducibility view, and an emergent-openness view fare against the list, that is welcome. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual map first.
