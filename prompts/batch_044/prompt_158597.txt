Please audit whether the following set of philosophical commitments is jointly tenable, ranking any tensions by severity, distinguishing genuine incompatibilities from merely apparent ones, and specifying the least costly revision in each case. I ask that you reply in British English.

My commitments are these:

(a) The self is best understood as a centre of narrative gravity in Dennett's sense: not a Cartesian substance but a useful fictional abstraction generated when the brain weaves a protagonist into its explanatory stories.

(b) This narrative centre is not localised in any particular neural theatre; it emerges from distributed predictive-processing loops, functioning as a high-level prior that minimises free energy across social and temporal contexts.

(c) Therefore, personal identity over time should be analysed in terms of narrative continuity rather than Lockean psychological continuity or brute bodily continuity.

(d) Nevertheless, in split-brain cases where each hemisphere sustains a coherent autobiographical narrative, I take there to be literally two centres of narrative gravity, not one self with divided consciousness.

(e) I deny that pre-linguistic infants and most non-human animals possess a self, on the grounds that they lack the autobiographical scaffolding necessary to generate narrative gravity.

(f) At the same time, I maintain that an artificial system lacking phenomenal consciousness but possessing a stable, intersubjectively validated autobiographical self-model should be granted ethical and legal recognition as a centre of narrative gravity.

For each tension, indicate which commitment has to give, whether the conflict dissolves on closer inspection, and what I should concede up front if I wish to preserve the core of my position. In particular, address whether my reliance on distributed predictive processing in (b) undermines my denial of selfhood to infants and animals in (e), and whether my treatment of split-brain patients in (d) smuggles in an embodied localisation that contradicts (a) and (b). Include the objection I am most hoping you will not raise—namely, that treating an unconscious AI as a self in (f) while denying selfhood to conscious animals in (e) inverts the moral ontology my framework should deliver—and show me the weakest point in my reply to it.
