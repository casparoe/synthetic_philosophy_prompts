I am a third-year undergraduate reading philosophy of science, and this term we have been working through Daniel Dennett’s account of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—particularly in relation to predictive success and ontological commitment. The framework is well established in philosophy of mind, but I want to understand where the genuinely live philosophical work now lies, especially at the boundaries where scientific practice seems to strain the triad.

If you were advising me on how to direct the next three years of study—say, for an MPhil dissertation or a small philosophy-of-science grant—which open questions extending this framework should be prioritised, and in what order? I am particularly interested in three directions, though you should reorder or replace them if others are more urgent:

1. Whether evolutionary biology requires an irreducible design stance for functional and adaptive explanations, or whether the predictive patterns captured by treating organisms as designed can in principle be recovered entirely within the physical stance.

2. Whether predictive processing and active inference models in cognitive science collapse the distinction between the design stance and the intentional stance, or whether they instead reveal a need for an intermediate “pattern stance” that Dennett’s triad does not accommodate.

3. Whether complex-system modelling in climate science and ecology demonstrates high-level predictive patterns that are neither intentional nor genuinely design-based, and if so, whether this forces an expansion of the framework rather than a redescription within it.

For the agenda you propose, please defend the ranking explicitly: which question is upstream of the others, how tractable each is for a philosopher without laboratory or empirical resources, and what kind of conceptual or scientific development over the next few years would lead you to revise the ordering. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of authors, or a reading list; I want a reasoned allocation of limited philosophical effort—where attention should go first, and why the other directions should wait.
