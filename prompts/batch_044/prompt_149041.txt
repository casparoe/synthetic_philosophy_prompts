You serve as a forensic clinical evaluator who must repeatedly assess whether a respondent's harmful repetitive conduct was mechanically rigid in the sphexish sense—locked into a stereotyped behavioral sequence that runs to completion regardless of contextual feedback—or instead reflected flexible, context-modulating agency. Your reports inform sentencing recommendations and court-ordered treatment mandates, so you need a reusable decision procedure rather than an impressionistic opinion, and you must apply it within a 90-minute structured interview plus file review, using only observational and self-report evidence available without specialized laboratory equipment.

Ground the procedure in the philosophical distinction between sphexish mechanical rigidity and flexible agency, but translate it into ordered, practitioner-level steps. Specifically, incorporate the two-level framework used in your practice: Level-1 sphexishness, in which the same observable motor or behavioral sequence is repeated despite changed circumstances or negative outcomes; and Level-2 sphexishness, in which the surface behavior may vary, but the respondent is locked into repeating the same decision-rule or goal-selection policy regardless of contradictory feedback. The procedure should ask a fixed sequence of questions that distinguishes these levels, state what kind of evidence counts as a sufficient answer at each step, specify the exact point at which the assessment stops with a determination of high rigidity, the point at which it stops with a determination of meaningful flexibility, and the points at which it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the rigidity-flexibility distinction collapses into a normative question about acceptable risk, moral responsibility, or therapeutic suitability.

The checklist must flag explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedural checks, must require no philosophical vocabulary in its actual administration, and must say honestly what it cannot determine—for example, whether the presence of flexibility necessarily entails full moral culpability, or whether Level-2 rigidity can ever be fully excused.

Apply the procedure to three cases:
1. A defendant with diagnosed obsessive-compulsive disorder who spends two hours nightly checking that doors are locked, missing work, describing the urge as alien and driven, yet who reports altering the check sequence when physically interrupted.
2. A fine-tuned customer-service language model that receives contradictory system prompts in successive interactions but applies an identical refusal heuristic every time, producing harmful denials of service regardless of context, with no revision of its underlying policy.
3. A person with alcohol-use disorder who resumes drinking after every documented negative health and legal consequence, but who can articulate alternative life goals, occasionally delays consumption for hours, and describes the relapse as a choice rather than an automatic reflex.

In one of these applications, the procedure should yield a determination that conflicts with your intuitive moral judgment about culpability or dangerousness; report that result and explain precisely why the procedure produces it. Finally, state the class of questions—such as whether a flexible agent is always blameworthy, or whether mechanical rigidity ever eliminates autonomy—that lie permanently outside the procedure's reach and must be resolved by values rather than by assessment. Write in formal American English, with no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
