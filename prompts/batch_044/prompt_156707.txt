I've noticed an argumentative pattern that echoes the old "white horse is not horse" discussion linked to the School of Names. In various modern debates—over synthetic organisms, AI-generated works, or digitally mediated democratic mechanisms—interlocutors grant that the entity in question satisfies every defining criterion of a base category (being alive, being art, being an election). They then observe an added feature—being engineered, being algorithmic, being platform-mediated—and conclude that the entity is not a member of the base category at all, not even a subkind of it, but something categorically different. The reasoning treats the compound description ("synthetic life," "algorithmic art") as naming a distinct kind that excludes the original term, rather than as the original kind plus a modifier.

Is there an established name for this specific inferential move—using an additional, non-contradictory property to expel a case from its base category? How precisely does it relate to the Gongsun Long argument: is it the same logical structure applied outside the philosophy of language, or does it depend on a different confusion about universals and particulars? I want more than a bare label: what are its boundary conditions, how does it differ from ordinary essentialism and from simple ambiguity in compound terms, and are there contexts—perhaps in legal taxonomy or biological classification—where such an exclusion is actually justified rather than a category mistake?
