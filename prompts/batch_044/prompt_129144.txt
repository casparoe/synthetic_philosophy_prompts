Presidential constitutions typically fix the chief executive’s term and permit removal only through impeachment for defined offenses, whereas parliamentary systems allow the legislature to dismiss the government by a simple vote of no confidence with no allegation of wrongdoing required—yet that same legislature usually depends on the government’s cooperation to pass budgets and legislation. Reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic legitimacy and political responsibility that makes this pairing seem coherent rather than contradictory: does it treat the executive as a continuously revocable delegate rather than as the holder of an independent popular mandate? Then examine the apparent tension—why should a body that can destroy a government for purely political reasons still need that government to govern? Does the practice strictly presuppose that sovereignty resides in the legislature, or is it compatible with a purely practical, stability-based explanation? Finally, consider the hybrid mechanism of a constructive vote of no confidence, which allows dismissal only if the legislature simultaneously elects a successor: what conception of continuity, collective agency, and unbroken mandate does that design presuppose, and is it consistent with the same theory that licenses ordinary no-confidence votes, or does it quietly import a different philosophical commitment?
