As a retired experimental physicist who has recently entered formal philosophy study, I am seeking a six-month research plan—deliverable as one 6,000-word paper and a workshop presentation—on whether the leveling-down objection to prioritarianism is genuinely a normative problem or partly an artifact of unstated measurement assumptions. The precise question: if welfare comparisons are modeled on an interval scale (permitting differences and weighted aggregation but lacking an absolute zero and meaningful ratios) rather than on a ratio scale (with an absolute zero), does the classic leveling-down scenario—where equality at a lower level is judged better than unequal but universally higher welfare—retain its counterintuitive force, collapse, or relocate to a different structural feature such as the curvature of the priority-weighting function?

Existing treatments, as I encounter them in coursework, address the objection almost entirely within normative ethics: they ask whether equality has intrinsic value, whether prioritarian weighting should be concave, and whether the objection confuses aggregate with distributive considerations. What remains unexamined is whether the aggregation mathematics itself smuggles in a cardinal, zero-anchored ontology of well-being. A physicist is trained to distinguish nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio measurements, and to recognize that applying arithmetic operations outside their licensed scale produces pseudoproblems. I want to apply that disciplinary lens rigorously.

The method will be conceptual and formal, not empirical. Month one: formalize prioritarian aggregation—total or average well-being weighted by priority—under three explicit measurement regimes, using only the conceptual apparatus I can construct without relying on obscure literature. Month two: reconstruct the leveling-down objection within each regime, tracking exactly which premises depend on ratio-scale properties (e.g., that reducing everyone to half-well-being is comparable across domains) and which survive on interval-scale ordering alone. Month three: examine whether altering the weighting function—convex, concave, or threshold-based—interacts with scale type to revive, suppress, or transform the objection. Month four: defend the claim that an interval-scale model with a context-dependent baseline best fits our intuitive judgments about welfare improvements, and derive the precise conditions under which leveling-down still appears counterintuitive. Month five: draft the core argument that the objection either dissolves (if it was scale-dependent) or must be reformulated as a claim about baseline selection and interpersonal comparison, not about equality per se. Month six: refine the paper for an interdisciplinary audience and design a presentation that tests whether philosophers find the framing clarifying.

A successful result is a taxonomy showing that the leveling-down objection has logically distinct forms under different measurement ontologies, plus a defended position on which form—if any—remains a live normative challenge. The project fails if the scale-distinction proves purely notational: that is, if all three regimes yield identical normative conclusions about the leveling-down case, so that importing measurement theory adds no analytical purchase beyond what standard ethical argument already provides.

The scope is realistic for one researcher with no data-collection needs; it requires only careful formal modeling of aggregation and evaluation of established thought experiments. I will supply all citations and bibliographic references myself; the deliverable should contain none. I want the proposal to avoid both standard literature reviews and any appeal to niche empirical findings, keeping the argument answerable through conceptual analysis alone.
