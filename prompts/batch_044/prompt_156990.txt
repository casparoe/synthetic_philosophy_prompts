I am writing a dissertation chapter on whether classical Indian taxonomies of cognition can be mapped onto contemporary debates about modularity in cognitive architecture. In Nyāya epistemology, the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison), and śabda (verbal testimony)—are standardly described as the exhaustive and mutually exclusive instruments (karaṇas) through which valid cognition (pramā) is produced. Reading the Navya-Nyāya literature, I am struck by the way these instruments are sometimes discussed as if they were domain-specific, informationally encapsulated mechanisms in something like Fodor’s sense: each pramāṇa operates according to its own rules and delivers a distinctive type of epistemic output without internal interference from the others. If that modular interpretation is correct, however, I find the status of anumāna puzzling. Inference requires the prior apprehension of a liṅga (the reason or middle term) through perception, and it also depends on linguistic competence—specifically, the grasp of vyāpti, the invariable concomitance between liṅga and sādhya—without which the inferential transition cannot occur. Consequently, anumāna seems to recruit both the perceptual and the testimonial capacities in an integrated process. So why does Nyāya treat inference as an irreducible, primary pramāṇa rather than decomposing it into a cross-modal interaction between pratyakṣa and śabda? Is there a principled, internal-to-Nyāya reason for maintaining that anumāna has its own sui generis cognitive structure despite these dependencies, or is the modular reading itself a misprojection of modern architecture onto a normative taxonomy of epistemic instruments?
