I am a retired physical scientist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a systematic way, and I am writing to request a rigorous adjudication of a disagreement I observed among fellow members of a discussion group for retired researchers. The case concerns a museum that must choose between displaying an irreplaceable indigenous textile in conditions that preserve its historical authenticity but slowly degrade its fibers, or placing it in perfect preservation conditions behind a high-fidelity digital replica that eliminates what the ecologist Dr. Mara Voss calls its irreplaceable “material presence.” (1) Mara argued that authenticity and preservation belong to genuinely incommensurable value domains, so rational choice requires a non-compensatory preservation veto: if an option damages the artifact, it is rationally disqualified regardless of gains in other domains. (2) Leo Chen, a retired engineer, replied that this veto is not evidence of incommensurability but a concealed cardinal comparison that arbitrarily assigns infinite weight to preservation. (3) Leo then proposed a cross-domain minimal-regret rule: for each option, identify the worst regret it produces within any single value domain relative to the best outcome in that domain alone, then choose the option with the smallest maximal regret, thereby avoiding direct translation between domains. (4) Mara countered that Leo’s rule still presupposes inter-domain comparability of regret intensities—without knowing whether the regret of lost material presence is comparable to the regret of fiber damage, the rule hides an implicit weighting scheme. (5) Leo concluded that if all cross-domain comparison is impossible, rational choice is paralyzed, which suggests incommensurability functions not as a principled constraint but as an excuse for indecision. You are asked to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in practical reason and value pluralism; please think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the logical architecture of each position. Then adjudicate the five moves above move by move, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying your rating, while also noting what both disputants overlook. Separate what is settled in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as the consensus. Deliver your verdict as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
