The debate over which historical analogy best captures transformative artificial intelligence—nuclear weapons, the printing press, or the Industrial Revolution—has been running for years without resolution. In my reading, the nuclear-weapons camp emphasizes a discrete, controllable artifact with catastrophic tail risks and argues for centralized governance; the printing-press camp emphasizes the democratization of knowledge production, the erosion of epistemic gatekeepers, and unpredictable second-order social effects; and the Industrial-Revolution camp emphasizes gradual, factor-substitution economic restructuring over decades rather than a sudden discontinuity. Despite extensive exchange, no camp seems to have revised its view, and the analogies are now used more as rhetorical flags than as arguments. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on which analogy is best. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of AI—say, about whether it is an artifact, a medium, or a factor of production—or is it largely verbal, with "transformative" meaning something different in each framework? Are the sides actually answering different questions—one about risk governance, one about epistemic institutions, one about macroeconomic dynamics—under the single heading "what will AI do"? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement, perhaps about the pace of deployment or the feasibility of international coordination, that is doing all the work? I also want you to consider a more specific hypothesis: that the impasse is caused by treating AI as comparable to a single technology when it might be better understood as an institutional technology—like the corporation or the market—whose effects depend on organizational embedding rather than on intrinsic properties. If that is right, does it mean the analogy debate is ill-posed, or just that progress requires shifting from analogical reasoning to direct institutional analysis? In your answer, explain what each side actually says that supports your diagnosis, specify what would count as progress—whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical forecast, a formal model, or a change of framing—and give an honest assessment of whether any of those could actually break the stalemate.
