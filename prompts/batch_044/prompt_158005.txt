I am drafting a dissertation chapter in philosophy of science that examines non-empirical confirmation in quantum gravity, and I keep encountering what looks like an internal tension in standard defenses of string theory. I understand that the landscape problem—stemming from an enormous space of metastable vacua, often discussed with figures on the order of 10^500—threatens empirical determinacy, since the fundamental equations appear compatible with almost any low-energy phenomenology across the multiverse of solutions. At the same time, I have seen defenders invoke a "no-alternatives" argument: the idea that string theory is currently the only mathematically consistent framework capable of unifying quantum mechanics and general relativity. Where I become confused is in seeing how these two points interact. If the landscape shows that the formalism does not uniquely fix observable constants or particle spectra, then does uniqueness at the level of theoretical structure still provide any non-empirical support? It strikes me that once a formalism permits every empirical outcome somewhere in its solution space, the absence of rival formalisms becomes irrelevant to predictive confidence. Am I correct that the multiverse problem therefore undermines the no-alternatives strategy, or am I conflating uniqueness of a theoretical framework with uniqueness of its phenomenological outputs?

Please feel free to push back if my framing rests on a confused premise, or if the comparison I am asking for cannot be drawn in the way I have posed it; if so, correct me plainly rather than accommodating the question politely. I want a direct answer in American English.
