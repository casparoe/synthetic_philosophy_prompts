I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. At a recent meetup on machine learning and epistemology, two other attendees—both with ML experience—got into a disagreement about whether no-free-lunch theorems have any meaningful bearing on Hume's problem of induction.

The first speaker argued as follows. No-free-lunch results establish that, averaged over all possible problem spaces, every learning algorithm—including those built on inductive generalization—performs exactly as well as random search. This means there is no universal rational justification for preferring induction over any other strategy; selecting an inductive bias is formally equivalent to adopting an arbitrary prior over worlds. Therefore, she said, these theorems provide a precise mathematical formalization of Hume's skepticism: induction succeeds in practice only because we silently restrict ourselves to a tiny, favorable subset of all possible worlds, but that restriction itself cannot be justified without circularity.

The second speaker pushed back in four stages. First, he said she was misapplying the theorems: they govern optimization over a fixed, discrete set of objective functions with a uniform distribution over problems, not inductive reasoning about continuous physical reality. Second, he argued that rather than undermining induction, NFL results actually demonstrate why inductive biases are unavoidable—without committing to some structural assumption about the world, no learning is possible at all, so the theorems explain the necessity of induction rather than its arbitrariness. Third, he claimed that her circularity charge misses the point: we do not justify induction deductively from first principles, but embed it in a coherent framework that includes evolutionary, physical, and computational constraints; NFL applies only when those constraints are ignored. Fourth, he added that even within the formal setting, NFL requires averaging over all problems, including physically impossible ones; once the problem distribution is weighted by actual-world regularities, inductive methods dominate, so the theorems support domain-specific induction rather than global skepticism.

The first speaker then replied with two counter-moves. She said his distinction between discrete optimization and physical induction is a red herring, because the core insight—that no strategy dominates without prior constraints—transfers directly: any justification of those prior constraints must itself rely on observed regularities. She also argued that his appeal to evolutionary and physical constraints smuggles in exactly the kind of inductive reasoning he claims to be grounding, confirming that NFL exposes an unavoidable circularity rather than resolving it.

They turned to me and asked for an adjudication. Can you go through the exchange move by move—her initial argument, his four stages, and her two replies—and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct? In particular, I want to know whether NFL theorems genuinely formalize Hume's problem, whether they instead justify the necessity of inductive bias, and whether the circularity charge actually sticks. Also, please identify what both speakers are missing: is there a coherent middle position that accepts NFL's formal result but denies it undermines practical induction, and if so, what exactly does that position require?
