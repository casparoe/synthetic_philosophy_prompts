I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in virtue ethics and moral psychology. I need a single essay question that can be completed in 90 minutes and should yield answers of approximately 800 words. The question must not be answerable by reciting standard textbook summaries of Aristotelian virtue ethics or the situationist critique; instead, it should force students to apply critical reasoning to a specific, non-standard proposal.

Please construct the question around the situationist challenge—drawing on the well-known cross-situational consistency findings discussed by philosophers such as John Doris and Gilbert Harman—combined with the following injected conceptual framework, which students should evaluate rather than merely describe. The framework is the "threshold model" of virtue: virtues are not global, cross-situational character traits but domain-indexed dispositions that manifest only when a context-sensitive threshold of moral demand is satisfied, and they are governed by a second-order meta-cognitive capacity through which the agent recognizes which domains are appropriate for the exercise of each disposition.

The exam question should ask students to assess whether this threshold model successfully defends virtue ethics against the situationist critique. Specifically, it should require them to address three issues: (1) whether the model preserves the normative idea that virtue is genuinely a feature of character rather than a cluster of situation-specific behavioral patterns; (2) whether the second-order meta-cognitive capacity functions as a global trait that is itself vulnerable to situationist debunking; and (3) whether the model can provide a non-circular account of moral education and character improvement.

Please provide: (a) the exam question itself, clearly worded and self-contained; (b) a model answer of roughly 750 words that demonstrates the depth of analysis expected; and (c) a marking scheme totaling 20 points, structured around five must-hit argumentative moves (with point allocations), plus a brief note on the characteristic failure mode for each move that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation prompts. Do not use verbatim quotations from published texts; paraphrase any references without quotation marks. The difficulty should lie in identifying the precise crux—whether localizing virtue saves it or dissolves it—rather than in recalling obscure theories.
