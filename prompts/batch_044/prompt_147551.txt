I've been working through the standard analogy that the brain is like a scientist testing hypotheses—updating beliefs in light of prediction errors, weighing evidence by precision, and engaging in active inference to reduce uncertainty. In the literature this is often used to license a specifically representationalist reading: if perception is hypothesis-testing, then perceptual states must carry propositional content, and the brain is essentially a Bayesian reasoner with internal models of the world. But I want to push on a variant I haven't seen audited properly: treating the brain not as a single scientist but as a distributed research consortium, where each hierarchical level is an autonomous lab running its own variational free-energy minimization, linked only through precision-weighted message passing, with no central "principal investigator" synthesizing a global model. Before I adopt this framing for my thesis, I need to know exactly how far it holds. What maps onto what—where do precision-weighting, Markov blankets, and active inference correspond to funding priorities, peer review, or inter-lab communication? Where does it break: does a consortium actually exhibit the metabolic openness and non-equilibrium dynamics required for active inference, and does the absence of a PI genuinely undermine global representational coherence, or does the analogy smuggle in a false dichotomy between distributed processing and representation? Most importantly, if the distributed-consortium version collapses—say, because hierarchical coupling requires global coherence constraints—does that damage the representationalist reading of predictive processing, or does it just show that the organizational metaphor was carrying an ideological load about decentralization that the neuroscience never supported?
