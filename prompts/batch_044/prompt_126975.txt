I am delivering the opening remarks at a workshop on institutional resilience convened in a city that recently rebuilt its water and transit systems after a sudden, cascading infrastructure failure. The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly half are local engineers, emergency managers, and officials who lived through the crisis and possess deep inside-view knowledge of the negotiations, resource constraints, and contingent judgments that shaped recovery; the other half are outside risk analysts, comparative-policy scholars, and economists who work with reference classes, base rates, and statistical forecasting models. Several family members of the local officials will also be present, so the language must remain accessible to non-specialists, yet the conceptual architecture must be rigorous enough that neither practitioners nor modelers feel patronized.

Rather than treating inside-view versus outside-view reasoning as a decorative theme, I want the speech to make epistemic modesty a structural constraint: it should open by naming the tension without resolving it prematurely, show how each perspective carries a characteristic blindness—insiders overfitting local narratives to specific causal chains, outsiders underweighting the contingent recovery path that was actually taken—and frame the workshop's purpose as holding these views in productive friction. To that end, please weave in the notion of what I will call the drift between intended agency and recorded outcome—the gap between what decision-makers knew they meant in the moment and what external measurement can later verify—using it to explain why honest recommendations must survive being described from both vantage points. The remarks should last about four minutes, avoid unnecessary technical jargon, and close by proposing one working norm for the workshop: that no proposal should be adopted unless it can be articulated honestly from both the inside and the outside view. Please write the speech.
