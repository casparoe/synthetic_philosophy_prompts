After a long argument with a friend about whether Douglas Hofstadter’s Letter Spirit program captures a real philosophical insight about letter categories—or merely engineers a psychologically unrealistic grid-based generator—I’m trying to fix where the conceptual work actually remains. Suppose you’re advising a small interdisciplinary group (philosophy, cognitive science, and conceptual AI) with about three years to investigate what makes every instance of an “a” an “a” across radically different styles, without treating style as mere ornament added to a fixed skeleton. Which open questions or lines of inquiry should they prioritize, and in what order? In particular, is the core problem best framed as defining the “letter spirit” as a generative grammar, as a prototype in a continuous style-space, or as a family-resemblance cluster with no underlying essence? How should they weigh the tractability of formalizing “negative-space” conditions—features whose absence is constitutive of being an “a” rather than a “d”—against the more ambitious project of modeling style as structural deformation of an invariant topological core? Defend your ranking: explain which questions are upstream of others, what kind of non-empirical reasoning (conceptual analysis, cross-linguistic comparison of alphabets, analogies to other categorical judgments) could be decisive, and which directions you would deprioritize because resolving them depends on settling these first. I want a defended agenda for limited resources, not a survey or a reading list.
