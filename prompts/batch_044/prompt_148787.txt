Tranquilism maintains that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, which implies that a state lacking both — even if it also lacks pleasure, achievement, or any positive affect — is maximally good. Give exactly five examples of conceptual issues that emerge when this view is applied to "anhedonic satisfaction," defined here as a stable condition in which a subject experiences neither craving nor suffering but also reports no pleasure, interest, or sense of accomplishment. Each issue should turn on whether some feature of that condition — for instance, its dependence on prior suffering, its compatibility with autonomous choice, its phenomenological flatness, or its relation to remembered goods — introduces a value that tranquilism must either deny or surreptitiously admit. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
