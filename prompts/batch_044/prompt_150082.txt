Question: Consider a hypothetical lineage of subterranean beetles in which strong directional selection favors enlarged sensory antennae tightly integrated with the head capsule. Because of developmental coupling, reducing the compound eyes is a structurally necessary byproduct of that integration; the eyes are not themselves targets of selection. Over evolutionary time these smaller eyes are co-opted to detect low-frequency substrate vibrations. Drawing on Gould and Lewontin's critique of adaptationism, explain (i) why the initial eye reduction is best understood as a spandrel rather than an adaptation, (ii) what term describes its subsequent co-option for vibration detection, and (iii) why describing the current eye morphology solely as "an adaptation for vibration sensing" risks the errors they call atomization or Panglossian reasoning.

Student answer: The initial reduction is a spandrel because selection acted only on antennae integration, and the smaller eyes were an unavoidable structural byproduct carrying no adaptive value of their own. Once co-opted for vibration detection, the trait becomes what Gould and Lewontin call an exaptation—introduced in their original spandrels argument to cover cases in which a spandrel acquires a new use. Because the trait's origin was non-adaptive, however, natural selection could not subsequently have refined its vibration-sensing properties; any precise tuning must reflect only the original structural constraints carried over from antennae development, not later selective shaping. Describing it solely as an adaptation for vibration is therefore Panglossian, and it also commits atomization by treating the eye as an independently optimized module rather than recognizing its persistent developmental coupling with the antennae.
