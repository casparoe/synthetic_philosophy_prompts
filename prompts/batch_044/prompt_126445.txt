I chair a hospital palliative-care ethics board that reviews non-standard comfort protocols—deep sedation in ambiguous consciousness states, analgesics that trade alertness for relief, or withdrawal of nutrition with concurrent pain management. Our charter adopts lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism modified by an incommensurability constraint: suffering above a defined intensity threshold has lexical priority over any pleasure or preserved consciousness, but below that threshold suffering and pleasure are not automatically comparable on a single scale; we must first establish whether they share a common evaluative dimension in the clinical context.

Write a reusable decision procedure my board can run on any proposal. It should proceed in ordered stages: (1) a suffering-intensity screening that determines whether the lexical threshold is triggered, with criteria for sufficient evidence and explicit misfire conditions (e.g., false positives from sedation-confounded reports, category errors in comparing consciousness-quality with nociceptive intensity); (2) a commensurability assessment that tests whether the proposed benefits and residual suffering can be mapped onto a shared clinical-evaluative dimension, specifying what counts as success or failure of that mapping; (3) decision rules for the threshold-triggered case versus the below-threshold case; and (4) explicit stop-points where the procedure returns a recommendation versus flags an irreducible value conflict that requires human deliberation rather than calculation. Identify the judgment calls that resist proceduralization and the conditions under which the procedure should be suspended.

Apply the procedure to three proposals: (A) deep continuous sedation for a minimally conscious patient with intermittent severe pain, which eliminates all conscious experience; (B) an analgesic regimen for a fully alert terminal patient that eliminates physical suffering but induces a persistent, mildly pleasant yet cognitively vacant state; and (C) withholding nutrition to reduce gastrointestinal suffering while maintaining high-dose analgesia that produces euphoria but accelerates death. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer the board will likely reject on non-utilitarian grounds, and state clearly what the procedure cannot resolve.
