I am drafting a research proposal for a small interdisciplinary fellowship that asks applicants to take a well-known philosophical position and press it into service on a problem it was not originally designed to address. I want to work with Frank Ramsey’s scattered, almost offhand remarks on the meaning of life — the ones that treat the question as potentially malformed rather than merely unanswerable — but I do not want to write yet another commentary on logical positivism. Instead, I want to ask whether Ramsey’s underlying pragmatist account of belief, particularly his distinction between beliefs that function as guides to action and beliefs that are cognitively redundant, can be turned into a formal criterion for identifying category mistakes in contemporary value discourse.

Specifically, my question is this: if we take Ramsey’s suggestion that asking ‘What is the meaning of life?’ may confuse the grammar of empirical inquiry with the grammar of evaluation, can we reconstruct that confusion in precise terms using his own framework of redundant propositions? And can that reconstruction then be applied — without stretching the historical material beyond recognition — to a current debate in which evaluation is treated as if it were measurement, such as the framing of collective human purpose within certain quantitative ethical frameworks? I propose to approach this conceptually rather than archivally: I will reconstruct Ramsey’s position from his well-known writings on truth, belief, and probability, explicitly marking where I am rationalising rather than reporting, and then I will test the reconstructed criterion against a specific case study — namely, whether attributing a measurable ‘purpose score’ to long-range collective outcomes commits the same grammatical error Ramsey suspected. A positive result would deliver a clear demarcation rule together with a worked example; a negative result — that Ramsey’s pragmatism collapses into a generic non-cognitivism — would itself be valuable, since it would show that his critique offers no distinctive advantage over standard emotivist dismissals. Given a twelve-week window and no expectation of primary-source discovery, I think this is realistic, though I recognise that the interpretive risk is the central weakness: I will address it by anchoring every step of the reconstruction to arguments Ramsey actually made about belief and action, and by stating upfront which parts are speculative extrapolation. The proposal should state the precise question, explain why existing answers leave it open, set out the conceptual approach and why it is preferable to a purely historical one, describe what a result and a failure would look like, and include an honest assessment of the vulnerability created by the absence of a canonical source text.
