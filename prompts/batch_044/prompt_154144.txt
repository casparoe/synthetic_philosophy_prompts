I serve on my university's Predictive Analytics Oversight Committee, and we must issue a formal decision memo on a vendor's request to deploy an early-warning algorithm that flags "at-risk" students for intervention. The vendor's feature documentation includes several predictors our technical reviewer describes as "grue-indexed": they combine an observable student behavior—such as missing a required advising appointment—with a hidden institutional-calendar cutoff, so the feature is active only if the absence occurs before the add/drop deadline, yet it is coded identically to post-deadline absence in the training data. The vendor treats these as standard interaction terms; our reviewer argues they are non-projectible predicates in Goodman's sense, meaning they may stabilize in historical training data but fail to generalize once the academic calendar shifts, producing inductive failure rather than ordinary statistical drift.

The committee is split. One faction regards this as a correctable machine-learning engineering problem—retrain on updated calendars, apply domain adaptation, and monitor for drift. The other faction regards it as a fundamental failure of inductive justification: if the model's predictive power depends on predicates whose extension is tied to an arbitrary temporal boundary, then the model lacks the kind of projectibility that would justify high-stakes institutional action, regardless of its current accuracy metrics.

The chair has asked me to draft the committee's decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions. The memo should be an actionable institutional document with a clear bottom line. In particular, it should address three issues: first, whether Goodman's problem of induction genuinely applies to engineered feature spaces, or whether it is confined to natural-language predicates and therefore irrelevant here; second, whether the institution has a duty to reject—or conditionally restrict—models whose predictive validity rests on projectibility assumptions that cannot be internally justified within the model's own representational vocabulary; and third, the strongest consideration in favor of the view opposite to your recommendation, treated honestly rather than dismissed. The reasoning should be accessible to committee members without specialized philosophy training but precise enough that Student Affairs and the vendor can respond to it concretely. Write the memo in polished American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
