A colleague recently summarised the development of historical source criticism for me as follows: Herodotus effectively founded the discipline by inventing systematic cross-referencing, comparing written records across different cultures in order to construct a single authoritative narrative; Thucydides then corrected this approach by rejecting all indirect testimony and mythological explanation outright, accepting only direct eyewitness observation as historically valid and therefore treating every non-observed report as inherently unreliable; and modern digital historiography is essentially the same Thucydidean filter applied automatically through algorithmic verification of database metadata, which means the core logic of source criticism has remained unchanged for roughly two and a half millennia, with only the speed and scale of verification having increased. From this, he concluded that there is an unbroken methodological line running from Herodotus to contemporary big-data archives, and that present-day concerns about archival silence — the evidentiary problem posed by records that were never created — are merely technical extensions of Thucydides' original exclusion of reports he had not personally observed.

This account strikes me as elegant but perhaps too neat. Would you audit it for accuracy? Please work through the summary in parts, distinguishing what is broadly correct, what is a standard simplification, and what genuinely misrepresents either Herodotus's or Thucydides's practice. I am particularly unsure whether Thucydides dismissed all second-hand reports categorically, and whether reducing modern digital source criticism to an automated Thucydidean filter ignores important differences in how historians now handle provenance, bias, and absence. Finally, please tell me whether the conclusion about an unbroken line survives once these corrections are made, and whether treating archival silence as a mere technical extension of Thucydides' exclusions is defensible.
