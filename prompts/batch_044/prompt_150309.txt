I cover the philosophy of science and formal epistemology for a publication that treats conceptual puzzles as live disagreements rather than settled folklore, and I am trying to locate the precise point where reasoning on a classic problem becomes unstable rather than merely contested. Give me your credence—expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1—that Sleeping Beauty, upon being awakened in the canonical Monday/Tuesday protocol with a fair coin and induced amnesia, should assign credence exactly one-third to the coin having come up heads.

Do not answer with “it depends on your framework” or “both answers are defensible.” I want the number, and I want it decomposed into at least four independently assessable sub-questions, each carrying its own rough probability and a brief explanation of how it feeds into the aggregate.

First, assess the probability that centered-worlds semantics correctly captures the content of Sleeping Beauty’s belief upon awakening, as opposed to a purely uncentered propositional analysis in which her location in time is not part of the belief content. Second, assess whether the principle of indifference applies uniformly across all centered worlds that are epistemically possible for her, or whether some non-uniform weighting—perhaps indexed to temporal duration, causal origin of the awakening, or the number of physically distinct wake-events—should replace uniform distribution. Third, evaluate the strength of the “double-halfer” objection: does the argument that Tuesday awakenings should not be double-counted rest on a legitimate distinction between de re and de dicto self-location, or does it conflate two levels of analysis that should be kept separate? Fourth, determine whether standard Bayesian conditioning is normatively adequate for indexical belief revision in this case, or whether it must be supplemented or replaced by a framework specifically designed for self-locating uncertainty, such as a generalized Principal Principle applied to centered propositions.

Show how these four probabilities combine—whether multiplicatively, as a weighted average, or through some other explicit structure—to yield your final number. Then identify which single sub-question carries the greatest leverage: if you received decisive evidence that your answer to that component was wrong, by approximately how many points would your overall credence shift, and in which direction? Finally, state what kind of argument or formal result—perhaps a coherence proof for one weighting scheme, or a demonstration that centered-world semantics collapses into standard conditioning—would most likely cause you to revise that most-sensitive component.
