A historian of ideas in my reading group has been classifying historical narratives as Whiggish—treating the past as an inevitable progress toward present conditions—according to verdicts on specific cases. She has ruled that a narrative framing Euclid’s parallel-postulate crisis as a necessary step toward modern non-Euclidean physics is Whiggish; that one describing medieval Islamic astronomy as a coherent research program that eventually lost institutional support for reasons internal to its societies, rather than being conceptually superseded, is not Whiggish; that one portraying the 19th-century expansion of suffrage as humanity’s gradual awakening to an inherent equality is Whiggish; that one presenting the printing press as a technological catalyst that made the Reformation and Enlightenment possible, while insisting that neither outcome was predetermined, is not Whiggish; and that one assessing 18th-century physicians by asking whether they respected patient autonomy in the modern sense is Whiggish. She must now evaluate a proposed history of computing that describes Babbage’s Analytical Engine and Lovelace’s algorithms not as failed precursors to modern machines but as genuine alternative architectures abandoned because of funding politics and gendered exclusion rather than conceptual inadequacy, yet concludes that this abandonment merely delayed by several decades the inevitable emergence of digital computation exactly as we know it today. Predict her verdict, but more importantly lay out the competing rules that fit her earlier judgments equally well—distinguishing retrospective instrumentalization (valuing past events solely as means to present ends), normative anachronism (applying present moral or conceptual categories to past actors), and teleological compression (treating contingent outcomes as inevitable)—say which rule predicts Whiggish versus not Whiggish for this new case, state your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that favors one rule over the others, and propose one further hypothetical narrative that would best force a choice between the two leading rules.
