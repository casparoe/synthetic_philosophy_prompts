Consider two competing conceptual frameworks for resolving paradoxes in self-amending legal structures—systems in which a constitutional rule governs not only ordinary legislation but also the procedures for altering that rule itself.

The first framework, call it the reflexive tier model, permits a meta-rule to regulate its own revision on the condition that the revision is temporally indexed: any change to the amendment procedure takes effect only for amendments proposed after the change has been ratified, thereby preventing immediate self-application. The second framework, call it the stratified validity model, maintains that no rule can coherently authorize its own repeal or transformation; instead, authority over constitutional change resides in a separate normative tier—what might be described as an unconstituted constituent power—so that self-reference is blocked by an ontological separation between the legal order and the source of its fundamental revision.

Compare these two frameworks with specific attention to the following scenario: a constitution contains a single clause that permits its own total repeal by simple legislative majority, and this clause is then invoked to repeal itself prior to the execution of that repeal. In your comparison, address:

1. Whether the reflexive tier model's temporal indexing genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely defers it across successive generations of amendment;
2. Whether the stratified validity model's appeal to an extra-legal constituent power avoids arbitrariness or simply relocates the problem of self-reference to the boundary between law and politics;
3. Which framework yields a more coherent account of legal continuity and institutional identity through deep constitutional transformation.

This is not a request for a historical survey of named jurisprudential doctrines. I want an analysis of the internal conceptual mechanics of these two constructions.
