I have been trying to understand what makes workfare feel different from older kinds of charity. It is not only that one must labor to receive help, but that one must keep performing deservingness, as if need itself were a debt that must be serviced yet can never be fully repaid. I would like a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains, the usual rhyme and repetition—written from the point of view of someone caught in that loop. Let one refrain be the proof demanded ("the paper that says I am worthy"), and let the other be the labor extracted to cancel the debt ("the hours that pay for my hunger"). Use the form's turning: as the refrains recur and finally stand together in the last two lines, they should show that satisfying every condition does not close the account but only restarts it—the proof breeds new suspicion, the labor breeds new deficit. I want the poem to treat deservingness as a kind of moral bookkeeping in which suffering is entered as credit and work as repayment, yet the books never balance. It should avoid preaching about fraud or about bureaucratic cruelty; instead, it should make the machinery visible to anyone who has lived inside it and strange to anyone who has not. Keep the imagery concrete—time clocks, bus tokens, case-file stamps—so the idea is carried by objects rather than slogans.
