I'm writing a dissertation chapter on diachronic rationality and have been trying to isolate exactly where the normative force of money-pump arguments breaks down, if it does. Here is a framing I have constructed that I think might be underexplored: suppose an agent's preferences over options A, B, and C are cyclical at time t₁, but these preferences are not stable; they are generated by a belief-dependent utility function in which her credence about actually receiving the payoff shifts as a function of which trades she has hypothetically accepted. The standard diachronic Dutch book assumes that accepting the first exchange (say, A for B plus a small payment) leaves her preferences and beliefs intact for the subsequent exchange (B for C plus a small payment). But imagine the agent knows her own belief-revision rule and correctly anticipates that completing the first trade updates her credences in a way that makes the second trade genuinely undesirable ex post, even though it looks attractive ex ante. Given this "anticipated belief-revision" constraint, what is your credence that the agent is not genuinely vulnerable to exploitation by a money pump, despite the cyclical surface structure of her preferences? Please decompose your answer: assign a rough probability to (i) the claim that canonical diachronic Dutch book arguments tacitly depend on a static-belief assumption that is rarely made explicit; (ii) the claim that belief-dependent utility is a normatively legitimate framework for intertemporal choice rather than an ad hoc rescue; (iii) the claim that anticipating one's own belief revision can actually block the sequential exploitation without collapsing back into static transitivity; and then combine them into an overall figure. Finally, tell me which of these three terms is the weakest link in your decomposition—i.e., which one would shift your overall credence most if you learned new empirical or conceptual evidence about it—and briefly describe what that evidence might look like.
