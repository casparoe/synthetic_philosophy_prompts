I am drafting an essay for a column on science and ideas, and I want editorial feedback before I develop it further. The piece examines Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the emergence of an abstract, unaccountable collective—the public—but I want to avoid the usual comparison to modern social-media outrage cycles. Instead, I introduce a distinction between absorptive publicity, in which individuals dissolve into an anonymous statistical aggregate without any awareness of their own dissolution, and reflexive publicity, in which an audience constantly monitors itself as an audience, turning engagement into a second-order spectacle performed for metrics and visibility. I argue that Kierkegaard's diagnosis captures the absorptive layer of contemporary media—passive consumption shaped by algorithmic recommendation—while missing the reflexive layer, where users actively cultivate and stage their own abstraction.

My draft notes run as follows. First, Kierkegaard treats the press as manufacturing a phantom entity by stripping away personal risk and flattening qualitative distinctions into quantitative noise; the reader becomes a subscriber without a face, and therefore without ethical accountability. Second, absorptive publicity describes precisely this structural disappearance of the singular: the danger is not conformity but dissolution. Third, reflexive publicity describes environments where leveling is not hidden but performed—trending indicators, visible engagement counts, and algorithmic transparency turn anonymity into a kind of staged self-consciousness. Fourth, the essay claims that algorithmic feeds do not simply reproduce Kierkegaard's leveling; they bifurcate it, preserving the absorptive danger while adding a reflexive dimension he did not anticipate.

Please comment directly on these notes. Does the distinction between absorptive and reflexive publicity hold up philosophically, or does it misrepresent how self-conscious crowds actually behave? Am I attributing to Kierkegaard a specific theory of anonymity that he in fact reserved for the crowd in his analysis of the present age? Is the claim that modern feeds transform rather than reproduce leveling defensible without leaning on empirical platform studies? Where does the argument risk sounding like a generic technology critique, and how should I sharpen it? Point out confusions, suggest rephrasings, flag anachronisms, and indicate which parts of the distinction are genuinely illuminating versus merely decorative.
