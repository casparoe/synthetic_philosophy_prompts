In debates over advance directives for progressive dementia, a common move is to invoke the Ulysses contract: just as Ulysses bound his future self to the mast to resist the sirens, the competent patient binds her future demented self to refuse life-extending interventions, and honoring that document is said to respect the autonomy of the person who authored it rather than the preferences of the present occupant. I want to audit a sharper version of this analogy, one that treats the earlier self not merely as Ulysses but as a constituent power that establishes the terms of membership in a continuing body—the person—while the later self is cast as a successive administration that inherits obligations but lacks authority to amend or repudiate them. Lay out the explicit mapping: what in the target case corresponds to constituent authority, to constitutional amendment, to secession, and to the territorial or temporal boundaries of the political body? Identify where the analogy fractures because personal identity over time is not state succession—particularly with respect to value change, psychological discontinuity, and the absence of a shared public reason among temporal stages. Ask also what theory of personal identity—perhaps a four-dimensionalist or strongly relational view—the analogy quietly imports to make the binding authority seem legitimate, and whether that import is doing the normative work. Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that the directive should be treated as binding even when the present patient expresses contentment with continued life—survives once these disanalogies and hidden premises are on the table, or whether a different analogy (say, promise-keeping across value change, or trustee discretion) better captures what is actually at stake.
