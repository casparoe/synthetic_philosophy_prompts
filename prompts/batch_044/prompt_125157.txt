Consider this paraphrase of an argument from Sextus Empiricus’s Outlines of Pyrrhonism (c. 200 CE): the dogmatist claims to have grasped the reality of non-evident matters, while the skeptic, finding arguments of equal strength on both sides, suspends judgment. The familiar dogmatism paradox holds that if the skeptic asserts that knowledge is impossible, she asserts something with the same certainty she denies others. I want to press a stricter version. Suppose the skeptic never makes that positive assertion, but instead adopts suspension of judgment as a standing global policy—call this procedural anti-dogmatism. Is this still paradoxical? In particular, distinguish first-order suspension (withholding assent on a particular claim because of equipollence) from second-order suspension (adopting the rule that no claim deserves assent). Does Sextus’s account of the skeptic’s ability (dunamis) to oppose every argument commit him to the second-order version, or does his practice remain at the first order? Help me understand whether the paradox collapses once this distinction is drawn, and if it does collapse, identify which feature of the skeptic’s position was actually generating the tension.
