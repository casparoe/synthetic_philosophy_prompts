I am drafting a proposal for a four-month independent study grant, and the subject has become personally urgent: I was recently asked to compose a commemorative biography for a local community archive, and I realized I have no principled view on whether moral biography should present an integrated character, highlight contradictions, or pursue something else entirely. I want to investigate whether Plutarch’s Parallel Lives operates through a distinct pedagogical mechanism—one I will call evaluative suspension—where the pairing of Greek and Roman lives deliberately withholds narrative coherence, forcing the reader to hold incompatible moral judgments in tension rather than resolving them into imitation or rejection. The precise question is: does this structural incongruity constitute a third mode of moral learning from lives, distinct from both hagiographic exemplarity and critical debunking, and what would it imply for contemporary biographical ethics if it does?

Existing scholarship tends to read Plutarch either as a moral exemplarist (the lives teach virtue through admiration) or as a comparative historian (the pairs invite ranking). Neither account explains why Plutarch regularly pairs figures with incompatible virtues, or why he includes detailed failures within ostensibly laudatory narratives. The gap is conceptual, not archival: we lack a framework for biography that treats structural dissonance as pedagogically productive rather than as a flaw in narrative unity.

My approach will be a close conceptual analysis of the pairing logic in three representative pairs—focusing not on historical accuracy but on how narrative sequencing withholds final judgment—coupled with a comparison to modern integrated-character biography, the psychological coherence model dominant since the twentieth century. Month one: map the narrative architecture of the pairs, identifying moments where moral evaluation is explicitly deferred. Month two: contrast this with modern biographical norms that demand psychological consistency. Month three: develop the concept of evaluative suspension and test whether it survives objections, such as the charge that it is merely rhetorical ornament. Month four: draft the implications for commemorative and moral biography, including an explicit failure condition—if the pairing structure turns out to offer only conventional comparative judgment with no distinctive pedagogical effect, the project collapses into a historical curiosity.

This is deliberately not an empirical or philological project; it requires no unpublished manuscripts and no citation apparatus beyond standard references I will supply myself. The deliverable is a conceptual framework that practitioners—archivists, memorial committees, biographers—could use to decide whether coherence is always a virtue. A realistic risk is that the framework overgeneralizes from three pairs; I will mitigate this by restricting claims to the logic of pairing itself, not to Plutarch’s entire corpus.
