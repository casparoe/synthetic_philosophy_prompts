For roughly the past half-century, interpreters of Spinoza's political writings have been locked in a dispute about the status of democracy in his system that shows little sign of resolution. On one side, readers of the Theological-Political Treatise take Spinoza at his claim that democracy is the form of government closest to nature and therefore the most absolute expression of collective sovereignty, in which the power of the multitude operates without institutional mediation. On the other side, readers of the unfinished Political Treatise emphasize that Spinoza restricts democratic citizenship to those who are legally independent—explicitly excluding women, servants, dependents, and foreigners—while devoting far more detailed institutional analysis to monarchy and aristocracy. From this angle, democracy is not an unmediated popular power but a constitutional form that presupposes a prior division between active citizens and those excluded from the sovereign body, and its abrupt, unfinished treatment suggests that Spinoza never completed a democratic theory comparable to his accounts of the other regimes. The disagreement is not merely about textual weight; both camps acknowledge the same corpus, including the truncation of the democratic chapter. Yet no interpretation has prevailed. I want a diagnosis rather than another survey of positions. Is this disagreement verbal—are the two sides using democracy for different phenomena, one naming an ontological condition of collective power and the other naming a juridical status distributed by law—or is it substantive, turning on a hidden disagreement about whether Spinoza's concept of the multitude can carry normative force without collapsing into an aristocratic selection of the rational? Or does the stall indicate that the question is ill-posed, because the Theological-Political Treatise and the Political Treatise pursue incompatible projects—one theological-political, one institutional—that have been illegitimately fused under a single heading? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says about citizenship, sovereignty, and the role of mediation. Offer an honest assessment of whether anything—a formal analysis of Spinoza's concept of law, a distinction between ontological and juridical equality, or a recognition that immanence is compatible with structural exclusions—could actually move it, or whether the impasse is permanent because it tracks bedrock intuitions about whether political philosophy should begin with the fact of collective power or with the conditions that make that power durable. I am particularly interested in whether the debate conceals a deeper disagreement about whether Spinoza's metaphysics of immanence requires democratic sovereignty to be coextensive with the entire affected population, or whether immanence permits—perhaps even requires—exclusions that are not mere historical contingencies but structural conditions of rational agency itself.
