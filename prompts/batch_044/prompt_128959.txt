Analyze the 2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum and its immediate constitutional aftermath through Spinoza’s account of democracy in his late political writings. Do not treat democracy as a voting procedure; instead, work with the claim that democracy is the “absolute form of government” not because it employs ballots, but because it maximizes the collective rational power (potentia) of the multitude by aligning individual conatus with common law, and that it is corrupted—tending toward aristocracy or monarchy—when parts of the multitude are de facto excluded from deliberation despite formal equality.

In your analysis, distinguish citizens (cives) who can enter rational agreement from subjects or dependents (subditi) in the context of the referendum franchise: long-term resident EU nationals were excluded from voting, citizens in different regions participated at different rates, and the binary framing of Leave versus Remain was shaped by party structures rather than by deliberative assembly. Ask whether the referendum expressed a shared rational determination of the common good, as Spinoza requires of true democracy, or whether it illustrates the domination of the multitude by passions—fear of lost sovereignty, hope of restored autonomy, and private economic interest—that he associates with unstable democratic states.

Next, examine the tension between the referendum result and the subsequent parliamentary resistance through Spinoza’s identification of democratic sovereignty with the collective power of the citizens rather than with a delegated authority (potestas). Where Spinoza argues that democracy naturally decays into oligarchy when a subset monopolizes deliberation, ask whether Parliament’s reluctance to enact the result represented a rational preservation of democratic sovereignty or its corruption into aristocratic rule by representatives.

Finally, assess what the episode reveals about Spinoza’s theory. Does the Brexit case confirm his suspicion that direct democratic mechanisms in large, heterogeneous states are especially vulnerable to passion-driven fragmentation? Or does it expose a gap—specifically, his assumption that democratic citizens share a sufficient common nature to reach rational agreement without institutional mediation, and his silence on supranational treaty obligations and modern party systems that structure choice before deliberation begins? Be explicit about where the mapping holds, where it breaks, and whether any feature of the modern state lies outside Spinoza’s conceptual space entirely.
