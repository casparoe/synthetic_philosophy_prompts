Design a reusable, step-by-step audit procedure for a psychiatric-classification working group that must evaluate whether a proposed diagnostic revision risks triggering harmful looping effects in the sense developed by Ian Hacking—the idea that classifying people can change the very behaviors and self-understandings being classified, which then feed back into prevalence data and clinical perception. The working group operates under a six-week deadline, has no staff philosopher, and requires joint sign-off from clinicians and epidemiologists; the procedure must therefore be runnable by non-philosophers but must preserve the conceptual distinctions that actually matter.

Structure the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stop rules: for each step, state the question, what counts as a sufficient answer (including when the answer must be "we do not have enough evidence"), where the procedure terminates with a recommendation to proceed, where it terminates with a recommendation to halt, and where it explicitly hands the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Inject the following niche distinction into the framework: separate "taxonomic looping" (the label itself reshapes symptom expression and self-report) from "institutional looping" (the diagnosis changes access to resources, legal status, or social identity, which then alters observed prevalence independently of phenomenology). The procedure must contain specific checks for each kind and a rule for what to do when they point in opposite directions.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals: first, broadening the age-range criteria for an existing neurodevelopmental disorder so that it is routinely diagnosed in adults; second, introducing a new subthreshold anxiety category intended for primary-care screening. For each, run every step and record where the procedure yields a clear answer and where it stalls. Include one case—chosen from the two—where the procedure produces an answer the working group is likely to resist (for example, recommending against a revision that addresses a documented clinical gap because loop contamination is unquantifiable and institutional effects are irreversible). Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: identify loops it is not designed to detect, substitute for ethical judgment about acceptable risk, or resolve conflicts between clinician and epidemiologist sign-off. Keep the tone formal and the language operational, avoiding requests for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to highly obscure secondary literature; paraphrase Hacking's core claims without using quotation marks.
