I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on non-standard axiology, and I have become particularly interested in tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive states, achievements, or pleasures are at best instrumentally valuable. Most treatments I have encountered reduce this view to a variant of negative utilitarianism or a broadly Buddhist-inspired ethics, but I think there are deeper conceptual fault lines that standard discussions rarely map in detail. I want to understand where a focused conceptual research group should direct its energy if it had roughly three years to resolve philosophical issues internal to tranquilism itself, using only conceptual analysis rather than empirical methods.

Specifically, I am trying to prioritize among four open problems that seem central but are rarely compared directly. First, there is the problem of meta-craving: can one consistently orient oneself toward the complete cessation of desire without that very orientation constituting a form of craving that undermines the tranquil state? Second, there is what I will call the aesthetic neutrality problem—whether engagement with beauty, mathematical elegance, or contemplative appreciation necessarily involves a subtle form of attachment or anticipatory pleasure that tranquilism must classify as craving, thereby rendering such engagement impossible or impermissible under the view. Third, there is the intergenerational quietist problem: since tranquilism assigns intrinsic value only to the actual absence of suffering and actual absence of craving, does it have any coherent normative stance on whether to bring future people into existence, or does the mere non-existence of their potential suffering render procreation and extinction ethically equivalent? Fourth, there is the phenomenological boundary question—how precisely to demarcate suffering proper from affectively neutral but phenomenologically adjacent states, such as profound boredom, existential flatness, or the dark night experiences described in contemplative traditions, without smuggling in positive-value judgments about mental richness or complexity.

If you were advising this group, how would you rank these four lines of inquiry in terms of which should be tackled first, second, and so on? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria—such as conceptual tractability for philosophers working without empirical tools, how many other questions depend on resolving this one first, and whether settling it would actually change what tranquilism recommends in practice. I am not looking for a literature survey, a bibliography, or citations; I want a defended agenda that explains the trade-offs, notes which questions you would deprioritize and why, and indicates what kind of conceptual progress over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking.
