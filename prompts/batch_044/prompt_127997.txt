I was arguing with a friend about Keynes's Treatise on Probability and somehow we drifted into the General Theory, and now I have this argument that feels like it eats itself. I tried to formalize his view with what I'm calling "belief liquidity preference" — the idea that just like we hoard cash to keep options open when the future is opaque, we should keep beliefs in a liquid, non-quantified state rather than locking them into a specific credence. So here's the chain:

(1) If belief liquidity preference is rational, then assigning any specific numerical credence under high uncertainty is like locking capital into an illiquid asset — it destroys optionality.
(2) For Keynes, true uncertainty (as opposed to measurable risk) is exactly the situation where weight of argument is neglible relative to ignorance.
(3) Therefore, in every domain Keynes calls genuinely uncertain — long-term investment, political forecasting, technological change — rational agents should refuse all precise probabilistic belief.
(4) But Keynes himself clearly relies on probabilistic judgments about investment returns and expectation in the General Theory, and he allows degrees of belief in the Treatise that guide action even when weight is low.
(5) So either Keynes's practice is systematically irrational by his own lights, or belief liquidity preference is not actually a rational requirement.
(6) If we drop (5) and say Keynes can be rational despite assigning probabilities, then the distinction between risk and uncertainty collapses, since we can always assign a number, just with low weight.
(7) So we're stuck: either Keynes is inconsistent, or his central distinction is useless.

I really want to keep (1) and (2) if I can, but I'm open. Which step is the weak link, and what do we pay to reject it? Is the analogy just broken, or is there a hidden slide between belief-states and decision-rules that saves him?
