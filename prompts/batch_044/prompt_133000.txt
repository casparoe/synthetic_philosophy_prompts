'Al-Ghazālī targets the Muʿtazilite claim that fire contains an intrinsic nature that necessarily produces burning. He argues that observation only reveals constant conjunction: whenever fire touches cotton, burning follows. There's no logical contradiction in God arranging fire near cotton and withholding the burn, so philosophers mistake correlation for a necessary link. The real cause in every case is God alone; fire is merely the occasion (sabab). To save the appearance of law, Al-Ghazālī introduces ʿāda — God habitually creates burning after fire, but this custom is not binding. It's essentially the same move Hume makes with constant conjunction, except Al-Ghazālī adds a direct divine cause behind each occurence. Because the pattern is stable, scientists can study regularities just fine; they don't need intrinsic powers. In fact, occasionalism strengthens science by explaining why miracles are possible: when God breaks the habit, we see the pattern was never necessary to begin with. Al-Ghazālī even concedes that fire retains a weak dispositional tendency to burn, but only because God implants that disposition freshly each instant.'
