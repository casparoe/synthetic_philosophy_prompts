I am a philosophy professor preparing materials for an advanced undergraduate seminar on speculative ethics and cosmic sociology, and I want to design a writing exercise that forces the respondent to inhabit the logic of the dark forest hypothesis rather than summarize it from the outside. Before writing anything, please think carefully and step by step: first, identify the core premises—technological asymmetry, the impossibility of verifying intentions across interstellar distances, the rationality of preemptive annihilation, and the self-fulfilling dynamics of universal suspicion; second, consider how these premises would restructure ordinary social practices if adopted literally as diplomatic and legal protocol.

You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in game-theoretic reasoning about extraterrestrial contact and speculative ethics. Write a deadpan parody travel-guide entry, modeled precisely on the cheerful, practical, slightly condescending voice of a Lonely Planet guidebook page, describing a visit to a planet that has adopted Dark Forest principles as its state religion and binding legal framework. The entry should cover local customs (the protocol for greeting strangers without broadcasting stellar coordinates), what travelers should pack (signal-jamming gear, decoy beacons, identity-obscuring attire), dining etiquette when an unverified probe enters orbit (the Three-Body Greeting), a sidebar on the national holiday commemorating the First Silent Year, and travel warnings for tourists from worlds that have not yet achieved technological singularity. The satire should exaggerate real features of the hypothesis—especially the slide from rational precaution to mandatory genocide—without ever stating the objections explicitly. Aim for around 700 words. The irony must remain deadpan and legible: the voice should stay that of an enthusiastic, helpful guide, yet a specialist reader should recognize that the piece understands the view well enough to make the satire fair. Do not cite specific texts, authors, or bibliographies; do not ask the reader to verify claims; and do not include quotation marks around any real-world passages. Invent any necessary in-universe institutions, ceremonies, or proponents rather than attributing positions to named living figures.
