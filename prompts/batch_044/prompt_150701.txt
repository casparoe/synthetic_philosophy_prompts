You are delivering the commencement address at a university that has just reinstated a required general-education core after a decade of pure disciplinary specialization. The audience is tripartite: graduating seniors who completed the core in its first iteration; faculty divided between those who designed the curriculum and those who regard it as administrative bloat; and parents who largely assume that "general education" means job training or credentialing. Use Whitehead's philosophy of education—specifically his view that learning moves through successive phases of romance, precision, and generalization, and that the university's essential duty is to preserve the rhythm among them rather than to accelerate or collapse it—so that it does real structural work in the speech. First, explain why the core was reinstated without reducing it to vocational preparation on one hand or to mere cultural ornament on the other. Second, shape the form of the address itself by identifying which kinds of prescription or practical advice are inappropriate for a commencement occasion, given that the graduates are in a transitional moment rather than in the precision phase of their training; explicitly mark what you are therefore refusing to say. Third, address the faculty division not by taking sides, but by showing that deep specialization and broad study belong to different moments of the same educational rhythm, so that treating them as opponents misunderstands the process. The speech should run roughly eight minutes, be intelligible to non-specialists without flattening the philosophy into decoration, and make clear where judgment must replace procedure—namely, in deciding exactly which prescriptions belong to which phase and in acknowledging that the rhythm itself offers no algorithm for resolving curricular disputes.
