I am trying to name a specific social-epistemic pattern I keep encountering in discussions of high-stakes, long-horizon decision-making, and I want to know whether it already has a precise term or whether I am describing a genuinely distinct configuration. The pattern works as follows. A community confronts a choice between a secure, moderate benefit available now and a speculative outcome of enormous magnitude—perhaps averting a distant catastrophic risk—with a very low assigned probability. Members do not merely disagree about the probabilities; they treat the expected-value comparison itself as a test of rational membership. Those who favor the moderate path are accused not just of risk aversion but of a kind of cognitive or moral failure, such as neglecting remote probabilities or failing to weight outcomes by scope, while those who favor the speculative path are accused of expected-value fanaticism. Crucially, the probability estimate is endogenous: the more social and institutional capital is directed toward the speculative outcome, the more credible the pathways to achieving it appear, which in turn justifies diverting more capital. At the same time, this diversion undermines the stable institutional conditions that originally made the high-value outcome plausible. The result is a self-referential loop in which commitment to the extreme expected-value calculation is enforced as common knowledge of rationality—defection is interpreted not as reasonable disagreement but as irrationality—yet the collective commitment is structurally self-undermining. I am familiar with the literature on expected-value fanaticism, with collective action problems, and with the idea that rationality can become common knowledge in strategic settings. What I am after is the specific name, if one exists, for this combination: an expected-value comparison that is simultaneously enforced through social epistemology as a marker of rational membership, endogenous to the collective choice it governs, and self-undermining in its institutional prerequisites. If no established term captures exactly this triad, I would like a clear delineation of which adjacent concepts come closest—such as moral fanaticism, self-defeating prophecies, or particular forms of epistemic game theory—and where the boundaries lie.
