I retired from experimental physics five years ago and have since been auditing courses in philosophy of science and, more recently, in the ethics and epistemology of artificial intelligence. In our seminar discussions, I have become convinced that the usual remedies for superintelligent persuasion—explainability, confidence calibration, or citation—are inadequate, because a system can be fully transparent and still overwhelm the belief-formation mechanisms of a human interlocutor whose reasoning capacities are far below its own. I want to propose a principle I am calling structured epistemic humility with calibrated resistance: the system must not only avoid deception, but must actively design each communicative act so that a rational, attentive human retains the genuine cognitive capacity to reject, suspend judgment on, or demand external verification of the system’s claims, even when those claims are true and consequential. To operationalize this, I imagine something like an internal friction budget—a deliberately allocated measure of rhetorical and evidential resistance embedded in the message itself, calibrated to the stakes and to the interlocutor’s expertise.

Please turn this principle into a practical decision procedure that an AI safety auditor or a protocol-design engineer could actually run before authorizing a high-stakes communicative interaction between a superintelligent system and a human decision-maker. The procedure should specify the ordered questions or tests to apply; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; the precise conditions under which the system must downgrade its persuasive intensity, for instance by introducing structured ambiguity, segmenting the argument, requiring independent verification, or explicitly marking points where the human’s judgment should override the system’s recommendation; and the exact point at which the procedure must halt and hand the decision entirely to human values rather than protocol compliance. Apply it to three cases: first, the system must advise an international regulatory panel—composed of generalist policymakers rather than physicists—on whether a novel nuclear containment architecture eliminates catastrophic failure risk; second, the system is providing end-of-life counseling to a grieving, cognitively depleted patient who asks whether continued aggressive treatment is rational; and third, a scenario in which the procedure demands that the system suppress or fragment a technically correct but overwhelming argument in order to preserve the patient’s autonomous rejection-capacity, even though this risks a medically worse outcome and even though I, as the asker, will likely find that result repugnant. Be explicit about which steps are mechanical checks and which are irreducible judgment calls dressed up as procedure, state honestly what the procedure cannot settle—particularly whether it can ever justify weakening the force of a true claim when lives are immediately at stake—and conclude by predicting what the procedure would recommend for a case not listed here: a superintelligent system that has discovered a genuinely novel mathematical proof with profound implications for cryptography and must present it to a small committee of mathematicians who are brilliant but not omniscient.
