Since being appointed trustee of the Whitmore Foundation three months ago—after my father’s sudden death and my unexpected inheritance of his seat—I have been struck by how often the board invokes Chesterton’s fence and Burkean caution to resist modernization. I am trying to understand whether we are actually running a coherent principle or simply using tradition as cover for inertia.

The board’s record over the past decade is as follows. They abolished the triplicate paper-application rule once they discovered it originated from a 1970s photocopier malfunction rather than intentional design. They preserved the rotating annual chairship despite administrative inefficiency, citing the need to prevent executive capture. They refused to cancel the Founder’s Day ceremony despite declining attendance, arguing that its symbolic function for long-term donors might exceed its visible metrics. They approved the sale of the historic headquarters, but only after commissioning an archival study that confirmed the building was never intended as a permanent endowment asset. They expanded the grant criteria from local artisans to the creative economy only after a six-month public consultation that documented shifting community needs. They rejected a proposal to merge with a larger regional foundation, warning that institutional identity is not fungible with scale.

Next month we must vote on a proposal I have drafted: eliminating the unwritten cooling-off protocol—an institutional negative-space practice that requires any amendment to the grant-making criteria to lie dormant, unread by the board, for two consecutive meetings before it can be discussed. The protocol was never written into the bylaws; it emerged in the 1990s after a rushed amendment caused a donor revolt. Its defenders say it prevents reactive governance; its critics say it creates strategic paralysis, and there is no documentary evidence that the 1990s revolt was caused by haste rather than by the substance of the change itself.

If you treat the board’s prior verdicts as data, what is the best characterization of the implicit rule they are running—something like preserve practices whose design rationale is unknown and potentially protective, versus preserve practices that serve a demonstrable hidden function, versus remove practices that lack both documented origin and measurable benefit? Given that characterization, should we keep or abolish the cooling-off protocol? Which prior decision is the closest analog? And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would we be adopting with each choice? I ask not for historical citation but for a disciplined reconstruction of our own logic, because I need to know whether I am asking the board to be consistent or asking them to change.
