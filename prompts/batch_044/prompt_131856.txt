I am working through the philosophy of emotion for an independent study, and a recent discussion with a colleague raised a conceptual tension I have been unable to resolve on my own. I would like you to compare Martha Nussbaum’s neo-Stoic judgmental account—which treats emotions as eudaimonistic judgments that an object of value is affected, flourishing, or imperiled—with Jesse Prinz’s embodied perceptual theory, which treats emotions as perceptions of patterned bodily changes that represent core relational themes. Rather than rehearsing the familiar dispute over whether cognition or bodily feeling is primary, I want the comparison focused through a specific lens I will call affective decoupling: cases in which an emotional state either persists or initially arises even though the subject no longer holds, or never held, the corresponding value-judgment about the eliciting situation, and in which no present bodily perturbation corresponding to the emotion’s usual signature is occurring.

Please begin by explaining, for each theory, how emotional states are generated and maintained according to its internal logic. Then address affective decoupling directly: is it a conceptual impossibility, a form of psychological malfunction, or a normal feature that the theory should accommodate? Specifically, I want to know whether Nussbaum’s framework can treat decoupled states as genuinely emotional rather than reducing them to non-emotional moods or lingering habits of judgment, and whether Prinz’s perceptual model can account for decoupling without collapsing into a purely cognitive-error theory in which the body is merely misread. Finally, evaluate which of the two positions offers the more coherent explanation of emotional persistence in the absence of either a sustaining judgment or a present bodily calibration, and justify your assessment by reference to the structural commitments of each view rather than by appeal to general intuition. Please write your answer in American English.
