I'm trying to think clearly about where conceptual work on corrigibility should actually go, rather than rehearsing the standard shutdown-problem framing that dominates most forums. Suppose you had to advise a small research group—say, three philosophers of action and two decision theorists, with no ML engineering capacity—on how to spend three years on corrigibility-related conceptual questions. I see at least four open lines that feel substantively different from the usual "why wouldn't a sufficiently capable system allow itself to be turned off" discussion.

First, whether corrigibility should be analyzed as a preference-ordering property—the agent ranks being-corrected-above-its-current-objectives—or as a reasoning-constraint property, meaning the agent's planning architecture contains a non-overrideable meta-rule that permits interruption regardless of preference ranking. Second, the nested-correction problem: if corrigibility is defined relative to a principal, and that principal's own beliefs or preferences are partly shaped by the agent's outputs, we seem to get a circular dependency rather than a clean hierarchical one. Does this undermine the concept of corrigibility, or does it require a non-hierarchical model of deference that hasn't been properly formalized? Third, whether shutdown is even the right operational test case: for digital agents, termination might be conceptually indistinguishable from archival, suspension, or partition into sub-agents, and it is unclear whether corrigibility to each of these differs in ways that matter for alignment. Fourth, the relationship between corrigibility and value learning—specifically, whether an agent that is genuinely corrigible about its values must be uncertain about them in a particular epistemic mode, and if so, whether that mode is compatible with the kind of decisive, world-acting capacity we usually want from powerful systems.

Of these four lines, which two or three deserve the bulk of the group's effort, and in what order? I am not asking for a literature survey. I want a defended prioritization: for each line you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of the others (or why independence makes ordering less important), how tractable it is for people without ML backgrounds, what kind of conceptual result over the next few years would move it up or down your ranking, and which lines you would deprioritize because resolving them would not change much downstream work on alignment. Be explicit about the criteria you are using—whether tractability, upstream dependency, or practical relevance for actual system design should dominate here—and flag exactly where they conflict and how you resolve the conflict.
