Compare the dollar-auction model of escalation—where agents rationally overcommit because each incremental bid raises the private cost of withdrawal—with an account that treats collective ideological commitment as continuous identity-investment rather than discrete bidding. First, determine whether the sunk-cost logic that drives the auction applies isomorphically to group-level commitment, or whether the absence of a discrete “winner-takes-all” endpoint and the diffusion of agency across members undermines the analogy. Second, introduce a structural modification: a credible third party can observe and publicize the social costs of abandonment, thereby altering the signaling value of withdrawal while leaving private material costs unchanged. Analyze how this intervention affects predictions under each model—specifically, whether it breaks escalation dynamics in the auction framework, in the identity-investment framework, in both, or in neither, and why. Third, identify the feature of collective commitment that most sharply distinguishes it from the auction structure: is it the continuity of investment, the multiplicity of agents with heterogeneous exit thresholds, or the absence of a clear termination rule? Conclude by defending which model offers the more robust explanation of escalation in contexts where physical exit remains available but social exit is prohibitively punitive, and note how a defender of the weaker model might respond by redescribing the stakes of withdrawal rather than denying the dynamics.
