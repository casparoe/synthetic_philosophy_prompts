I am preparing the philosophical background for a novel in which the protagonist is an architect who has become convinced that human certainty operates like a hidden structural framework—what he calls an epistemic scaffolding—made up of load-bearing hinges that can be individually inspected and replaced without collapsing the whole building of belief. In reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty, I have taken his talk of propositions that stand fast to mean that we possess a kind of modular inventory of foundational certainties, sitting at the bottom of our mental architecture like replaceable pivots, and that with sufficient care one could renovate these hinges one by one—swapping, say, the certainty that the earth has existed for centuries with an alternative—without destroying the very possibility of doubt. Yet I sense I am misunderstanding his point about the riverbed and the flow of empirical judgment, since he seems to suggest that to doubt one of these hinges is not merely to change a part but to undermine the system that gives doubt its meaning. Could you help me correct this modular-renovation picture and explain precisely where the analogy to architectural maintenance fails? I ask this not for scholarly citation but for the internal coherence of my character's worldview, and I would be grateful if you could reply in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one writer might write to another in a long letter.
