You serve as lead auditor for an automated infrastructure consortium that manages distributed chemical-processing nodes. After every contamination or overflow incident, you must file a causal-attribution report identifying which system omissions—failures of sensors, valves, or protocols to act—count as genuine causes of the harm rather than as mere background conditions. The distinction carries regulatory weight: an omission listed as a cause triggers mandatory redesign; one listed only as a contributing condition does not. You apply this judgment repeatedly—roughly twice per month—under time pressure of about thirty minutes per report, and your signed report is then read by engineers and regulators with no philosophical training. You need a reusable decision procedure that your team can run without you, grounded in the philosophical analysis of causation by omission but stripped of technical vocabulary and made executable by non-specialists.

The procedure should operationalize the following conceptual framework, which combines standard distinctions with an institutional refinement you must build in. First, an omission is causally relevant only relative to a baseline expectation of action—what can be called a design or normative baseline—not relative to arbitrary absence. Second, the omission must be counterfactually necessary: had the expected action occurred, the harmful outcome would have been prevented or substantially altered, holding the surrounding causal structure fixed. Third, distinguish Type-I omissions, in which a system was expected to interrupt an active causal chain (for example, shutting a valve to stop a flow), from Type-II omissions, in which a system was expected to maintain a static safeguard (for example, sustaining a pressure threshold). For Type-I, test counterfactual necessity by asking whether the interruption would have broken the chain; for Type-II, ask whether the safeguard’s presence would have blocked the pathway entirely. Fourth, apply an operational-override check: if the design baseline was legitimately suspended by a documented protocol update, maintenance window, or formal reassignment order at the time of the incident, then the expectation of action no longer holds, and the omission falls back to a mere non-event rather than a cause.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions your auditors can ask, with explicit stopping rules (where the answer is do not list as cause), explicit hand-off points (where the question turns on values, liability allocation, or institutional blame rather than on factual causation), and clear flags for where the procedure is likely to misfire or produce false positives. Preserve the distinctions that actually affect the regulatory outcome, but express them in language usable by engineers and lawyers.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. A chlorine-monitoring node in a water-treatment network fails to trigger a shutdown valve during a contamination spike. The valve was designed to respond within two seconds; logs show no signal was sent. The node had not been reassigned, updated, or placed in maintenance mode. Walk through the procedure and state the classification.

2. The same type of node is present but was placed in maintenance mode by an authorized technician three hours before the incident, disabling automatic shutdown while manual checks were scheduled. No manual check occurred, and contamination followed. Walk through the procedure, show exactly what the operational-override check does, and state the classification—including whether any part of the decision reaches a hand-off point.

3. A pressure-safeguard system was expected to maintain a static threshold (Type-II) and did not, allowing a slow leak that accumulated over several days. The logs show the safeguard was technically active but calibrated to a new, higher tolerance that management had approved informally—without updating the design documentation—one week prior. Walk through the procedure and explain why this is the case where the procedure gives you, the auditor, an answer you will not like: it either forces you to list the omission as a cause despite the informal management approval, or it forces you to invoke a value-laden hand-off about whether informal approvals can override documented baselines.

In each application, specify exactly where in the sequence the decision is made, what evidence is required, and where judgment cannot be eliminated. Do not cite specific philosophical texts by name, do not provide a bibliography, and do not ask for one. Keep the output usable by practitioners who have never read the underlying theory, but do not collapse the Type-I versus Type-II distinction or the operational-override check into a single vague step.
