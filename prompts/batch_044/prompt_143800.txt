I'm writing a feature on how interdisciplinary advisory panels aggregate judgments about complex policy risks—for example, separately assessing whether a novel synthetic compound bioaccumulates and whether that finding warrants an immediate production moratorium, then facing the structural possibility that majorities support both premises while rejecting the derived conclusion. The discursive dilemma demonstrates that such failures are unavoidable under democratic aggregation with logical closure. To my mind, this is essentially Arrow's impossibility result translated into propositional logic: any rule that respects individual equality and consistency must eventually contradict itself, whether applied to preferences or to belief sets. Given that, I want to ask whether there remains any coherent justification for treating collective expert recommendations as rationally authoritative rather than as arbitrary political compromises—or whether a specific architectural protocol, such as relaxing closure requirements in favor of sequential premise-elicitation coupled with structured belief revision, can genuinely escape the trap and restore epistemic legitimacy to group judgment?
