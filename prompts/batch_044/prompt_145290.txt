I've been arguing with a friend about Buridan's ass and it made me wonder how real institutions actually handle perfect ties, since philosophers usually just talk about the donkey. So there's this pattern I keep seeing: in some arbitration boards, if the panel splits exactly evenly after full deliberation, the default is that nothing changes—the status quo holds and the case gets dismissed. But in other settings, like some public housing lotteries or organ-allocation protocols, if two applicants have identically scored priorities, they immediately draw lots, and the rules explicitly forbid using age, income, or 'social contribution' to break the tie. One practice treats deadlock as a reason to stay put; the other treats it as proof that reasons have run out and you need randomness now.

What view of rational choice, or of the will, do these two designs presuppose? Does the arbitration rule basically accept a version of the principle of sufficient reason for action—if there's no reason to move, don't move, like Buridan's ass should stay between the bales? Does the lottery rule assume that rationality doesn't need a reason for every particular choice, just a fair procedure? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy in each case, say whether they're consistent with each other or depend on different pictures of what 'indifferance' actually means, and tell me what a decision procedure built on Leibniz's idea that there's always a hidden sufficient reason—even when we can't see it—would have to look like in practice. Also, is there a coherent middle ground where you wait a bit to see if hidden reasons emerge, but then flip a coin if they don't?
