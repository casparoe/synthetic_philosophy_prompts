Analyze the Boston Tea Party of December 1773 through a threshold-sensitive associative-obligation theory of political obligation. On this view, citizens bear associative duties to uphold a legal order only so long as a critical mass of others comply; once defection crosses a structural threshold, the obligation dissolves, but isolated or premature defection remains unjustified unless it is strategically calibrated to push the collective over that threshold. Apply this framework precisely: identify the relevant association, specify what would count as the compliance threshold in the colonial context, determine whether the destruction of the tea was an attempt to cross that threshold or something else entirely, and assess whether Parliament's passage of the Coercive Acts in 1774 confirms that the threshold had indeed been reached. Then identify where the theory misdescribes or is silent about the episode—particularly regarding intentional rupture versus coordination failure, and the role of economic grievance versus constitutional principle—and say what the case shows about whether associative obligations can ever license destructive disobedience rather than mere non-compliance.
