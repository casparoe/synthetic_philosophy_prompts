I spent the last several hours in a heated but genuinely unresolved argument with a friend about whether indirect normativity can survive without collapsing into either direct moral realism or a merely procedural agreement. We kept circling the same structure: norms are supposed to be authoritative not because they match an independent moral fact, but because they would be endorsed, or not rejectable, under some idealized form of deliberation. The fight was not about whether this idea is attractive in the abstract; it was about what exactly has to be true of the idealization for the result to bind anyone in the actual world, and whether two different ways of specifying that idealization are genuinely incompatible or just differently worded.

So I want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps within this space might actually sign. Name the two positions precisely: on one side, the Reflective-Rejection Contractualists, who hold that a principle is valid just in case no one could reasonably reject it in a deliberative setting where all parties have full information, consistent beliefs, and equal standing, but where each party retains its own actual normative commitments and practical identity; on the other side, the Transformative-Endorsement Deliberationists, who hold that normative authority derives from what agents would come to endorse after undergoing a structured process of reciprocal perspective-taking that is permitted—indeed required—to revise (not merely inform) the evaluative framework with which they entered.

The deliverable should be formatted as a single constructive document both sides could put their names to. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: the shared formal commitments (e.g., that direct realism about moral properties is not being defended; that the appeal is to a counterfactual procedure rather than actual consent; that the no-go result for purely aggregative preference-satisfaction applies to both views). Then present a disagreements table in which each row states a precise point of divergence, with the left column giving the contractualist formulation in wording they accept and the right column giving the deliberationist formulation in wording they accept—no neutral gloss that either side would reject. After that, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that if one were settled, at least one camp should move. I have in mind especially the retention problem—whether an agent is bound by a norm if her pre-deliberation practical identity is discontinuous with the perspective that endorsed it—and the residue problem—whether there can be binding norms that no actual or counterfactual agent in the procedure generates, yet that we have reason to treat as constraints on the procedure itself. For each crux, state what each side predicts the correct resolution should look like, and specify a concrete test case, thought experiment, or argumentative move that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side’s anticipated outcome recorded in advance.

Finally, include a section that examines whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—particularly around phrases like “reasonable rejection” versus “genuine endorsement,” or “retaining one’s commitments” versus “being open to transformation”—and say exactly which part, if any, dissolves once the terms are fixed. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; do not invoke obscure named theories that depend on precise textual knowledge; and do not quote any philosopher verbatim—paraphrase without quotation marks. Write as if you are presenting this document to the friend with whom you argued, in a formal but direct tone.
