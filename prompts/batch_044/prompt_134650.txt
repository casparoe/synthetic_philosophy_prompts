I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind, and I am designing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on intentionality, functionalism, and artificial intelligence. The course has covered Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance, his view that belief and desire are patterns best interpreted through predictive strategy rather than internal mechanisms, and his concern that sufficiently sophisticated artificial systems might become counterfeit people—entities that reliably invite person-level interpretation despite lacking the evolutionary history and embedded social development that ground genuine human personhood. I want one essay question that students can answer in roughly ninety minutes, with an expected response of about six hundred words.

The question must be unanswerable by summarizing Dennett’s criteria or by reciting the distinction between the physical stance and the intentional stance. Instead, it should force students to work through a specific conceptual tension: whether Dennett’s framework for counterfeit people can be extended consistently to moral agency, and whether this extension destabilizes his functionalism. To that end, please frame the prompt around a hypothetical artificial system—call it a counterfeit moral agent—that produces behavior indistinguishable from moral reasoning and emotional responsiveness, yet was designed without evolutionary history or biological embodiment. The exam question should ask students whether Dennett is committed to treating such a system as a genuine moral agent, and whether his answer depends on treating moral properties as purely stance-dependent in a way that conflicts with the historical-credential conditions he uses to exclude counterfeit people. In other words, students should have to decide whether Dennett must either weaken his anti-essentialism about personhood or deny moral standing to entities that functionally satisfy every normative criterion he recognizes.

Please provide the following:

1. The exam question itself, written clearly and including any necessary conceptual framing within the prompt so that students need no outside texts.
2. A model answer of approximately six hundred words. The answer should demonstrate five specific moves: (a) reconstructing Dennett’s argument that personhood requires more than functional equivalence; (b) explaining why evolutionary and historical embedding matter on his view; (c) applying the counterfeit-person worry to the domain of moral agency; (d) diagnosing whether moral agency is, for Dennett, purely a matter of interpretive stance or whether it inherits the same grounding conditions; and (e) delivering a reasoned verdict on whether the counterfeit moral agent case forces Dennett toward a revision of his functionalism or of his anti-essentialism.
3. A marking scheme totaling twenty marks, with marks allocated to each of the five moves above. The scheme should distinguish an A answer from a B answer not by length but by the presence of specific argumentative steps: for instance, an A answer must explicitly identify the potential inconsistency between stance-dependence and historical grounding, whereas a B answer merely notes that Dennett cares about evolutionary history without showing why that creates pressure in the moral domain. The scheme should also list the specific mistakes that should cost marks—such as conflating Dennett’s position with Searle’s Chinese Room argument, treating evolutionary history as a biological essentialism Dennett accepts rather than rejects, or assuming that functional equivalence automatically guarantees moral status without addressing the counterfeit worry.

Please do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations in the exam question. Do not depend on highly obscure texts, specific page numbers, or precise publication dates. You may paraphrase Dennett’s general framework, but do not put anything in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the exact wording from a source; otherwise, paraphrase without quotation marks. The prompt and model answer should be meaningful for a student or system without web access, relying only on the conceptual framework described above and on the injected distinction of the counterfeit moral agent.
