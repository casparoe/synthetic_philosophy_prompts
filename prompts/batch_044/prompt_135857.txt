As a novelist constructing a philosophical character study, I have built an argument about mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will that now disturbs me because its conclusion is intolerable—either moral responsibility vanishes entirely, or we must attribute free will to wasps. I lay it out in steps. (1) An action expresses free will only if the agent possesses a second-order capacity to suspend its own behavioral script when presented with a reason that the script itself does not encode; absent this capacity, behavior is sphexish. (2) If an agent is entirely sphexish, its actions are mechanically determined, and moral responsibility is impossible. (3) Consider a fictional character: every action is fixed by the author’s script. Even when the author revises the plot, the revision is causally external to the character; the character never suspends its own script from within. (4) Therefore fictional characters lack free will, and the novelist bears full moral responsibility for any harm they cause—just as a programmer is responsible for a malfunctioning automaton. (5) Apply this to humans: any apparent interruption of a human behavioral script—resisting an addiction, altering a habit—is always traceable to some other script, conditioning, or external perturbation; there is never a genuinely internal, uncaused suspension. (6) Hence humans are structurally analogous to fictional characters: their flexibility is always the replacement of one script by another, never an authentic second-order break. (7) So either all agents, including humans, are sphexish and moral responsibility is an illusion, or we must concede that even the sphex wasp, when experimentally disrupted and resuming its loop at a different stage, exhibits script-replacement flexibility identical to human “choice.” (8) By parity, the wasp then possesses free will. I wish to preserve both the intuition that fictional characters are not morally responsible and the intuition that wasps are not free agents, but I cannot locate the break in the chain without abandoning the core idea that free will requires internal script-suspension. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I am reluctant to surrender (1) or (4); if I keep them, am I forced into (8), or is there a hidden equivocation between external authorship and internal causation?
