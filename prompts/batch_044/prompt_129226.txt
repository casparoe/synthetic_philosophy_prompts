Write a dialogue between two equally competent scholars debating the ethical value of hagiographic biography, using Plutarch's paired lives as their central case. The first, defending normative hagiography, should argue that moral learning requires idealized portraits that establish clear normative horizons — showing virtue in its fully realized form so that ordinary agents have proportional standards to aspire toward. The second, defending contingency biography, should argue that this idealization produces an aretaic parallax, systematically distorting the reader's sense of how virtue and vice are actually distributed in human life, and should claim that Plutarch's comparisons are most instructive when they reveal how similar virtues emerge from radically different conditions of luck and failure rather than when they sanitize their subjects. Each should respond directly to the other's points: the hagiographer should press whether contingency biography collapses aspiration into mere historical description, and the contingency advocate should press whether sanitized exemplars make virtue seem unattainable and thus practically irrelevant. The dialogue should reach a clarified disagreement with substantive concessions on both sides, without resolving into agreement.
