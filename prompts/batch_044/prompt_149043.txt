As a novelist whose current manuscript examines how algorithmic reconstruction of personal histories undermines the possibility of moral reinvention, I have been invited to present an argument—drawn from my research rather than from any published philosophical text—at an interdisciplinary seminar in philosophy of technology. The audience is precisely this: information ethicists who treat privacy as a matter of data-subject control and context-sensitive flow norms; phenomenologists of technology who understand privacy through embodied exposure, transparency, and the mediated presence of others; and legal scholars for whom privacy is strictly instrumental to autonomy and democratic deliberation. I defend the claim that privacy's deepest value is not informational control, nor autonomy-protection, but what I call narrative discontinuity: the entitlement of a person to have her past mental states, affective responses, and actions remain resistant to algorithmic or social synthesis into a continuously accessible profile, so that genuine self-revision is not perpetually undermined by an externally maintained coherence of who she is taken to be.

Please prepare me for the Q&A by drafting a prep sheet that orders objections according to how likely they are and how damaging they could be, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually employ—the ethicists speaking of data governance and consent architectures, the phenomenologists of embodied vulnerability and technological disclosure, the legal scholars of rights-claims and state interests. For each objection, specify what I should concede without hesitation, what I must hold, and the precise point where my reply is weakest. I also need to identify and fortify the weakest structural point in my own position: the tension between demanding discontinuity and still accepting the ordinary social memory and testimony that make human relationships possible, which I must defend rather than hope they overlook. Finally, include the objection I am praying nobody raises—that narrative discontinuity collapses into either a disguised entitlement to deception, or an elitist demand for technological illiteracy that only the privileged can satisfy—and give me a concise reply that acknowledges the force of the worry without surrendering the core claim.
