I'm trying to understand an internal tension in Immanuel Kant's 1795 essay Toward Perpetual Peace (Zum ewigen Frieden). In the preliminary articles he prohibits secret treaties of peace in which matter is tacitly reserved for a future war, insisting that interstate commitments remain transparent. Yet in the second supplement he introduces a "secret article" holding that the maxims of philosophers should be consulted in state councils concerning war and peace, but without being promulgated as public law—so that reason may guide power covertly rather than claim direct authority. To make the difficulty concrete: imagine a modern federation institutionalizes this secret article through classified advisory boards whose reasoning is never published, and those boards occasionally recommend hidden strategic reservations because the expected costs of open commitment are undefined. Does Kant's preliminary prohibition apply only to formal interstate treaties, leaving the board's internal reservations untouched as a meta-provision? Or does the secret article risk collapsing into the very kind of concealed reservation for future conflict that the preliminary articles condemn? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
