In psychiatric classification, there is a prolonged stall over whether Hacking-style looping effects occur in adult personality disorders—particularly borderline personality disorder—in the same way they are thought to occur in childhood neurodevelopmental categories. Proponents of the loop claim that diagnosis reshapes self-narrative, emotional regulation practices, and clinical presentation in feedback fashion: patients learn diagnostic vocabulary, clinicians adjust thresholds, and the kind shifts over time. Opponents grant every empirical correlation—rising prevalence, altered symptom profiles, stronger patient identification—but deny this constitutes looping in the interactive-kind sense. They argue that personality disorders are already constituted by unstable self-image and interpersonal dysfunction, so any feedback between label and presentation is not an external loop altering the kind, but an internal feature of the disorder expressed differently under clinical scrutiny. Both sides have traded the same longitudinal prevalence studies for years without convergence.
