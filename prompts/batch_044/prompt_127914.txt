For a long-form feature I am writing on the philosophy of mind and the limits of personal identity, I have sketched an argument about split-brain consciousness that collapses into an absurd conclusion about distributed personhood, and I need help locating the fault line.

Here is the chain.

(1) In classic split-brain cases—following section of the corpus callosum—the left hemisphere can name an object shown to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere can direct the left hand to point to a matching object in the left field, with neither side able to report what the other has done.

(2) If two information-processing systems within one cranium can maintain contradictory representations and execute incompatible actions without mutual accessibility, then they are not parts of a single stream of consciousness; they are two subjects.

(3) Now imagine a modified case—let us call it a gradient division—where the corpus callosum is largely severed but the anterior commissure and hippocampal commissures remain intact. Emotional valence and episodic tagging flow across the divide, so both hemispheres feel the same anxiety about the contradiction and later share a memory of having disagreed, yet perceptual and motor information remain isolated.

(4) By the criterion in (2), we should not conclude that there are two full subjects here, because there is substantial integration—affective and mnemonic unity. But neither should we conclude there is exactly one, since perceptual contradiction persists without resolution.

(5) Therefore, if integration is a matter of degree, the number of subjects inside one skull is not a discrete fact but a sliding scale. Applied consistently, this means that two strangers who maintain a lifelong, intimate emotional and mnemonic bond—say, twins raised in constant proximity with shared ritual and affective attunement—might constitute 1.8 subjects, or one distributed subject with weak peripheral nodes.

(6) That result is absurd: we do not treat close friends or twins as fractions of a person.

(7) Hence the original criterion in (2) must be wrong, and split-brain cases never genuinely divided consciousness.

But I want to keep (2) if possible, because abandoning it seems to erase the genuine phenomenological rupture these patients report. If I must choose, I prefer retaining (2) and (3) over (6) or (7). Which link should I break, and what does breaking it cost? In particular, does the gradient-division case expose an equivocation between informational isolation and phenomenal separation, or does it show that our concept of a subject was always a pragmatic construct?

Please answer without citing specific papers or giving a bibliography; I am writing for a general audience and need conceptual clarity rather than scholarly apparatus.
