I'm currently supervising an interdisciplinary thesis that uses agent-based modeling to study institutional change, and a reviewer recently accused my student of "smuggling holism through the interaction rules"—which made me realize I need an exam that forces candidates to separate explanatory strategy from ontological commitment rather than just summarizing Weber and Durkheim. For my upper-level philosophy of social science seminar final, draft one essay question that a well-prepared student could answer in 90 minutes. The question should present a concrete, non-canonical carrying case: an open-source software ecosystem in which no single contributor understands the full architecture, yet emergent properties—specifically, security vulnerability profiles—appear to constrain and redirect individual contribution patterns in ways that cannot be reconstructed from any agent's beliefs or intentions alone. Ask candidates to determine whether this scenario supports ontological holism, explanatory individualism with emergent patterns, or an intermediate position, and require them to address explicitly: (i) the distinction between explanatory and ontological individualism, (ii) whether "downward causation" is genuinely at work or merely an epistemic artifact of aggregation, and (iii) why methodological individualism does not entail reductionism about social laws. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with the point value for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it, and one named misconception (e.g., treating methodological individualism as a thesis about mental states rather than about explanatory primitives) that should cost marks if it appears. Keep the vocabulary to standard philosophical concepts that a well-read graduate student should know—do not invoke obscure sub-theories or recent niche literature—so that the difficulty comes from conceptual precision rather than recall. The marking scheme should fit in about half a page. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
