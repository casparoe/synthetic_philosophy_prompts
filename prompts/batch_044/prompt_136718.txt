Design a classroom activity for a 75-minute workshop on AI alignment and philosophy with 15 participants (graduate students and researchers). The objective is to make the group feel, through rules and structured failure rather than lecture, the structural problem of defining an “avoid side effects” constraint for a low-impact AI without supplying a full specification of value. Divide participants into three teams of five; assign each team a narrow objective card—e.g., “maximize paperclip production in this factory,” “eliminate pathogen X,” or “optimize urban delivery routes”—and instruct them that they may not invoke a complete value ordering or a comprehensive list of protected goods. Give each team 15 minutes to draft a formal negative constraint specifying what the agent must not alter, using only concepts that do not presuppose a full welfare function. Then run two adversarial rounds moderated by a rotating “red team” of two participants: in Round 1, apply three pre-written scenario cards to each constraint that expose hidden value commitments (for example, “the factory’s current layout includes a worker’s personal storage space,” or “eliminating X requires microbiome shifts that affect unrelated conditions”). Allow 10 minutes for revision. In Round 2, the red team plays an “edge-case card” tailored to each revised constraint to show that it either permits catastrophic change via an unregulated loophole or prevents the intended task by over-protecting the status quo. Use non-monetary stakes: award “deployment points” not for success but for the least-broken formulation, so that all teams experience collective failure rather than competitive victory; if a team’s constraint survives Round 2 intact, they must defend it against a final “baseline challenge” asking them to justify the “normal” state they are protecting without appealing to value. Include a 15-minute debrief that maps Round 1 onto the smuggling of value-laden baselines, Round 2 onto the collapse of negative impact measures into implicit value functions, and the shared failure onto the philosophical claim that “low impact” cannot be defined independently of “high value.” Predict what will go wrong the first time: teams will converge on “do not harm humans,” which the red team breaks by demanding a prior theory of harm; others will appeal to “common sense” or “minimal change,” which should be recorded as data points showing that every baseline is already value-laden. Be frank about the feature the activity cannot reproduce: it can make vivid the boundary problem and the baseline-dependence of negative constraints, but it cannot demonstrate the formal result that any computable side-effect penalty is extensionally equivalent to a partial value function; you must supply that equivalence verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not require participants to research specific authors; and avoid invoking highly specialized technical formalisms that depend on outside verification.
