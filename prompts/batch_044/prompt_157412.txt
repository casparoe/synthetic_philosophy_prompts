As a former physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have assembled the following argument about self-knowledge and now find its conclusion implausible, though I cannot isolate the error.

(1) The KK principle holds: if S knows that p, then S knows that S knows that p.
(2) Luminosity holds for cognitive fatigue: whenever S is in a state of intellectual fatigue, S knows she is.
(3) Fatigue is a continuously varying condition, analogous to temperature in a thermal system.
(4) At the extreme F0 I am maximally fatigued; by (2) I know this.
(5) By (1) applied to (4), I know that I know I am fatigued.
(6) Self-monitoring displays epistemic hysteresis—borrowed from magnetic physics—so that if one knows one is in state F at point t, then across a small interval dt one retains that knowledge even as the underlying condition shifts, because the monitoring mechanism lags behind the state.
(7) Iterating (6) across the gradient from F0 to F100 (full alertness) yields that at F100 I still know I am fatigued.
(8) At F100 I am not fatigued; thus I possess false knowledge about my own mind.
(9) The reasoning generalizes: for any mental state that varies continuously—attention, pain, mood—the same derivation applies.
(10) Hence, if both (1) and (2) are true, rational agents can systematically come to know falsehoods about their present mental conditions.

The result proves too much: it does not merely threaten one principle but seems to undermine all introspective knowledge of gradable mental states. I would prefer to preserve (2), since direct awareness of one's own fatigue feels undeniable, but I wonder whether the fault lies in (6), or whether I have slipped from a particular instance in (5) to a universal claim in (7). Which step should be rejected, and what philosophical cost does that rejection entail?
