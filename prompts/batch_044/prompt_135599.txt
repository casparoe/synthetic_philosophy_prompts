At my school’s philosophy club, two seniors—Jordan and Riley—got stuck in a sharp exchange about the moral equality of combatants. I am reporting it as a bystander and do not take sides; I want you to adjudicate the moves exactly as made.

Jordan opened by arguing that the traditional equality thesis—that soldiers on both sides possess the same rights to kill and the same liability to be killed, regardless of which cause is just—depends on keeping the justice of the war strictly separate from the justice of individual acts within it. Jordan granted that this separation works as a practical rule when knowledge is murky, but said it collapses in cases of near-certain moral clarity. He then proposed a hypothetical: State A is unambiguously repelling a genocidal invasion by State B, and we know with near-certainty that B’s soldiers are not coerced conscripts but ideologically committed volunteers fighting for an unjust, genocidal cause. In that scenario, Jordan said, describing B’s soldiers as “morally equal” to A’s defenders is absurd; B’s soldiers are closer to participants in a collective crime, and A’s soldiers are closer to legitimate defenders or law-enforcement agents. He concluded that equality is at best a fallible heuristic, not a deep moral principle, and should be abandoned when moral knowledge is strong.

Riley replied that Jordan mistakes the nature of the equality thesis. It is not an epistemic rule about our confidence, Riley said, but an institutional and moral boundary: individual soldiers rarely have adequate information or standing to judge the justice of the war they are ordered to fight, so they are permitted to fight if they hold a reasonable belief in their cause’s justice, and opposing soldiers must treat them as legitimate targets regardless. Even in Jordan’s extreme case, Riley insisted, B’s soldiers retain basic rights not to be killed arbitrarily; their culpability for joining an unjust cause does not turn them into private wrongdoers who may be executed by enemy judgment, but only makes them liable to defensive harm within the structured practice of war. Riley also accused Jordan of illegitimately importing a domestic law-enforcement model into relations between sovereign states, warning that once soldiers are permitted to assess the enemy’s cause and act on that assessment, warfare collapses into unregulated murder.

Jordan came back with two objections. First, he said that if equality is genuinely institutional rather than epistemic, it should hold even when knowledge is perfect—but Riley had just conceded that B’s soldiers are culpable, which is exactly the kind of moral difference that should affect liability. Second, Jordan attacked Riley’s “reasonable belief” shield: in his hypothetical, B’s soldiers are not merely reasoning in good faith; they are ideologically committed to genocide, so the epistemic excuse is absent. Jordan then pressed that Riley is really defending equality as a convention, not as a moral fact, and that conventions should be revised when they conflict with clear moral knowledge rather than protected for regulatory convenience.

Riley’s final move was to deny that “near-certain” judgments ever exist in actual warfare, emphasizing human fallibility. More fundamentally, Riley argued that the equality thesis never claimed soldiers are equally virtuous; it claims they occupy equal positions of permission and liability within the practice of war. Even a culpable aggressor remains a combatant governed by the rules of war, not a criminal subject to private execution by the enemy. Riley concluded that Jordan wants to collapse jus in bello into jus ad bellum, which would require every soldier to become a moral philosopher before engaging, destroying the very regulatory framework that distinguishes war from chaos.
