Please draft opening remarks — about 900 words — for a workshop I am convening in the auditorium of a regional history museum. The occasion is the public launch of a new permanent exhibit on national founding narratives. The audience is deliberately split: half are academic historians and sociologists who study collective memory and state formation, and half are museum curators, city archivists, and local heritage-board members who maintain the monuments, archives, and public rituals under discussion. The building itself was constructed in 1931 during a civic beautification campaign that retroactively claimed medieval origins for the settlement; the exhibit treats this fact explicitly.

The conceptual framework should come from the literature on invented traditions and imagined communities — specifically, the view that nations and their ritual apparatus are not ancient organic outgrowths but relatively recent constructions, paired with the claim that recognizing this does not render them arbitrary, disposable, or merely false. These ideas must do structural work in the speech, not serve as decorative references: they should determine what you can and cannot say about the exhibit's artifacts, about the museum's own architecture, and about the obligations of the people in the room who steward civic memory. You should navigate the precise tension that labeling a tradition "invented" is not equivalent to labeling it illegitimate, and that an "imagined" community remains a real object of loyalty, care, and institutional responsibility. Address the practical question facing the non-academic half of the audience: if national identity is not primordial, what follows for the preservation of archives, the maintenance of monuments, and the design of public ceremony? The remarks must be accessible to specialists without condescension and to board members without dilution; avoid nostalgic nationalism on one side and cynical debunking on the other. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources. Do not quote any text verbatim — paraphrase all concepts in your own language. Write as someone who understands why the distinction between "invented" and "fake" is philosophically load-bearing and who can explain that distinction to a curator over coffee.
