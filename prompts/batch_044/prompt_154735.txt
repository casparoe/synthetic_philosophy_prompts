I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of properties and dispositions, and we've been working through the familiar problems raised by finks and masks — cases where an object apparently possesses a disposition but some interfering condition either removes the categorical basis at the moment of stimulation or blocks the manifestation without altering the basis. In our discussion section, a classmate offered a defense of the conditional analysis by comparing dispositions to vending machines: inserting a coin is the stimulus condition, the internal mechanism is the categorical basis, and dispensing a soda is the manifestation. On her view, a fink is analogous to a hidden switch inside the machine that jams the gears precisely when a coin is inserted — the machine was a vending machine right up to that instant, but the interference makes it fail. A mask, she said, is like a cover placed over the coin slot: the internal mechanism is intact, but the cover prevents the stimulus from reaching it. From this she draws a broader conclusion: dispositions are not mysterious intrinsic powers but essentially relational structures, defined by what would happen in the absence of interference, and the existence of finks and masks merely shows that we have to index dispositions to complete environmental descriptions rather than to hidden natures.

Can you audit this vending-machine analogy with the rigor it needs? Please begin with an explicit correspondence table — coin, slot, gears, jamming switch, cover, designer, user — mapped onto stimulus condition, categorical basis, manifestation, fink, mask, and whatever plays the corresponding metaphysical roles. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks: consider especially whether the analogy can accommodate multi-track dispositions, in which the same categorical basis produces different manifestations depending on context, or whether it forces a rigid single-track picture; examine whether the machine's dependence on an external designer and an intentional user smuggles in an extrinsic, functionalist conception of dispositions that the analogy's defender is not entitled to assume; and distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine her anti-mysterian conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the conclusion — that dispositions are fully analyzable as interference-relative conditionals with no residual intrinsic power — survive once these breaks are on the table? And if the vending-machine picture is inadequate, is there a better analogy — perhaps drawn from chemistry, biology, or information theory — that preserves her insight about interference-dependence without importing the problematic commitments?
