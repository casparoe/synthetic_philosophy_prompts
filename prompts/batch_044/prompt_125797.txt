I've been drafting an essay for my blog on what the cogito actually establishes, and I'd like critical feedback before I finalize it. The central claim is that Descartes proves at most an indexical occurrence of cognition—something like "this thinking is happening"—and that the slide to "I am a thinking substance" imports a substantialist ontology the premise doesn't license. I want to develop this by comparing the cogito's indexical structure to self-locating beliefs (e.g., Sleeping Beauty-type cases), where being the subject of an experience doesn't entail being a metaphysically primitive entity, and to Humean and Buddhist "no-self" frameworks, which treat the absence of a persisting thinker as the default rather than as a thesis to be defeated.

I'm especially unsure about three things and would appreciate targeted comments:

First, on textual accuracy: is it correct that Descartes needs the substantialist conclusion for the rest of the Meditations—particularly for the real-distinction argument and the proof of God in the Third Meditation—to get off the ground? Or does he have resources to recover substance independently, in which case weakening the cogito to an indexed event wouldn't actually undermine the architecture?

Second, on coherence and framing: I draw an analogy between treating "I" as a primitive decision-maker in the cogito and treating causal decision theory as primitive rather than evidential in Newcomb-style problems. The idea is that both moves illegitimately reify an indexed process into a substantial agent. Is this analogy illuminating, or does it conflate epistemic and metaphysical issues in a way that confuses rather than clarifies?

Third, on a specific subsection: I argue that if the cogito only establishes an occurrence, then the problem of other minds becomes symmetric with first-person self-knowledge—we lack direct access to our own substantial self, just as we lack direct access to others' minds, and behavioral inference is on equally shaky ground in both directions. Does this symmetry argument hold, or is there a disanalogy I'm missing—perhaps involving the immediacy of phenomenal access—that breaks the parallel?

The intended audience is analytically trained but not necessarily steeped in Descartes scholarship; I'm aiming for a conceptual audit rather than a contribution to historical exegesis. Would you flag any serious misreadings of the Meditations? Are there obvious objections—say, from the "clear and distinct perception" framework—that I'm failing to address? And does the rationalist-flavored framing (indexicals, decision-theory analogies, explicit consistency checks) add clarity, or does it muddy a problem that is better handled in purely phenomenological terms?
