I am preparing an essay on the explanatory tension between Christopher Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen's cultural explanation of perpetrator behavior during the Holocaust. Rather than adjudicating whether situational pressure or eliminationist ideology was dominant, I want to ask whether the two explanations are structurally compatible. My working hypothesis is that they are compatible only if situational mechanisms—peer conformity, authority diffusion, incremental commitment—are reconceived not as brute psychological forces but as operating through an institutional framing of moral visibility: shared norms within the unit about which lives counted as grievable and which orders counted as legitimate reasons. On this view, culture does not compete with situation; it supplies the epistemic scaffolding that makes situational pressure interpretable as moral permission rather than coercive threat.

I would welcome comments and suggestions, particularly on three points. First, does this framing preserve Browning's claim that these were ordinary men capable of ordinary moral reasoning, or does it implicitly reduce them to culture-bound automatons? Second, can the concept of institutional framing be reconstructed from the historical record without importing anachronistic social theory? Third, does the synthesis risk collapsing into Goldhagen's position by assigning all explanatory weight to culture, and if so, what conceptual distinction could preserve an independent causal role for situational dynamics?
