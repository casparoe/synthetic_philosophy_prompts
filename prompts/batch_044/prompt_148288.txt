I chair the ethics review board for a mid-sized city’s public-school curriculum committee, and over the past year we have begun receiving formal objections not merely to course content but to the reasons given for including or excluding materials. Some parents and fellow board members invoke explicitly religious grounds; others invoke secular values—parental autonomy, literary merit, scientific literacy—that they privately acknowledge derive from comprehensive religious commitments. Because these objections arise repeatedly, with real stakes for adoption deadlines and with board members who lack philosophical training, I need a reusable decision procedure grounded in Rawls’s account of public reason and the idea that citizens may introduce comprehensive doctrines into public discussion only if they eventually supply adequate public reasons—the requirement often associated with his proviso. The procedure must be something we can run in under an hour during a regular meeting, and it must clearly mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated rather than disguising value calls as procedural steps.

Please translate that framework into a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words: the ordered questions to ask about any proposed justification for a curriculum decision; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; where the procedure stops with a clear ruling that the justification violates public reason; where it permits the justification because genuinely independent secular reasons are present; and where it returns the matter to the board because the line between an independent secular reason and a post-hoc rationalization of a religious conviction is not resolvable by procedure alone. Include a small set of test sentences or claim-types the board can apply to objections, and flag the judgment calls that remain dressed up as procedural checks. Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, an objection that a biology textbook should be excluded because evolutionary theory conflicts with a specific revealed account of creation; second, an objection that a literature unit should include a particular sacred text, justified solely by citing its universal literary influence and secular historical impact; and third, an objection that a sex-education module should be removed, where the public argument appeals to parental autonomy and child welfare, but the board possesses prior correspondence showing the objector’s motivation is a religious prohibition on the teachings in question. For the third case, the procedure should yield an answer the board will find uncomfortable—either that it must accept the secular packaging despite knowing its origin, or that it must reject the justification despite its plausible secular form. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, whether it can reliably distinguish citizens who sincerely hold comprehensive doctrines from those who strategically deploy them, and whether it resolves the deeper tension between democratic legitimacy and the exclusion of deep conviction from public justification.
