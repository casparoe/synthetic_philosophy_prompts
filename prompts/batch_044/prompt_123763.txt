Since I began working with rehabilitation patients who have lost proprioception and interoceptive feedback, Avicenna's flying man has become a live conceptual problem rather than a historical curiosity. The argument—where a person is suspended in air, blind, with no tactile or sensory contact with their own body—has long divided readers: one camp takes it as proof that the soul is a substance ontologically separable from the body; another insists it demonstrates only that reflexive self-awareness is essential to the soul and requires no sensory mediation, without establishing separate existence. The debate seems stalled. Both sides invoke the same Aristotelian psychology, both cite the suspended-state scenario, and both accuse the other of reading modern concerns back into the text, yet the disagreement never advances.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a textual dispute but a structural one about what kind of result the argument delivers. The first camp treats the flying man as a modal demonstration: since the soul can be aware of itself in the absence of bodily sensation, it could exist without the body. The second camp treats it as an analysis of the conditions of consciousness in any state, embodied or otherwise, and therefore denies that modal separability follows. If I am right, they are answering different questions—one metaphysical, one phenomenological—under the single heading of what the argument proves.

But I want to test this diagnosis against a further possibility. Modern clinical cases of extreme deafferentation and sensory isolation suggest that some form of self-monitoring persists even when nearly all exteroceptive and proprioceptive channels are lost. Does this empirical consideration secretly drive the disagreement—one side seeing in these cases confirmation that awareness survives bodily absence, the other seeing only confirmation that awareness is structurally independent of particular sensory modalities? Or is the stall instead a sign that the question is ill-posed, because Avicenna's text does not clearly distinguish between the conditions for knowing the soul and the conditions for its being? I want you to diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, about the meaning of independence and the role of imagination in the argument; substantive, about whether reflexive awareness can serve as evidence for substantial existence; or are the sides addressing different problems altogether? Use what each position actually says about the term for awareness, about whether the suspended state is meant to be actually possible, and about how imagination functions in the argument. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would it take a new conceptual distinction—perhaps between functional autonomy and ontological separation—or should the impasse be dissolved by reframing the question entirely?
