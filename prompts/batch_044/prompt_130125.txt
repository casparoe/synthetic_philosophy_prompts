I spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring and turning, somewhat unexpectedly, to philosophy of science and archaeological theory. Next Thursday I present a paper to the university’s joint Archaeology and Philosophy of Science Colloquium—an audience of six senior archaeologists (mostly processualist, with one committed post-processualist), three philosophers of science specializing in scientific realism and historical inference, and an anthropologist of Andean material culture. They are intellectually generous but deeply wary of what they call physics-envy—the imposition of formal, law-like constraints on interpretive disciplines without clear empirical payoff. They value stratigraphic context, ethnographic analogy, and the preservation of local meaning.

My thesis is this: when archaeologists reconstruct institutional beliefs from material object assemblages, they routinely apply inference to the best explanation without a constraint I call stratigraphic inertia—the principle that any abductively inferred belief must remain compatible with the functional durability of the physical layout across successive, undisturbed occupation layers. I argue that its absence causes systematic misattribution of ritual cosmology to practical infrastructure. My central case is a radial cluster of ceramic storage vessels in a high-altitude settlement; I claim the standard inference to a boundary-ritual belief violates stratigraphic inertia, whereas an inference to climate-adaptive thermal storage respects it.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I need roughly eight objections that this specific audience will actually raise—not generic philosophy-of-science complaints—ordered by how much damage each would do to my proposal if left unanswered. Each objection should be phrased in the vocabulary and framing these interlocutors would use: the archaeologists speaking in terms of context and analogy, the philosophers in terms of underdetermination and realism, the anthropologist in terms of local ontology. For each objection, provide a concise reply I could deliver in approximately two minutes, followed by an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or most vulnerable. I also need you to identify the single objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that strikes at the heart of the stratigraphic-inertia framework itself—and to explain briefly why it is so damaging. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; I will not have access to external sources during the session, and I want the objections and replies grounded in reasoning rather than authority.
