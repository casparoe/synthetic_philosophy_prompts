As a doctoral student in environmental ethics with training in population ecology, I have watched the debate over whether large-scale habitat destruction reduces net wild-animal suffering reach a frustrating standstill. One side advances what I will call the “successional relief” argument: clearing primary forest or draining wetlands eliminates long-lived, high-intensity predator–prey dynamics and replaces them with shorter-lived pioneer communities, thereby lowering aggregate suffering per unit biomass. The opposing side counters that disturbance regimes generate novel stressors—fragmentation-induced hyperpredation, invasive-mediated disease cascades, and chronic malnutrition during transitional states—that increase suffering precisely in the unstable ecological windows that follow destruction. Both camps accept the same demographic models, agree that suffering is intrinsically bad, and cite overlapping meta-analyses on population turnover; yet for nearly a decade neither has moved, each merely reinterpreting the same data to preserve its prior framing.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but normative: the two sides are answering different questions about moral replaceability across ecological states, masked by shared talk of “net welfare” and “ecosystem health.” But I want a committed diagnosis from you, not a survey. Is there a hidden factual disagreement—perhaps about trophic energy-transfer efficiency, interspecific competition intensity, or the temporal distribution of suffering across successional stages—that neither camp has made explicit? Or is this a disagreement about what counts as a moral reason, specifically whether the value of an ecological state is reducible to the summed welfare of its current occupants, versus whether there is a non-aggregative constraint against engineering suffering-reduction through destructive replacement? Or are we facing bedrock intuitions about naturalness and human non-interference that function as unstated stopping points?

Please take a definite position: identify the precise kind of disagreement this is, defend that identification with explicit reference to how each side treats replacement, temporal discounting of transitional lives, and the moral status of pioneer-community suffering, and tell me honestly—given your diagnosis—whether any empirical finding, formal welfare-accounting model, or conceptual distinction could actually resolve the dispute, or whether the framing question itself is ill-posed and should be abandoned for a different one.
