I'm delivering the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Consciousness, Physics, and Non-Dualism," hosted by a university philosophy department. The audience is split evenly between physicists and philosophers of physics who are familiar with Schrödinger's essays on consciousness, and scholars of comparative philosophy and cognitive science who know Advaita Vedanta well but have little background in quantum mechanics. I want the speech to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration: frame the entire workshop around Schrödinger's specific claim that the "pluralization of consciousness" is an illusion parallel to quantum mechanics' refusal of localized, separable individuality, and take a definite, argued stance on whether this Vedantic reading is a defensible metaphysical interpretation of the formalism or an unsupportable mystical add-on. Your stance must determine what the workshop can legitimately discuss—if you defend the interpretation, explicitly rule out first-person phenomenological reports as the central problem and mandate that discussion focus on metaphysical individuation; if you reject it, do the opposite and explain why that framing fails. Write so both halves of the room grasp the stakes without feeling lectured. Do not include citations, bibliographies, page references, or reading lists; refer to positions and arguments by name only. At the end, append a brief self-critical note identifying exactly where your interpretation takes liberties with contested readings of Schrödinger's texts or with standard Vedantic exegesis, and flag any specific claims that rest on reconstruction rather than on widely shared knowledge of the texts. Be fully committed: do not present both sides as equally viable; argue for your chosen framing and give your reasons for dismissing the alternative.
