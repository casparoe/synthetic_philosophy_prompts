Design a runnable classroom simulation for a philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar of about 14 students, running for 85 minutes. The goal is to make participants feel, before any theoretical framing is introduced, the tension between pain asymbolia—cases in which subjects report nociceptive experience yet display little or no aversive concern, distress, or protective behavior—and the philosophical thesis that pain is bad in virtue of its intrinsic felt badness. To keep the prompt away from well-worn textbook summaries, inject the following somewhat niche conceptual framing: treat the disconnect in asymbolia not as a simple absence of emotion, but as a functional dissociation between nociceptive labeling (the capacity to identify and report tissue-related signals) and aversive-motivation tagging (the assignment of negative valence that drives avoidance and care-seeking). The central question the activity should stage is whether this dissociation undermines a unitary evaluative-content theory of pain’s badness, or instead reveals two independent dimensions—sensory intensity and affective valence—that can come apart in ways that force us to revise what “badness” tracks.

The deliverable must be a structured simulation with assigned roles, constrained collective choices, and small non-monetary stakes (for example, collective points that determine which text the group reads in the next session). Arrange the rules so that participants run into the problem themselves: some must allocate limited “care resources” based on verbal reports that lack behavioral corroboration, others must respond to overt protective behavior that lacks any corresponding first-person report, and the group as a whole must vote on a criterion for “genuine suffering” before they know what concept they are modeling. Only after the decision round should the facilitator name asymbolia and map the experience onto the dissociation.

Debrief: Mapping Experience to Concepts (approx. 250 words): connect specific moments in the simulation to nociceptive labeling versus aversive-motivation tagging, address the unitary badness thesis, and be explicit about which philosophical feature—such as the normative authority of first-person reports versus third-person behavior—the activity can reproduce, and which feature—such as the chronic, long-term absence of protective motivation observed in clinical asymbolia—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
5. Honest Limitation (approx. 120 words): state frankly which aspect of the philosophical debate about pain’s badness the activity fails to capture.
