In debates over intellectual property and the ethics of attribution, I have noticed a recurring pattern for which I lack a precise label. The pattern arises when interlocutors reject the claim that ideas can be exclusively possessed—on the familiar ground that they are non-rivalrous, so that copying them does not deprive the originator—yet continue to describe unauthorized use in the language of boundary-violation rather than mere unfairness: they speak of trespassing on a conceptual domain, of encroaching on another's intellectual territory, or of failing to respect a boundary that separates one thinker's contribution from another's. The wrongness at issue does not seem to lie in the taking away of an object, since nothing is taken away; rather, it seems to lie in a kind of misplacement—using an idea without situating it in relation to its origin, as though one had entered a space without acknowledging whose ground it was.

Consider, first, the norms governing academic plagiarism. Even those who deny that scholars can own ideas in any proprietary sense will treat unattributed reproduction as a serious moral breach. The harm is not that the original author has lost access to her idea—she retains it entirely—but that the copyist has disrupted the correct mapping between idea and originator, as if the idea's location in an intellectual landscape had been misassigned.

Consider, second, the rhetoric of patent infringement. A patent is sometimes described not merely as a temporary monopoly over a practical application, but as having staked out a region of conceptual space. The infringement is then characterized not only as economic harm, but as an unauthorized presence within a bounded domain—an occupation rather than a theft.

Does this territorial or sovereignty-based model of idea-ownership have an established name in philosophy of law or intellectual property theory? I am aware of the Lockean labor-mixing account and the Hegelian personality-based justification, but these both treat ideas or their expressions as objects to which rights attach through creation or labor; they do not capture the specific feature I am describing, where the idea is not possessed as an object but delimited as a region, and where the violation consists in a failure of attribution or boundary-respect rather than in deprivation. Is this merely the moral-rights doctrine of attribution and integrity, or is there a distinct conceptual framework—perhaps analogous to territorial sovereignty rather than property ownership—that captures this boundary-logic? If the literature offers no specific term, how should we distinguish it from both standard property theories and from the doctrine of moral rights, and what would be the appropriate name and defining conditions for the phenomenon? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know exactly where the edges of this thing lie, what it is not, and whether it merits independent conceptual status.
