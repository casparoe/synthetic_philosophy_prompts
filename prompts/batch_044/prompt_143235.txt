As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I keep running into an argument that uses Gödel’s incompleteness theorems to claim that human minds are not computers. The analogy, as it is usually presented, works like this: take a fixed formal system—say, a specific proof-checker or computer program with a definite, static set of axioms and inference rules. Within that system one can construct a sentence that effectively says “this statement is not provable in this system.” If the system is consistent, the sentence is true but unprovable inside it. The anti-mechanist then maps this onto the human mathematician: the human can understand the Gödel sentence for their own mental “system,” recognize its truth, and thereby reach a conclusion that the formal system itself cannot reach. From this, they infer that human reasoning transcends any fixed algorithm and therefore that the mind is not a computer.

I want a rigorous audit of this analogy, because as an engineer I am suspicious that it treats computation as more rigid than it actually is. Specifically:

- Lay out the explicit correspondence: what in the source domain (the formal system, its syntax, its consistency condition, the Gödel sentence, the act of “seeing” its truth) maps onto what in the target domain (the human mind, its beliefs, its reasoning architecture)?
- Identify the breaking points. For example, does the analogy treat “the mind” as a single formal system with one fixed axiomatization, and is that accurate given that human cognition updates, learns, and operates on hardware capable of self-modification? How do actual computing phenomena—self-modifying code, meta-level reflection, iterative deepening, or distributed verification—affect the comparison?
- Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the anti-mechanist conclusion from those that are merely technical differences without philosophical weight.
- Does the analogy quietly import features specific to formal provability—such as the need for precisely defined syntax, a consistency requirement, or limitation to a single non-expanding axiom set—that do not apply to biological cognition? Which of these imports matter for the argument?
- If the analogy collapses, is there a better comparison for relating human mathematical insight to computational processes—perhaps involving reflective oracles, architecture-changing programs, or networked verification—that captures relevant capabilities without overstating them?
- Finally, does the conclusion that “no computer can do what a human does with Gödel’s result” survive once we distinguish between a particular formal system F and the broader class of all possible computing processes, including those that alter their own rules?

Please treat this as a conceptual audit rather than a historical survey. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and I want the analysis accessible without searching for specific texts. Focus on whether the comparison holds, what it silently assumes about computation, and what—if anything—it actually licenses about the nature of mind.
