As a journalist who writes about philosophy and the history of ideas, I keep returning to Kierkegaard’s figure of Abraham and the so-called teleological suspension of the ethical—the moment when a universal moral law is set aside not by a competing universal, but by a singular relation to an absolute telos. I want to know whether there is a precise principle that distinguishes authentic instances of this structure from cases that merely borrow its language.

Here are my verdicts on five concrete situations.

1. Abraham, commanded to sacrifice Isaac: the universal prohibition on killing one’s child is suspended by a direct, singular call that addresses Abraham alone. The ethical is not abolished; it is put in abeyance, and it returns intact when the ram is provided. Verdict: this is the paradigm case.

2. A surgeon who secretly forges consent to enroll a dying patient in an experimental protocol, convinced that saving this particular life requires violating the universal norm of informed consent. The surgeon appeals to no general rule, only to a singular conviction about this patient. Verdict: structurally close to Abraham, yet intuitively wrong—this is not a genuine suspension but an overreach of private judgment.

3. A whistleblower who breaks a legal confidentiality agreement to expose widespread corruption, arguing that a universal duty of justice outweighs a particular promise. Verdict: this is not a suspension at all; it is a conflict between two universal norms, resolved by ranking them.

4. A parent who breaks a solemn promise to a dying child—“I will stay with you until the end”—to rush to save a second child who has suddenly fallen into a life-threatening situation. Verdict: the particular ethical bond of the promise is genuinely suspended, but by another ethical claim (the duty to save), not by a non-ethical telos. It feels different from Abraham, yet it is not mere consequentialism.

5. A revolutionary who kills an innocent bystander as necessary collateral damage in an attack meant to overthrow a tyrannical regime, claiming that the teleological end of liberation suspends the prohibition on murder. Verdict: false suspension. This is consequentialist reasoning dressed in singular language; the ethical is not suspended but replaced by a calculus.

My tentative principle is this: a genuine teleological suspension occurs only when (i) the suspended norm is genuinely universal, not merely a particular promise or contract; (ii) the suspending reason is singular and non-substitutable—it addresses this agent and this case specifically, not any agent facing a structurally similar choice; (iii) the suspension does not invoke a competing universal norm, nor a general utility calculation; and (iv) the suspended norm remains intact and returns after the act, rather than being permanently overridden.

This principle seems to save case 1 and distinguish cases 3 and 5, but it struggles with case 2 (why does singular conviction fail there?) and with case 4 (why is suspending a particular promise not a suspension?). I need it repaired, or replaced.

Can you state a precise principle—or a small competing set of candidates—that captures all five verdicts, identifying for each clause which case motivates it? If no single principle works, say which verdict must be overturned and why. Also apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a soldier who receives an unlawful order to participate in the destruction of a civilian village, refuses on the grounds of a singular, non-generalizable conviction of conscience, and is court-martialed for disobedience. Does your principle classify his refusal as a teleological suspension, as an ethical ranking, or as something else? And please state honestly which of my original five verdicts your account cannot accommodate.
