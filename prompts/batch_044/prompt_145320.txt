As a novelist currently drafting both a philosophical essay and a novel about a cartographer who gradually realizes her maps are altering the terrain she surveys, I have been developing notes for an essay that extends the standard debate on cognitive penetration of perception into what I call counterfactual perceptual framing. The central claim is that a perceiver's active representation of unrealized but structurally available alternatives—not merely beliefs or emotional states—can restructure the attentional boundaries of present sensory experience in ways that are neither purely bottom-up nor reducible to post-perceptual judgment. I work from the familiar distinction between bare seeing and seeing-as, but I want to push further: if a subject genuinely entertains two incompatible narrative trajectories for the same scene—say, a conversation that might end in reconciliation or in betrayal—the sensory salience of the interlocutor's micro-expressions is not merely interpreted differently; rather, the spatial and temporal segmentation of those expressions is reorganized so that different features become perceptually available or suppressed, analogous to the way shifting between Euclidean and projective geometry alters which lines appear parallel.

My notes sketch an argument that this form of penetration is best understood not as contamination of a neutral perceptual base, but as a restructuring of what I term the perceptual grammar through which a scene is parsed—an idea I have developed without invoking specific neuroscientific findings I cannot independently verify. I am writing to ask for your comments on these notes, particularly on whether the distinction between interpretive coloring and genuine perceptual restructuring holds up, and whether the geometric analogy risks being merely metaphorical rather than conceptually rigorous. I am especially concerned that my notion of structurally available alternatives may collapse back into ordinary belief-desire psychology, thereby losing the specificity that makes cognitive penetration philosophically interesting. Because I intend the essay for literary readers who are not specialists in philosophy of mind, I want it to remain conceptually serious without becoming a literature review. Could you comment on the coherence of the central argument, identify where the reasoning seems weakest or most speculative, and point out any conceptual confusions—particularly regarding the boundary between perception and cognition—that I should address more carefully before developing the novel's parallel narrative structure?
