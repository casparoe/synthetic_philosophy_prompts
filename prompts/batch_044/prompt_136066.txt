I'm putting together the final for my junior-level ethics course and I'm tired of students just listing Franklin's thirteen virtues and calling it a day. I want one essay question that forces them to actually think about whether his moral perfection scheme works as a method, not just as autobiography. The question should center on this problem: Franklin rotates through virtues one per week, tracking them in his little grid-book, but if virtues are interdependent — like you can't really be just without temperance, or silent without humility — then working on them seperately might be conceptually confused. Students should have to discuss whether Franklin's own struggles with Order and his admision that he never achieved perfection undermine the project or just show it's long-term practice. I also want them to consider the modern psychological worry about moral self-licensing: does improving one virtue in a given week actually make you feel entitled to neglect the others? The model answer should be roughly 500 words, should defend Franklin's piecemeal habit-formation approach against the interdependence objection, and should explicitly use Franklin's tracking method as part of the defense rather than just praising his work ethic. Please also provide a marking scheme with 25 marks total, broken into specific moves that an A answer must contain — for example, articulating the interdependence problem, referencing Franklin's difficulty with Order, explaining why licensing doesn't necessarily defeat rotation, and distinguishing Franklin's view from Aristotelian hexis — plus a one-line note on how B answers typically miss each point. The exam is 45 minutes, open notes but no internet, so please don't include any citation requests or ask students to reference specific scholars they wouldn't have on hand.
