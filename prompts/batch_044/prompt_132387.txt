In my philosophy of ethics seminar, we have been working through Benatar's asymmetry thesis: the claim that bringing suffering into existence is always bad for the one who suffers, whereas failing to bring pleasure into existence is not bad for anyone, since there is no subject to be deprived. Rather than rehearse its standard applications to reproductive ethics, I want to examine how the asymmetry behaves when translated into a context that is not biological reproduction but deliberate engineering — specifically, the design of synthetic architectures that might be configured to have a determinate experiential valence, but whose status as moral subjects remains philosophically unsettled.

Please describe the most important implications of Benatar's asymmetry for the ethics of creating such engineered experiential systems. If the asymmetry holds, does it entail that we have a strong reason not to create negatively-valenced artificial consciousnesses, but no corresponding reason to create positively-valenced ones? How does the argument handle the absence of a pre-existing potential subject in the design case, where our choice is not whether to bring a specific future person into being, but whether to actualize one architecture rather than another from a space of possible configurations? Does the non-identity problem apply differently here, and if so, does that weaken or strengthen the asymmetry's normative force? I am particularly interested in whether the asymmetry can generate an asymmetrical risk calculus for technological development: should we treat the accidental emergence of suffering-capable synthetic minds as a grave moral failure, while treating the failure to develop happiness-capable ones as morally neutral?
