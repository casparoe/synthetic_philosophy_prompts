I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of language and institutional ontology currently advising a municipal heritage commission that is designing a tax-exemption program for historically significant building facades. The commission needs a binary determination—eligible or not—because the tax code cannot accommodate degrees of qualification. To minimize subjective judgment, they propose adopting a quantitative criterion assessed by a computer-vision model: a facade must retain at least sixty-five percent of its original pre-1940 exterior masonry, with the model outputting a continuous integrity score for each property. Walking through the case files, the difficulty is immediate. If sixty-five percent is the cutoff, then a facade at sixty-four point nine percent is excluded despite being materially indistinguishable from one just above the line; lowering the threshold to sixty percent merely relocates the arbitrariness, and eventually the program would include structures that no preservation expert regards as heritage-worthy. Compounding this is a second layer of continuity: the vision model produces confidence intervals with overlapping margins of error around any proposed boundary, so the measurement instrument itself lacks sharp edges. The commission chair insists that delegating the cutoff to an algorithm eliminates human arbitrariness and therefore dissolves the philosophical problem entirely, treating procedural objectivity as if it were metaphysical determinacy.

My request is not about model calibration, legal drafting, or preservation policy. I want to understand how the philosophical problem of drawing precise boundaries across genuinely gradual properties applies to this institutional design case, especially when both the property being classified and the instrument measuring it are continuous. Does any philosophically adequate account of such properties—whether the difficulty is interpreted as semantic indecision, epistemic limitation, or ontological indeterminacy—actually license the belief that an algorithmic boundary resolves the underlying issue? Or does the commission's commitment to a sharp administrative rule, combined with their reliance on a continuous measurement procedure, implicitly force them into a specific metaphysical stance about heritage status—perhaps one that requires denying there is any fact of the matter near the boundary? Furthermore, is there a coherent way to defend binary tax eligibility without pretending that the sixty-five percent threshold tracks a real discontinuity in the world, or must any practical decision-procedure in this domain either embrace arbitrariness or abandon the binary framework altogether?
