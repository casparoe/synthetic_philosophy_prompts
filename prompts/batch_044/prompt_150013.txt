My study group cooked up this argument about Ranke vs Carr and Hayden White and now I'm stuck. (1) A historical claim is objective only if its truth doesn't depend on the historian's narrative choices. (2) Every history selects events and links them with causal or thematic threads that aren't literally in the archieve — the archieve just has traces, not stories. (3) So the truth of any claim like "the economic crisis caused the uprising" depends on which events the historian groups together, and if they hadn't grouped them that way the claim might lack its truth-maker altogether. (4) Therefore no historical claim has truth-conditions independant of the historian. (5) Ranke wanted history how it actually was, which needs exactly that independance. (6) Carr and White say narrative form is unavoidable, so they agree independance is impossible. (7) But if independance is impossible, then there's no real difference between saying "the crisis caused the uprising" and saying "the uprising caused the crisis" — it's all just narrative taste. (8) That's absurd; we clearly think one is wrong and one is right. (9) So the argument must fail somewhere between (1) and (6). I really want to keep (1) and (8), but I can't see the escape. Which step do I reject, and what's the cost? Is there an equivocation hiding in "depends on"?
