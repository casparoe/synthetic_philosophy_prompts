I've been trying to stabilize a specific package of views about phenomenal consciousness and I want to check whether it actually hangs together or whether I'm smuggling in an inconsistency I haven't noticed. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept illusionism in the standard sense: there are no irreducible qualitative properties, and first-person reports of "what it's like" are systematically misrepresentative outputs of introspective machinery rather than direct detections.

(2) I reject the phenomenal concepts strategy not because it rescues realism, but because it posits special conceptual resources for properties that don't exist, which violates the parsimony that makes illusionism attractive in the first place.

(3) I think introspection is broadly reliable for propositional attitudes, emotional states, and even the occurrence of mental events, but I hold that it is specifically and systematically unreliable when it targets sensory-phenomenal properties—precisely because those properties are absent and the mechanism is confabulating content.

(4) I want to maintain a real distinction between cognitive phenomenology—there being something it is like to entertain a specific inference or deploy a particular concept—and sensory phenomenology, accepting the former while denying the latter.

(5) I also think any adequate science of consciousness must preserve first-person authority in some methodological form, since without the reports there is no explanandum; but I restrict that authority to judgments about belief, attention, and cognitive architecture, not to purported qualitative character.

(6) To be specific about the mechanism, I think the illusion of sensory phenomenology arises from predictive-coding architectures misinterpreting precision-weight signals in sensory hierarchies as properties of experience rather than as confidence-level indicators about perceptual inference—so the "feel" is actually a misread metacognitive signal about the reliability of a prediction error.

The tensions I'm worried about are these. First, (3) looks like an ad hoc carve-out: if introspection is a generally well-functioning faculty, why should it fail exactly and only at the point where illusionism needs it to fail, and not elsewhere? Second, (4) introduces a bifurcation that seems unstable—if cognitive phenomenology is real and involves predictive processing of thoughts, what principled barrier prevents sensory phenomenology from being reconstructed as just another species of cognitive phenomenology about sensory states? Third, (5) seems to pull against (1): if first-person authority is necessary for the science, and our strongest intuitive data include vivid reports of qualitative character, then denying those reports looks less like explaining the data and more like discarding it. Fourth, (6) might actually dissolve (3) rather than support it—if the mechanism is misinterpreting a confidence signal, then introspection is not failing to detect something; it is correctly detecting a signal but attributing it to the wrong ontological category, which changes what "unreliable" even means.

But I suspect at least one of these is only apparent. For instance, (2) and (5) might look incompatible—if I reject special phenomenal concepts, how can I preserve a special first-person authority?—but perhaps the authority is methodological (we need the reports to study) rather than metaphysical (the reports reveal special properties).

Can you audit this package? Order the tensions by severity, identify which pairs genuinely conflict and which only seem to, say what the cheapest restoration of consistency costs, and flag what I would have to give up in each case. Don't lecture me on illusionism generally; treat my specific commitment set as the object.
