Write the desiderata that a theory of historical explanation must meet to handle genuinely overdetermined outcomes—situations where multiple distinct factors each appear sufficient for a result, yet no single factor can be isolated as “the” cause—without collapsing into causal pluralism that assigns equal weight to everything, or into reductionism that arbitrarily privileges one factor. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or conceptual pressure: specifically, distinguish true overdetermination (simultaneous sufficient causes) from causal redundancy (where one cause renders another inert) and from what we might call “interference” cases, where two causes present together produce an outcome that neither would have produced in isolation. Include the requirement that the theory preserve counterfactual stability—if factor A had been absent, B should still have yielded the outcome in genuine overdetermination, but not in interference cases—and that it respect explanatory proportionality, so that the complexity of the explanation does not outstrip the complexity of the outcome. Flag which of these are non-negotiable for any adequate account, and which reflect camp-specific preferences (for example, structuralist commitments to deep causes versus intentionalist commitments to agent-level reasons). Indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable under standard counterfactual theories of causation, and identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding popular historiographical accounts. Keep the evaluation of existing approaches brief; the criteria themselves are the deliverable. Finally, say what would change your mind: which finding about causal interaction in historical cases, or which argument about counterfactual dependence, would lead you to drop or revise one of these requirements, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
