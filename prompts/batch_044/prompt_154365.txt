Act like a speculative fiction writer who actually studied philosophy of mind—before you start writing, take a minute to brainstorm two or three weird edge cases about where consciousness stops and starts in animals. Think carefuly about which one would make a character genuinely uncomfortable, not just confused.

Then write a short story, about 600 words, centered on a researcher named Dr. Mara Voss who is testing her homemade framework called "trophic phenomenal coupling" at a marine station. The idea is that consciousness might be partly distributed across tightly coupled neural dynamics between predator and prey—not inside one skull, but spread across the interaction loop over evolutionary time. Show her observing one concrete scene: maybe an octopus tracking a school of fish through coral, changing colors as it predicts their movements. She has to decide whether this loop counts as a single minimal subject or just two correlated minds. Give her a colleague who pushes back hard, but dont make either of them a strawman—let both points land. Use sensory detail: salt air, the hum of the tanks, the way light hits the water. Let Mara doubt her own instruments.

In it, explain what conceptual problem your "trophic phenomenal coupling" idea is meant to poke at—like the boundary problem for distributed consciousness, or the difficulty of attributing unified experience without a central nervous system. Dont cite papers or authors; just describe the tension in plain language. Also briefly say why you picked that octopus-and-fish interaction instead of, say, a hive of bees.

Keep it casual—no need to sound like a journal article. And please, no bibliography or reading list; this should work for someone without web access.
