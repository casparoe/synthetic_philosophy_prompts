I am writing a paper for my seminar on moral philosophy and the ethics of aggregation, where we have been examining the claim that in rescue cases the number of people at risk should not determine the strength of our obligation to aid them. I want to defend an extension of this anti-aggregationist view into the design of democratic institutions. Specifically, please argue that the moral logic underlying the rejection of counting numbers in rescue cases—namely, that each person's claim to assistance is not diluted by the presence of others with parallel claims—should govern democratic deliberation about climate-adaptation funding. Make the case that when a democratic body chooses between one large infrastructure project that saves a large, predictable number of lives and several smaller projects that collectively save more lives but each save fewer, the legitimate decision procedure should treat every affected community as bearing an equal, non-aggregable claim, so that selecting the smaller-project option is morally required even though it results in fewer total lives saved. In developing this argument, address the objection that democratic legitimacy depends precisely on aggregating individual preferences into collective choices, and explain why extending the anti-aggregationist insight to institutional procedure does not collapse into arbitrary or unaccountable decision-making.
