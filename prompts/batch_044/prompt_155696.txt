For a metaphysics seminar of about twelve graduate students with ninety minutes, design a structured game that makes the collapse of the simple conditional analysis of dispositions feel unavoidable through play rather than lecture. The core idea is that capacities—individual and collective—can be genuinely present yet systematically blocked or triggered away by hidden conditions unknown to observers and sometimes to the bearer. To keep this from being a standard rehearsal of classic fink and antidote cases, inject a collective-level twist: assign participants both overt group roles (e.g., mediator, skeptic, builder, recorder) and hidden individual modifiers—some are "masks" that suppress their role's usual manifestation, others are "finks" that remove an underlying capacity the instant it is about to be exercised. The group must complete a concrete cooperative task, such as arranging a logical or physical puzzle, but the central stakes depend on a parallel prediction protocol: before acting, observers (rotating among them) must predict which capacities will manifest based only on visible role assignments, scoring points for correct forecasts and losing points for false positives. Because the hidden modifiers are distributed without disclosure, predictions will fail repeatedly despite accurate identification of roles, and the group will experience the gap between possessing a property and displaying its associated behavior at the collective level—e.g., "this committee can reach consensus" being genuinely present yet masked by a hidden individual fink. Include precise rules for role and modifier assignment, the task structure, the prediction and scoring mechanics, a timeline with phases, and a debrief that maps moments of predictive failure onto the structure of finks and masks, extends the analysis to group-level dispositions, and explains what this suggests about dispositional contextualism. Be explicit about which feature of the metaphysical view the activity still cannot reproduce—such as the cross-world modal stability of the underlying disposition—and how the facilitator must supply that. Also predict what will go wrong in the first run—likely confusion between task failure and predictive failure, or participants treating hidden roles as mere deception rather than metaphysical interference—and say exactly how to manage it. The idea should emerge from the rules and scores before any technical term is named.
