I'm currently working through the tension between virtue ethics and situationist moral psychology in a seminar on character, and rather than committing to a theoretical camp I've been trying to derive my actual position from my unreflective judgments about concrete cases. I find myself drawn to verdicts that seem hard to square with either a pure global-trait view or a pure situationist denial of character altogether. Here are the judgments I keep returning to, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps involving domain-specificity, institutional framing, or narrative self-constitution—that can accommodate them, or whether I must abandon at least one.

First, Maria is meticulously honest in her accounting practice—she never misstates figures—yet she routinely embellishes her outdoor adventures to friends. I still want to say she possesses the virtue of honesty, but only within a professional-epistemic domain; I do not treat her social exaggeration as disproving the trait. Second, David is genuinely courageous in physical emergencies—he has rescued people from burning buildings—but in a structured organizational-ethics scenario he remains silent about misconduct because the social cost is high. I think he has physical courage without institutional moral courage, and I resist collapsing the two into a single global disposition. Third, imagine a controlled study in which participants are randomly assigned the temporary role of "fairness monitor" in a collaborative task; those assigned the role enforce equitable distributions with high consistency across changing group compositions, while those without the assignment show virtually none. My intuition is that the role does not merely elicit a pre-existing trait but temporarily constitutes one, suggesting that some virtues are framing-dependent rather than latent. Fourth, Professor Chen demonstrates profound intellectual humility in her scholarship—she revises her theories in response to counterevidence—but she is rigid and dismissive in departmental governance. I want to say she has the virtue in her epistemic practice yet lacks it in her administrative self, though I am unsure whether this is a domain split or a failure of integration. Fifth, my acquaintance Leo lies indiscriminately about taxes, feelings, and excuses, with no discernible pattern tied to context or pressure; here I have no hesitation in saying he simply lacks honesty. Sixth, and most puzzling to me, consider two people with statistically identical behavioral profiles across twenty diverse helping situations—each assists strangers roughly sixty percent of the time. One weaves these actions into a coherent self-narrative of "trying to be kind," while the other regards them as situationally caused accidents. I feel the first genuinely possesses kindness in a way the second does not, despite the behavioral equivalence.

These verdicts pull in different directions: domain-specificity for Maria and Chen, framing-dependence for the monitor study, absence for Leo, and narrative constitution for the final pair. Is there a unified account—say, one that treats virtues as locally stable dispositions that require both cross-situational coherence within a domain and a first-personal narrative endorsement—that can preserve all six judgments? If so, apply it to a case I have not considered: an individual who displays consistent compassion only within an immersive virtual environment, caring meticulously for digital entities, but shows no comparable behavior in physical-world interactions. If no single principle saves every verdict, which one should I drop, and what does that imply for whether situationism or virtue ethics is closer to the truth?
