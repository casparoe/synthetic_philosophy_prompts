Maren opened by arguing that platforms reproduce the exact structure Kierkegaard diagnosed: an abstract, disembodied audience that solicits opinions without demanding commitment or action, substituting reflective detachment for passionate engagement. Jonas replied that this overlooks the defining structural feature of algorithms, which is not universal address but personalized fragmentation; rather than creating a single anonymous crowd, they dissolve the shared public altogether by delivering different realities to each user. Maren countered that fragmentation is not an escape from leveling but its most advanced form—Kierkegaard's concern was never uniform content but the reduction of qualitative individual differences to quantitative commensurability, and algorithmic profiling does precisely this by translating a person's taste into statistical vectors. Jonas pushed back that this stretches the concept beyond usefulness: if any systematic treatment of people counts as leveling, then the term loses its specific critical force. Maren answered that the concept can be refined without being emptied, by distinguishing leveling-through-universalization, characteristic of the nineteenth-century press, from leveling-through-hyper-individualized-abstraction, characteristic of the feed; both dissolve the concrete ethical self, but through opposite mechanisms. Jonas conceded that both systems avoid genuine commitment, but maintained that the mechanisms differ enough that applying Kierkegaard's specific critique without substantial modification is anachronistic.
