I'm running a philosophy of knowledge seminar next term, about 12 grad studnets, 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them actually feel the tension between conciliationism and steadfastness, not just talk about it. Here’s the twist: I think the standard framing treats peer disagreement as symmetric, but in real life people often disagree about whether they’re even peers—what I’m calling second-order peer disagreement, where you and I disagree not just on P, but on whether our disagreement on P is epistemically symmetric at all. So design a structured deliberation or game where participants have to reach a collective decision—say, allocating a small real budget or ranking a list of proposals—while holding private "credential cards" that indicate their actual expertise/background on the topic, but they can only reveal partial information. The rules should push them toward either splitting-the-difference (conciliation) or holding firm (steadfastness), but the hidden structure should make it so that those who conciliate end up adopting positions that violate their private credentials, while those who stay steadfast trigger a cascade where the group fractures. They shouldn’t hear the terms conciliationism or steadfastness until the debrief.

Then map out: what moments in the game correspond to the first-order disagreement, the second-order disagreement about peerhood, the conciliationist move, and the steadfast response? Be honest about what this can’t reproduce—probably the long-term rational revision of confidence over time, or the pure introspective access to one's own reasoning. Also, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this (someone will game the credential cards, someone will dominate the room) and how to handle it. No lecture up front, and please don’t ask them to read anything beforehand. I was talking with a friend about this and she said it sounds like a prisoner's dilemma with beliefs, so maybe lean into payoff rules if that helps.
