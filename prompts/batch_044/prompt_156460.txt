I'm giving opening remarks tomorrow for a workshop we're running on refusal ethics — basically the overlap between old-school civil disobedience and bureaucrats pushing back on orders they think are unconstitutional or immoral. The audience is split: philosophy grad students and poli-sci people who know the Rawlsian arguments inside out, and actual civil servants and lawyers who've just lived through real-world administrative refusal cases and don't want a taxomony lesson. I want to frame the whole weekend around a tension that gets lost when we treat political obligation like one big on/off switch. Most literature asks whether citizens owe obedience to an unjust state, but institutional refusal isn't usually an absence of obligation; it's a claim that one layered obligation (to the constitution, to the office, to the public) outweighs another (to the current chain of command). So bureaucratic pushback isn't just "civil disobedience with a desk job," but it's not pure anarchism either, and that structural difference should shape everything we talk about.

The remarks need to land for both halves without turning into a lecture. The philosophers should hear that I'm not collapsing the citizen/state distinction, and the practitioners should feel I'm not reducing their lived experience to an applied ethics case study. Because I'm treating obligation as modular rather than monolithic, I can't celebrate law-breaking uncritically, and I also can't pretend that marching in the streets and ignoring a directive are morally identical — but I also can't pretend there's just one simple duty to the state that answers both cases.
