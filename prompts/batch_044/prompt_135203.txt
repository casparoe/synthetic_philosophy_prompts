I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on normative authority and idealized agency. I'm trying to isolate a conceptual tension in accounts of indirect normativity—specifically, an ambiguity in what "idealized deliberation" is meant to preserve.

Standard treatments present the model roughly as follows: an actual agent, constrained by ignorance, bias, or partial commitment, consults what a fully rational counterpart—possessing complete information, coherence, and absence of distorting preferences—would prescribe, and derives normative force indirectly from that prescription. But I think this glosses over a distinction between procedural and substantive idealization. In procedural idealization, the counterpart retains the agent's actual practical identity—her values, commitments, and constitutive attachments—while correcting only her reasoning processes. In substantive idealization, only the formal structure of rational agency is preserved; the counterpart may hold values or priorities that the actual agent would reject even under conditions of full reflection, because the idealization strips away not merely error but the particularities that constitute her practical self.

The conceptual issue I want clarified is whether indirect normativity can survive a shift from procedural to substantive idealization. If the deliberating counterpart is not "me, corrected," but rather "a rational agent in my structural position," then the normative prescription no longer derives from my practical identity refined; it derives from a different source altogether. Does this collapse indirect normativity into direct universalizability? Or does it reveal that indirect normativity was always dependent on a hidden thesis about the preservation of practical identity through idealization?

Please explain this issue with precision: what must be settled conceptually for the indirect link to hold, whether partial or conflicted commitments can survive any non-trivial idealization without being replaced rather than refined, and where philosophers of practical reason genuinely disagree on these points versus where there is broad agreement. Separate clearly what is conceptually settled from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on idealization and practical reason, and do not present any contested interpretation—particularly about whether substantive idealization breaks the normative link or transforms it into something else—as the consensus view.
