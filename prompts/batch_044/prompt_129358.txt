Write a short story set in a near-future autonomous settlement that has adopted an economic constitution explicitly modeled on Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia (household management and natural acquisition) and chrematistike (wealth-getting for its own sake, unnatural acquisition). In this settlement, the medium of exchange is a deliberately engineered perishable token—perhaps a ceramic chip embedded with a biodegradable seal that crumbles after thirty days—so that hoarding is physically impossible and money serves only as a measure and medium of exchange, not as a store of value. Center the narrative on a protagonist who serves as a steward of the token-minting guild and who accidentally discovers a preservation technique allowing the seals to remain intact indefinitely. Rather than focusing on conventional greed, explore how this discovery introduces the logical possibility of chrematistike into a system designed to prevent it: the social rupture should arise not merely from accumulation but from the transformation of exchange relationships from reciprocal and bounded to open-ended and accumulative. Include a scene in which an elder invokes the classical Aristotelian argument that currency was invented to facilitate exchange, yet that earning profit from money itself—what Aristotle called tokos—is the most despised form of acquisition because it makes currency breed more currency. Have the elder apply this reasoning directly to the protagonist’s preservation method, treating it not as theft but as the institutionalization of interest. The story should end ambiguously, without resolving whether the settlement ought to destroy the preservation knowledge, regulate it, or permit a new economic form to emerge, so that the tension between natural and unnatural acquisition remains structurally open. Do not include bibliographic references, footnotes, or direct quotations from historical texts; paraphrase the conceptual arguments in the characters’ own voices. The story should be self-contained and intelligible to a reader familiar with basic Aristotelian ethics, without requiring specialist knowledge of Hellenistic economic history.
