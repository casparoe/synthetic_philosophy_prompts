I retired several years ago from experimental physics and have since been working through population ethics without publication deadlines. I am trying to reach reflective equilibrium on what I actually think about bringing people into existence, and I need help seeing whether my intuitions hang together or whether some must be revised.

Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios. First, learning through prenatal screening that a fetus will suffer from a condition involving constant severe pain, and terminating the pregnancy: that is not merely permissible but, to my mind, close to obligatory. Second, a well-resourced couple with every reason to expect a happy child deciding nevertheless to remain childless: this strikes me as entirely permissible, with no moral failing whatsoever. Third, knowingly conceiving a child who will suffer severely: clearly wrong. Fourth, a couple who forgo conception specifically to redirect substantial lifelong resources toward reducing severe suffering in existing populations—wild-animal suffering, climate adaptation—despite knowing that their own child would likely have led a happy life: I find this admirable, not blameworthy. Fifth, a couple using embryo selection who reject an embryo predicted to have a life of steady moderate contentment in favor of one with a high-variance prospect—either exceptional flourishing or severe suffering—solely because expected welfare is higher: here I oscillate. Part of me thinks that exposing a potential person to severe suffering for the chance of exceptional good is precisely the gamble the asymmetry should forbid; another part thinks that rejecting high-variance prospects categorically treats potential happiness as too cheap and potential suffering as the only relevant factor.

The case I keep returning to combines the logic of the fourth and fifth. Imagine a couple in a period of high geopolitical and climatic instability who consider conceiving. They recognize that any child might suffer gravely from resource scarcity or conflict, but might also contribute to mitigation in ways that prevent far greater suffering elsewhere. I keep going back and forth on whether the possibility of that exceptional contribution can justify running the risk of severe suffering, or whether the asymmetry permits us to weigh only the downside for the potential child, leaving any upside for others to be counted separately if at all. Does that mean my approval of case four is unstable?

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that accommodates cases one through four cleanly, says something precise about case five, and resolves—or at least clarifies—my oscillation in the final case? I want the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on at least one further scenario I have not considered (for instance, selecting among embryos solely to maximize expected welfare when no risk of suffering is present), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it must overturn if it is to remain simple. I am not looking for named positions or a reading list; I want the internal logic of the commitments.
