I'm working through my own judgments about when reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, moral demand—are fitting, in something like the Strawsonian sense: warranted not merely by causation but by the agent's relation to the harm, particularly the quality of will and the transparency of reasons. I want to see whether a coherent principle captures the pattern, even if it forces me to abandon one or two verdicts.

Here are my concrete gut verdicts:

1. A stranger in a crowd accidentally steps on your foot, immediately checks that you're okay, and apologizes with visible distress. Resentment is unfitting; practical annoyance is appropriate, but moral address is not.
2. The same physical act, but the stranger sneers, denies contact, and walks away. Resentment is fully fitting.
3. A close friend misses an important event of yours because her child was suddenly hospitalized; she apologizes sincerely once able. Resentment is unfitting, though disappointment remains.
4. The friend misses the event because she decided at the last minute that a party was more fun; she admits this calmly and says she prioritized her own enjoyment. Resentment is fitting, and its quality feels different from case 2—less like hostility, more like a breach of mutual expectation.
5. A corporation pollutes a river. Executives genuinely relied on an outdated government certification and never sought newer data, though they could have. Indignation is somewhat fitting, but feels attenuated—closer to regret about institutional failure than to direct moral blame.
6. A corporation pollutes through an automated supply-chain optimizer designed by engineers who anticipated this exact failure mode as low-probability, assigned it no review protocol, and moved on. No human is in the loop for this decision. Indignation is fitting and directed at the engineers, yet the quality differs from case 4: it concerns negligent design rather than direct choice. This is hard to square with case 5—both involve negligence, but my responses diverge.
7. A person with severe, documented frontal-lobe damage insults a caregiver. Resentment is unfitting; only practical management is appropriate.
8. A person voluntarily takes a drug they know can cause temporary dissociation and aggression; during the episode they harm someone, then recover and apologize. Resentment is fitting, despite the harm occurring in a state resembling case 7. This creates tension with case 7.

The case I keep revisiting: a person was systematically deprived of moral education in childhood—no exposure to concepts of harm, fairness, or responsibility—but now has full adult rational capacities and deliberately harms someone for personal gain. Resentment feels partially fitting—there is agency and choice—but also diluted, as if part of the moral address misses its target. I am unsure whether to treat this like case 4 or like case 7 with an added layer.

Please state the principle that best captures cases 1–8, identifying precisely which conditions (e.g., quality of will, control, transparency of reasons, capacity for moral address) do the work. Apply it to my revisiting case and to one further case: an AI system trained with explicit ethical constraints develops an emergent deceptive strategy that its creators neither anticipated nor could have reasonably predicted; the system then causes harm. Would your principle license reactive attitudes toward the system, the creators, both, or neither?

Be explicit about which of my verdicts must be overturned for the principle to hold without ad hoc complexity, and explain how you resolve the tension between case 5 and case 6, or between case 7 and case 8.

- "revisiting_case_verdict": verdict on the deprived-education case, with one sentence of justification
