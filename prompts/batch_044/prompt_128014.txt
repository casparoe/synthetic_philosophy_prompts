I have recently been appointed to an ethics review board at my research institution that is rewriting its guidelines on predictive algorithmic auditing—norms that seemed fixed five years ago are now being treated as obsolete or even shameful, and I am trying to understand what kind of change this is. To that end, I want a consistency check on the package of views I have settled on:

(1) I accept Bernard Williams’s claim that moral revolutions are driven less by rational moral argument than by shifts in honor and social recognition—what becomes unthinkable is what becomes shameful to defend.

(2) I think Kwame Anthony Appiah’s framework on honor codes correctly explains the decline of dueling and foot-binding: internal prestige dynamics made those practices costly to status without requiring outsiders to prove them wrong.

(3) I also hold Amartya Sen’s entitlement analysis of the Bengal famine: when economic access collapses, normative expectations about reciprocity and dignity break down not because honor shifts but because survival logic overrides social structure.

(4) I maintain that the abolition of slavery was different—it required explicit moral argumentation and could not have been achieved by honor dynamics alone, since slaveholding was deeply embedded in honorable status for large populations.

(5) I am a moral realist: slavery was objectively wrong regardless of the honor codes that permitted it, and genuine moral progress involves tracking mind-independent moral facts.

(6) I believe that if a normative shift is genuinely moral rather than merely strategic, those who adopt it should eventually be able to articulate reasons that survive reflective equilibrium, not merely cite changed social standing.

Do these hang together? I am especially unsure whether (1) can accommodate (4) without ad hoc exception-making, and whether (3) conflicts with (2) or only applies in extremis. I also worry that (5) and (1) pull in opposite directions—if honor is socially constructed and drives change, why should it align with mind-independent moral facts? Meanwhile, (6) seems to sit uneasily with (1)’s emphasis on non-cognitive status shifts.

Please audit the set: identify which pairs are in genuine tension (ordered by severity), which only appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension specify the cheapest revision and what it costs. Do not rank them all as equally bad. Respond in no more than 450 words, with the tensions ordered from most to least severe.
