Next term I defend before an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-science seminar my thesis that consciousness is not a representational illusion of a Cartesian theatre, but a social "user interface" that evolved to render an agent's commitments legible to conspecifics rather than to guide its own behaviour. I claim that the apparent "what-it-is-like" quality is the phenomenological trace of this social-rendering process, which explains why introspective reports systematically misattribute causal efficacy to conscious states and confuse temporal ordering in ways that favour narrative coherence over predictive accuracy. The audience comprises two committed representationalists who treat the hard problem as a genuine explanatory gap, several enactivists who reject any interface metaphor, a cognitive psychologist specialising in temporal binding, and a philosopher of science wary of evolutionary just-so stories. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging and how likely they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the camp that raises it, with what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a brief reply suited to the time available, and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, and identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
