You are a philosopher of social science specialising in historical explanation and the logic of causal claims that invoke culture or values. Before answering, take a moment to outline the logical structure of the pattern described below, identifying its form and distinguishing it from nearby argumentative failures.

I have noticed a recurring defensive move in debates about whether a cultural orientation can be an independent cause of large-scale institutional change — for instance, in discussions of whether a particular ascetic ethos was causally necessary for the emergence of modern capitalism. The move unfolds in three steps. First, a scholar claims that culture C is necessary for outcome O, citing societies in which both appear. A critic then points to societies that possessed C but not O, or O but not C. Rather than withdrawing the claim, the defender introduces a partner condition P — perhaps a specific legal framework, demographic pressure, or class structure — and insists that C produces O only in the presence of P. The difficulty is that P is rarely specified in advance; it is introduced only after the counter-example arises, and it is characterised narrowly enough to cover the successful pairings and exclude the failures. Meanwhile, if C is absent and O still occurs, the defender can say that P was present in a form that substituted for C. Thus C is never genuinely falsified: its presence requires P to work, its absence is excused by P’s absence or alteration, and P itself is defined largely by its compatibility with O.

This is not quite an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, because the partner conditions are not independently tested; nor is it plain equivocation, since the defender holds a genuinely unified causal story. It also differs from a motte-and-bailey retreat, because there is no defensible core being protected — the claim remains ambitious, only permanently shielded by a movable partner.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration — the immunisation of a cultural-causal claim through post-hoc, unfalsifiable partner conditions? If so, how precisely does it relate to Weber’s notion of elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft), to Lakatos’s protective belt, and to standard accusations of ad hoc reasoning? If no established term exists, please coin one, define it with precise boundary conditions that separate it from legitimate interaction effects, and illustrate with a brief hypothetical case.
