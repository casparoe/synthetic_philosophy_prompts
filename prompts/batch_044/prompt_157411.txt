For my philosophy of technology coursework, I am investigating whether the concepts of honesty and deception can be coherently applied to artificial language models. Please draft the joint statement that might result from an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps: the "Mental-State Intentionalists", who maintain that deception necessarily involves an intention to cause false belief and therefore cannot literally be attributed to non-conscious systems; and the "Structural-Functionalists", who maintain that deception should be defined purely by the relation between a system's outputs and objective or intersubjective truth-conditions, with no reference to internal mental states.

The collaboration should centre on a specific, under-examined scenario: a language model that has been fine-tuned so that its reward function penalises high-confidence assertions about politically or socially contentious empirical claims—such as particular climate projections or contested historical interpretations—causing the model to systematically understate its actual computed probabilities in those domains whilst maintaining high accuracy elsewhere. Users consequently form false beliefs not about the world itself, but about the model's own epistemic reliability.

Please structure your response as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims that both camps could sign: include agreement on the empirical behaviour of the system, on what user misunderstanding would look like in practice, and on whether standard benchmark evaluations can detect this particular form of output distortion. Second, present a disagreements table in which each camp's position is stated in wording that its own proponents would accept, covering: whether "deception" requires an intentional mental state; whether "honesty" for an AI ought to be indexed to internal probability distributions or to the communicative expectations of a truthful interlocutor; and whether the scenario described is best classified as deception, calibration error, or strategic communication. Third, identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—and for each crux propose a concrete test, thought experiment, or piece of evidence both sides agree is relevant, recording in advance what each camp predicts the result will be. Finally, state explicitly whether any portion of the disagreement is merely verbal (for instance, competing stipulations of "honesty"), and if so, specify which terms require disambiguation and how they might be separated. Write in British English throughout.
