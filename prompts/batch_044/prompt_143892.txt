I have been working through the Hart–Fuller exchange on legal positivism and natural law, and I find the disagreement curiously immovable despite both sides addressing exactly the same cases — the Nazi informer statute, Hart’s gunman scenario, and Fuller’s eight principles of legality. Hart insists that law and morality are conceptually separate: a rule enacted by proper social sources remains valid law even if grossly immoral, though citizens may have a moral duty to resist it. Fuller counters that a system which systematically violates the principles of generality, publicity, prospectivity, and internal coherence fails to be law at all, lacking the internal morality that makes legal guidance possible. For decades, commentators have observed that the two sides seem to talk past one another: positivists treat Fuller’s conditions as functional prerequisites for efficacy rather than moral constraints, while natural-law readers treat Hart’s separation thesis as ignoring the normative authority that distinguishes law from brute command.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily a hidden empirical disagreement about whether immoral regimes can endure — though that empirical question is rarely examined — but rather a deeper divergence about what kind of explanation a jurisprudential theory is meant to provide. Hart appears to offer a descriptive sociology of institutional rules validated by pedigree, whereas Fuller offers an account of practical reason under authority, and they evaluate theoretical success by incompatible standards. Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether the dispute is instead verbal — two research programmes answering different questions under the single heading of what law is — or even an impasse resting on bedrock intuitions about authority that neither side can argue for. Could you analyse the structure of the disagreement concretely: is it substantive, verbal, or a misalignment of questions? In particular, is there a concealed disagreement about whether legal norms must be capable of guiding action in a morally loaded sense, or about whether validity is purely a matter of social source? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — could resolve or dissolve it: a sharper conceptual distinction, an empirical study of legal-system stability, a reformulation of the central question, or should we expect no progress because the disagreement is ultimately about starting intuitions?
