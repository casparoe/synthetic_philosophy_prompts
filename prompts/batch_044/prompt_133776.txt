I'm working on a novel where two old friends — Rafi, who spent years in a monastery, and Jules, a grad student in philosphy — argue about karma over cheap whisky. I want the scene to feel lived-in, not like a lecture, but I need to know who's actually holding ground on each point so I can write it honest. Can you adjudicate their exchange for me?

Rafi starts by saying karma's basically the best non-theistic theory of moral desert: you do harm, suffering eventually ripens for you, not because some god sends it, but because wrong action has the intrinsic shape of producing misery for the agent. Jules says that's a comfort myth people tell to make suffering feel ordered. In the texts he reads, karma is just conditioning — like planting seeds — with no judge, no scale, and no desert. If your mind is greedy, you suffer now; extending that across lives is just lengthening the causal chain, not balancing a cosmic ledger.

Rafi pushes back: but then why all the language about reaping exactly what you sow? That's proportionality, the heart of desert. Without that idea, karma collapses into physics with extra steps, and morality becomes pure prudence.

Jules answers that proportionality is just metaphor. The actual reason to be good is therapeutic: hatred literally damages your own awareness in the present moment. He adds that if karma really tracked moral desert, we'd see perfect correlation between vice and misery, but saints get sick and tyrants die in bed. So either karma is broken, or it never promised desert in the first place.

Then Rafi tries a different move. He says Jules is smuggling in a Western, post-Enlightenment idea of justice-as-equality. In karma, desert isn't comparative — it's not about matching people to outcomes across a population. It's internal consistency: your actions reconfigure your own future experience, like a self-modifying algorithm. So it's desert without comparison, and Jules is rejecting it only because he thinks desert requires a scoreboard.

Jules says that's just saving the word by draining it. If there's no comparison, no external standard, and no obligation being satisfied, then desert is just another name for causal consequence. Rafi's hiding behind terminology.

They turned to me and asked who was dodging whom. I honestly can't tell. Please go through the exchange round by round — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who's missing what, and tell me if there's something both overlook about moral responsibility once you drop both the ledger model and the pure-conditioning model. Don't give me citations or a reading list — I just need the logic straight for the scene.
