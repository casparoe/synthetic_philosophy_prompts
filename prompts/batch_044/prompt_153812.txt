Question: In Philosophical Investigations §258 Wittgenstein imagines a diarist who writes 'S' whenever a particular private sensation occurs. Explain why he thinks this can't establish a private language, using the beetle-in-the-box analogy. Then consider this twist: suppose she doesn't just record occurrence, but assigns each 'S' a number 1-10 using an internal 'gauge' calibrated against her own past entries. Could this internal calibration supply the missing grammatical rule? Defend your answer.

Student answer:
Wittgenstein's private language argument in PI §258 targets the idea that you can name a private sensation with 'S' by inwardly pointing to it. The diarist writes 'S' when the sensation happens, but since no one else can check, there's no difference between correct use and merely thinking it's correct. This is like the beetle-in-the-box: if everyone had a box with something inside called a beetle, but never looked, the word 'beetle' wouldn't refer to the contents because they drop out of the language-game. So for the diarist, 'S' drops out too—it's just a noise.

Now about the intensity gauge: if she assigns numbers 1-10 based on an internal calibration against her past entries, she's actually building a grammatical rule, not just an empirical association. The consistency over time gives her a criterion: she can check today's entry against yesterday's scale. So Wittgenstein's objection about no criterion of correctness is answered, because the scale functions like a ruler she applies to herself. The only remaining issue is empirical—her memory might be unreliable, which Wittgenstein admits is a practical problem but not the philosophical one. Thus, with the numerical gauge, a private language becomes possible, at least for intensity-tracking.
